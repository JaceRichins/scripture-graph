# What happens to people who never heard of Christ?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
This is the question on which the Restoration has its most distinctive and
most generous answer. Most of humanity has lived and died without hearing
the gospel; a God who condemned them for it would be neither just nor
loving, and Christians have always felt the problem. Latter-day Saints
teach that the gospel is preached to the dead in the spirit world
([[1 Peter 3]]–[[1 Peter 4]], [[D&C 138]]), that the ordinances they could
not receive in life are performed for them by proxy in temples
([[1 Corinthians 15]]:29), and that every person will have a full and fair
chance to accept or reject Christ before judgment. Salvation is still
through Christ alone; the opportunity is universal.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- The New Testament itself says Christ "preached unto the spirits in
  prison" and that "the gospel was preached also to them that are dead"
  ([[1 Peter 3]]:19, [[1 Peter 4]]:6) — verses historic Christianity has
  struggled to use, and which the Restoration takes literally.
- Paul mentions baptism for the dead as a practice known to the
  Corinthians and argues *from* it ([[1 Corinthians 15]]:29); early
  Christian groups practiced it.
- The doctrine answers the moral problem cleanly: God judges by "the light
  they have received" and gives more, rather than by accident of birth
  ([[2 Nephi 9]], [[Moroni 8]] on children; [[D&C 137]] on those who "would
  have received it").
- Joseph F. Smith's 1918 vision ([[D&C 138]]) is canonized scripture on the
  organization of that preaching — a rare case of a modern revelation
  expanding a New Testament seed.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- If everyone gets a full chance after death, why urgency about missionary
  work, and why would earthly choices matter?
- Proxy ordinances for people who never asked for them (including
  historical figures and Holocaust victims, which caused real offense)
  seem presumptuous.
- Two verses in 1 Peter and one in 1 Corinthians are a slender textual base
  for a large doctrine; most exegetes read them differently.
- Other traditions solve the problem without temples — inclusivism (Rahner,
  Vatican II) holds that Christ saves the sincere of every faith without
  ordinances.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Latter-day Saints reply that the chance after death is *equal*, not
*easier* — those who reject the gospel knowingly in life are in a different
position ([[D&C 76]]) — and that missionary work brings blessings now, not
only later; that proxy ordinances confer no obligation, since the dead
remain free to accept or refuse; that the Church restricted work for
unrelated famous persons and Holocaust victims precisely because of the
offense; and that the doctrine's scriptural base is small because the
Restoration claims to *restore* what was lost — the point is that something
this important survives in the New Testament only in fragments.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
This is a place where the Restoration is at its most coherent and most
humane: it takes the hardest problem of Christian exclusivism and answers
it with universal opportunity and Christ-alone salvation at once. The
textual base is real but thin, and the practice of proxy work needs the
care the Church now takes with it. A believer can present this doctrine
without hedging; a critic should note that it answers the moral objection
better than most alternatives, whatever one thinks of the temple.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
