# Is the Old Testament historically reliable?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
It depends on the period. For the divided monarchy onward (roughly 900 BC
and later) the biblical record is repeatedly confirmed by Assyrian and
Babylonian inscriptions, seals, and archaeology — kings named in Kings and
Chronicles appear in foreign annals, Hezekiah's tunnel and Sennacherib's
siege are attested on both sides. For the united monarchy the evidence is
thinner but real (the Tel Dan inscription names the "house of David"). For
the exodus, the conquest, and the patriarchs, archaeology has found no
direct evidence and some counter-evidence (Jericho's walls predate Joshua;
no trace of a large Sinai encampment), and most scholars treat those
narratives as containing memory shaped by later theology rather than
reportage. The primeval history (Genesis 1–11) is a different kind of
literature altogether.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Dozens of biblical figures are attested outside the Bible: Omri, Ahab,
  Jehu, Hezekiah, Manasseh, Jehoiachin (his rations listed in Babylonian
  tablets), Cyrus, Sanballat, and more; no other ancient national history
  is this well anchored.
- Details that later writers would not know — Egyptian names in the Joseph
  story, the price of a slave in Genesis matching second-millennium rates,
  treaty forms in Deuteronomy matching Hittite patterns — suggest early
  material survives in the traditions.
- The Israelite emergence in the Canaanite highlands around 1200 BC, a
  distinct culture with no pig bones, is archaeologically visible and fits
  the biblical claim of a people who came from outside.
- The Book of Mormon independently attests the existence of the brass
  plates (a text of the Law and prophets) by 600 BC, which is earlier than
  some critical datings of those books allow.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- No Egyptian record of Israel in Egypt or of a mass departure exists; two
  million people crossing Sinai would leave traces, and none are found.
- Excavations at Jericho, Ai and other conquest sites show them unoccupied
  or already destroyed in Joshua's period; the "conquest" looks more like
  gradual settlement.
- The Pentateuch shows signs of composition from multiple sources centuries
  after the events, with the exile as a major shaping moment.
- Chronicles and Kings sometimes disagree with each other and with the
  inscriptions on numbers and details.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believing scholars note that "no evidence" in Sinai is expected for a
nomadic group of any plausible size (the Hebrew word for "thousand" may
mean a clan unit, lowering the numbers dramatically); that Egypt did not
record defeats; that Joshua itself describes a partial conquest and
long coexistence ([[Judges 1]]), so the archaeology and the text are
closer than the popular contrast suggests; and that late composition of
the final form is compatible with early sources — which is what the
brass plates in [[1 Nephi 5]] describe. Latter-day Saints also hold that
the text has suffered losses ("plain and precious things," [[1 Nephi 13]]),
which lowers the stakes of any particular discrepancy.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The Old Testament is reliable history where it can be checked and
unverified — not disproven — where it cannot, with the exodus and conquest
narratives the genuine hard cases. A believer should not claim archaeology
"proves the Bible"; it confirms much of the monarchy and is silent or
awkward on the earlier periods. A skeptic should not claim it "disproves"
the exodus; absence of evidence for a small nomadic group three thousand
years ago is what one would expect. The theological claims of these books
never rested on their being modern history.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
