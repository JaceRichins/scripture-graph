# Did Joseph Smith copy the Book of Mormon from other books?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Three candidate sources have been proposed since the 1830s: Solomon
Spalding's unpublished romance *Manuscript Story* (the "Spalding theory"),
Ethan Smith's *View of the Hebrews* (1823, a theory that Native Americans
descend from the lost tribes), and, more recently, *The Late War* and other
KJV-style texts of the period. The Spalding manuscript was found in 1884 and
does not resemble the Book of Mormon; the theory survives only by positing a
second, lost manuscript. *View of the Hebrews* shares the general
Israelite-origins idea that was common in Joseph's region but almost none of
the Book of Mormon's narrative, names or theology. No direct copying has
ever been demonstrated; what critics can show is that the *ideas* of
Israelite Indians and KJV diction were in the air.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Spalding's actual manuscript (Oberlin College) is a Roman-era romance in
  a different style with different names and no religious content of the
  Book of Mormon's kind; the theory's defenders had to invent a second
  manuscript no one has seen.
- B. H. Roberts, a General Authority, made the strongest *internal* case
  for *View of the Hebrews* influence in the 1920s; his own listed parallels
  are mostly generic (Israelite origins, a lost book, a prophecy of
  gathering), and the Book of Mormon contradicts Ethan Smith on key points.
- The witnesses to the dictation, including hostile ones, saw no books or
  manuscripts; the dictation ran without pauses for consultation.
- Computational stylometry studies conflict, but the better-designed ones
  find multiple distinct authorial voices in the text rather than one
  nineteenth-century author.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Direct copying is a straw man; the serious claim is that Joseph absorbed
  his environment — the Israelite-Indian theory, Methodist revivalism,
  anti-Masonry, republican ideals — and composed from it, and the text does
  reflect all of these.
- *View of the Hebrews* was published in Vermont near where Oliver Cowdery
  grew up (its author was Cowdery's family's minister), which is an
  uncomfortable coincidence.
- Long stretches of KJV Isaiah and Matthew are in the book verbatim; that
  is copying, whatever its explanation.
- Ancient-sounding names and structures can be produced by a gifted
  imitator steeped in the Bible.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believers accept that Joseph's environment shaped the *English* of the
translation and do not need the text to be free of its era's idiom; they
argue that "absorbed the environment" cannot explain the features that were
*not* in the environment — Hebrew literary forms (chiasmus, colophons,
covenant-renewal patterns), the Arabian route, names later attested in
ancient sources, and a coherent internal chronology — and that the text
argues *against* several ideas common in 1829 (it is not anti-Catholic, it
is ambivalent about the American Republic, and its Indians are not the ten
tribes). The KJV quotations are addressed under their own question.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The specific source theories have not held up: Spalding is refuted by his
own manuscript, and *View of the Hebrews* is a thematic cousin at most. The
broader "environmental composition" theory is the real alternative to
antiquity, and it cannot be refuted by finding no single source — it has to
be weighed against the evidence for ancient features. Honest people weigh
that evidence differently; what is not honest is to say either that a source
has been found or that the text shows no trace of 1829.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
