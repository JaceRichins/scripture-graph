# Why did God command the conquest of Canaan?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The commands in Deuteronomy and Joshua to destroy the Canaanite nations
are the hardest passages in the Bible, and they should be read as hard.
The texts give their own reason — the nations' practices, including child
sacrifice, and the danger of Israel's assimilation ([[Deuteronomy 9]],
[[Deuteronomy 20]]) — and the history of interpretation adds others: that
"utterly destroy" is ancient Near Eastern war rhetoric (the same books
report the nations still present afterward); that the commands were
limited to specific cities and a single generation, never a general
license; and that later scripture, above all Jesus, moves decisively away
from holy war. Latter-day Saints also hold that the Bible has not come to
us intact, and that a command recorded is not the same as a command given.
None of these makes the texts comfortable. They are not meant to be.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Joshua itself undercuts a literal total destruction: cities said to be
  wiped out are inhabited by Canaanites in the next chapter and in Judges;
  "left none remaining" is the same hyperbole found in Egyptian and
  Moabite victory inscriptions.
- The commands are explicitly tied to a moral rationale and are bounded —
  Israel is warned it will suffer the same fate for the same sins
  ([[Leviticus 18]]), which it does.
- Rahab, the Gibeonites, and Ruth show Canaanites and Moabites spared and
  incorporated; the category was religious, not ethnic.
- The trajectory of the canon runs from the ban to "love your enemies";
  Jesus rebukes disciples who want fire on a Samaritan village
  ([[Luke 9]]). The Book of Mormon's most admired warrior refuses
  aggressive war ([[Alma 48]]).
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Hyperbole or not, the texts command killing women and children and
  praise its execution; softening the language does not remove the
  command.
- "They were wicked" is the justification every conqueror gives; the
  Canaanites did not write their side.
- If God could command this once, the argument from divine command can
  justify anything, and it has been used to.
- A God who orders the death of infants for the sins of their parents is
  not the God Jesus describes.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believers take different lines and the vault should present them fairly:
(1) the texts are accurate and the command was a unique judgment by the
one being entitled to give it, on nations given four centuries to repent
([[Genesis 15]]); (2) the texts record Israel's understanding of God's will
in its own idiom, and that understanding was partial — the Latter-day Saint
doctrine that scripture is the word of God through fallible writers makes
this reading available without abandoning inspiration; (3) the rhetoric is
conventional and the reality was limited warfare against fortified sites.
What believers agree on: the passages cannot ground any later violence, and
the God fully revealed in Christ is the standard by which they are read.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
There is no reading that makes these chapters easy, and the tradition's
best interpreters have said so for two thousand years. The honest position
for a believer is that the canon's own arc — and Christ himself — is the
lens, that the Restoration's view of scripture leaves room for human
distortion in the record, and that discomfort here is a sign of moral
health, not weak faith. The honest position for a critic is that these
texts are answered within the Bible itself, not only from outside it.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
