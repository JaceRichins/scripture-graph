# Why does the Book of Mormon quote Isaiah and the King James Bible?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Roughly a third of Isaiah is quoted in the Book of Mormon — [[2 Nephi 12]]
through [[2 Nephi 24]] alone reproduce Isaiah 2–14 — plus Malachi and the
Sermon on the Mount ([[3 Nephi 12]]–[[3 Nephi 14]]), all in language that
closely tracks the King James Version, including some passages scholars
consider late or mistranslated in the KJV. The text's own explanation is
that the Nephites carried the brass plates (an Isaiah text) and that Christ
delivered the sermon himself. The translation's *English* is a separate
question from its *source*: a KJV-shaped rendering could be how a revealed
translation reached 1829 readers, or it could be evidence that the KJV was
the source. Both readings have to account for the hundreds of small
variants where the Book of Mormon text differs from the KJV.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- The quoted Isaiah differs from the KJV in hundreds of places, and some of
  those variants agree with ancient witnesses (Dead Sea Scrolls, Septuagint)
  against the KJV — not many, but not zero.
- Quoting a familiar translation is what translators of scripture into
  English have always done (the KJV itself borrowed heavily from Tyndale);
  the register signals "scripture" to the reader.
- The Book of Mormon's use of Isaiah is interpretive and structural
  ([[2 Nephi 25]] explains why Nephi quotes him), which is a literary
  argument, not just a copying one.
- A text pretending to antiquity would be expected to avoid the obvious
  tell; the openness of the borrowing is at least odd for a forger.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- The Book of Mormon reproduces KJV *italicized* words (translators'
  insertions) and known KJV errors, which an independent ancient source
  would not contain.
- Isaiah 40–66 (quoted in the Book of Mormon) is dated by most critical
  scholars to the exile or later — after Lehi left Jerusalem with the brass
  plates in 600 BC.
- The Sermon on the Mount in 3 Nephi follows Matthew's Greek-derived
  wording, including phrases that likely originated in Greek transmission.
- The simplest explanation of KJV language is a KJV on the table.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believing scholars respond variously: that the translation was revealed in
the "language of the reader" and the KJV *was* that language; that the
Deutero-Isaiah dating rests on assumptions about prophecy that a believer
need not share, and that even on critical dating some "late" material may
be earlier; that the Book of Mormon Isaiah's variants show a text being
worked with, not copied; and that the witnesses' consistent testimony of no
Bible on the table has to be weighed, not waved away. Skousen's critical
text project has catalogued the variants precisely so that the argument can
be had on the evidence.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The KJV dependence in the English text is real and is the strongest
text-critical argument against a straightforward "ancient document rendered
word for word." Whether it is also an argument against an ancient *source*
depends on what one thinks translation by revelation would look like — and
that is not a question the variants can answer. A believer can say honestly:
the English came through a nineteenth-century mind steeped in the KJV; the
claim is about the source, not the diction. A critic can say honestly: the
diction is exactly what a nineteenth-century composition would produce.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
