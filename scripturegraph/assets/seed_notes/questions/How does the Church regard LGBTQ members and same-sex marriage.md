# How does the Church regard LGBTQ members and same-sex marriage?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Church teaches that marriage between a man and a woman is ordained of
God and central to his plan (The Family: A Proclamation to the World, 1995),
that sexual relations belong only within that marriage, and that same-sex
marriage is therefore not sanctioned. At the same time it teaches that
same-sex attraction is not itself a sin, that members who experience it are
full members with every blessing available to those who keep the same
standard of chastity asked of all unmarried members, and that harshness,
exclusion and cruelty toward LGBTQ people are contrary to the gospel. The
tension between those two commitments is real, is felt most by LGBTQ
members and their families, and is the subject of ongoing teaching, policy
adjustment, and honest pain within the Church.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- The scriptural and historical witness on marriage is consistent across
  the canon: [[Genesis 2]], Jesus's own citation of it ([[Matthew 19]]),
  and Restoration scripture ([[D&C 49]]) all define marriage as man and
  woman; the Church is not inventing the standard.
- The Church distinguishes attraction from action and asks the same
  chastity of all unmarried members; the standard is not singled out for
  one group, however differently it lands.
- The Church has supported legal protections for LGBTQ people in housing
  and employment (Utah, 2015), softened policies (2019 reversal of the 2015
  handbook rules on children of same-sex couples), and repeatedly condemned
  mistreatment.
- The Church's doctrine of the family — eternal marriage, exaltation as a
  couple — is the theological reason the question is hard for it in a way
  it is not for some other churches; the position follows from the
  theology, not from animus.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Asking a class of people to forgo marriage and intimacy for life, on the
  basis of an orientation they did not choose, is a heavier burden than the
  "same standard" framing admits; loneliness and mental-health outcomes
  among LGBTQ members are documented.
- The Church's political involvement (Proposition 8 in 2008) and the 2015
  policy caused lasting harm and were later partly walked back, which
  suggests to critics that the position is more contingent than the
  theology claims.
- The Church has changed on marriage before (plural marriage) and on
  categories once thought fixed (race); why not this?
- Eternal-family theology has no clear place for gay members, and "it will
  be worked out in the next life" is thin comfort now.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believing responses do not deny the burden; the better ones name it and
argue that the gospel has always asked things of disciples that the world
finds unreasonable, that the doctrine of marriage is not a policy the
Church is free to change but a revealed pattern it would need revelation to
alter, and that the Church's task is to hold the standard *and* to make
belonging real — which it has done imperfectly and is trying to do better.
Many faithful LGBTQ members and their families are the ones doing the
hardest and most honest work here, and their voices belong in this question.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
This is a question where the doctrine is clear and the lived cost is high,
and where honesty requires saying both without softening either. The
Church's position is not likely to change without revelation it has not
claimed; its practice toward LGBTQ members has changed and is still
changing. A believer can hold the doctrine of eternal marriage and grieve
what it asks of some; a critic can grant the theological coherence and still
judge the burden unjust. A vault that pretends this is easy would be lying
to the people who most need it to be truthful.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
