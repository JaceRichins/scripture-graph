# Is the Book of Abraham a translation of the papyri?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The surviving fragments of the papyri Joseph Smith acquired in 1835 are
ordinary Egyptian funerary texts (a Book of Breathings for a priest named
Hor, and parts of a Book of the Dead), dated to roughly the first centuries
BC — many centuries after Abraham. Egyptologists, including believing ones,
agree that the characters on the surviving fragments do not say what the
Book of Abraham says. The Church acknowledges this. The live questions are
whether the source text was on a portion of the papyri now lost, whether
"translation" here means revelation prompted by the papyri rather than
decoding them, and whether the book's content bears marks of antiquity that
its Egyptian wrapper does not.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Only a fraction of the original papyri survived (most were lost in the
  1871 Chicago fire); eyewitnesses described a long roll, so the source of
  the text cannot be checked directly.
- Several details in the book — Abraham's near-sacrifice, the "plain of
  Olishem", a god named Elkenah, the covenant narrative — have parallels in
  ancient texts Joseph Smith could not have known (e.g. the Apocalypse of
  Abraham, the Testament of Abraham), which some scholars find striking.
- The Facsimile 1 scene (a figure on a lion couch with a standing figure)
  has been read as unusual among lion-couch vignettes in ways some believing
  Egyptologists (Kerry Muhlestein, John Gee) argue fit the book's account.
- Joseph's "Egyptian Alphabet and Grammar" documents show he and his scribes
  were attempting something with the characters; what, exactly, is debated
  in both directions.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- The papyrus fragment that Joseph's manuscripts place beside the opening of
  the Book of Abraham is the Hor Book of Breathings; the characters do not
  match the English text in any known sense of translation.
- Joseph's explanations of the facsimiles (e.g. Facsimile 3's figures as
  Abraham, Pharaoh, and a prince) do not match the Egyptian labels, which
  identify Osiris, Isis, and the deceased Hor.
- The Kirtland Egyptian Papers suggest Joseph believed he was working from
  these very characters, which the "missing roll" theory has to set aside.
- The text's astronomy and cosmology (Kolob, governing stars) read to many
  as nineteenth-century speculation, not ancient science.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believing scholars offer two main frameworks: a *missing-papyrus* view (the
Abraham text was on a portion now lost) and a *catalyst* view (the papyri
prompted a revelation, as the Bible prompted the Joseph Smith Translation,
with the resulting text ancient in origin but not written on those sheets).
The Church's Gospel Topics essay presents both. Defenders add that ancient
Egyptian funerary documents were sometimes reused for Semitic religious
texts, and that the facsimile explanations, while not Egyptological, may
reflect how a Jewish-Egyptian community in the Greco-Roman period reused
Egyptian iconography.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
This is the Restoration question where the documentary case against a
conventional translation is strongest, and honest believing scholarship says
so. The book's value for a believer rests on revelation and on its content —
the Abrahamic covenant, premortal life, creation by councils — rather than on
a demonstrable link between Egyptian characters and English sentences. A
reader should know that the "missing papyrus" and "catalyst" positions are
responses to a real problem, not a refutation of it, and that the ancient
parallels are intriguing rather than conclusive.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
