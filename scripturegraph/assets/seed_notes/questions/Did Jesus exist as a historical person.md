# Did Jesus exist as a historical person?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Yes. The existence of Jesus of Nazareth as a first-century Jewish teacher
executed under Pontius Pilate is accepted by essentially all professional
historians of the period, including secular and skeptical ones. Independent
non-Christian references (Josephus, Tacitus), the early date of Paul's
letters (which mention Jesus's brother James as a living man Paul had met),
and the multiple independent Gospel traditions make "mythicism" a fringe
position. What is debated is not whether Jesus lived but what he said and
did, and what the Gospels' portraits owe to memory versus later theology.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Tacitus (*Annals* 15.44, c. AD 116) reports that "Christus" was executed
  under Pilate in Tiberius's reign — a hostile Roman source with no
  Christian motive.
- Josephus (*Antiquities* 20.200) refers in passing to "the brother of
  Jesus who was called Christ, James by name"; the passage is accepted as
  authentic by nearly all scholars, unlike the more disputed *Testimonium*.
- Paul, writing in the 50s, knew Peter and James personally ([[Galatians
  1]]–[[Galatians 2]]) and treats Jesus as a recently living man with a
  family, a Last Supper and a crucifixion.
- The criterion of embarrassment: the tradition preserves things no
  inventor would choose — a baptism by John for repentance, a crucifixion
  (a curse in Jewish law, a disgrace in Roman eyes), disciples who fail.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- No contemporary eyewitness writing survives; the earliest sources are
  two decades later and all Christian.
- Josephus's longer passage (*Testimonium Flavianum*) has clearly been
  tampered with by Christian copyists, which casts a shadow on the shorter
  one.
- Mythicists argue that Paul's Jesus is a celestial figure and that the
  Gospels are later narrative fictions built from scripture (Carrier,
  Price).
- The miracle stories and infancy narratives are legendary in form, which
  raises the question of where legend stops.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Historians answer that a two-decade gap is short by ancient standards
(Alexander's earliest surviving biographies are centuries later); that
Paul's references to Jesus's birth "of a woman," Davidic descent, brothers,
and crucifixion are not celestial; that the tampered *Testimonium* still
has a probable authentic core and the James passage is independent of it;
and that legendary elements in a biography do not make its subject a
legend — they are normal in ancient lives of real people. Mythicism has no
foothold in university history departments, and not because of piety.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
This one is settled as far as history settles anything: Jesus lived, taught,
gathered disciples and was crucified. The open questions are what he
claimed about himself and what happened afterward — the subjects of the
neighboring questions. A believer can cite the consensus without apology; a
skeptic should not confuse "the Gospels contain theology" with "the Gospels
have no history."
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
