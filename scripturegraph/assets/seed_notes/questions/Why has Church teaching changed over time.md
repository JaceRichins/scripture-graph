# Why has Church teaching changed over time?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Because the Church claims *continuing* revelation, not a closed deposit:
"we believe that He will yet reveal many great and important things"
(Articles of Faith 9). Plural marriage was practiced and then ended; the
priesthood and temple restriction was imposed and then lifted; Brigham
Young's Adam–God teaching was repudiated by later prophets; temple ceremonies
have been revised repeatedly; the Word of Wisdom moved from counsel to
requirement. Latter-day Saints distinguish *doctrine* (the core of the
gospel, which they hold constant) from *policy*, *practice* and leaders'
personal views (which change). Critics observe that the line between those
categories is drawn after the fact. Both observations are accurate.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Scripture itself shows revealed practice changing: circumcision and the
  law of Moses set aside ([[Acts 15]]), the gospel opened to Gentiles by a
  vision Peter resisted ([[Acts 10]]), Nephi commanded to do what the law
  forbade ([[1 Nephi 4]]). Change by revelation is the biblical pattern.
- The Church's foundational claim is a living prophet, which makes change
  a feature of the system rather than an embarrassment to it; a church that
  never changed would have refuted its own premise.
- The core has in fact been stable: Christ's atonement, the Restoration
  through Joseph Smith, the Book of Mormon, priesthood authority, temples,
  the family — the 1830 and 2020 Church teach the same center.
- The Church has become more, not less, transparent about its changes (the
  Gospel Topics essays, *Saints*, the Joseph Smith Papers).
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Things once taught as eternal doctrine (plural marriage as necessary for
  exaltation, the priesthood restriction, Adam–God) were later called
  policy or error — which suggests "doctrine" means "what we currently
  teach."
- Some changes track external pressure (federal law in 1890, civil rights
  and expansion in 1978, cultural shifts since), which fits a sociological
  model of institutional adaptation better than revelation.
- If prophets can be that wrong that long, on what basis is the current
  teaching trusted?
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believers reply that revelation arriving when circumstances press is exactly
how scripture describes it (Peter's vision came when Cornelius was already
at the door); that prophets have never been claimed to be infallible — Joseph
Smith said so bluntly — and that Latter-day Saints sustain leaders as
authorized, not as inerrant; and that the test of a teaching is its
alignment with the scriptures and the united voice of the First Presidency
and Twelve, which is a real criterion even if it is applied over time. They
concede that leaders have sometimes taught opinion as doctrine and that the
Church has been slow to say so.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
A church with living prophets will change, and it has; the honest questions
are which changes were revelation, which were correction, and how a member
tells the difference in real time. The Church's own answer — the canon and
the united senior councils — is a workable standard, not a mechanical one.
Faith here means trusting a process that has visibly self-corrected while
knowing that "self-corrected" implies "was wrong." That is uncomfortable and
it is also the biblical story of God's people.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
