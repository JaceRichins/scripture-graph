# Does DNA evidence disprove the Book of Mormon?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Population genetics finds that the ancestry of Indigenous peoples of the
Americas traces overwhelmingly to East Asia via ancient migrations, with no
detected Near Eastern signal. That is a real problem for the older popular
reading that Lehi's family were the principal ancestors of all Native
Americans — a reading the Book of Mormon text does not require and the
Church no longer teaches. It is not a test the text can pass or fail as
written: a small founding group entering a populated continent 2,600 years
ago would very likely leave no detectable genetic trace today. DNA closes
one reading of the book; it does not, and cannot, settle the historicity
question.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- The text itself hints at other peoples: population sizes that grow faster
  than a single family could supply, references to peoples "in the land"
  that are never explained, and a geography that reads as regional rather
  than hemispheric.
- Genetic drift, founder effects and the bottleneck of European contact
  (which erased an estimated 90% of Indigenous population) make the loss of
  a small lineage's signal expected rather than surprising.
- We do not know what Lehi's or Sariah's DNA looked like; "Israelite DNA"
  is not a defined marker, and the Middle East of 600 BC was itself
  genetically mixed.
- Careful believing geneticists (Ugo Perego, Michael Whiting) made these
  points before the Church's essay did; the essay adopts them.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Church leaders and the book's own introduction (until 2006: "the
  principal ancestors of the American Indians") taught the hemispheric
  reading for generations; the retreat to a "limited geography" looks like
  moving the goalposts after the data came in.
- "We would expect no evidence" is unfalsifiable; a claim that predicts
  nothing explains nothing.
- The text's scale (millions of Nephites and Lamanites, vast wars) sits
  awkwardly with a lineage too small to leave a trace.
- Ancient DNA studies now sample remains going back thousands of years and
  still find no Near Eastern component.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Defenders concede the hemispheric model is dead and argue it was always an
inference by readers, not a claim of the text; that limited-geography
readings predate DNA science by decades (John Sorenson's work in the 1980s);
that the unfalsifiability charge cuts both ways — absence of evidence for a
small group is what the science itself predicts; and that "Lamanite" in the
text and in Church usage has long been a covenant and cultural category more
than a strictly genetic one, which is why the 2006 wording changed.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
DNA settles what many members believed for a century — that Native Americans
descend chiefly from Lehi — against them. It does not settle whether Lehi
lived. A believer should be honest that the limited-geography model became
mainstream partly because it had to, and that "no evidence expected" is a
defense, not a confirmation. A critic should be honest that the science, as
science, cannot rule out a small ancient Near Eastern migration. The
question the evidence leaves open is the one faith has always had to answer.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
