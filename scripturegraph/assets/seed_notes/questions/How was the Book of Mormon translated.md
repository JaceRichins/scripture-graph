# How was the Book of Mormon translated?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
By Joseph Smith's own description, "by the gift and power of God." The
witnesses who watched describe a process: Joseph used the Nephite
interpreters (two stones in a breastplate, later called the Urim and Thummim)
and, for most of the text, a single seer stone placed in a hat to block the
light, dictating to a scribe ([[Oliver Cowdery]], [[Emma Smith]], [[Martin
Harris]] and others) without the plates open before him. The manuscript
shows a dictation of some 270,000 words in roughly 65 working days with
almost no revision. The mechanics are better documented than most people
assume; what happened inside the process is precisely what a document
cannot show.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Multiple independent witnesses — believers and later critics alike —
  agree on the hat, the stone, the absence of notes or books, and the
  fluent dictation (Emma Smith's 1879 interview; David Whitmer's *Address to
  All Believers*).
- The original manuscript (Royal Skousen's critical text project) shows a
  dictated text: spelled-by-sound names, scribal errors of hearing, no
  evidence of drafting.
- The speed and internal complexity (hundreds of consistent names, internal
  cross-references, embedded ancient forms) are hard to account for by
  extemporaneous composition — the text itself is the evidence.
- The Church's own publication of the seer stone photographs (2015) and the
  Gospel Topics essay on translation put the process in the open.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- If the plates were not consulted, what was their purpose? Critics read the
  stone-in-hat as folk magic (Joseph's earlier treasure-seeking used the same
  stone) rather than translation.
- The text contains King James phrasing, including KJV translation errors
  and italicized words, suggesting dependence on the English Bible rather
  than an ancient source.
- Nineteenth-century ideas (revival rhetoric, anti-Masonic themes, Indian
  origins theories) appear in the text; critics see Joseph's environment,
  not antiquity.
- Church art long showed Joseph reading from the plates; the change in how
  the process is described looks to some like concealment.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Defenders note that revelation through physical instruments is thoroughly
biblical (Urim and Thummim, the rod, the ephod); that "translation" in
Joseph's usage meant conveying by revelation, not decoding characters; that
KJV language is what a 1829 English audience would expect a scripture to
sound like and may reflect the translation's target register rather than its
source; and that the plates' role as tangible evidence for witnesses is
itself a purpose. The art question is fair: depictions lagged the documents,
though the documents were never secret.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The process is well attested: stone, hat, dictation, speed, no notes. The
explanation is where belief and skepticism part — either a genuine
revelatory translation or a remarkable feat of oral composition. The
strongest honest claim for believers is that the mechanism is stranger than
Sunday-school art but better documented than critics allow; the strongest
honest claim for skeptics is that the same documents describe a folk-magic
instrument. The text's content, not the hat, is where the argument actually
has to be settled.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
