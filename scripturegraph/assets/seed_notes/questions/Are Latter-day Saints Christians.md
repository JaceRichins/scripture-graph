# Are Latter-day Saints Christians?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
By the plain meaning of the word — people who worship Jesus Christ as the
divine Son of God, Savior, and risen Lord, and try to follow him — yes,
unambiguously; the Church's name, scripture, ordinances and worship center
on him. By the narrower meaning some other Christians use — acceptance of the
creeds of the fourth and fifth centuries (Nicaea, Chalcedon) as the test of
orthodoxy — no, and Latter-day Saints do not claim otherwise: they hold that
the Godhead is three distinct persons united in purpose, that God the Father
has a glorified body, and that the creeds represent a departure from the
original faith. So the answer depends on who is defining "Christian," and
honesty requires saying so rather than arguing about the word.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- The Book of Mormon's stated purpose is to convince readers "that Jesus is
  the Christ" (title page); it names him thousands of times and its climax
  is his ministry in [[3 Nephi 11]]–[[3 Nephi 28]].
- Latter-day Saint worship, baptism, sacrament, and temple rites are
  explicitly in Christ's name; the Church's 1995 statement of belief and
  its 2000 apostolic declaration "The Living Christ" are Christ-centered
  creeds in all but name.
- The New Testament itself never uses the later creeds as the test of
  discipleship; its own test is confessing Jesus as Lord and doing his will
  ([[Romans 10]], [[Matthew 7]]).
- Early Christianity was more diverse on the nature of God than the creeds
  later allowed; "subordinationist" and embodied-God views existed before
  Nicaea.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Trinitarian Christians hold that rejecting the Trinity as classically
  defined is rejecting the Christian God; on that definition, a religion with
  three separate beings and a corporeal Father is a different religion using
  the same names.
- Additional scripture, a modern prophet, exaltation (humans becoming like
  God), and the claim that all other churches lost authority put the Church
  outside historic Christianity by its own choice.
- The word "Christian" has a historical community attached to it; a new
  movement cannot simply claim it by self-description.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Latter-day Saints reply that the creeds are post-biblical philosophy, that
the Bible's language about the Father and Son (Jesus praying to the Father,
"my Father is greater than I," Stephen seeing Jesus at God's right hand)
reads naturally as distinct persons, and that deification language appears
in the New Testament ([[2 Peter 1]]: "partakers of the divine nature") and
in early church fathers (Irenaeus, Athanasius). They also note that
"Christian" in the first century meant followers of Christ, which the
Church's members demonstrably are, and that being labeled non-Christian by
other Christians has a long and unhappy history.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
Both sides are right about the facts and disagree about the definition.
Latter-day Saints are Christians in the biblical and ordinary sense and are
not creedal Christians in the theological sense, and they say so themselves.
The dispute is really about whether the creeds are a faithful development or
a departure — a question of history and theology, not name-calling. A
believer need not win the word to hold the faith; a critic should at least
say which definition is in play.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
