# VerbalTextual Parallel in Jacob 1

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[Jacob 1#^jacob-1-7|Jacob 1:7]] combines the ordered provocation, temptation, and wilderness vocabulary of [[Psalm 95#^ps-95-8|Psalm 95:8]] and [[Hebrews 3#^heb-3-8|Hebrews 3:8]] with their surrounding warning about divine wrath and exclusion from God's rest. This strongly supports Psalm 95/Hebrews 3 or KJV-shaped mediation in the final English wording. Translation, harmonization, memorized biblical idiom, and direct nineteenth-century reuse remain viable mechanisms, so the relationship does not determine antiquity, historicity, or inspiration.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Jacob 1:7 reads 'as in the provocation in the days of temptation while the children of Israel were in the wilderness.' Psalm 95:8 (KJV) reads 'as in the provocation, and as in the day of temptation in the wilderness,' and Hebrews 3:8 (quoting the psalm) reads 'as in the provocation, in the day of temptation in the wilderness.' All three share the same three-part collocation -- provocation, day(s) of temptation, wilderness -- in the same order, which is not the phrasing used in the underlying narrative source, Numbers 14.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The ordered provocation, temptation, and wilderness vocabulary, reinforced by the surrounding wrath and exclusion-from-rest language, strongly supports mediation through the Psalm 95/Hebrews 3 complex. This need not imply conscious citation: translation, harmonization, memorized biblical idiom, and direct literary reuse are all possible mechanisms of the same mediation.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
This is consistent with, and one more data point in, the corpus's well-established pattern of KJV-shaped phrasing appearing at points of thematic overlap with the Bible (the same phenomenon documented at higher confidence elsewhere, e.g. bom-isaiah-textual-dependence, bom-helaman10-matthew18-intertext). It does not by itself indicate anything about the passage's date or origin.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Psalm 95 joins Israel's wilderness provocation with divine wrath and exclusion from God's rest, and Hebrews 3 applies the same formulation to a Christian audience. Jacob 1:7 reproduces that exhortational complex while urging its audience to come to Christ and enter God's rest. The best-supported reconstruction is that the final English wording was mediated through this biblical complex, whether through KJV-shaped translation, harmonization, memorized devotional language, or direct modern reuse.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The cluster strongly supports Psalm 95/Hebrews 3 or KJV-shaped mediation in the final English text. It poorly discriminates among an ancient discourse rendered into biblical English, revelatory or editorial harmonization, habitual use of memorized biblical language, and nineteenth-century composition, so it provides no comparable support for a particular historical-origin model.

**Inspiration:** Nothing: this is an observation about the English phrasing of the final text, and is compatible with an inspired or an uninspired origin equally.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Does not establish the direction of dependence, the translation mechanism (loose translation vs. revelatory harmonization vs. direct nineteenth-century composition), the antiquity of the underlying Nephite text, or anything about historicity or inspiration. It is a translation-language observation only, and it is not independent of the corpus's broader, already-registered KJV-mediation pattern -- it should not be added as separate cumulative weight alongside that general finding.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Tight/literal ancient-Vorlage translation** — predicts: Thematic content (a wilderness-rebellion reference) would be expected; verbatim KJV phrase-cluster matching an unrelated later psalm is not specifically predicted, though it is not excluded if the translator rendered the idea into the nearest familiar English idiom.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
- **Loose/dynamic translation of an ancient source** — predicts: A translator producing natural nineteenth-century biblical English would be expected to reach for exactly this kind of familiar KJV collocation when rendering a wilderness-rebellion reference.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Nineteenth-century composition using memorized/liturgical scripture** — predicts: An author steeped in the KJV and its liturgical use (Psalm 95 as a daily-office text, expounded in Hebrews 3-4) would very plausibly reproduce this exact phrase cluster when writing a wilderness-rebellion warning.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Translation-process rendering of an ancient wilderness-rebellion reference into available KJV idiom, without implying the ancient source text quoted this specific psalm. — *plausible*: Matches the corpus's well-documented general tendency to render biblical-register content in KJV phrasing; consistent with tight- or loose-translation models alike.
- Independent, non-literary reference to the shared wilderness-rebellion tradition (also in Numbers 14) without direct dependence on Psalm 95/Hebrews 3 wording specifically. — *possible*: The underlying event is common biblical knowledge, but Numbers 14 does not use this three-part phrase, so this alternative explains the theme but not the specific wording match as well as a textual-relationship model does.
- Nineteenth-century use of a proverbial, liturgically memorized phrase (the Venite) current in period religious speech, independent of any specific-verse citation intent. — *independently supported*: Psalm 95 and its use in Hebrews 3-4 were exceptionally well-known texts in the early American religious environment, making unconscious or habitual echo more plausible here than for a comparatively obscure KJV passage.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
A three-element verbatim phrase cluster from a famous, liturgically prominent psalm turning up in an ancient Jewish text (e.g., a Qumran hymn or pseudepigraphon) echoing Psalm 95 would be treated as a plausible scriptural allusion, moderately-to-strongly supportive of a textual relationship, while a specialist would still flag the psalm's liturgical ubiquity as a reason common quotation, rather than dependence on one specific manuscript tradition, remains live. That is the treatment applied here; this note is not scored more harshly or more leniently than that comparandum.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.68) for the proposition: *Jacob 1:7's final English wording reflects textual or translation-level mediation through the KJV-shaped Psalm 95:8–11 / Hebrews 3:8–11 wilderness-warning complex.*

The distinctive ordered vocabulary and accompanying wrath-and-rest framework strongly support Psalm 95/Hebrews 3 or KJV-shaped mediation in Jacob 1:7's final English form; missing matched controls and the inability to identify the mechanism keep the weight at the lower end of the strong band.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: Psalm 95 is among the most frequently read/recited psalms in the Christian tradition and is separately quoted and expounded at length in Hebrews 3-4, giving it far higher-than-average cultural availability compared to more obscure KJV passages matched elsewhere in the corpus. No systematic search of period religious literature has been done to establish how common this exact three-part collocation was outside of direct quotation of these two biblical passages; that search would materially sharpen this weight in either direction.

What would move this: Weight would rise if a systematic search of comparable early-19th-century American religious prose and sermons showed this exact three-part collocation (provocation / day(s) of temptation / wilderness) essentially confined to direct quotation of Psalm 95 or Hebrews 3, rather than circulating as loose devotional idiom. Weight would fall if such a search instead found the phrase used loosely and often as generic religious rhetoric independent of citing either specific verse.
