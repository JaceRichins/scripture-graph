# Textual Interdependence in Mosiah 13

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Mosiah 13:12-24 closely reproduces the Ten Commandments in the order and much of the wording of Exodus 20:4-17, strongly supporting a direct literary/translation-level relationship with the Decalogue tradition. Because the Decalogue is one of the most liturgically fixed texts in the Israelite tradition, this close match is exactly what an authentic ancient source, a harmonizing translation, and direct modern reuse would each independently predict, so it establishes dependence without discriminating a transmission mechanism, date, or ancient authorship.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Mosiah 13:12-24 reproduces all ten commandments in the same order and close wording as Exodus 20:4-17, with only minor variation.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
Abinadi's recitation of the commandments before Noah's priests is presented as a direct citation of the traditional Decalogue.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
Establishes textual dependence on the Exodus 20 tradition at the level of the received English text; the Decalogue's known liturgical fixity in Israelite religious practice means this is close to the strongest positive-control case available for a 'memorized ancient text should survive transmission intact' hypothesis.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Read as Abinadi's defense before Noah's priests, Mosiah 13:12-24 fits as a direct citation of the Ten Commandments the priests themselves would be bound by; on the ancient-record model this is exactly the kind of fixed liturgical text expected to survive transmission intact, and on the translation/composition models it is the KJV Decalogue reproduced to make the same rhetorical point. Either way, the correspondence puts Noah's priests in the position of being judged by their own quoted law.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
Steelmanned: the Decalogue is among the most fixed, liturgically memorized texts in the Israelite tradition, so an authentic record plausibly transmitting something very close to the received formula is exactly what an ancient-source model predicts -- this is a case where close correspondence is genuinely expected under the ancient model, not merely tolerated. That said, it equally is exactly what translation-harmonization and direct modern reuse predict, so while the dependence itself is very strongly evidenced, it does not discriminate among the three models.

**Inspiration:** Nothing: confirming textual dependence on the Decalogue tradition says nothing about whether the passage or its transmission was divinely inspired.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Does not establish the language or date of an underlying source, ancient authorship, or independence from the King James Bible.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Ancient citation of a memorized Decalogue rendered via KJV-influenced translation** — predicts: Very close correspondence, consistent with the Decalogue's known liturgical fixity. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Translation-level harmonization to the familiar KJV Exodus wording** — predicts: Same close correspondence. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **19th-century composition directly reusing the KJV Decalogue** — predicts: Same close correspondence. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- KJV-mediated rendering of an authentic, memorized ancient Decalogue — *plausible*: Well-supported in principle by the Decalogue's documented liturgical fixity in ancient Israelite religion
- Direct 19th-century reuse of the KJV Exodus text — *plausible*: Fully sufficient on its own to produce the observed correspondence
- An independent oral Decalogue tradition diverging from Exodus wording — *ad hoc*: The match tracks specific KJV translation choices too closely to be an independent convergent oral rendering
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
A Second Temple-era text (e.g. the Nash Papyrus) reproducing the Decalogue closely is treated as ordinary evidence of liturgical transmission fixity, not composition; the same standard applies here.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.84) for the proposition: *Abinadi's recitation of the commandments (begun in Mosiah 12, continued in Mosiah 13:12-24) stands in a direct literary/textual dependence relationship with the Exodus 20 Decalogue tradition.*

The extensive verbal correspondence between Mosiah 13:12-24 and Exodus 20:4-17 strongly supports a direct literary/translation relationship with the Decalogue tradition, but because the Decalogue is a liturgically fixed text this correspondence is predicted about equally by authentic ancient transmission, translation-level harmonization, and direct modern reuse.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: The Decalogue is a positive-control case: it is expected to be one of the most stably transmitted texts in any authentic ancient Israelite-descended tradition, so a close match here is less surprising under the ancient model than comparable overlap would be for less fixed genres.

What would move this: A documented pre-1830 non-KJV Decalogue-numbering or wording quirk appearing uniquely in Mosiah 13 would raise weight toward an independent ancient transmission; confirmation that every variant reading in Mosiah 13 traces to known 1830-era KJV printings would strengthen the direct-modern-reuse alternative.
