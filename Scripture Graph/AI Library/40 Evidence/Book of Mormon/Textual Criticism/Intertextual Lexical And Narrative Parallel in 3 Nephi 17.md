# Intertextual Lexical And Narrative Parallel in 3 Nephi 17

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
The clustered disability vocabulary and mass-healing setting in [[3 Nephi 17#^3ne-17-7|3 Nephi 17:7]], [[3 Nephi 17#^3ne-17-9|17:9]], and [[Matthew 15#^matt-15-30|Matthew 15:30-31]] moderately support a literary or translation-level relationship. Because healing catalogues are conventional and 3 Nephi expands and rearranges Matthew's list, the evidence identifies a meaningful resemblance without determining direct dependence, direction, or compositional date.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Third Nephi 17:7 and 17:9 describe a multitude bringing people who are lame, blind, maimed, dumb, and otherwise afflicted to Jesus, who heals them. Matthew 15:30-31 likewise depicts a multitude bringing the lame, blind, dumb, maimed, and others to Jesus for healing. The passages share both a clustered disability catalogue and the mass-healing narrative setting, although 3 Nephi expands and rearranges the list.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The combined lexical and narrative overlap is more suggestive than either a generic healing story or one shared disability term. It supports a Matthew-related final-form echo, while the expanded and reordered catalogue prevents treating 3 Nephi as a simple verbatim quotation.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The passage portrays the Nephite ministry of Jesus through a Gospel-like healing scene emphasizing compassion, comprehensive healing, and collective participation. The form belongs to a broader ancient and Christian convention of summarizing healings through catalogues of disabilities.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Matthew 15 summarizes Jesus's healing activity by cataloguing several disabilities within a scene where a multitude brings sufferers to him. Third Nephi 17 adopts the same recognizable narrative form while enlarging the list and emphasizing Jesus's compassion and the healing of every person. A Matthew-mediated rendering or adaptation explains the lexical cluster directly; a shared healing tradition or conventional catalogue explains the broader scene with fewer expectations of exact wording.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The cluster moderately supports Matthew/KJV mediation or a shared Gospel tradition. It does not distinguish an ancient repeated event rendered in biblical language from translation harmonization, deliberate allusion, or nineteenth-century adaptation.

**Inspiration:** The parallel itself implies nothing about inspiration; inspired translation, remembered tradition, literary allusion, and ordinary adaptation can all produce it.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallel does not determine direction of dependence, show that an underlying ancient-language text contained Matthew's wording, date either tradition, or independently authenticate the reported healings.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Both passages independently use a conventional catalogue of illnesses associated with Jesus's healings. — *plausible*: Healing summaries commonly name blindness, lameness, deafness, and related disabilities, though the shared cluster including "maimed" is more specific.
- Both descend from a shared type of remembered Jesus-healing tradition without direct textual dependence. — *possible*: The common setting permits this reconstruction, but no independent source preserving the proposed shared episode or wording has been identified.
- A translator used Matthew's KJV diction to render a separate ancient healing event. — *plausible*: This fits the Book of Mormon's broader use of biblical English and explains both similarity and expansion.
- A nineteenth-century composer adapted Matthew 15 while adding further disabilities and narrative detail. — *plausible*: Direct access to the KJV and the preservation of the mass-healing setting make this a straightforward account.
- The complete overlap is accidental. — *possible*: Individual terms are common, but pure coincidence becomes less satisfactory when the clustered vocabulary and scene structure are considered together.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->
