# Textual-Theological Contrast in Alma 21

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
In Alma 21:6, the Amalekite cites sanctuaries and assembled worship as evidence of righteousness and grounds for rejecting the need to repent. Aaron's reply in vv.7 and 9 redirects the exchange to Christ, resurrection, and atonement, showing that the chapter does not treat religious participation alone as sufficient evidence of righteousness or redemption. The passage argues against that sufficiency claim, not against sanctuaries or communal worship themselves.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
In Alma 21:6 an Amalekite cites his people's sanctuaries and communal worship as grounds for denying any need to repent. Aaron does not dispute the existence of the sanctuaries or worship; he asks about belief in the coming Son of God (v.7) and teaches redemption through Christ's death, suffering, and atoning blood (v.9).
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The exchange contrasts confidence grounded in visible religious participation with Aaron's teaching about repentance and redemption through Christ. Its target is the sufficiency claim — that worship practice alone proves righteousness — not worship or sanctuaries themselves.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The passage illuminates how the narrative frames intergroup religious dispute: shared reference to God and worship does not imply agreement about sin, repentance, Christ, or redemption. Because the Amalekite position reaches the reader only through a polemical narrative voice, it should be read as the chapter's characterization rather than a full account of historical Amalekite belief.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On the chapter's own theological logic, the Amalekite treats visible institutions as proof that his people need no repentance, while Aaron redirects to the Son of God and the atonement. The exchange is a focused argument about the basis of righteousness and redemption, leaving communal worship itself unaddressed as a practice.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->
