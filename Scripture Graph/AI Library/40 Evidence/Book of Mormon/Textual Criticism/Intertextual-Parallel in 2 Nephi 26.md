# Intertextual-Parallel in 2 Nephi 26

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
2 Nephi 26:16 reproduces, in matching order, the three distinctive elements of Isaiah 29:4 KJV (speaking out of the ground, speech low out of the dust, a voice as of a familiar spirit), immediately preceding Nephi's explicit long quotation of Isaiah 29 in chapter 27. This strongly supports a direct literary or textual relationship between the passages, but does not discriminate an ancient allusion later rendered in KJV idiom from nineteenth-century reuse of the KJV text itself.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
2 Nephi 26:16 describes a destroyed people whose speech is 'low out of the dust' and whose voice is 'as one that hath a familiar spirit,' reproducing, in the same order, the same three distinctive elements found in Isaiah 29:4 KJV. Nephi is narrated as quoting Isaiah 29 at length in the immediately following chapter (2 Nephi 27), not 'two chapters later' as the original note stated.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The clustering of three unusual, specific phrases in the same order is too distinctive to be an independent coincidence; some literary or textual relationship between the two passages exists in the final text.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
Consistent with any model in which Isaiah's writings (on the brass plates, per the narrative, or as a translation source) were available to the author or translator at this point in composition; the immediately following explicit Isaiah 29 quotation in ch. 27 makes a deliberate echo narratively plausible.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a reconstruction in which an ancient author had access to an Isaiah text and used it to color his own prophetic language, this echo fits naturally as a deliberate compositional flourish just before the formal quotation in the following chapters. On a reconstruction in which the text's biblical-sounding language reflects the translation or production process drawing on the available KJV, the same echo is equally well explained. Both reconstructions are live; the passage does not adjudicate between them.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
Supports reading the final text as deliberately weaving Isaiah 29 language into Nephi's own prophecy, which is compatible with (but does not require) an ancient author working from an Isaiah source. It gives no discrimination between that scenario and translation-level or nineteenth-century use of the same KJV wording.

**Inspiration:** This bears on the text's literary/textual history, not on whether it is inspired; either mechanism is compatible with an inspired or uninspired production.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Does not establish whether the borrowing happened anciently, during a harmonizing translation process, or in nineteenth-century composition. This is a single-verse dependent instance of the same phenomenon covered by bom-isaiah-textual-dependence and must not be added to that issue's evidence as if independent.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Ancient allusion rendered in KJV-style translation** — predicts: Close wording because a genuinely ancient allusion to a known biblical text is rendered by the translation into the idiom of the receptor language's standard Bible. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Independent ancient composition, coincidentally similar** — predicts: Vaguer or partial overlap, not three distinctive phrases in matching order. Fit with this evidence: *strongly challenged*.
- **Nineteenth-century composition drawing on the KJV** — predicts: Exact or near-exact reuse of memorable KJV phrasing. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Nephi, having Isaiah on the brass plates and about to quote Isaiah 29 explicitly, deliberately echoes it here as an ancient literary device — *plausible*: Fits the immediate narrative context (ch. 27 quotes Isaiah 29 directly) and is textually permitted, though not independently verified
- The wording reflects direct nineteenth-century dependence on the 1829 KJV text during the Book of Mormon's production, rather than translation of an independently ancient passage — *plausible*: The exactness of the three-phrase match is at least as well explained by direct reuse of a known English text as by translation of an ancient original
- Two independent ancient authors coincidentally used the same unusual triple-phrase cluster — *ad hoc*: The specificity and ordering of the match make independent coincidence implausible without invoking an unevidenced shared idiom
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
A comparable triple-phrase verbatim cluster shared between two passages in different books of the Bible (e.g., a psalm echoing prophetic judgment language) would be treated as evidence of literary dependence or common liturgical language, not as proof of any particular date or authorship — the same restraint applies here.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.72) for the proposition: *2 Nephi 26:16 reflects a direct textual/translation-level relationship to the specific KJV wording of Isaiah 29:4, rather than independently and coincidentally similar phrasing.*

The near-verbatim, three-part overlap with Isaiah 29:4 strongly supports some form of direct literary or textual relationship between the passages, but it does not by itself indicate whether that relationship arose anciently, in translation, or in nineteenth-century composition.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: The three-part phrase cluster (ground/dust/familiar spirit) is distinctive rather than a generic English idiom; no comparable cluster appears elsewhere in the KJV outside Isaiah 29:4, which lowers the chance of independent coincidence but was not evaluated against a systematic search of all Book of Mormon destruction language for competing candidates.

What would move this: A matched-corpus search showing this exact three-part cluster occurs nowhere else in the KJV outside Isaiah 29:4 would raise confidence that the match is a deliberate, specific echo rather than generic judgment-oracle language; finding the same three-element cluster recurring as stock KJV destruction phrasing elsewhere would lower it.
