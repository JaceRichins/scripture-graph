# Intertextual Verbal Parallel in 3 Nephi 7

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[3 Nephi 7#^3ne-7-8|3 Nephi 7:8]] reproduces the paired dog-and-sow imagery and order of [[2 Peter 2#^2pet-2-22|2 Peter 2:22]], while [[Proverbs 26#^prov-26-11|Proverbs 26:11]] independently attests only the dog-and-vomit half. This strongly supports biblical intertextuality or translation-level mediation in the received English text, but shared proverb, KJV-shaped translation, editorial harmonization, and direct modern reuse remain viable historical explanations.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Third Nephi 7:8 compares apostasy first to a dog returning to vomit and then to a sow returning to wallowing in mire. Second Peter 2:22 presents the same dog-and-sow pair in the same order and similar English wording. Proverbs 26:11 independently attests the dog-and-vomit half but not the paired sow image.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The same two proverbs, sequence, and closely corresponding wording strongly support an English textual relationship with 2 Peter 2:22. Proverbs shows that the dog image had an older independent history, while the paired sequence makes derivation solely from Proverbs insufficient.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The English Book of Mormon passage participates in the biblical proverbial tradition represented most fully by 2 Peter. The evidence identifies English biblical mediation more securely than it identifies source-language dependence, chronological direction, or authorship.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a shared-proverb reconstruction, an ancient source applies traditional animal-return imagery to communal relapse, and the translation adopts the familiar KJV formulation. On a harmonization reconstruction, the received wording deliberately aligns the warning with 2 Peter. On a modern-composition reconstruction, the paired New Testament proverb is directly repurposed to summarize the people's apostasy.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The strongest supportive reading is that an ancient source used a traditional dog-and-sow warning and that translation rendered it through familiar KJV wording. Direct modern use of 2 Peter predicts the same surface evidence at least as readily, so the parallel strongly supports biblical intertextuality but offers little independent discrimination for Book of Mormon antiquity.

**Inspiration:** Biblical intertextuality in the English wording implies nothing by itself about inspiration.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallel does not establish that an ancient Nephite source quoted 2 Peter, determine whether the source contained both proverbs, identify the direction of dependence, or decide between ancient-source and modern-composition models.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Ancient shared proverbial tradition** — predicts: Dog and possibly sow imagery used for relapse, with wording that may differ across languages and transmissions.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Ancient source with KJV-shaped translation** — predicts: A source-level proverb or relapse comparison rendered through the complete English pair in 2 Peter.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Editorial or revelatory harmonization** — predicts: Later alignment of the passage with the familiar canonical proverb.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Nineteenth-century composition using 2 Peter** — predicts: The same two images, order, and KJV-like phrasing applied to the narrative situation.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Independent convergence from Proverbs alone** — predicts: The dog-and-vomit image but does not adequately predict addition of the sow-and-mire image in the Petrine order.. Fit with this evidence: *strongly challenged*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Both passages inherit a broader proverbial tradition. — *independently supported*: Second Peter calls the material proverbial, and Proverbs 26:11 independently attests the dog-and-vomit saying; independent evidence for the complete paired form is less clear.
- An ancient source was translated using the familiar KJV wording of 2 Peter. — *plausible*: Translation accommodation explains both the traditional content and the close English form without requiring source-language identity.
- A modern composer directly reused 2 Peter 2:22. — *plausible*: The complete paired saying and its order are readily available in the KJV and closely match the Book of Mormon wording.
- A later editor or revelatory process harmonized an earlier saying with 2 Peter. — *possible*: This explains the wording but lacks an independently recoverable pre-harmonized textual form.
- The dog and sow comparisons were independently combined by coincidence. — *ad hoc*: It does not explain why both members recur in the same order and with closely corresponding English expressions.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
The same paired, ordered verbal proverb in two biblical books would be scored strongly for textual or traditional relationship while leaving priority and transmission unresolved. The existing assessment applies that standard evenly and does not inflate the parallel into evidence for a particular authorship model.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->

The paired dog-and-sow imagery, preserved order, and close English wording strongly support mediation through or deliberate engagement with 2 Peter 2:22, but they do not distinguish KJV-shaped translation from modern reuse.
