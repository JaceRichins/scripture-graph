# Comparative Textual Difference in Moses 3

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Moses 3:5 adds a spiritual-creation explanation absent from Genesis 2:5, and Moses 3:17 adds an agency clause absent from Genesis 2:17. These differences securely show that Moses 3 expands the corresponding Genesis wording and are doctrinally significant within Latter-day Saint scripture. By themselves, however, they do not discriminate among ancient restoration, modern revelation, inspired revision, and ordinary nineteenth-century expansion.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Relative to the corresponding King James Genesis 2 wording, Moses 3:5 adds an explanation that creation occurred spiritually before naturally, and Moses 3:17 adds that Adam may choose for himself. These are genuine textual additions, not exact reproductions of the cited Genesis clauses.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The additions can be read as revelatory restoration or clarification, but they can also be read as modern theological expansion. Textual difference alone does not identify the source, date, or authority of the added material.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The differences establish a literary relationship in which Moses 3 revises or expands Genesis wording. They are compatible with restoration, revelation, inspired revision, and ordinary modern rewriting, and therefore provide almost no discrimination among those models without external source or chronology evidence.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The additions are important for Latter-day Saint theology, particularly spiritual creation and moral agency. As evidence for ancient provenance or revelation, however, their mere presence is neutral because the principal rival models all predict additions to Genesis.

**Inspiration:** Textual expansion alone implies nothing about inspiration because both inspired revision and ordinary human rewriting can add coherent theological material.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The differences do not establish antiquity, translation from an ancient manuscript, historical accuracy, revelatory origin, or falsity of those possibilities.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- The clauses restore material lost from the received Genesis tradition. — *possible*: The additions are textually compatible with restoration, but this comparison supplies no manuscript witness or other independent support.
- The clauses are modern revelatory clarification rather than restored ancient wording. — *possible*: This fits a revelation model but cannot be tested from the textual difference alone.
- The clauses are nineteenth-century interpretive expansions of Genesis. — *plausible*: Expansion and doctrinal glossing are ordinary features of rewritten scripture, and this model directly predicts textual additions.
- The additions adapt religious or intellectual ideas available in the modern environment. — *possible*: This would explain the additions if specific sources or parallels were demonstrated, but none are identified in the note.
- A scribal or transmission process generated the additions. — *ad hoc*: The clauses are coherent theological expansions, and the note supplies no manuscript or copying evidence for accidental transmission.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a restoration model, the spiritual-creation and agency clauses could preserve theological content once present in an older textual tradition and later lost from the transmitted Hebrew of Genesis 2 — a coherent reconstruction consistent with Joseph Smith's broader 'plain and precious things' framework. On a modern-composition model, the same clauses read as ordinary doctrinal elaboration of a familiar KJV base text, of a kind common in nineteenth-century religious rewriting. Both fit the bare textual comparison equally; nothing in the wording itself favors one reconstruction over the other.
