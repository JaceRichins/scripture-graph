# English Textual Intertextuality in Alma 5

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Alma 5 contains several close biblical-English parallels, including [[Alma 5#^alma-5-19|Alma 5:19]] with [[Psalm 24#^ps-24-4|Psalm 24:4]], [[Alma 5#^alma-5-38|Alma 5:38]] with [[John 10#^john-10-3|John 10:3]], [[Alma 5#^alma-5-42|Alma 5:42]] with [[Romans 6#^rom-6-23|Romans 6:23]], [[Alma 5#^alma-5-52|Alma 5:52]] with [[Matthew 3#^matt-3-10|Matthew 3:10]], and [[Alma 5#^alma-5-57|Alma 5:57]] with [[Isaiah 52#^isa-52-11|Isaiah 52:11]]. Their cumulative specificity strongly supports biblical mediation in the received English text, although common sermon imagery, a large search space, and dependence among the examples reduce the weight. The evidence creates tension for an unmediated pre-Christian verbatim-source reading but does not distinguish loose translation from nineteenth-century composition.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Alma 5 contains multiple parallels to biblical passages, including "pure heart and clean hands" with Psalm 24:4, the shepherd's voice and sheep with John 10, "wages" and "death" with Romans 6:23, the ax at the tree's root followed by fruit, hewing, and fire with Matthew 3:10, and separation from uncleanness with Isaiah 52:11. The parallels vary in precision: some closely reproduce wording and sequence, while others share common religious imagery.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
Taken together, the parallels indicate sustained use of biblical language in the received English text. The close Psalm, Matthew, and Pauline formulations are more probative than generic shepherd, judgment, or book-of-life imagery, but the cluster should be treated as one manifestation of biblical register rather than several fully independent discoveries.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The cluster is compatible with an ancient sermon rendered or expanded through KJV language, as well as with nineteenth-century composition by a biblically immersed author. Because several close English formulations come from New Testament texts conventionally later than Alma's narrative setting, the evidence creates real tension for a naive verbatim-source model that assigns the same English formulations directly to a pre-Christian ancient record.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The strongest supportive reading is that a revelatory or loose translation could intentionally express older Israelite and Christian teachings through familiar biblical English; the Psalm and Isaiah material could also belong naturally in an Israelite inheritance. This keeps an ancient-source model viable but does not independently support it, while the New Testament-like wording fits modern composition or translator mediation more directly.

**Inspiration:** Biblical dependence in the English wording does not determine whether Alma 5 is inspired, since inspired translation, editorial adaptation, and modern composition make different historical claims without settling spiritual value.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallels do not establish conscious quotation in every instance, the direction of every relationship, identical source-language wording, nineteenth-century authorship, or the absence of an ancient source.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Tight-control ancient translation without later-language mediation** — predicts: A pre-Christian sermon should not repeatedly reproduce distinctive wording from later New Testament texts unless those formulations derive from earlier traditions or are supplied by revelation.. Fit with this evidence: *some difficulty*.
- **Loose or revelatory ancient translation** — predicts: The English rendering may freely use familiar KJV language to express ancient or revealed concepts.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Ancient source with translator or editor harmonization** — predicts: Older content can coexist with later biblical wording introduced during transmission or translation.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Nineteenth-century Christian composition** — predicts: Dense reuse of familiar Old and New Testament diction within a conversion sermon is expected.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Independent development from common motifs** — predicts: Generic shepherd, purity, judgment, and life imagery may recur, but multiple close phrase-and-sequence correspondences are less expected.. Fit with this evidence: *some difficulty*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- A loose translator rendered an ancient sermon through conventional KJV language. — *plausible*: It explains both older biblical themes and later English formulations, though the lost source prevents testing how much wording came from translation.
- A nineteenth-century author assembled familiar biblical expressions into a Christian sermon. — *plausible*: The KJV was readily available and the model directly predicts language drawn from both Testaments.
- The motifs independently descend from broadly shared Jewish or Christian traditions. — *plausible*: This can explain common images such as shepherds, purity, judgment, and divine records, but it less fully explains the closest English formulations.
- An ancient source was expanded or harmonized by a later translator or editor. — *possible*: Such mediation is textually possible and common as a general mechanism, but no independent underlying manuscript identifies the proposed layers here.
- All similarities are coincidental. — *ad hoc*: It treats a dense set of phrase-level and sequence-level correspondences as unrelated accidents and explains less than the biblical-register models.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.68) for the proposition: *The received English text of Alma 5 substantially draws on biblical, especially King James, wording and image clusters rather than having acquired all similarities through independent formulation.*

The density and specificity of several parallels strongly support biblical-English dependence in Alma 5, while the evidence does not distinguish loose translation of an ancient discourse from nineteenth-century composition.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: Biblical sermons commonly reuse shepherd, purity, judgment, tree, fire, death, and book-of-life imagery, and many possible Book of Mormon phrases can be searched against the entire Bible. No matched sermon corpus, candidate count, or preregistered set of parallels is provided. The cumulative case remains substantial because several parallels combine wording, imagery, and sequence, but they share the common cause of pervasive biblical register and must not be counted as wholly independent evidence.

What would move this: Assessment dated 2026-09-03. The weight would rise if a preregistered comparison found Alma 5's phrase clusters markedly rarer in matched nineteenth-century sermons and independent English religious prose than in texts directly using the KJV; it would fall if controlled studies showed similarly dense clusters routinely arise in nondependent sermons or if the closest correspondences disappeared under precise textual comparison.
