# Biblical Intertext in Jacob 1

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[Jacob 1#^jacob-1-7|Jacob 1:7]] combines provocation, temptation, wilderness, divine wrath, and exclusion from God's rest in the same scriptural complex found in [[Psalm 95#^ps-95-8|Psalm 95:8]], [[Psalm 95#^ps-95-9|Psalm 95:9]], [[Psalm 95#^ps-95-10|Psalm 95:10]], [[Psalm 95#^ps-95-11|Psalm 95:11]], [[Hebrews 3#^heb-3-7|Hebrews 3:7]], [[Hebrews 3#^heb-3-8|Hebrews 3:8]], [[Hebrews 3#^heb-3-9|Hebrews 3:9]], [[Hebrews 3#^heb-3-10|Hebrews 3:10]], and [[Hebrews 3#^heb-3-11|Hebrews 3:11]]. The clustered sequence strongly supports deliberate intertextuality in the final English text, but Psalm 95 and Hebrews 3 represent one underlying biblical tradition rather than independent corroboration. Ancient allusion, KJV-shaped translation or revelation, and nineteenth-century composition remain viable mechanisms.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Jacob 1:7 combines entering God's rest, God swearing in wrath that some will not enter, the provocation, temptation, and Israel's wilderness experience. Psalm 95:8–11 presents the same wilderness complex, including provocation, temptation, divine wrath, and exclusion from rest; Hebrews 3 quotes and expounds that passage in closely corresponding KJV language.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The number, proximity, and order of the correspondences strongly support deliberate engagement with this single biblical tradition in the final English text. Because Hebrews 3 quotes Psalm 95, the two biblical passages are one intertextual complex rather than two independent witnesses.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The correspondence is compatible with an ancient author invoking an Israelite wilderness tradition, an ancient source translated or harmonized into familiar KJV wording, revelatory reuse, or direct nineteenth-century composition from the Bible. It strongly identifies a textual tradition but does not distinguish among those historical mechanisms.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
The passage fits naturally within the biblical wilderness-warning tradition: Israel's provocation becomes a warning that Jacob's audience must accept Christ and enter divine rest. On an ancient-allusion reconstruction, Jacob invokes an inherited Israelite warning subsequently rendered in biblical English; on translation-harmonization, revelatory, or modern-composition reconstructions, the familiar KJV form supplies the wording. All explain why the final text compresses the tradition into one exhortation.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The cluster strongly supports deliberate biblical intertextuality in Jacob 1:7. It offers little independent discrimination between ancient-source and nineteenth-century-composition models because both can readily account for the relationship, although it substantially challenges an accidental or wholly independent-wording account.

**Inspiration:** The presence of this intertext by itself implies nothing about whether Jacob 1 is inspired, because inspired translation, ordinary literary reuse, and modern composition can all employ received scripture.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The intertext does not establish whether Jacob directly used Psalm 95, whether Hebrews influenced the English wording, whether an ancient source stood behind the passage, or whether the relationship arose through translation, revelation, redaction, or modern composition.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Jacob invokes an ancient Israelite wilderness tradition represented in Psalm 95. — *plausible*: The wilderness rebellion and exclusion from divine rest form a coherent Israelite tradition, although the surviving English diction cannot identify the wording of an underlying source.
- A translator or revelatory process renders an ancient warning in the familiar language of Psalm 95 and Hebrews 3. — *plausible*: This accounts for the KJV-shaped wording while allowing an ancient conceptual source, but it does not independently identify the translation mechanism.
- A nineteenth-century composer draws directly or indirectly on the KJV biblical complex. — *plausible*: The KJV was available, and the Book of Mormon's broader use of biblical English independently establishes that this vocabulary belonged to its compositional environment.
- The cluster arose accidentally from common religious vocabulary. — *possible*: Individual terms such as wrath and rest are common, but their combination with provocation, temptation, and the wilderness makes pure coincidence a comparatively poor explanation.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->
