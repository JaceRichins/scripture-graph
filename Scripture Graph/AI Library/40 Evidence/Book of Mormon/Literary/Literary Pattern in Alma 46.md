# Literary Pattern in Alma 46

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Alma 46:12-27 develops a garment pattern that is not just narratively repeated but explicitly interpreted by Moroni, who quotes Jacob and invokes Joseph's rent coat to explain the covenant. That in-text self-interpretation is strong evidence of deliberate literary/theological design in the final text. Garment rending itself is an independently attested mourning/distress gesture, not a documented covenant convention, so Alma 46's covenant use is a notable extension the text builds on top of that base gesture. The design finding gives no independent traction on historicity, language of composition, or inspiration.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Moroni rends his coat and writes a covenant slogan on it (v.12); he waves the rent piece before the people (v.19); the people run together, gird on armor, and rend their own garments as a covenant token (vv.21-22); Moroni then explicitly interprets the whole action through Joseph's coat 'rent by his brethren' and Jacob's words that a remnant of the garment 'had not decayed' (vv.23-26), quoting Jacob directly.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
Moroni's own reported speech supplies the connective tissue: he directly quotes Jacob and names Joseph to explain the garment action just narrated. That makes deliberate design a strongly warranted inference from explicit textual linkage, not itself a bare assertion of design — the text asserts the Joseph/Jacob connection, and design is what best explains why that connection is asserted at all.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
Compatible with an ancient Israelite-descended culture deliberately invoking its own Joseph tradition (possibly heightened by Mormon's later abridgment), or with a modern author consciously building on the well-known Genesis Joseph narrative and the LDS tradition's expanded Joseph-coat material (cf. Joseph Smith Translation, Genesis 50), which covers similar ground.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Ancient Near Eastern and biblical convention already made rending one's garment a recognized gesture of mourning and covenant-making (Joshua 7:6; Ezra 9:3), so the base action in Alma 46 would have been intelligible either in an ancient Israelite-descended culture or to a nineteenth-century Bible reader. What the passage adds beyond that convention is Moroni's own explicit tie to the Joseph story and Jacob's remnant prophecy. On the reconstruction that this is an ancient record whose author (or later editor, Mormon) deliberately invoked available Joseph tradition, or on the reconstruction that it is a modern composition consciously built on Genesis, the passage reads as one unified symbolic argument either way; the text itself does not adjudicate between these reconstructions.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
Strongly supports that the final canonical text is deliberately, explicitly organized around a coherent garment/covenant/remnant symbol complex - stronger than an externally inferred pattern because the in-text speaker himself supplies the interpretive key. It does not support that the underlying events are historical, that the text originated anciently, or that any particular language or era produced it; those questions remain exactly as open after this observation as before it.

**Inspiration:** Nothing directly follows about inspiration: literary coherence is compatible with inspired composition, careful ancient editorial work, or skilled uninspired authorship alike.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Semitic or Hebrew composition, ancient authorship, the historicity of Moroni's speech or the covenant scene, or divine inspiration.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Ancient record, later Mormon abridgment** — predicts: an inherited or amplified Joseph tradition, possibly sharpened by later editorial arrangement. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Tight translation of an ancient source** — predicts: the pattern originates with the ancient author and survives translation intact. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **19th-century composition using Genesis as template** — predicts: the same explicit self-interpretation, since Genesis 37 and Joseph's coat were widely known to any 1829 Bible reader. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Ordinary reporting of a known ANE/biblical garment-tearing gesture of mourning or distress, later linked to an available biblical allusion — *plausible*: Garment rending as a mourning or distress gesture is independently attested (Joshua 7:6; 2 Samuel 1:11; Ezra 9:3), so the base gesture needs no literary invention. None of these texts establish rending specifically as a covenant-making act, so Alma 46's covenant use is itself a distinctive extension rather than a documented biblical convention; that does not explain away the additional, explicit Joseph/Jacob speech layered on top of the gesture.
- A modern author consciously crafts the same symbolic complex using Genesis as a template — *plausible*: Nothing about the passage requires ancient composition; a writer with access to Genesis and a rhetorical goal could construct this deliberately.
- Mormon (the in-narrative editor) added or sharpened the Joseph/Jacob connection while abridging — *possible*: Consistent with the text's own claim to be an abridgment, but unverifiable independent of the text itself.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
A biblical narrative with an equally explicit self-referential typological aside (e.g., a Chronicles narrator interpreting an earlier king's act through prior prophecy) would receive the same score here: strong evidence of deliberate final-form design, none-band for whether the underlying event happened as narrated. This note is held to the same standard as the comparable biblical 'Internal Literary Pattern' entries (0.7-0.92) rather than judged more harshly or more generously.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.8) for the proposition: *The rending-of-garments sequence in Alma 46:12-27 (Moroni's coat, the people's garments, and the Joseph/Jacob interpretation) reflects deliberate literary/theological design in the final-form text, rather than an incidental sequence of events only retrospectively perceived as patterned.*

Moroni's own speech explicitly ties the rent-garment action to Joseph's coat and Jacob's remnant prophecy, which is strong evidence that the final text deliberately builds one continuous garment/covenant/remnant symbol rather than a reader-constructed connection; this bears only on final-form design and gives no independent traction on the text's date, language, historicity, or inspiration.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: Explicit narratorial self-interpretation invoking a specific earlier scriptural story (rather than a merely inferred structural echo) is a comparatively distinctive device; because the connection is directly quoted ('let us remember the words of Jacob') rather than reconstructed by an outside analyst, the look-elsewhere/denominator concern that governs chiasmus-style pattern claims applies with much less force here.

What would move this: A test bearing on the design proposition itself: independent evidence that the Joseph/Jacob interpretive material in vv.23-26 is a later editorial insertion distinguishable from the narrated action in vv.12-22 (e.g., a manuscript or source-critical seam) would lower confidence in single-author deliberate design; the absence of any such seam, plus the tightness of the vocabulary linkage (rend/remnant/preserved), supports reading it as one continuously designed unit.
