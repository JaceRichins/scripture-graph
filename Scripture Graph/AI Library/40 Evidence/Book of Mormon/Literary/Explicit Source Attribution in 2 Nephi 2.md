# Explicit Source Attribution in 2 Nephi 2

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
2 Nephi 2:17 explicitly presents Lehi's account as a supposition based on things he has read and on “that which is written.” That observation is near-certain, but an actual ancient act of reading, rhetorical appeal, editorial framing, and modern characterization predict the wording about equally; the verse therefore provides none-band support for historical ancient source use. It neither identifies the source nor independently bears on historicity or inspiration.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
2 Nephi 2:17 has Lehi introduce his account of an angel's fall as a 'supposition' based on 'things which I have read' and 'that which is written,' without naming the source.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The text presents this teaching as derivative interpretation of prior writings rather than original revelation.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
Compatible with a real ancient source Lehi drew on, a rhetorical convention for lending scriptural weight to an argument, or a modern author using biblical-sounding self-citation; because no specific quoted text is identifiable, the claim cannot be checked against any known source.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
Weak: unlike the Isaiah chapters, there is no verifiable target text, so this observation cannot be tested the way verbatim correspondence can. It shows the passage's rhetorical posture, not evidence of a real underlying source.

**Inspiration:** Nothing: an unverifiable claim to be drawing on prior writings says nothing about whether the passage is inspired.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Does not identify a specific source, does not prove dependence on any surviving or lost textual form, and does not verify the historical circumstances of composition.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **ancient dependence on a real lost source** — predicts: vague self-citation exactly like this, since the source is unavailable to check. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
- **rhetorical convention invoking authority** — predicts: same vague self-citation language, used for persuasive weight. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **modern composition using scriptural register** — predicts: same phrasing, drawing on biblical idiom ('it is written'). Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Rhetorical appeal to scriptural authority rather than description of an actual reasoning process. — *plausible*: 'That which is written' is a stock biblical idiom (cf. NT usage) usable rhetorically without a specific citable text.
- A synthesized tradition or multiple sources rather than one identifiable text. — *possible*: The text gives no basis to narrow this down.
- Modern authorial invention of a plausible-sounding source claim. — *plausible*: Requires no assumption beyond ordinary literary composition and matches the vagueness of the claim.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
An unverifiable self-citation in the Bible (e.g., Numbers 21:14's 'Book of the Wars of the LORD') is treated as an interesting but untestable claim, not strong evidence of a real lost source; the same restraint should apply here rather than crediting this passage 0.8 strength for a claim that cannot be checked against anything.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->
