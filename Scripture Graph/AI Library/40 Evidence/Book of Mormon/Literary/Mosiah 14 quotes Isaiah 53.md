# Mosiah 14 quotes Isaiah 53

> [!textual] The observation
> [[Mosiah 14]] reproduces the Suffering Servant song of [[Isaiah 53]]
> essentially verbatim (KJV wording), placed in Abinadi's trial speech as the
> scriptural core of his testimony of Christ — see the parallel verse map in
> the study guide's Related Scriptures section.

<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[Mosiah 14]] closely reproduces [[Isaiah 53]] in sustained sequence and KJV-shaped English, strongly supporting dependence on or mediation through the Isaiah/KJV textual tradition. Abinadi's continuation in [[Mosiah 15]] shows how the final text reads the servant passage christologically, but the quotation does not distinguish an ancient source rendered through biblical English from revelatory harmonization or direct nineteenth-century reuse. Relevant treatments include Royal Skousen, *The Book of Mormon: The Earliest Text* (Yale, 2009); John A. Tvedtnes, “Isaiah Variants in the Book of Mormon” in *Isaiah and the Prophets* (1984); and Grant Hardy, *Understanding the Book of Mormon* (Oxford, 2010).
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Mosiah 14 reproduces Isaiah 53 as a sustained chapter-level quotation with close wording, sequence, and verse-by-verse correspondence to the KJV textual tradition. Abinadi then interprets the quotation christologically in [[Mosiah 15]].
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The length, order, and close English wording strongly support deliberate textual mediation rather than an accidental resemblance. The critical limitation is that the correspondence identifies the Isaiah/KJV tradition as an immediate textual environment but does not identify which translation or compositional mechanism produced that relationship.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The quotation securely places Mosiah 14 within the reception history of Isaiah 53 in the Book of Mormon's final English form. It is compatible with an ancient Isaiah quotation rendered through familiar biblical English, revelatory harmonization, or nineteenth-century adaptation, while creating difficulty for a model in which the English wording is independent of the received Isaiah/KJV tradition.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
The quotation functions as the textual foundation for Abinadi's christological argument in Mosiah 15. On an ancient-source-and-harmonized-translation reconstruction, Abinadi cites an inherited Isaiah text that the English translation renders in familiar biblical language; on a nineteenth-century-composition reconstruction, the KJV supplies both the quoted chapter and the vocabulary around which the exposition is constructed. Both reconstructions explain the final-form relationship, while the chapter-length correspondence securely identifies Isaiah 53 as the controlling intertext.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The quotation strongly supports a real textual relationship with Isaiah 53 and is consistent with the narrative claim that Abinadi possessed an Isaiah tradition. It does not independently verify Nephite possession of Isaiah, and the KJV-shaped English gives little leverage for choosing between believing translation models and direct nineteenth-century reuse.

**Inspiration:** The textual relationship itself implies nothing about whether the Book of Mormon is inspired, because inspired translation, revelatory reuse, and ordinary literary adaptation can all produce it.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallel does not establish an independent ancient textual witness to Isaiah 53, the historical provenance of Abinadi's discourse, the direction or date of mediation, or ancient Book of Mormon authorship.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Ancient quotation translated independently of the KJV** — predicts: An Isaiah 53 quotation could preserve the chapter's content and order, but an independent translation would not specifically predict sustained agreement with familiar KJV wording and translation choices.. Fit with this evidence: *some difficulty*.
- **Ancient quotation rendered or harmonized through KJV English** — predicts: A genuine ancient quotation could appear in received biblical language if the translation process intentionally used the KJV for passages already available in English.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Revelatory reuse or modernization** — predicts: A revelatory process could reproduce Isaiah 53 in familiar English and then extend it through Abinadi's interpretation.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Nineteenth-century direct adaptation** — predicts: An author working with the KJV would be expected to reproduce Isaiah 53 closely and build a christological exposition around it.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- An ancient Isaiah quotation was translated or harmonized into familiar KJV language. — *plausible*: This explains both the underlying Isaiah content and the received English wording, although the translation or harmonization mechanism is not independently identified by this passage.
- The passage is a nineteenth-century direct reuse of the KJV followed by an original exposition. — *plausible*: The KJV was directly available, and sustained wording and order are expected under this model.
- Mosiah 14 and Isaiah 53 independently express conventional suffering-servant ideas without a textual relationship. — *ad hoc*: Independent development does not adequately explain the chapter-length sequence and close English wording.
- Both texts descend from an ancient Isaiah tradition, while the English correspondence arose during translation. — *independently supported*: The antiquity and transmission of Isaiah 53 are independently established; what remains unverified is whether Mosiah 14 possessed that tradition before the production of its English text.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.84) for the proposition: *Mosiah 14's quotation of Isaiah 53 is textually dependent on or mediated through the Isaiah/KJV textual tradition.*

The sustained sequence and close English wording strongly support dependence on the Isaiah 53/KJV textual tradition, while leaving ancient quotation with KJV-shaped translation, revelatory harmonization, and direct nineteenth-century adaptation unresolved.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: Extended, verse-ordered verbal correspondence is far less common by chance than isolated biblical diction, and the passage is presented as a continuous quotation rather than discovered by selecting scattered similarities from a large corpus. A precise claim about uniquely KJV-specific readings still requires a verse-level inventory against earlier Hebrew, Greek, and English witnesses.

What would move this: The weight for specifically KJV mediation would move upward if the earliest recoverable text repeatedly preserved rare, diagnostic KJV renderings or errors against earlier witnesses; it would move downward if numerous diagnostic readings instead aligned independently with pre-KJV textual witnesses while systematically departing from the KJV.
