# Internal Source Claim in Alma 3

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Alma 3:11-12 has the narrator declare the Nephite record 'true' and privilege it over the Lamanite tradition, but this is self-attestation within the very record being assessed, not independent corroboration. Sincere ancient record-keeping, one-sided ancient historiography, rhetorical self-authorization, and modern composition all predict the identical claim, so it gives only none-band support for the account's actual accuracy, completeness, or neutrality — consistent with the registry's treatment of the same evidence class elsewhere (2 Nephi 11, 3 Nephi 17, 3 Nephi 7, Alma 24-26).
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Alma 3:11-12 states that those who believed the Nephite tradition and records (rather than the Lamanite tradition) 'have kept the records which are true of their people, and also of the people of the Lamanites.'
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The narrator asserts that the Nephite record-keepers held the accurate account of both peoples, privileging the Nephite tradition over the rival Lamanite oral tradition. The verse asserts the Nephite record's own truth rather than making an explicit, itemized claim that every element of the Lamanite tradition is false.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
Compatible with any ancient historiographic tradition in which the party that keeps the surviving record also asserts its own reliability — a pattern attested widely in royal annals and biblical self-presentation alike.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Ancient historiography regularly preserves the record of only the winning or record-keeping party, who characteristically asserts the reliability of their own account against a rival oral tradition -- a pattern visible in royal annals and in biblical self-presentation alike. On that reconstruction, Alma 3:11-12 is an ordinary example of a one-sided ancient record defending its own reliability, which fits comfortably whether the underlying record is ancient, later-redacted, or a modern composition modeling ancient historiographic convention; the verse cannot by itself distinguish among these.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
Gives almost no independent support for the account's actual accuracy or impartiality, because sincere record-keeping, one-sided historiography, rhetorical self-authorization, and modern composition all predict this same self-attestation about equally well.

**Inspiration:** Nothing: a record's claim about its own accuracy says nothing about whether its content is divinely inspired.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The historical accuracy, completeness, or neutrality of the Nephite account, or the historicity of the underlying conflict.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Sincere ancient record keeper** — predicts: An explicit claim that one's own record is the true one. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **One-sided ancient historiography / rhetorical authorization** — predicts: The same claim, used to privilege one group's tradition over a rival's. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Modern literary composition** — predicts: The same self-authenticating narratorial claim. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- The assertion functions rhetorically to authorize the Nephite version of a disputed shared history. — *plausible*: Self-authorization of one's own group's record against a rival's is a common ancient historiographic move.
- Sincere record-keeping without guaranteed neutrality, completeness, or accuracy. — *plausible*: Good faith and factual accuracy are separable.
- The claim is an internal narrative device with no external referent to adjudicate. — *possible*: As self-attestation it cannot be checked against anything outside the text.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
Self-referential claims of a record's truthfulness appear throughout ancient historiography and scripture and are never treated as independent corroboration of their own accuracy in either corpus; the same non-evidentiary treatment applies here, matching the registry's parallel witness-corroboration issues.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->
