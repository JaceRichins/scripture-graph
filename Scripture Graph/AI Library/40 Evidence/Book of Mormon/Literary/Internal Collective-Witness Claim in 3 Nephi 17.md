# Internal Collective-Witness Claim in 3 Nephi 17

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
3 Nephi 17:25 portrays approximately 2,500 men, women, and children as individually seeing and hearing the resurrected Christ, making the narrative's claim to public rather than private revelation unmistakable. Because those witnesses are reported through one narrator rather than preserved as separately identifiable accounts, the verse gives only none-band support for the event's historicity while clearly illuminating how the final text authenticates the communal experience.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
3 Nephi 17:25 states that a multitude of about 2,500 men, women, and children saw, heard, and bore record, adding that they witnessed the event individually, 'every man for himself.' This is one narrator's collective-witness report, not approximately 2,500 separately preserved testimonies.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The wording presents the appearance as public, communal, and individually perceived rather than private or restricted to a small leadership group; its headcount and comprehensive language also perform an authenticating function within the narrative.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
A specific crowd estimate and individualized-perception language are compatible with a historical mass event, but the same features can arise through communal amplification or literary authentication. The single narrative source does not permit the individual witnesses to be identified or independently compared.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The number and witness formula cannot be treated as thousands of independent attestations because all are mediated by one narrator. The verse supplies only none-band support for the event's historicity, though it strongly establishes that the final text portrays Jesus's appearance as a broadly shared public experience.

**Inspiration:** Nothing: an internal witness-count claim cannot itself indicate whether the underlying event, if any, was real, embellished, or invented.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Does not establish that the event occurred historically, that approximately 2,500 individual testimonies were collected, or that the reported unanimity and crowd estimate remained unchanged during transmission.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Historical mass-witnessed theophany** — predicts: A narrative record may report a large crowd, shared perception, and an approximate headcount.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
- **Communal memory with amplification** — predicts: A remembered gathering may acquire an expanded crowd estimate or stronger language of unanimous perception.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Literary authentication** — predicts: A narrator may employ a specific number and individualized-witness formula to present an event as public and credible.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Redactional elaboration** — predicts: A later editor may expand a smaller or less specific tradition into a mass-witness report.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Approximately 2,500 people witnessed the event as described. — *possible*: The narrative explicitly reports this, but no independently identifiable witness account or external record corroborates the event or number.
- A historical gathering was remembered with amplification of its size or unanimity. — *plausible*: Collective memory can preserve a historical nucleus while enlarging collective claims, though no earlier version establishes that development here.
- The wording functions as literary authentication without preserving recoverable individual testimony. — *plausible*: The emphatic crowd estimate and individualized-perception language directly serve that rhetorical function in the final narrative.
- The reference to men, women, and children emphasizes comprehensive communal participation. — *plausible*: The inclusive wording makes the community-wide scope of the experience narratively important, regardless of historical status.
- A later editor amplified an earlier, less specific witness tradition. — *possible*: A known general transmission mechanism, but no variant or earlier textual form independently supports it here.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
A comparable claim of a precise, large witness count in an extrabiblical text (e.g., legendary multiplication of witnesses in later Christian hagiography) would be treated by historians as an authenticating narrative convention rather than proof of the stated number. 1 Corinthians 15:6 is treated as a stronger case specifically because of external checkability at the time of writing; the same distinction, applied evenhandedly, is what keeps 3 Nephi 17 in the none band rather than granting it the same credit.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**None** (supports, evidence_strength 0.14) for the proposition: *The event described in 3 Nephi 17 was historically seen and heard by approximately 2,500 individual witnesses.*

The text makes an unmistakably strong internal claim to broad, individualized public witness that structurally echoes 1 Corinthians 15:6, but because it lacks the external checkability that gives the Pauline parallel its evidential force, and because a rhetorical authentication device would produce identical language, it offers only none-band evidence that a real historical mass event occurred.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: No matched ancient or nineteenth-century control corpus has been supplied, so no quantitative inference should be drawn from claims that crowd estimates or collective-witness formulas are common. The secure evidentiary denominator is one narrative source reporting approximately 2,500 witnesses, not 2,500 separately preserved testimonies — structurally unlike Paul's appeal to living, consultable witnesses.

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On the text's own narrative reconstruction, a large mixed-age crowd gathered at the temple in Bountiful and each person is said to have had an individualized sensory experience of the resurrected Christ, mirroring but not replicating the structure of Paul's list of resurrection witnesses. Because Mormon's abridgment is the sole surviving witness to this account, the reconstruction that best fits the evidence available is 'a claim of mass, individualized witness transmitted through one hand,' which is compatible with, but does not itself corroborate, an underlying historical event.
