# Internal Ethical Framing in Alma 43

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Alma 43:45-47 strongly frames this battle as defense of homes, liberties, families, lands, and worship, while verse 54 portrays Moroni stopping the killing after seeing the surrounded enemy's terror. The surrender context permits a partly tactical explanation of the halt, and the account remains a one-sided Nephite presentation. The passage therefore supports a bounded local ethical framing, not a universal war doctrine or independent verification of historical conduct.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Alma 43:45-47 associates the Nephite cause with defending homes, liberties, families, lands, and worship, and says they are not guilty of the first or second offense. In verse 54, after Moroni sees the terror of the surrounded Lamanites, he orders his soldiers to stop shedding their blood.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The strongest critical qualification is that this is the Nephite narrator's legitimating account and that Moroni's halt also facilitates surrender negotiations. Even so, the explicit defense catalogue and the narrated cessation of killing strongly establish a local ethical framing in which this battle is justified as defensive and lethal force is not presented as limitless.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The passage is compatible with an actual community articulating restraints on warfare, but one-sided ancient histories and constructed war narratives also idealize their own side's motives. Its strongest historical-literary conclusion concerns the final text's moral presentation, not actual Nephite conduct.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
It strongly supports a bounded local reading of the chapter's stated war ethic and challenges readings that portray this particular battle as an unqualified celebration of conquest or killing. It supplies little independent support for Nephite historicity or for the claim that the narrated conduct occurred exactly as presented.

**Inspiration:** This literary and ethical framing by itself implies nothing about whether the text is divinely inspired.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
It does not establish a universal Book of Mormon theory of war, impartiality in the narrator's description, Moroni's private motives, historical compliance with the stated ideal, or a purely merciful rather than partly tactical explanation of verse 54.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Bounded local defensive-war framing** — predicts: Explicit justification by defense of community goods together with narrative approval of stopping lethal violence once the enemy is surrounded and terrified.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **One-sided Nephite legitimation** — predicts: A narrator portrays the in-group as reactive, morally justified, and restrained regardless of the full historical circumstances.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Primarily tactical halt** — predicts: Moroni stops killing to secure surrender or negotiate from a position of control rather than to express a general mercy principle.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
- **Ancient or modern literary composition** — predicts: Either period can produce a coherent defensive-war ideal and an idealized protagonist.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Moroni's halt is primarily a surrender tactic. — *plausible*: The following exchange concerns surrender terms, so tactical advantage is part of the immediate context; this does not erase the narrator's explicit attention to stopping bloodshed.
- The narrator idealizes the motives and restraint of his own side. — *independently supported*: In-group legitimation is common in war narratives, and Alma 43 supplies no independent Lamanite account against which to test the portrayal.
- The narrative preserves an ethic actually practiced by a historical Nephite society. — *possible*: The account permits this reconstruction, but the passage itself is the only witness offered here.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
A biblical or classical war narrative's claim that its own side fought defensively would be treated as strong evidence for the narrator's ethical framing but not as neutral verification of motives or conduct. The same distinction is appropriate here; the existing note was properly strong about the local literary claim and restrained about history.

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.7) for the proposition: *Alma 43 normatively frames Nephite violence in this battle as defense of community goods and portrays continued killing as limited once the surrounded enemy is overcome.*

The defense catalogue in Alma 43:45-47 and Moroni's order to stop the killing in verse 54 strongly support a bounded defensive-war framing for this episode, while the one-sided narration and surrender context prevent extending that conclusion to actual historical conduct or a universal war ethic.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: War narratives commonly legitimate an in-group by presenting its violence as reactive and restrained, so the framing is not distinctive evidence of historicity. That base rate does not weaken the direct textual evidence for what Alma 43 itself asks readers to regard as justified conduct.

What would move this: The weight would rise if nearby text explicitly connected Moroni's halt to a general rule forbidding unnecessary killing; it would fall if the chapter made clear that the halt was exclusively tactical or if the defense catalogue were shown to describe only reported motives that the narrator subsequently repudiates.

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Within the battle narrative, the defense catalogue supplies the stated justification for Nephite resistance, while the halt in verse 54 supplies a narrated limit at the point of overwhelming advantage. On the strongest reconstruction, Alma 43 presents justified warfare as reactive and directed toward preserving community goods, with restraint expected once further killing is no longer needed to obtain surrender.
