# Internal Ethical Framing in Alma 43

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Alma 43:45-47 provides strong evidence that the narrator frames this particular Nephite cause as defensive of families, liberty, land, and worship rather than a quest for monarchy or power. Verse 54 depicts a limit on continued killing after Moroni sees the enemy's terror, although surrender strategy is also a plausible explanation. This supports a bounded local ethical framing, not a universal doctrine or impartial verification of historical conduct.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Alma 43:45-47 frames Nephite fighting as motivated by defense of homes, liberty, family, and worship, citing a divine command not to be slain for a 'first or second offense.' Verse 54 reports Moroni halting the killing once the surrounded army shows terror.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
Together the verses construct an in-narrative ethical code tying legitimate violence to defense and capping it once the immediate threat is neutralized.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
As a claim about the text's own internal moral self-presentation this is close to an uncontested literary observation. It says nothing by itself about whether any real ancient society practiced this ethic or whether the account faithfully records an actual event.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
It strongly supports, as a textual/literary matter, that the narrative voice articulates a bounded rather than unlimited-conquest war ethic here. It is much weaker evidence that this reflects genuine historical Nephite restraint, since the account is narrated entirely from the Nephite side and a self-interested or idealizing narrator would produce identical wording.

**Inspiration:** Nothing about divine inspiration follows; at most this bears on the text's own stated ethical values, a literary claim a believer and a critic would read the same way.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
It does not establish that every Nephite war in the text followed this standard, that Nephite conduct actually matched the stated ideal, or that verse 54's stopping of bloodshed was principled rather than tactical (the surrounding verses show Moroni already negotiating terms).
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **text intends a coherent theological ethic of justified defensive war** — predicts: explicit, repeated defense-justification language and a mercy-motivated stopping point. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Nephite-authored self-idealizing war narrative (real ancient but biased source)** — predicts: the same framing regardless of actual historical conduct. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **modern composition articulating 19th-century just-war intuitions through a first-person narrator** — predicts: the same wording. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Moroni's halt in v.54 is tactical/preparatory to demanding surrender rather than a general act of mercy. — *plausible*: The surrounding verses show Moroni negotiating terms, so a purely mercy-driven reading is not the only fit.
- The account idealizes Nephite motives because it is a one-sided, in-group narrated source. — *plausible*: No independent (e.g., Lamanite-perspective) account exists to check the framing against.
- The passage reflects a genuine historical ethic actually followed by a real ancient people. — *possible*: Internally coherent but wholly unverifiable externally.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
A one-sided victor's account in the Bible (e.g., Israelite conquest narratives in Joshua) framing its own violence as divinely sanctioned and defensive is routinely read by critical scholars as self-presentation, not neutral history; the same standard applies here rather than treating scripture's self-description as automatically more reliable.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->
