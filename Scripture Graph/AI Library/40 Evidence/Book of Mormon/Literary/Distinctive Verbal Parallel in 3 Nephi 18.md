# Distinctive Verbal Parallel in 3 Nephi 18

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
The 'sift you as wheat' wording in 3 Nephi 18:18 closely matches Luke 22:31, differing mainly in audience. This is weak evidence of a KJV-mediated textual relationship: the match is real and distinctive, but because it is one instance of a broader, already-counted pattern of KJV diction in the Book of Mormon, and because ancient-text-in-KJV-idiom, loose translation, and direct modern composition all predict it about equally, it does not discriminate well between models of the text's origin. It says nothing about whether either passage is inspired.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
3 Nephi 18:18 has Jesus tell an assembled multitude 'Satan desireth to have you, that he may sift you as wheat,' wording that matches Luke 22:31 (spoken privately to Simon) almost verbatim, varying mainly in addressee (plural multitude vs. singular Simon) and verb tense/mood.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The six-word idiom 'sift you as wheat' is distinctive rather than generic scriptural phraseology, so the match is unlikely to be coincidental wording; some textual relationship (in composition, translation, or oral tradition) is the more natural reading.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The parallel is compatible with several unrelated historical scenarios: Jesus repeating a memorable image in more than one setting, the Nephite record echoing an already-known dominical saying, or the English rendering (whatever its date) drawing on familiar KJV vocabulary to express a similar concept.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
For believers this is sometimes read as internal confirmation that 3 Nephi preserves authentic, Christ-authored teaching consistent with the Gospels; for critics it is read as evidence of literary dependence on the KJV text available to a 19th-century author. Both readings share the same underlying textual fact and neither is established by the wording alone.

**Inspiration:** This wording match speaks to a compositional/translational relationship between two texts, not to whether either passage's underlying teaching is divinely inspired.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallel does not establish whether the relationship is historical repetition by Jesus, translation into familiar KJV idiom of an independently ancient saying, or direct compositional borrowing from Luke; it also does not by itself favor any one of these over the others.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Tight-control ancient translation into KJV idiom** — predicts: An ancient saying, however originally phrased, could be rendered into recognizably KJV-Gospel language by a translator working in a King James idiom, producing this match without literary borrowing.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Loose/dynamic-equivalence translation model** — predicts: Familiar biblical cadence would be used to render a conceptually similar teaching, so an idiomatic match is expected and uninformative.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **19th-century composition using the KJV as a literary source** — predicts: Direct borrowing of a well-known, distinctive Gospel phrase into a new composition is exactly what this model predicts.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Independent historical repetition of the same saying by Jesus** — predicts: Predicts the same wording only if two independent traditions each preserved it with unusual precision, a stronger requirement than the other models.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Jesus repeated the same 'sift as wheat' image in a different ministry setting. — *possible*: Teachers reuse memorable images, but this requires two independent traditions to preserve nearly identical wording, which is not independently attested.
- The wording arises from the pervasive KJV-influenced translation idiom used throughout the Book of Mormon, regardless of the underlying source's actual wording. — *plausible*: The Book of Mormon demonstrably uses King James phrasing throughout (e.g., the Isaiah chapters), so this is a documented pattern, not an ad hoc rescue.
- The phrase is a direct compositional borrowing from Luke by a 19th-century author. — *plausible*: Consistent with the well-attested general presence of KJV New Testament language throughout the Book of Mormon.
- Coincidental independent wording. — *ad hoc*: A six-word idiomatic match this specific is unlikely by chance; positing coincidence adds no explanatory value once KJV-idiom explanations are available.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
If an extra-biblical ancient text contained a near-verbatim echo of a distinctive canonical saying, scholars would default to literary dependence or shared oral tradition, not coincidence, and would not treat the match as strong evidence of authentic independent utterance; the same standard is applied here.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Weak** (supports, evidence_strength 0.22) for the proposition: *The distinctive 'sift you as wheat' wording in 3 Nephi 18:18 reflects the same KJV-mediated textual source as Luke 22:31, rather than an independently-worded ancient utterance.*

The near-verbatim, six-word match with Luke 22:31 is real and unlikely to be coincidental, but because both an ancient text rendered in KJV idiom and outright 19th-century literary borrowing predict the same wording, it only weakly discriminates between competing models of the text's origin.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: This is a single, short (six-word) idiomatic match against one KJV verse, not a sustained sequence; comparably distinctive short KJV phrase matches recur elsewhere in Book of Mormon discourse, so this is one instance of an already-counted broader pattern of KJV-influenced diction rather than an independent data point.

What would move this: A cluster of many independent, equally distinctive KJV New Testament matches concentrated in Christ's Book of Mormon discourses would strengthen the composition-dependence reading; credible independent (non-KJV-derived) attestation of the same image in an unrelated ancient source would weaken it and support translation-idiom or historical-repetition explanations instead.
