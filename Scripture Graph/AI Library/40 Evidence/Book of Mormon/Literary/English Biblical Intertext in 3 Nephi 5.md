# English Biblical Intertext in 3 Nephi 5

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
The exact expression "breathing out threatenings" in 3 Nephi 5:5 and Acts 9:1 moderately supports mediation through the English biblical tradition. Its distinctiveness weighs against wholly independent formulation, but absent pre-1830 controls and a search denominator, it does not identify direct borrowing or distinguish loose translation from nineteenth-century composition.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
3 Nephi 5:5 and Acts 9:1 share the exact three-word expression "breathing out threatenings." The surrounding objects and narrative settings differ. No matched pre-1830 English control corpus or corpus-wide count of candidate Book of Mormon–Bible comparisons has been supplied.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The uncommon shared expression most naturally reflects English-level biblical mediation or inherited biblical register. It supports a relationship with the English Acts tradition more securely than conscious, direct copying specifically from Acts 9:1, because indirect circulation through sermons and scriptural diction has not been excluded.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The wording creates difficulty for a tight-control model in which the received English reproduces an ancient expression without later-language biblical mediation. Loose translation, translation-level harmonization, and nineteenth-century composition all accommodate the wording well, so this datum does not distinguish whether ancient content underlies the English.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The strongest supportive reconstruction is that a translator used familiar KJV language to render a broadly corresponding ancient idea; the Book of Mormon's pervasive biblical register makes that historically coherent as a translation model. The same wording is also directly expected from nineteenth-century composition, so the parallel preserves but does not preferentially support an ancient-source model.

**Inspiration:** The wording implies nothing by itself about inspiration, because inspired translation and ordinary composition can both employ inherited biblical language.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallel does not establish conscious quotation, direct use of Acts rather than its wider English reception, a particular source-language expression, ancient or modern authorship, or divine inspiration.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- A translator rendered ancient content using familiar KJV-style English. — *plausible*: The received Book of Mormon independently exhibits pervasive biblical diction, although no source-language witness tests this particular reconstruction.
- A nineteenth-century author reused the expression directly from Acts or indirectly from Acts-influenced discourse. — *plausible*: KJV familiarity and religious discourse provide ordinary routes by which the expression could enter the text.
- The expression had become conventional scriptural diction rather than being borrowed directly from Acts. — *possible*: This would still constitute English biblical mediation, but its prevalence outside Acts-dependent writing has not been measured.
- The expression arose independently by coincidence. — *possible*: A three-word match can occur accidentally, but the participial metaphor is more distinctive than an ordinary doctrinal formula.
