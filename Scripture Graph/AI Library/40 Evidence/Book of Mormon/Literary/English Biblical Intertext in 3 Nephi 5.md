# English Biblical Intertext in 3 Nephi 5

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
The exact three-word expression "breathing out threatenings" provides moderate evidence that 3 Nephi 5:5 was mediated through the English biblical tradition represented by Acts 9:1. Its distinctiveness weighs against pure coincidence, but absent pre-1830 controls and a search denominator, it does not identify direct borrowing, a translation mechanism, or ancient versus modern authorship.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
3 Nephi 5:5 and Acts 9:1 share the exact three-word expression "breathing out threatenings." The surrounding objects differ, and no matched pre-1830 English corpus or corpus-wide candidate denominator has been supplied.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The marked shared expression supports English-level biblical mediation. Dependence on the broader Acts/KJV English tradition is better supported than direct borrowing from Acts 9:1 specifically, because intervening sermons or conventional biblical diction have not been excluded.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The wording creates tension for a no-modern-admixture translation model. Loose translation and nineteenth-century composition both predict such English biblical phrasing well, so the datum does not distinguish them or determine whether ancient content underlies the English.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
A translator's use of familiar KJV diction is plausible and preserves an ancient-source model, but this reconstruction is not independently verified by an underlying manuscript. Modern borrowing is comparably plausible.

**Inspiration:** The wording implies nothing by itself about whether the text is inspired; inspired translation and ordinary composition can both employ inherited biblical language.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallel does not establish conscious quotation, direct use of Acts rather than its wider English reception, a particular source-language expression, ancient or modern authorship, or divine inspiration.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- A translator rendered ancient content in familiar KJV-style English. — *plausible*: The broader received text independently exhibits pervasive biblical diction, although no source-language witness verifies this reconstruction.
- A nineteenth-century author borrowed the expression directly or through Acts-influenced discourse. — *plausible*: KJV familiarity and pre-1830 religious discourse provide ordinary transmission routes.
- The expression was already conventional scriptural diction. — *possible*: This would still imply English biblical mediation, but its prevalence outside Acts-dependent writing has not been measured.
- The wording arose independently by coincidence. — *possible*: A three-word match can occur accidentally, although the metaphor is more distinctive than an ordinary religious formula.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->
