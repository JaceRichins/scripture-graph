# Narrative Parallelism in Alma 22

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Lamoni's and his father's conversion accounts share a matched sequence -- prayer, collapse, household attendance, rising, household conversion -- that moderately supports intentional literary parallelism in the final text. This patterning gives only none-band support for the historicity, medical explanation, or divine causation of either underlying event.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Both Lamoni (Alma 18:42-43; 19:1,12) and his father (Alma 22:18,22-23) collapse as if dead after praying for divine self-revelation, are attended by household members, and rise to minister to and convert their households.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The repeated structure -- prayer, collapse, attendance by family, rising, and household conversion -- reads as a deliberate narrative template applied to both father and son rather than two independently and differently narrated episodes.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
This kind of repeated structure is compatible with two historically similar but independently occurring events narrated with shared conventions, with an editor imposing a common template on two source reports, or with a single author constructing both scenes as a matched literary pair.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Ancient narrative literature frequently reinforces a theological point by narrating two related figures' experiences with matching structure and vocabulary -- a recognized technique for signaling that the same divine pattern is at work in both cases (compare paired call or healing narratives in the Gospels). Read against that convention, the Lamoni/father collapse-and-conversion pair fits as a deliberate application of this technique: a plausible reconstruction is that the record-keeper (or the underlying tradition) is using the repetition to present father and son as undergoing the same divinely patterned conversion, which explains the tight correspondence without, on this evidence alone, telling us whether either collapse was a real physiological event, a supernatural one, or a narrative device.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The close structural and verbal correspondence strongly supports intentional literary parallelism in the final text's construction of these two scenes. It gives only none-band support for the historicity of either event, for a specifically medical explanation of the collapses, or for a divine cause, since all three models predict the same narrative repetition equally well.

**Inspiration:** Nothing about inspiration follows from the parallelism; deliberate patterning is equally available to an ancient redactor and a modern author.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Does not establish historical independence between the two episodes, a medical nature for either collapse, or divine causation for either conversion.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Independent historical reports of two similar events** — predicts: some resemblance from shared cultural/religious convention, but not necessarily the tight verbal-structural match observed. Fit with this evidence: *some difficulty*.
- **Editorial shaping of a common template onto both accounts** — predicts: exactly this kind of close structural and verbal correspondence. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Nineteenth-century composition using a repeated pattern** — predicts: the same close correspondence, produced deliberately for narrative effect. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- The repetition may be a literary pattern rather than a record of two independently corroborating events. — *plausible*
- Collapse-and-recovery is a conventional feature of conversion narratives generally, not a specially designed father-son pair. — *plausible*
- Neither account supplies an external witness independent of the shared narrative source. — *plausible*
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
Two structurally similar conversion or healing scenes within one Gospel would be read the same way: real evidence of narrative patterning, not independent proof that two historically distinct, medically or supernaturally caused events both occurred as described.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->
