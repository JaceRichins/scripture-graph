# Internal Narrative Evidence in Alma 55

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Alma 55:18-19 gives explicit third-person narratorial commentary that Moroni 'did not delight in murder or bloodshed' and forwent an opportunity to kill drunken, incapacitated Lamanite guards, choosing capture of the Lamanites and liberation of the previously-held Nephite prisoners instead. This strongly supports that the final text deliberately frames the episode as a moral statement about restraint, consistent with the text's broader idealization of Moroni (Alma 48:17-18). It does not establish historicity, a general pacifist ethic, compliance with modern law of war, or approval of the episode's other tactics.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Alma 55:19 gives explicit first-person-adjacent narratorial commentary: Moroni 'did not delight in murder or bloodshed,' and 'for this cause he might not bring upon him injustice, he would not fall upon the Lamanites and destroy them in their drunkenness,' even though 55:18 notes he could have. The sequence (55:20-24) then narrates capture, encirclement, and liberation of Nephite prisoners instead.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The verse is not mere action-reporting but explicit authorial moral commentary attached to a real tactical opportunity Moroni forgoes.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
Compatible with a narrator who is genuinely committed to portraying Moroni as an ethical exemplar throughout the text, and equally compatible with the same commentary being composed after the fact to justify or moralize a choice that also carried practical advantages (a functioning, liberatable Nephite garrison and an intact city).
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On the deliberate-ethical-commentary reconstruction, the narrator pauses the action to explain Moroni's internal motive, a marked departure from action-only reporting that signals intentional moral teaching. On the idealizing-narrator reconstruction, this is one more data point in a text that elsewhere states outright that Moroni was a paragon (Alma 48), so the explicit praise here is unsurprising rather than newly informative. Both reconstructions are compatible with every verse cited; the note treats them as live alternatives rather than picking one.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
Strongly supports that the final text intends this episode to be read as an ethical statement about restraint toward incapacitated enemies. It does not by itself establish that the underlying event occurred, that Moroni actually held this internal motive, or that the account is not retrospective idealization.

**Inspiration:** Nothing independent: the same explicit moral framing is equally at home in a genuine ancient ethical tradition, an idealizing later editor, or a 19th-century author crafting an exemplary hero.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Does not establish historicity, a general pacifist ethic, compliance with modern laws of armed conflict, or approval of the episode's other tactics (exploited intoxication, deception, or the forced labor described later in the chapter).
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Deliberate narratorial ethical commentary** — predicts: explicit first-person moral language attached to a described real choice. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Idealizing/hagiographic narrator** — predicts: the same explicit praise, selectively emphasized because Moroni is elsewhere in the text called a paragon (cf. Alma 48:17-18). Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Retrospective justification of a tactically advantageous choice** — predicts: moral language layered onto a decision that also secured a working, liberatable garrison and an intact city, without requiring the stated motive to be primary. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- The narrator idealizes or selectively reports Moroni's conduct elsewhere in the text (cf. Alma 48:17-18), so this passage may be one more instance of that pattern rather than a neutral report. — *plausible*: Independently supported by Alma 48's explicit encomium to Moroni, making a hagiographic reading a live, non-ad-hoc alternative.
- Capturing rather than killing also offered clear practical advantage: an intact, functioning garrison of Nephite prisoners to liberate and reuse. — *plausible*: The text itself notes the freed Nephites 'did join the army of Moroni,' so a tactical motive is directly attested alongside the stated ethical one.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.78) for the proposition: *The final text's explicit third-person narratorial commentary (55:19) deliberately frames Moroni's choice to capture rather than kill incapacitated enemies as an intentional ethical statement about restraint in war.*

The explicit third-person narratorial commentary in 55:19, paired with the forgone opportunity in 55:18, strongly supports that the final text deliberately frames this episode as an ethical statement, but it does not establish the episode's historicity, a general pacifist ethic, or that the stated motive was Moroni's only or dominant one.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: Explicit narratorial moral commentary on a commander's restraint is not the default mode of ancient war narration (which more often reports acts without editorializing); its presence here is somewhat more textually marked than an ordinary action report, which is part of why the literary-design reading is strong rather than merely moderate.

What would move this: A comparable episode elsewhere in the text where Moroni is reported killing incapacitated enemies without narratorial censure would weaken the 'consistent ethical exemplar' reading; further explicit moral asides of this kind clustered specifically around Moroni (beyond Alma 48 and 55) would strengthen the deliberate-design reading.
