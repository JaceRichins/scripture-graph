# Nahom and the NHM altars

> [!evidence] The observation
> 1 Nephi 16:34 says Ishmael was buried "in the place which was called
> [[Nahom]]" ([[1 Nephi 16#^1ne-16-34|1 Ne. 16:34]]). Votive altars from the
> Bar'an temple near Wadi Jawf, Yemen (c. 7th–6th century BC), carry the
> South-Arabian tribal name **NHM** (Nihm/Naham), and a tribal region of that
> name still exists in roughly the area where Lehi's party would turn east.

<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[1 Nephi 16]]–[[1 Nephi 17]] describes Ishmael's burial at a pre-existing [[Nahom]] and a later eastward turn; South Arabian inscriptions independently attest an ancient NHM tribal affiliation, but they do not identify the Book of Mormon locality, its vocalization, or Ishmael's burial. The correspondence weakly supports authentic Arabian knowledge, while flexible route reconstruction, an unknown search denominator, and possible pre-1830 map mediation keep it from independently establishing the journey or ancient authorship. Sources retained: S. Kent Brown, “New Light: Nahom and the ‘Eastward’ Turn” (JBMS 12/1, 2003); Warren P. Aston, Lehi and Sariah in Arabia (2015); Burkhard Vogt and colleagues' Bar'an temple excavation reports; and the Philip Beale critique and responses in Interpreter/JBMS.

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
1 Nephi 16:34 says Ishmael was buried in a pre-existing place called Nahom. South Arabian inscriptions conventionally transliterated NHM occur on altars from the Bar'an temple excavation at Ma'rib and identify a tribal affiliation present around the relevant broad period. Later geography also preserves an NHM/Nihm name in western Yemen. The inscriptions do not themselves identify Lehi, Ishmael, a burial place, the Book of Mormon's vocalization, or the precise location where the tribe lived.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The apologetic identification connects the unvocalized South Arabian NHM with Nahom and places it near a reconstructed southward route followed by an eastward turn toward a proposed Bountiful in Dhofar. This is geographically possible and more specific than a merely generic Arabian setting, but the route, vocalization, territorial extent, burial association, and Bountiful endpoint are reconstructed rather than directly established by the inscriptions.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The evidence is compatible with the existence of an NHM-associated people in ancient South Arabia and with an itinerary passing through their territory. It is also compatible with a modern author encountering a related map name or obtaining a chance match after investigators searched many Arabian names and route configurations.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The approximately right-period NHM attestation is genuinely interesting and weakly increases the plausibility that Nahom reflects authentic Arabian knowledge, especially if nineteenth-century access to the name was unlikely. Its force is reduced by uncertain vocalization, the difference between a tribal designation and an identified burial locality, flexible route reconstruction, an unreported search denominator, and documented pre-1830 maps containing a related name. The current 0.50 assessment is therefore reduced because the observation does not discriminate at a moderate level between the principal origin models.

**Inspiration:** This geographic correlation says nothing by itself about divine inspiration, which is not entailed by either an ancient identification or a modern source explanation.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
It does not establish that Nahom is the same place as the NHM inscriptions, that Ishmael was buried there, that the proposed route or Bountiful identification is correct, or that Lehi's journey and the Book of Mormon narrative are historical.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Nahom and South Arabian NHM are a chance consonantal resemblance selected from a large field of possible Arabian names. — *plausible*: Semitic writing commonly foregrounds consonants, vocalization is uncertain, and the number of names and route locations examined has not been reported.
- A nineteenth-century geographic source containing Nehhm or a related spelling ultimately supplied the name. — *possible*: Such spellings appeared on pre-1830 European maps, but direct evidence that Joseph Smith or an associate consulted a relevant map has not been supplied.
- The inscriptions identify a tribe whose territory included the narrative's burial location. — *possible*: The inscriptions independently attest an NHM affiliation, but an offering at Ma'rib does not by itself locate the tribe's territory or a place called Nahom.
- The southward itinerary and eastward turn independently locate Nahom near NHM territory. — *plausible*: The proposed route follows real Arabian travel constraints, but its stopping points and endpoint remain sufficiently flexible for retrospective fitting.
- The consonantal correspondence alone identifies the names as linguistically identical. — *ad hoc*: That conclusion assumes the required vocalization and referent without direct inscriptional evidence.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Weak** (supports, evidence_strength 0.3) for the proposition: *The name Nahom and its narrative location preserve authentic pre-exilic South Arabian geographic knowledge that is materially more expected from an ancient source than from nineteenth-century composition.*

The right-period South Arabian NHM attestation is an interesting geographic correspondence that weakly supports authentic Arabian knowledge in 1 Nephi, but uncertain identification, route flexibility, possible map mediation, and an unknown search denominator prevent moderate or strong weight.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: No matched-control study or search denominator is supplied. Researchers could compare numerous Arabian names, variant transliterations, tribal designations, possible routes, stopping points, and coastal endpoints; this look-elsewhere opportunity materially lowers the evidentiary value. Ancient Near Eastern texts frequently preserve consonantal place and tribal names, while approximate matches across related Semitic languages are not rare enough to make three consonants decisive.

What would move this: Weight would rise if securely provenanced pre-exilic evidence located an inhabited or burial locality vocalizable as Nahom at a narrowly predicted route junction, together with a comprehensive matched-name analysis and strong evidence that the name was unavailable in Joseph Smith's environment. It would fall if pre-1830 access to the relevant map name were demonstrated, linguistic work excluded the proposed vocalization or referent, or matched controls showed comparable three-consonant route matches to be common.
