# If-and conditionals in the Original Manuscript

> [!language] The observation
> The Original Manuscript and 1830 edition contain Hebrew-like *if…and*
> conditionals that are ungrammatical in English — e.g. the original text of
> [[1 Nephi 17#^1ne-17-50|1 Ne. 17:50]]: "if he should command me that I
> should say unto this water be thou earth **and** it should be earth."
> Joseph Smith removed the *and*s in the 1837 edition. Biblical Hebrew
> regularly builds conditionals this way (*wə-* apodosis).

<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Royal Skousen's Critical Text Project reports multiple if-and conditionals in the earliest recoverable text, including material associated with [[1 Nephi 17]] and [[Helaman 12]], and later regularization confirms that some wording was regarded as nonstandard. The pattern is compatible with a Semitic calque, but marginal English precedents, oral dictation, KJV-style coordination, and the absence of a matched-corpus denominator limit it to weak support for a Semitic source. Sources retained: Royal Skousen, Analysis of Textual Variants of the Book of Mormon and “The Original Language of the Book of Mormon” (JBMS 3/1, 1994); treatments in Echoes and Evidences (2002); and critical discussions in Dialogue/Sunstone.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
The earliest recoverable text contains multiple conditionals in which an if-clause is followed or coordinated by and, including the construction represented in 1 Nephi 17:50 and a cluster in Helaman 12. Some were subsequently regularized. The existence of nonstandard conditional syntax is well supported, but the supplied note does not provide a complete inventory, a stable definition of qualifying examples, or matched frequencies from comparable dictated, biblical, pseudo-biblical, and Early Modern English texts.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
A Semitic-source reading treats the conjunction introducing the consequence as a retained calque. That is a possible explanation, but calling the form something English would never produce is too strong: marginal English precedents and conjunction-heavy oral syntax exist, and editing a conspicuous construction shows that it was perceived as nonstandard rather than identifying its source language.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The syntax is compatible with a relatively literal translation from a Semitic source. It is also compatible with spontaneous dictation, KJV-influenced archaism, Early Modern English survivals, or interaction among those mechanisms.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The repeated construction deserves study because it was present early and was not simply introduced by later editors. Without a controlled denominator or evidence that the precise construction is substantially more frequent in translated Semitic texts than in comparable English dictation and pseudo-biblical prose, it supplies only weak evidence for a Semitic source. The current 0.50 score is reduced because the leading models presently predict occasional marked coordination with insufficiently known frequency.

**Inspiration:** This syntactic feature does not independently indicate whether the text is divinely inspired; inspired and non-inspired origin models can produce either translated or dictated marked syntax.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
It does not establish that every cited conditional has the same cause, that the construction is uniquely Hebrew, or that the Book of Mormon as a whole was translated from a Hebrew document.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Tight translation of an ancient Semitic text** — predicts: Repeated source-language conjunctions may be carried into English even when they produce marked conditional syntax.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Loose translation of an ancient text** — predicts: Target-language smoothing should usually remove such calques, although occasional source interference remains possible.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
- **Nineteenth-century oral dictation** — predicts: Planning pressure, restarts, and additive conjunctions can generate irregular protasis-apodosis structures.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Archaizing or Early Modern English-style composition** — predicts: Marginal constructions found in older English or biblical-register prose may recur without a Semitic source.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Scribal corruption** — predicts: An occasional added conjunction could occur in transmission, but repeated instances across the text would require multiple changes.. Fit with this evidence: *some difficulty*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Oral dictation produced additive and-conjunctions across clause boundaries. — *plausible*: The Book of Mormon's oral dictation is historically established, and additive coordination is a known property of spontaneous speech, although a matched quantitative study is still needed.
- The construction reflects marginal Early Modern English usage. — *independently supported*: English precedents reportedly exist, refuting uniqueness; whether their type and frequency adequately predict the Book of Mormon distribution remains disputed.
- KJV-saturated archaizing style encouraged conjunction-heavy conditional syntax. — *plausible*: Biblical English influence is pervasive in the Book of Mormon, but the causal connection to this precise construction has not been quantified.
- Scribes independently introduced the conjunctions. — *possible*: Scribal additions occur generally, but repeated examples and early attestation make a wholesale scribal explanation less economical.
- Later removal proves that the readings came from Hebrew rather than English. — *ad hoc*: Editing proves that the wording was considered awkward; it does not identify the mechanism that originally produced it.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Weak** (supports, evidence_strength 0.2) for the proposition: *The earliest Book of Mormon's if-and conditional constructions are translation calques materially more expected from an ancient Semitic source than from English dictation or archaizing composition.*

The earliest text contains real and sometimes edited if-and conditionals, but existing English and dictation-based alternatives plus the absence of matched frequencies make them only weak evidence for translation from a Semitic source.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: At least a dozen candidates are claimed within the entire Book of Mormon, but the total number of conditional clauses searched, the inclusion rules, and frequencies in matched corpora are not supplied. Because investigators can search hundreds of syntactic features across a long text, both feature-selection and corpus-wide look-elsewhere costs are substantial.

What would move this: Weight would rise if a preregistered analysis found the narrowly defined construction substantially enriched in translations from ancient Hebrew relative to large matched corpora of dictated, KJV-imitative, Early Modern, and nineteenth-century prose. It would fall if those controls reproduced the Book of Mormon rate and distribution, or if textual analysis showed that most examples are classification artifacts or scribal additions.
