# Literary-Context Evidence in Isaiah 14

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
The immediate literary referent of the fallen figure is strongly identified as a human imperial ruler: the speech is introduced against the king of Babylon, observers call him a man, and his fate is described through kingdoms, prisoners, burial, land, people, and descendants. This contextual evidence weighs against treating verses 12–15, in isolation, as referring only to Satan.

**Does not establish:** It does not establish that no secondary, typological, or canonical application to Satan is legitimate.

**Alternative explanations:**
- The historical king may function typologically as an image of Satan without ceasing to be the immediate referent.
- A prophetic double-reference reading may apply the language to both an earthly tyrant and a premortal rebel.
- Some theological readings understand the address as shifting from the earthly king to the spiritual power behind him.
