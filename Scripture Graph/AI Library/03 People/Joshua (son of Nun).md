# Joshua (son of Nun)

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Joshua son of Nun—called Oshea in the scout roster and Jehoshua by Moses (Numbers 13:8, 16)—is portrayed as Moses's assistant, a military commander, one of the two faithful scouts, and the publicly commissioned leader who brings Israel across the [[Jordan River]] into [[Canaan]]. His canonical role includes warfare, public reading of the [[Law of Moses]], covenant administration, and allocation of tribal inheritances with Eleazar. The narrative records both major successes and complications: he protests after defeat at Ai, and Joshua and Israel's leaders make the Gibeonite treaty without seeking the LORD's counsel. Conquest violence is integral to the account and should be considered with [[Why did God command the conquest of Canaan]]; the narrative itself does not settle modern historical and archaeological questions. Joshua son of Nun must also be distinguished from the postexilic high priest Joshua in Zechariah and Haggai and Joshua the Beth-shemite in 1 Samuel 6.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
Joshua's formation occurs under [[Moses (prophet)]]. He commands Israel against Amalek (Exodus 17:9-14), accompanies Moses toward Sinai (Exodus 24:13), remains at the tabernacle (Exodus 33:11), and asks Moses to forbid Eldad and Medad before Moses answers that he wishes all the LORD's people might receive the spirit (Numbers 11:27-29). As a scout, Joshua and [[Caleb]] urge Israel not to fear or rebel and are excepted from the judgment upon the numbered wilderness generation (Numbers 14:6-9, 30, 38).

His succession is unusually explicit in the final form of the narrative. The LORD selects him as a man in whom is the spirit; Moses presents him before Eleazar and the congregation, lays a hand upon him, charges him, and transfers some of his honor (Numbers 27:18-23). Deuteronomy repeats the public charge and calls Joshua full of the spirit of wisdom after Moses lays hands upon him (Deuteronomy 31:7, 14, 23; 34:9). Numbers 27:21 nevertheless differentiates his role from Moses's by directing him to stand before Eleazar for judgment through the Urim. [[Joshua 1]] grounds his leadership in courage, obedience, and continual attention to the written law (Joshua 1:6-9).

At the Jordan, the LORD promises to magnify Joshua so Israel will know that he is with Joshua as he was with Moses (Joshua 3:7; 4:14). Joshua turns the crossing into intergenerational instruction through twelve memorial stones (Joshua 4:4-7, 21-24). In [[Joshua 5]], the captain of the LORD's host declares the ground holy and tells Joshua to remove his shoe (Joshua 5:13-15), closely echoing Exodus 3:5. The literary parallel is strong, but the figure's precise identity is not stated.

The campaigns dominate Joshua's public career. The narrative attributes the instructions for [[Jericho]] to the LORD and depicts Joshua carrying them out, including destruction of the city and preservation of Rahab (Joshua 6:2-25). It likewise portrays him commanding the southern and northern campaigns (Joshua 10:7-14, 40-42; 11:6-15). Joshua 10 represents Joshua as addressing the sun and moon and says that the LORD fought for Israel; the passage alone does not determine the account's physical mechanism or independently establish its historical occurrence. Joshua 11:23's broad statement that Joshua took the whole land must be read alongside Joshua 13:1 and Judges 2:21, which acknowledge land and peoples remaining. Proposed explanations include campaign victory without complete occupation, different geographical scales, conventional ancient conquest hyperbole, and distinct literary traditions; the text does not select among all these modern frameworks.

Joshua is not presented without complication. After Ai's first defeat he mourns and protests, and the LORD redirects him toward Israel's covenant violation (Joshua 7:6-13). At Gibeon, Joshua and Israel's leaders make a treaty after failing to ask counsel of the LORD, then preserve the Gibeonites because of the oath while imposing subordinate service (Joshua 9:14-27). Joshua also writes and publicly reads the law (Joshua 8:30-35), divides inheritances with Eleazar and the tribal heads (Joshua 14:1-2; 18:1-10; 19:51), and renews the covenant at Shechem (Joshua 24:1-26). He dies at 110. Joshua 24:28-31 and Judges 2:6-9 closely connect his generation and the elders who outlived him with continued service to the LORD; their near-verbatim wording establishes literary relationship, not independent corroboration.
<!-- SG:END scripture-profile -->
