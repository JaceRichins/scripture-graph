# Paul (apostle)

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Paul—also called Saul of Tarsus—is the former persecutor who became the New Testament’s most extensively documented missionary to the Gentiles and the named sender of a major body of Christian letters. Acts identifies Saul and Paul as the same person (Acts 13:9), not as [[Saul (king)]], and traces his movement from hostility toward Jesus’s disciples to preaching that Jesus is the Christ (Acts 8:1–3; 9:1–22). Paul describes his vocation as a calling by Jesus Christ and divine grace rather than a merely human appointment (Galatians 1:1, 13–17; 1 Corinthians 15:8–10). His importance is immense, but he did not work alone or appear at Christianity’s beginning: the Jerusalem apostles, established disciples, inherited traditions about Jesus, and coworkers such as [[Barnabas]], [[Silas]], and [[Timothy (companion of Paul)]] all precede or accompany his ministry.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
The canonical portrait begins with Saul guarding the witnesses’ clothes at [[Acts 7]] and approving Stephen’s death, then ravaging the church and seeking authority to arrest disciples (Acts 7:58; 8:1–3; 9:1–2). [[Acts 9]], [[Acts 22]], and [[Acts 26]] present his encounter with the risen Jesus as the decisive reversal. The accounts agree on the encounter and commission but vary in details of presentation; Paul’s own letters more briefly attest that God revealed His Son to him and that he saw the risen Lord (Galatians 1:13–16; 1 Corinthians 9:1; 15:8–10). Ananias’s courageous ministry and Paul’s baptism make [[Conversion of Paul]] both a divine calling story and a story of human discipleship (Acts 9:10–18; 22:12–16).

Paul was a Jew from [[Tarsus]] in [[Cilicia (Bible Dictionary)]], trained under Gamaliel, a Pharisee, and a Roman citizen by birth (Acts 21:39; 22:3, 25–29; 23:6). Those overlapping identities appear throughout his work: he reasons from Israel’s scriptures in synagogues, preaches among Gentiles, and uses Roman legal protections. From [[Antioch]], he and [[Barnabas]] were set apart for missionary labor (Acts 13:1–4). His journeys through places including [[Ephesus]], [[Corinth]], and [[Athens]] show a persistent but adaptable witness: scriptural argument in synagogues, engagement with Gentile hearers, formation of congregations, appointment of elders, and sustained pastoral correspondence (Acts 13:14–43; 14:21–23; 17:2–34; 18:1–11; 19:1–20).

Gentile inclusion became a defining issue. Paul and Barnabas resisted making circumcision a prerequisite for salvation and participated in the deliberation recorded in [[Acts 15]]. Paul also says James, Cephas, and John recognized his mission to the Gentiles (Galatians 2:7–10). Yet cooperation did not remove conflict: Paul reports opposing [[Peter (apostle)]] at Antioch when Peter withdrew from eating with Gentiles, behavior that influenced other Jewish believers (Galatians 2:11–14). The account is strong evidence of candid disagreement among recognized apostles, though it supplies Paul’s perspective and does not record Peter’s response.

Paul’s teaching centers on [[Jesus Christ]], grace, covenant membership, holy communal life, and [[Resurrection]]. He reads [[Abraham (patriarch)]] as father of believing Jews and Gentiles because Abraham’s faith was reckoned as righteousness before circumcision (Romans 4:1–12; Galatians 3:6–9, 26–29). He treats the [[Law of Moses]] as holy yet unable to produce the life God gives through Christ, and as a custodian whose role must be read in relation to promise and fulfillment (Romans 7:7–12; Galatians 3:15–25). He deliberately contrasts [[Adam]] and Christ in explaining sin, death, grace, obedience, and restored life (Romans 5:12–19; 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45–49). In [[1 Corinthians 15]], he makes Christ’s resurrection indispensable to Christian proclamation and hope.

His character in the texts is forceful, intellectually agile, affectionate, sometimes severe, and remarkably durable. He can confront Peter publicly (Galatians 2:11–14), rebuke congregational misconduct (1 Corinthians 5:1–13), describe coworkers with warmth (Romans 16:1–16), support himself through labor (Acts 18:1–3; 20:33–35), and continue preaching amid beatings, imprisonment, and shipwreck (Acts 16:22–25; 21:13; 27:1–44; 2 Corinthians 11:23–28). Acts ends not with his death but with Paul in [[Rome]], guarded yet receiving visitors and preaching openly (Acts 28:16–31).

## Teachings & Conference
<!-- SG:BEGIN conference -->
Modern conference teaching in the supplied library uses Paul chiefly as a witness that discipleship can transform both identity and direction. [[To Be a Follower of Christ (Elder Alfred Kyungu, October 2021)]] reflects on Saul’s conversion as a model of becoming Christ’s follower. [[The Healing Ointment of Forgiveness (Elder Kevin R. Duncan, April 2016)]] stresses that God sees a person’s potential beyond a hostile past: the persecutor became a devoted and fearless disciple. [[“Then Will I Make Weak Things Become Strong” (Elder Kevin S. Hamilton, April 2022)]] similarly presents the change from persecuting Pharisee to apostle as an example of transformation.

[[Blessed and Happy Are Those Who Keep the Commandments of God (Elder Von G. Keetch, October 2015)]] shifts attention to Ananias: Paul’s spiritual beginning also depended on another disciple obeying a difficult command. Earlier treatments in [[Conference Report, April 1925 (General Conference (full report), April 1925)]], [[Conference Report, April 1929 (General Conference (full report), April 1929)]], and [[Conference Report, October 1926 (General Conference (full report), October 1926)]] likewise emphasize the persecutor’s transformation and subsequent apostolic witness. That recurring application is faithful to Paul’s broad canonical arc, but devotional retellings should not erase the process visible in Acts—Ananias’s ministry, Paul’s reception among sometimes-wary disciples, years of labor, conflict, suffering, and continued dependence on grace.
<!-- SG:END conference -->

## Related
<!-- SG:BEGIN related -->
Begin with [[Conversion of Paul]] and read [[Acts 9]], [[Acts 22]], and [[Acts 26]] together; their shared core and differing narrative details provide the fullest canonical account of his calling. Then follow [[Barnabas]], [[Silas]], and [[Timothy (companion of Paul)]] to see that Paul’s [[Missionary Work]] was sustained by a network rather than by a solitary hero.

For Paul’s place in the early church, pair [[Acts 15]] with [[Jerusalem conference]], [[Antioch]], [[Peter (apostle)]], and [[Jews and Gentiles]]. For his theology, the most fruitful paths are [[Faith]], [[Grace]], [[Law of Moses]], [[Abraham (patriarch)]], [[Adam]], [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], and [[Resurrection]]. His principal urban settings include [[Tarsus]], [[Ephesus]], [[Corinth]], [[Athens]], and [[Rome]]. Two necessary critical extensions are [[Did Paul invent Christianity]], which tests claims about his influence against evidence for a pre-Pauline church and inherited traditions, and [[What did Paul teach about women]], which should hold together his women coworkers and the genuinely disputed restrictive passages.
