# Adam

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Adam is the canonical first man and primordial ancestor: Genesis narrates his formation, vocation, marriage, transgression, expulsion, family, and death ([[Genesis 2]]; [[Genesis 3]]; [[Genesis 5]]). Restoration scripture substantially expands the portrait, presenting him as a worshipper, recipient of the gospel, prophet, record keeper, patriarch, and priesthood head ([[Moses 5]]; [[Moses 6]]; [[D&C 107]]). It also explicitly identifies him as [[Michael (archangel)]] (D&C 27:11; D&C 107:54), an identification not made within the biblical Michael passages themselves. Later scripture interprets Adam chiefly in relation to [[Jesus Christ]]: Adam is associated with fall, death, and condemnation, while Christ brings grace, justification, resurrection, and life (Romans 5:12-19; 1 Corinthians 15:21-49). These are clear claims of the canon; their acceptance as externally verified history is a separate question addressed by [[Were Adam and Eve real people]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
**Creation and naming.** Genesis 2, Moses 3, and Abraham 5 each narrate God forming Adam, giving him work and a commandment, and bringing the animals for him to name — "whatsoever Adam called every living creature, that was the name thereof" (Genesis 2:19-20; Moses 3:19-20; Abraham 5:20). All three then describe God causing a deep sleep, taking a rib, and forming Eve, whom Adam names: "This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh: she shall be called Woman" (Genesis 2:21-23; Moses 3:21-23; Abraham 5:15,17). Abraham 5 orders some of these events differently than Genesis and Moses, so the three should be read as parallel witnesses rather than identically sequenced ones, and Moses and Abraham closely follow Genesis-type material rather than standing as fully independent sources.

**Temptation and Fall.** D&C 29 supplies the moral background Genesis leaves implicit: the devil "was before Adam," already in rebellion, and "tempted Adam, and he partook of the forbidden fruit and transgressed the commandment... because he yielded unto temptation" (D&C 29:36,40). In the garden, Adam and Eve hide at the sound of God's voice; God's question ("Where art thou?") is followed by judgment on the serpent, then the woman, then Adam, and by the sentence that the ground would be cursed and eaten in sorrow "for thy sake" (Genesis 3:8-9,17; Moses 4:14-15,17,23).

**Patriarch, teacher, and priesthood head.** After Eden, Adam "began to till the earth," fathered children with Eve, and the couple "called upon the name of the Lord" though "shut out from his presence" (Moses 5:1-4); he offered commanded sacrifice before knowing its meaning and was taught it stood for the Only Begotten (Moses 5:5-11). Adam later called his sons to repent and named Seth "another seed, instead of Abel, whom Cain slew" (Moses 6:1-2); his household kept "a book of remembrance... in the language of Adam" and a genealogy called "the book of the generations of Adam" (Moses 6:5,8) — a record-keeping role Genesis 5 echoes with its own toledot heading (Genesis 5:1-4). D&C 107 makes Adam head of a priesthood ordination line: he personally ordained Seth at 69 and blessed him three years before his death, and ordained Enos at 134 "by the hand of Adam" (D&C 107:41-45); the text does not say Adam ordained Cainan, only that Cainan met him journeying to Shedolamak before his own later ordination (D&C 107:45).

**Michael, the archangel.** D&C 27:11 and D&C 107:54 explicitly identify Adam as Michael, "the prince of all, the ancient of days" — a canonical identification within the Doctrine and Covenants itself, not merely later commentary on it. On that basis, revelations describing Michael sounding the resurrection trump and leading heaven's armies against the devil (D&C 29:26; 88:112-115), and "the voice of Michael" amid Restoration events (D&C 128:20-21), describe Adam. Michael also appears in Daniel 10 and in Jude 1:9's dispute with the devil over Moses's body, but neither passage itself equates that Michael with Adam.

**Type of Christ.** Paul calls Adam "the figure of him that was to come" and builds an asymmetrical contrast — Christ's grace exceeding, not merely balancing, Adam's transgression (Romans 5:14-17). 1 Corinthians 15 narrows the pairing to resurrection: "as in Adam all die, even so in Christ shall all be made alive"; "the first man Adam was made a living soul; the last Adam was made a quickening spirit" (1 Corinthians 15:22,45).

**Doctrinal reference point and genealogical anchor.** 2 Nephi 2 argues the Fall was necessary for mortality and joy: "Adam fell that men might be; and men are, that they might have joy" (2 Nephi 2:19-25). King Benjamin's angel and Moroni both ground the innocence of children and the natural man's condition in "the fall of Adam," while insisting children are saved through Christ rather than condemned by inheritance (Mosiah 3:11-26; Moroni 8:8-20). Beyond Genesis 5's ten-generation line to Noah, Jude calls Enoch "the seventh from Adam" (Jude 1:14-15), and Luke's genealogy of Jesus runs backward through David, Abraham, and Noah all the way to Adam and God (Luke 3:23-38) — the furthest point either New Testament genealogy reaches.

## Teachings & Conference
<!-- SG:BEGIN conference -->
The indexed conference record consistently identifies Adam with Michael, but the reports do not all add the same degree of explanation. [[Conference Report, October 1898 (General Conference (full report), October 1898)]] and [[Conference Report, October 1899 (General Conference (full report), October 1899)]] repeat the formulation “Michael, or Adam,” together with the titles father, prince, and Ancient of Days—language closely tied to D&C 27:11. [[Conference Report, April 1916 (General Conference (full report), April 1916)]] makes an important boundary explicit: Michael became Adam, but Adam was not Jehovah, the Son. [[Conference Report, October 1925 (General Conference (full report), October 1925)]] emphasizes Adam's premortal identity as Michael the Archangel and treats him as a leader before earth life. These reports document Latter-day Saint teaching about Adam; their claims should be distinguished from what Genesis, Daniel, or Jude state without Restoration revelation.
