# Mary Magdalene

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Mary Magdalene is a woman whom Jesus healed and whom Luke names among the women who accompanied Jesus and the Twelve and supported them from their resources (Luke 8:1–3). Each Gospel names her in its passion or resurrection narrative. Matthew, Mark, and John explicitly place her at the crucifixion; Matthew and Mark place her at the burial site; and all four associate her with the subsequent visit to the tomb. [[John 20]] gives her the fullest individual account: she moves from grief and mistaken recognition to announcing to the disciples that she had seen the risen Lord (John 20:11–18). The canonical texts distinguish her from [[Mary (mother of Jesus)]] and other women named Mary, and they never identify her as a prostitute, Luke 7's unnamed sinful woman, or Mary of Bethany.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
**Healing and ministry.** Luke introduces Mary as one from whom seven devils had departed and as one of the women accompanying Jesus and the Twelve and supporting them from their resources (Luke 8:1–3; [[Luke 8]]). The text does not provide a modern diagnosis or associate the condition with sexual wrongdoing.

**Crucifixion and burial.** Matthew and Mark name Mary among women watching the crucifixion from afar (Matthew 27:55–56; Mark 15:40), while John places her among women standing by the cross (John 19:25). These descriptions need not refer to the same moment, but the accounts should not be silently collapsed. Matthew and Mark then explicitly place Mary at the tomb where Jesus was laid (Matthew 27:61; Mark 15:47), creating internal narrative continuity with her return after the Sabbath.

**The tomb accounts.** The Gospels give different named rosters. Matthew names Mary Magdalene and the other Mary (Matthew 28:1); Mark adds Mary the mother of James and Salome (Mark 16:1); Luke names Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James, and other women among the reporters (Luke 24:10); John narrates Mary Magdalene individually (John 20:1). These may be selective lists, but no Gospel itself supplies the later composite roster.

**Recognition and witness.** In [[John 20]], Mary sees the displaced stone and alerts Peter and the other disciple (John 20:1–2). After remaining at the tomb in grief, she sees angels and then Jesus, initially supposing him to be the gardener (John 20:11–15). She recognizes him when he calls her by name (John 20:16). Jesus directs her to carry his message to the disciples, and she reports that she has seen the Lord (John 20:17–18). Within John's narrative, she is the first person explicitly shown recognizing the risen Jesus.

**Disbelief and textual qualification.** Luke says the women's report initially seemed to the apostles like idle tales, though Peter then investigates (Luke 24:9–12; [[Luke 24]]). Mark 16:9–11 explicitly says Jesus appeared first to Mary and that her report was disbelieved, but these verses belong to Mark's textually disputed longer ending. John's account therefore should carry the primary weight for Mary's individual encounter. Across the accounts, the resulting portrait is of a healed, materially contributing, persistent disciple and resurrection witness; it is a cross-Gospel synthesis, not a complete biography supplied by any one evangelist.
<!-- SG:END scripture-profile -->

## Teachings & Conference
<!-- SG:BEGIN conference -->
General conference teaching has usually presented Mary Magdalene through the themes of healing, steadfast discipleship, grief transformed by resurrection, and personal witness. [[Certain Women (Linda K. Burton, April 2017)]] draws on Luke 8's women who ministered to Christ, while [[Spiritually Whole in Him (President Camille N. Johnson, April 2025)]] describes Mary as both healed by and made whole in Jesus Christ.

Resurrection messages emphasize her constancy at the tomb. [[The Resurrection of Jesus Christ (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, April 2014)]], [[He Is Risen! (President Thomas S. Monson, April 2010)]], [[I Know That My Redeemer Lives! (President Thomas S. Monson, April 2007)]], and [[The Grave Has No Victory (Reyna I. Aburto, April 2021)]] retell the women's early visit and the announcement that Christ had risen. [[Sunday Will Come (Elder Joseph B. Wirthlin, October 2006)]] uses the grief surrounding the cross to frame Christian hope that sorrow is not final.

[[Encounter at the Empty Tomb (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, April 2026)]] and [[’Tis Eastertide No One Walks Alone (Elder Gerrit W. Gong, April 2026)]] focus particularly on Mary's encounter with the risen Lord, including her initial supposition that he was the gardener. [[Eternal Life—to Know Our Heavenly Father and His Son, Jesus Christ (Elder Robert D. Hales, October 2014)]] uses Jesus's message to Mary about ascending to “my Father, and your Father” to teach the relationship offered through Christ. These sermons apply Mary's witness devotionally; that application should remain distinguishable from historical arguments about how the Gospel traditions developed.
<!-- SG:END conference -->

## Related
<!-- SG:BEGIN related -->
Read [[Luke 8]] for Mary's healing, discipleship, and material support; [[Matthew 27]] and [[Mark 15]] for her named presence across crucifixion and burial; and [[John 20]] for her grief, recognition, commission, and testimony. Compare [[Matthew 28]], [[Mark 16]], and [[Luke 24]] to see both the women's shared prominence and differences among the accounts.

The central connected subjects are [[Jesus Christ]], [[Crucifixion of Jesus Christ]], and [[Resurrection of Jesus]]. [[Mary (mother of Jesus)]] is a distinct person despite their shared presence in John 19:25. [[Peter (apostle)]] and [[Apostles]] illuminate the reception of the women's report. For the larger historical and theological inquiry, continue to [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]]; Mary's testimony is important evidence within that study but does not settle it by itself.
