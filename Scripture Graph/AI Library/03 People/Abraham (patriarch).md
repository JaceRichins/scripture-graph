# Abraham (patriarch)

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Abraham—called Abram before Genesis 17—is the ancestral patriarch through whom the canon develops promises of land, posterity, nations, blessing, and covenant belonging. His story runs chiefly through [[Genesis 11]]–[[Genesis 25]]: call and migration, worship in Canaan, rescue of Lot, covenant, the births of Ishmael and Isaac, the binding of Isaac, Sarah’s burial, and Abraham’s death. The text presents neither a flat hero nor a simple moral example. Abraham trusts, worships, intercedes, and obeys, but he also acts from fear in the sister-wife episodes and participates in a household arrangement that brings suffering to Hagar. Later writings make him a theological ancestor: [[Paul (apostle)]] uses his pre-circumcision faith to explain Gentile inclusion, Jesus invokes him in teachings about repentance, covenant identity, judgment, and [[Resurrection]], and Latter-day Saint scripture expands his priesthood and premortal teachings. Traditional BCE dates are approximate; the canon supplies relative ages but no secure absolute chronology.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
The arc begins with Terah's family and the movement from Ur toward Canaan ([[Genesis 11]]). In [[Genesis 12]], the Lord calls Abram from his country and kindred, promises to make of him a great nation and a blessing to all families of the earth, and brings him into Canaan (Genesis 12:1-8). His altar-building and invocation of the Lord recur in [[Genesis 13]] (13:3-4, 18); the chapter also notes that Canaanites already inhabited the land (12:6). After separating from Lot, Abram rescues him in [[Genesis 14]], receives Melchizedek's blessing, pays tithes, and refuses the king of Sodom's wealth (14:12-24).

The promised future repeatedly appears impossible. Abram is childless in [[Genesis 15]], yet believes the promise of innumerable seed, and his faith is counted as righteousness (15:2-6). [[Genesis 16]] then records Sarai — not God — proposing Hagar as a means of obtaining children; Abram complies, and conflict follows (16:1-6). Doctrine and Covenants 132:34 later frames this same episode as something God commanded Abraham — a divine-command framing genuinely absent from Genesis 16's own narration, a difference worth preserving rather than silently harmonizing. In [[Genesis 17]], God changes Abram's name to Abraham ('father of many nations'), establishes circumcision as the covenant's sign, and specifies that Sarah will bear Isaac (17:5-21). Isaac's birth and circumcision fulfill that promise in [[Genesis 21]] (21:1-5) when Abraham is 100; Ishmael also receives divine preservation and promises (16:10-12; 21:13-21).

Abraham's characterization is not flattened into pure sainthood. [[Genesis 18]] pairs urgent hospitality with sustained intercession for Sodom (18:1-8, 22-33). By contrast, [[Genesis 12]] and [[Genesis 20]] twice show him presenting Sarah as his sister out of fear for his life, exposing her to a foreign ruler's household before divine intervention restores her (12:11-20; 20:1-18); Genesis 20:12 notes Sarah really is his half-sister, but that detail does not erase the stated fear or its consequences, and the narrator never pronounces an explicit verdict on the episodes either way. Whether the two episodes are separate incidents or a single tradition told twice is a genuine, unresolved question among scholars.

Genesis 22's test brings promise and command into their sharpest tension: Abraham proceeds toward offering Isaac until an angel intervenes, a ram is substituted, and the promises are renewed (22:1-18). Hebrews 11:17-19 later interprets his willingness as faith that God could raise Isaac from the dead — an explicit New Testament interpretation, not a motive Genesis itself states. Abraham afterward mourns Sarah and purchases the cave of Machpelah as a burial property (not a general territorial claim) in [[Genesis 23]], arranges Isaac's marriage to Rebekah through an unnamed servant (sometimes inferred, not confirmed, to be Eliezer of Genesis 15:2) in [[Genesis 24]], and gives Isaac the principal inheritance while sending the sons of his concubines away with gifts in [[Genesis 25]] (25:5-6). He dies at 175; Isaac and Ishmael bury him beside Sarah (25:7-10). Notably, Genesis 12:4 gives his age at departure from Haran as 75, while Abraham 2:14 gives 62 for the same event — a real, unreconciled discrepancy between the two accounts.

Later scripture turns Abraham into sustained theological argument more often than new narrative. Romans 4:9-12 stresses that his faith was reckoned as righteousness before circumcision; Galatians 3:6-9, 26-29 calls those of faith Abraham's children and those in Christ his seed — broadening covenant kinship without, by itself, resolving every dispute about ethnic Israel. Jesus and John the Baptist both reject reliance on Abrahamic ancestry without corresponding repentance or works (Matthew 3:7-10; Luke 3:7-9; John 8:33-40), while Jesus also calls a healed woman a daughter of Abraham and the repentant Zacchæus a son of Abraham (Luke 13:16; 19:9), and invokes 'the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob' at the burning bush as evidence for the resurrection (Exodus 3:6; Mark 12:26-27; Luke 20:37-38). Luke 16:19-31 places Abraham within a parable of reversal and judgment, but its imagery of 'Abraham's bosom' should not by itself be read as a complete map of the afterlife.

The Latter-day Saint book of Abraham adds a first-person account: Abraham seeks priesthood blessings, rejects his fathers' idolatry, is delivered from an attempted sacrifice, migrates by revelation, and is shown premortal intelligences and the heavens (Abraham 1:1-18; 2:3-11; 3:1-28), with the creation account proper following in Abraham 4-5. Its accepted scriptural status within the Church and the separate, disputed historical questions about its production from the papyri should be kept distinct — see [[Abraham, Book of (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Translation and Historicity of the Book of Abraham (Essay)]]. Latter-day revelation further extends Abraham's profile: he received priesthood from Melchizedek (D&C 84:14), the 'dispensation of the gospel of Abraham' was restored through Elias (D&C 110:12), and he 'hath entered into his exaltation' (D&C 132:29-31). The traditional BCE dates commonly attached to his life are approximate reconstructions — Genesis gives relative ages and intervals, not an absolute calendar.

## Teachings & Conference
<!-- SG:BEGIN conference -->
[[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]] uses the change from Abram to Abraham to emphasize the covenantal calling to become a father of many nations, tying Abraham’s identity to the gathering of Israel. The linguistic explanation is devotional teaching anchored to Genesis 17:5; it should not be made to carry more historical weight than the scriptural name-change narrative itself.

[[The Power of Deliverance (Elder L. Tom Perry, April 2012)]] presents Abraham as “the father of the faithful” whose blessed life nevertheless included sustained challenges. Elder Perry draws attention to Abraham’s tent and altar at Mamre in Genesis 13:18, using the altar as a pattern of worship amid an unsettled life.

[[Claim the Exceeding Great and Precious Promises (Elder Spencer J. Condie, October 2007)]] emphasizes the apparent impossibility of the promised birth to aged Abraham and Sarah and the subsequent fulfillment in Isaac (Genesis 17:17–19; 18:14; 21:2). The message treats Abraham’s story as encouragement to trust divine promises, while the full scriptural profile also preserves delay, doubt, family conflict, and costly testing.
