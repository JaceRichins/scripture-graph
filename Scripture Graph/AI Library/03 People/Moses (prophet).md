# Moses (prophet)

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Moses is the central human figure of Israel’s Exodus and Sinai traditions: a Hebrew child preserved under oppression, an exile called by God, the prophetic adversary of Pharaoh, Israel’s deliverance leader, covenant mediator, lawgiver, intercessor, and wilderness judge. Exodus through Deuteronomy presents him with unusual access to God (Exodus 33:11; Numbers 12:6–8) while also preserving his reluctance, anger, exhaustion, and failure at Meribah. Later scripture remembers him not merely as a national founder but as a standard of prophetic authority: Malachi commands Israel to remember the law given through him (Malachi 4:4), the New Testament relates him typologically to [[Jesus Christ]], and the Book of Mormon reads the [[Law of Moses]] as pointing toward the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. [[Moses 1]] expands the Latter-day Saint portrait with a vision of divine identity, creation, temptation, and God’s salvific purpose. These canonical claims establish Moses’s theological importance within scripture; by themselves they do not settle modern historical questions about the Exodus, traditional fifteenth-century-BC dates, or the composition of the Pentateuch.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
Moses’s story begins under threat. Pharaoh orders Hebrew sons killed, but Moses is preserved through the actions of his mother, sister, and Pharaoh’s daughter (Exodus 1:22; 2:1–10). His attempt to intervene against Egyptian violence ends with an Egyptian’s death and Moses’s flight to [[Midian]] (Exodus 2:11–22). At Horeb, God calls the hesitant exile to return to [[Egypt]] and bring Israel out of bondage (Exodus 3:1–12). Moses does not appear naturally confident: he doubts that Israel will believe him, protests that he is slow of speech, and receives [[Aaron (brother of Moses)]] as a spokesman (Exodus 4:1–17).

Before [[Pharaoh]], Moses becomes the bearer of the repeated demand to let Israel go (Exodus 5:1; 7:1–6). At the [[Red Sea]], he directs the frightened people toward the Lord’s deliverance (Exodus 14:10–31). Yet liberation is only the beginning of his task. At [[Mount Sinai]], Moses carries God’s words to Israel and Israel’s answer back to God, records the covenant, and administers its ratification (Exodus 19:3–9; 24:3–8). He also consecrates Aaron and his sons for priestly service (Leviticus 8:1–13). These scenes ground his lasting association with the [[Sinai covenant and Ten Commandments]], [[Covenants]], and the [[Law of Moses]].

The narrative’s strongest character portrait may be its refusal to make Moses one-dimensional. He intercedes after the golden calf and offers to be blotted from God’s book if Israel cannot be forgiven (Exodus 32:11–14, 30–32). Numbers calls him very meek and depicts him falling on his face before opposition (Numbers 12:3; 16:4). Nevertheless, he breaks the first tablets in anger, orders judgment after the calf, and commands severe violence against Midianite survivors (Exodus 32:19–28; Numbers 31:13–18). Numbers 31 is morally disturbing on its face and should not be sanitized: Moses orders the deaths of male children and women who had known a man while preserving the young girls.

Moses also reaches a point of collapse under the people’s complaints, asking why their burden has been placed upon him and wishing for death (Numbers 11:10–15). God’s response includes seventy elders who share the burden (Numbers 11:16–17, 24–25), complementing Jethro’s earlier counsel to delegate judgment (Exodus 18:13–26). At Meribah, Moses and Aaron fail to sanctify God before Israel, and Moses is barred from leading the people into [[Canaan]] (Numbers 20:7–13; Deuteronomy 32:48–52). He commissions [[Joshua (son of Nun)]] and dies after viewing the land from [[Mount Nebo (Pisgah)]] (Numbers 27:12–23; Deuteronomy 34:1–9). Deuteronomy’s epitaph nevertheless declares that no prophet had arisen in Israel like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face (Deuteronomy 34:10–12).

Later scripture develops this memory in several directions. [[Miriam]], Aaron, and Moses are named together as leaders sent before Israel (Micah 6:4). Malachi joins the command to remember Moses’s law with the promise of [[Elijah]] (Malachi 4:4–6). At the [[Transfiguration]], Moses and Elias appear with Jesus; Luke says they discuss the “decease” Jesus will accomplish at [[Jerusalem]], and the divine voice commands the disciples to hear the Son (Matthew 17:1–8; Luke 9:28–36). Acts applies Deuteronomy’s promised prophet like Moses to Jesus (Deuteronomy 18:15–19; Acts 3:20–23; 7:37). The comparison honors Moses while giving Jesus interpretive priority.

In Latter-day Saint scripture, [[Moses 1]] portrays Moses seeing God, learning that he is God’s son and has an appointed work, resisting [[Satan]], and beholding the earth’s inhabitants and God’s purpose (Moses 1:1–7, 12–22, 27–39). [[Chapter 17 The Great Plan of Salvation]] identifies this chapter with material revealed during Joseph Smith’s Bible revision beginning in 1830. Within Latter-day Saint faith it functions as restored revelation; that doctrinal status is distinct from an independent historical demonstration that its wording derives from an ancient Mosaic manuscript.

## Teachings & Conference
<!-- SG:BEGIN conference -->
Modern Latter-day Saint teachers most often use Moses to illuminate discipleship rather than to reconstruct his biography. In [[It Works Wonderfully! (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, October 2015)]], President Dieter F. Uchtdorf invokes Moses’s despair under the burden of leadership (Numbers 11:11–15) to show that even a great prophet can feel overwhelmed without being abandoned by God. In [[Deceive Me Not (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, October 2019)]], Elder Gary E. Stevenson draws on [[Moses 1]]: God teaches Moses his divine identity before Satan attempts to redefine and deceive him, making the episode a pattern for resisting false claims about one’s identity. In [[Receiving a Testimony of Light and Truth (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, October 2014)]], President Uchtdorf relates Moses 1:33, 35 to modern awareness of innumerable worlds. That comparison is offered devotionally; temporal priority over modern astronomy does not, without further analysis, make the passage a scientific prediction. These uses preserve a consistent emphasis: Moses’s greatness lies alongside dependence on God, vulnerability, and a received rather than self-created identity.

## Related
<!-- SG:BEGIN related -->
Begin with [[Exodus 3]] for Moses's call, [[Exodus 18]] for delegated leadership, [[Exodus 32]] for intercession and covenant crisis, [[Numbers 11]] for prophetic exhaustion, [[Numbers 20]] for Meribah, [[Numbers 31]] for the morally difficult Midianite campaign, and [[Deuteronomy 34]] for his death and canonical epitaph. [[Moses 1]] supplies the distinctive Latter-day Saint vision account.

Key people are [[Aaron (brother of Moses)]], his spokesman and priestly collaborator; [[Miriam]], his sister and fellow leader; [[Pharaoh]], his principal opponent; [[Joshua (son of Nun)]], his successor; and [[Elijah]], who appears with him at the [[Transfiguration]]. For major themes, follow [[The Exodus from Egypt]], [[Mount Sinai]], [[Sinai covenant and Ten Commandments]], [[Law of Moses]], and [[Covenants]]. The ethical problems raised by Numbers 31 belong beside [[Why did God command the conquest of Canaan]], while questions concerning the historical Moses, Exodus chronology, and Pentateuchal formation belong beside [[Is the Old Testament historically reliable]].
