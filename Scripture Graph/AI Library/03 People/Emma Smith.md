# Emma Smith

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Emma Hale Smith is the Emma Smith addressed by name in [[D&C 25]] (July 1830, Harmony, Pennsylvania) and [[D&C 132]] (1843, Nauvoo), and is identified in D&C 132:51 as Joseph Smith's wife — one of only a few individuals directly named in latter-day revelation. The canon's portrait is narrow but unusually direct, and strikingly bifurcated in tone. D&C 25 calls her God's "daughter" and an "elect lady," and charges her to comfort and accompany Joseph, serve as scribe when needed, devote her time "to writing, and to learning much," expound scripture, and select sacred hymns for the young church — a charge historically linked to her 1835 hymnal, *A Collection of Sacred Hymns*. D&C 132:51-56 addresses her within the conflict over Joseph's plural marriages: it frames a command to her as a trial comparable to Abraham's, commands her to receive those given to Joseph, to cleave to him "and to none else," and to forgive his trespasses — with destruction stated as the consequence of noncompliance. Neither revelation records Emma's own response, consent, or feelings; that has to come from the wider historical record. Beyond the canonized text, Emma is credited with a central role in the 1842 founding of the Relief Society and preserved a blessing from 1844 (the original now lost) shortly before Joseph's martyrdom. After Joseph's death she remained in Nauvoo rather than joining the westward exodus, and in testimony published in 1879 through her son [[Joseph Smith III]] — who led the RLDS Church, which rejected plural marriage as a matter of doctrine — she denied that Joseph had practiced it, a denial in evident tension with D&C 132 that historians continue to debate (see [[Why did Emma Smith deny plural marriage]]).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
**D&C 25 (July 1830, Harmony, Pennsylvania).** The revelation names Emma directly: "Hearken unto the voice of the Lord your God... Emma Smith, my daughter" (25:1), a designation immediately generalized — "all those who receive my gospel are sons and daughters in my kingdom." She is called "an elect lady, whom I have called" (25:3). She is charged to go with Joseph, to comfort him, and "be unto him for a scribe... that I may send my servant Oliver Cowdery whithersoever I will" (25:5-6) — a provision that frees Oliver for other work rather than stating a strict contingency. She is to be "ordained under his hand to expound scriptures, and exhort the church" (25:7), language whose bearing on later questions about office and priesthood authority is disputed and not settled by the verse alone. Her time is to be given "to writing, and to learning much" (25:8) — an explicit, personal charge to sustained learning — and she is to make a selection of sacred hymns for the church (25:11), with righteous song described as a prayer to God (25:12). The chapter closes, "this is my voice unto all" (25:16), extending its counsel outward without erasing its named, particular recipient. Verse 4 mentions unspecified "things which thou hast not seen"; identifying these with the gold plates is a common devotional gloss the verse itself does not support.

**D&C 132:51-56 (1843, Nauvoo).** Within the larger revelation on eternal and plural marriage, the text turns to Joseph and Emma specifically. Verse 51 addresses Joseph ("you") concerning "mine handmaid, Emma Smith, your wife," commanding that she "stay herself and partake not of that which I commanded you to offer unto her," and framing the episode as a trial comparable to Abraham's — the verse does not itself specify what was offered. Verse 52 commands that Emma "receive all those that have been given unto my servant Joseph, and who are virtuous and pure," with destruction threatened for those who falsely claim purity; verse 54 commands her to "abide and cleave unto my servant Joseph, and to none else," stating the Lord "will destroy her if she abide not in my law." Verse 56 commands her to forgive Joseph's unspecified trespasses so that her own may be forgiven. This language is coercive on its face and is not softened here. Crucially, the text records only commands directed at Emma — never her response, consent, or state of mind; how she actually reacted has to be reconstructed from the wider historical record (see [[Why did Emma Smith deny plural marriage]]).
