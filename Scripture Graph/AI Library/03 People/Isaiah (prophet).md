# Isaiah (prophet)

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Isaiah son of Amoz was a prophet associated with [[Jerusalem]] and the kingdom of Judah. The superscription places his visions in the reigns of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz, and [[Hezekiah]] (Isaiah 1:1), locating the named prophet principally in the eighth century BCE. [[Isaiah 6]] portrays his divine commission; subsequent narratives show him addressing Ahaz during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis, performing an embodied sign concerning [[Egypt]] and Ethiopia, counseling Hezekiah during the [[Assyria|Assyrian]] emergency, transmitting both warning and healing promises, and announcing future loss to [[Babylon]]. Chronicles also attributes writings about Uzziah and Hezekiah to him (2 Chronicles 26:22; 32:32), without specifying a formal court office.

The book bearing Isaiah's name became a major canonical source for language about holiness, repentance, judgment, the remnant, [[Gathering of Israel]], Zion, divine kingship, and messianic salvation. Its compositional history must be distinguished from the biography of the eighth-century prophet: traditional readings commonly attribute the whole book to Isaiah, while predominant historical-critical models understand its final form as including later exilic or post-exilic voices or editorial development. The number, dates, and extent of those layers remain debated.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
Isaiah's canonical profile begins with his call. In [[Isaiah 6]], a vision of the enthroned Lord exposes his uncleanness; symbolic cleansing precedes his willingness to be sent, and he receives a difficult commission to hearers who will often fail to perceive (Isaiah 6:1–13). His family participates in his public witness: Shear-jashub accompanies him to meet Ahaz, Maher-shalal-hash-baz receives a prophetic name, and Isaiah describes himself and his children as signs in Israel (Isaiah 7:3; 8:1–4, 18).

Isaiah speaks amid national emergencies. He tells Ahaz not to fear and calls him to faith during the Syro-Ephraimite crisis (Isaiah 7:1–17). He later walks "naked and barefoot" for three years as a sign of the coming humiliation of [[Egypt]] and Ethiopia, though the precise extent of the nakedness is debated (Isaiah 20:2–6). When Sennacherib threatens [[Jerusalem]], Isaiah answers [[Hezekiah]] in the Lord's name and reports that the king's prayer has been heard ([[2 Kings 19]]; [[Isaiah 37]]); Chronicles also depicts Isaiah and Hezekiah praying together (2 Chronicles 32:20).

When Hezekiah becomes mortally ill, Isaiah first announces death and then conveys a new divine promise of healing and fifteen additional years after the king's petition. He also directs the application of figs and mediates the requested sign (2 Kings 20:1–11; Isaiah 38:1–8, 21). The text does not describe Hezekiah's prayer as repentance. After Hezekiah displays his treasures to Babylonian envoys, Isaiah announces that treasures and royal descendants will eventually be carried to [[Babylon]] (2 Kings 20:12–19; [[Isaiah 39]]). Chronicles separately attributes writings concerning Uzziah and Hezekiah to Isaiah (2 Chronicles 26:22; 32:32), though it does not establish that these records equal the present book or that Isaiah held a formal historiographic office.

Isaiah's later canonical reception is extensive. [[Nephi (son of Lehi)]] says Isaiah is difficult for readers unfamiliar with Jewish prophetic conventions and appeals both to the spirit of prophecy and to knowledge of Jewish history and setting (2 Nephi 25:1–6). Nephi and [[Jacob (son of Lehi)]] liken Isaiah to their covenant communities while retaining his concern for the house of Israel (2 Nephi 6:4–5; 11:2, 8). Jesus commands the Nephites to search Isaiah (3 Nephi 23:1–3), and [[D&C 113]] interprets images from Isaiah 11 and 52. Romans, Philippians, and Revelation likewise quote or adapt Isaiah in teaching about gospel proclamation, Christ, Israel, Zion, and final restoration. Such reception demonstrates Isaiah's extraordinary canonical influence but does not by itself establish the earliest meaning or human authorship of every passage.

## Teachings & Conference
<!-- SG:BEGIN conference -->
General conference teaching has generally approached Isaiah through Christ, scripture, and discipleship. In [[Strengthened by the Atonement of Jesus Christ (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, October 2015)]], Elder Dallin H. Oaks uses Isaiah 53:4 concerning the Messiah bearing griefs and sorrows together with Isaiah 41:10 concerning God's strengthening help. In [[One More Day (Elder Taylor G. Godoy, April 2018)]], Elder Taylor G. Godoy retells Isaiah's warning to Hezekiah, the king's prayer, and the subsequent fifteen-year extension (Isaiah 38:1–5) to teach about the value of additional time.

[[Scriptural Witnesses (Elder Russell M. Nelson, October 2007)]] names Isaiah among Old Testament prophets understood to anticipate the Book of Mormon. [[Conference Report, October 1921 (General Conference (full report), October 1921)]] presents Isaiah as a witness of the Atonement. The historical [[Conference Report, April 1929 (General Conference (full report), April 1929)]] defends traditional unity of Isaiah by observing that the narrator of John 12 attributes citations from Isaiah 53 and Isaiah 6 to Isaiah. This documents Latter-day Saint awareness of the authorship debate, but New Testament citation under the received book's name does not by itself settle modern questions about sources, editorial development, or single authorship.
