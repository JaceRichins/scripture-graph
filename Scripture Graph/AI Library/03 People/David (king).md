# David (king)

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
David is presented in 1 Samuel as Jesse's youngest son, a shepherd from [[Bethlehem]] whom [[Samuel (prophet)]] anoints while [[Saul (king)]] still reigns (1 Samuel 16:11-13; 17:12-15). First Samuel says Jesse had eight sons, while 1 Chronicles 2:13-15 lists David as the seventh; the texts do not explain the numerical difference. David becomes a fugitive, then king over Judah at [[Hebron]], and finally king over all Israel from [[Jerusalem]]; the narrative assigns him a forty-year reign (2 Samuel 2:4; 5:1-10).

The canon gives no warrant for either a spotless hero or a merely failed ruler. David displays courage, loyalty to [[Jonathan (son of Saul)]], restraint toward Saul, willingness to seek divine direction, and responsiveness to correction. His reign also includes conquest and severe violence, and he gravely abuses royal power by taking [[Bathsheba]] and arranging Uriah's death (2 Samuel 11). Nathan condemns these acts, David confesses, and the narrative holds forgiveness together with consequences and lasting harm (2 Samuel 12-18). The promise concerning David's house and throne becomes foundational to [[Davidic kingdom established]] and to New Testament presentations of [[Jesus Christ]] as David's son, Lord, root, and offspring.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
**Election and exile.** [[Samuel (prophet)]] anoints David while [[Saul (king)]] remains king ([[1 Samuel 16]]), creating a prolonged gap between designation and possession of the throne. In [[1 Samuel 17]], David confronts Goliath while explicitly crediting the LORD rather than sword or spear (1 Samuel 17:45-47). His covenant with [[Jonathan (son of Saul)]] protects him during Saul's persecution ([[1 Samuel 18]]; [[1 Samuel 20]]). David twice refuses to kill Saul as the LORD's anointed (1 Samuel 24:4-12; 26:7-12), yet [[1 Samuel 25]] also shows his threatened collective retaliation against Nabal's household and Abigail's successful intervention.

**Kingship and covenant.** David first becomes king over Judah at [[Hebron]] and later over all Israel (2 Samuel 2:4; 5:1-5). He captures [[Jerusalem]], brings the ark there, and receives the canonical promise that his house, kingdom, and throne will endure ([[2 Samuel 5]]; [[2 Samuel 6]]; [[2 Samuel 7]]). His rule expands Israelite power, but [[2 Samuel 8]] also reports the killing of many defeated Moabites. His care for Jonathan's son in [[2 Samuel 9]] fulfills a prior covenant commitment. Chronicles further emphasizes David's extensive preparations for the temple [[Solomon]] will build ([[1 Chronicles 22]]; [[1 Chronicles 28]]; [[1 Chronicles 29]]).

**Abuse, confession, and harm.** In [[2 Samuel 11]], David learns that [[Bathsheba]] is Uriah's wife, sends for and takes her, and later arranges Uriah's battlefield death. Nathan identifies David's conduct as evil and an abuse resembling a rich man's seizure of a poor man's only lamb ([[2 Samuel 12]]). David confesses, and Nathan says the LORD has put away his sin, but the narrative does not portray forgiveness as erasing harm: sexual violence, murder, rebellion, and bereavement fracture his household ([[2 Samuel 13]]; [[2 Samuel 15]]; [[2 Samuel 18]]).

**Canonical tensions.** The census accounts agree on David's culpability and confession but differ over the initial provocation: 2 Samuel 24:1 associates it with the LORD's anger, whereas 1 Chronicles 21:1 names Satan. Restoration scripture adds another difficult layer. Jacob 2:23-24 condemns the many wives and concubines of David and Solomon; Doctrine and Covenants 132:38-39 distinguishes authorized unions from David's sin concerning Uriah and Bathsheba and says David fell from his exaltation. That distinction offers an internal reconciliation for the marriage passages, but it is not stated in Jacob, and the adverse judgment about exaltation must be read alongside 2 Samuel 12:13's statement that David's sin was put away.

**Later reception.** New Testament writings repeatedly use Davidic language for Jesus: son or seed of David, David's Lord, the holder of David's key, and David's root and offspring (Matthew 1:1; 22:41-46; Romans 1:3-4; Revelation 3:7; 22:16). These texts strongly establish Davidic identity's importance in early Christian proclamation; they do not independently prove biological genealogy, the historicity of every David narrative, or every later theological conclusion.

## Teachings & Conference
<!-- SG:BEGIN conference -->
General conference teaching uses different moments in David's life without flattening them into a single moral verdict. [[Faithful to the End (Sister Andrea Muñoz Spannaus, April 2024)]] presents the young David's confrontation with Goliath as an example of faith and trust in Jesus Christ. That is a Latter-day Saint christological application; [[1 Samuel 17]] itself speaks explicitly of David's trust in the LORD.

[[Beauty for Ashes The Healing Path of Forgiveness (Sister Kristin M. Yee, October 2022)]] turns to [[1 Samuel 25]], where Abigail interrupts David's intended retaliation against Nabal's household, to teach forgiveness and peacemaking. [[Blessed Are All the Pure in Heart (Elder L. Whitney Clayton, October 2007)]] uses [[2 Samuel 11]] in the opposite register—as a warning that lust, concealment, and abuse of power can escalate into catastrophic sin. [[Conference Report, October 1920 (General Conference (full report), October 1920)]] recalls David's treatment of Saul in teaching charity and forgiveness, while [[Conference Report, October 1925 (General Conference (full report), October 1925)]] invokes Gad's message to David after the census. Together these teachings draw legitimate applications from distinct episodes, but none should be used to erase the others.
