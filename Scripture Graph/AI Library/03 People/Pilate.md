# Pilate

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Pontius Pilate was the Roman governor of [[Judea]] — Luke 3:1 places his term in the fifteenth year of Tiberius Caesar, alongside Herod's tetrarchy over Galilee — and the official before whom all four Gospels place Jesus's Roman trial. Because Jewish authorities lacked power to execute, the chief priests and elders, having resolved that Jesus should die, bind him and deliver him to Pilate (Matthew 27:1-2; Mark 15:1; Luke 23:1; John 18:31). Pilate's portrait is strikingly consistent yet complicated: he repeatedly declares that he finds no fault in Jesus (Luke 23:4, 14-15, 22; John 18:38; 19:4, 6), attempts release through the Passover prisoner-amnesty custom, and is warned off by his wife's troubling dream in Matthew's account alone (Matthew 27:19) — yet the same narratives have him order Jesus scourged, yield to pressure invoking loyalty to Caesar, and formally deliver Jesus to be crucified (John 19:1, 16). His handwashing (Matthew 27:24) symbolically disclaims guilt but does not undo the actions the same passage then attributes to him. Outside the Passion narratives, Luke preserves a report that Pilate killed some Galileans in connection with their sacrifices (Luke 13:1) — an episode whose date, scale, and cause go unrecorded beyond Jesus's brief comment on it — and Acts and 1 Timothy treat appearance 'before Pontius Pilate' as a fixed marker of Jesus's testimony and death (Acts 3:13; 4:27; 13:28; 1 Timothy 6:13). Several later Latter-day Saint periodicals and publications also reprint a purported written 'sentence' condemning Jesus attributed to Pilate; these are 19th- and 20th-century reproductions of a text whose ancient origin is not established, and one version even misidentifies Pilate as 'governor of Lower Galilee' rather than Judaea — a sign these belong in the historical record as curiosities to be labeled cautiously, not cited as first-century evidence.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
Luke first situates Pilate historically as governor of [[Judea]] under Tiberius (Luke 3:1), distinct from Herod, tetrarch of Galilee (Luke 23:1, 6), and separately reports that Pilate had killed some Galileans in connection with their sacrifices (Luke 13:1) — an incident Jesus cites only to reject the inference that the victims were unusually sinful (Luke 13:2-5).

In the Passion narratives, Jerusalem's leaders deliver Jesus to Pilate because they lack authority to execute (John 18:31), and the Roman hearing turns on politically actionable charges and the recurring question of kingship (Matthew 27:11; Mark 15:2; Luke 23:2-3; John 18:33). The four accounts select and emphasize differently. [[Mark 15]] gives a terse interrogation, Jesus's silence, Pilate's surprise, and his decision to satisfy the crowd. [[Matthew 27]] adds Pilate's wife's dream-warning (27:19) and the Barabbas exchange, then Pilate's handwashing (27:24) — immediately followed, in the same passage, by his release of Barabbas, order to scourge Jesus, and delivery of Jesus to be crucified (27:26), so the gesture does not erase his active role. The crowd's answering cry, 'His blood be on us, and on our children' (27:25), belongs to this one Passover-crowd scene and does not, on the text's own terms, extend into a hereditary verdict against Jewish people generally. [[Luke 23]] sends Jesus to Herod first, then has Pilate declare three separate times that no capital offense has been established (23:4, 14-15, 22), while still proposing chastisement and ultimately granting the crowd's demand. [[John 18]] and [[John 19]] develop the fullest dialogue: Jesus tells Pilate his kingdom 'is not of this world' and that he came to bear witness to the truth, prompting Pilate's unanswered 'What is truth?' (18:36-38); Pilate has Jesus scourged and presented as 'the man' and then mockingly as 'your King' (19:1, 5, 14); when the chief priests answer 'We have no king but Cæsar' (19:15), Pilate delivers Jesus to be crucified; and Pilate orders the inscription 'JESUS OF NAZARETH THE KING OF THE JEWS' fixed to the cross, refusing the priests' request to change it — 'What I have written I have written' (19:19-22). Told his own authority is at issue, Jesus answers that Pilate's power was 'given thee from above,' while still assigning 'the greater sin' to the one who delivered him — holding derivative authority and unequal human responsibility together rather than erasing Pilate's part (19:10-11).

The safest reading of Pilate's character stays close to what the narratives show rather than a reconstruction of his private motive: the Gospels agree he found insufficient grounds for a death sentence yet used his authority to have Jesus punished and executed. Fear, contempt, and political calculation are all plausible motives later readers have proposed, but the texts themselves do not adjudicate between them. Responsibility in the Passion narratives is distributed rather than singular — religious leaders accuse, a persuaded crowd demands death, Pilate authorizes it, and Roman soldiers carry it out (Matthew 27:20-26) — a pattern that neither erases Pilate's own role nor licenses collective blame against any other group.

After the crucifixion, Joseph of Arimathaea asks Pilate for the body (Mark 15:43-45; Luke 23:52; John 19:38), and Matthew alone reports that the chief priests, anticipating a theft claim, ask Pilate to secure the tomb; Pilate tells them, 'Ye have a watch: go your way, make it as sure as ye can' (27:65) — wording that can be read either as granting a Roman guard or as directing the leaders to use their own — after which they seal the stone (27:62-66). Mark 15:25 places the crucifixion at 'the third hour,' while John 19:14 has Jesus still before Pilate 'about the sixth hour' on the day of preparation — a real surface tension between the two Gospels' internal clocks that differing hour-conventions or approximate reporting may or may not resolve, with no settled answer available from the text alone. Acts remembers Pilate two ways at once: Peter says he 'was determined to let him go' (Acts 3:13), while the believers' prayer counts him among the rulers 'gathered together' against Jesus (Acts 4:27) — Acts holds both together rather than resolving them. First Timothy holds up Christ's 'good confession' before Pilate as a model for steadfast testimony (6:13).

## Teachings & Conference
<!-- SG:BEGIN conference -->
Modern apostles have generally treated Pilate as a moral and devotional contrast to Christ, not as a figure to rehabilitate. In [[None Were with Him (Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, April 2009)]], Elder Jeffrey R. Holland stresses the contradiction between Pilate's declaration that he found no fault and his decision to scourge and deliver Jesus; the washed hands intensify rather than remove the moral failure. [[Behold the Man! (Elder Dieter F. Uchtdorf, April 2018)]] turns Pilate's presentation of Jesus into an invitation to behold the suffering Savior. [[The Eye of Faith (Elder Neil L. Andersen, April 2019)]] draws on the dialogue in John 18:33–38 to teach about Christ's kingdom and witness of [[Truth]]. [[That They Do Always Remember Him (Elder Claudio R. M. Costa, October 2015)]] includes Christ's appearances before Pilate and Herod among the events disciples should deliberately remember.

For the surrounding people and setting, see [[Caiaphas]] and the Jewish council whose accusation Pilate rules on, [[Herod]] (Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee, who briefly examines Jesus in Luke's account), and [[Judea]] and [[Jerusalem]] for the Roman-administered setting. For the themes the trial itself raises, see [[Truth]] (Jesus's declaration that he came to bear witness to the truth), [[Kingdom of God]] ('my kingdom is not of this world'), [[Government and Laws]], and [[Justice and Mercy]] (Pilate's repeated verdict of 'no fault' set against his order to crucify).
