# Law of Moses

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, the **law of Moses** is Israel’s covenantal instruction associated with [[Moses (prophet)]]. Depending on context, the expression may denote the commandments and ordinances given at Horeb/Sinai (Malachi 4:4), a specific requirement such as circumcision (Acts 15:1-5), the sacrificial order (Alma 34:13-14), or the scriptural corpus called “the law of Moses” (Luke 24:44). It includes ethical, ritual, priestly, calendrical, and judicial material; it is not simply a synonym for animal sacrifice or the [[Ten Commandments]].

“Torah” means instruction and can have a broader literary and theological range than the English word “law.” Likewise, the later Christian moral/ceremonial/civil division is an interpretive classification, not a three-part taxonomy stated by the biblical text itself.

Restoration scripture uses “the law of carnal commandments” in connection with a preparatory gospel and lesser priesthood (D&C 84:23-27). In historical Latter-day Saint discourse, “Mosaic law,” “lesser law,” and “carnal commandments” are often treated as closely related. They should not be conflated without qualification: “carnal” in that usage does not demonstrate that every command was spiritually empty, and some commandments found in Mosaic texts are also represented as existing before or after the Mosaic dispensation. [[A Compendium of the Doctrines of the Gospel]], for example, argues this point about [[Tithing]].
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon presents the law as genuinely given by God, binding during its appointed covenantal era, and incapable of saving independently of divine grace and atonement. Abinadi therefore says both that his hearers should keep it “as yet” and that salvation does not come by the law alone (Mosiah 13:27-28). Nephi likewise joins observance to steadfast faith in Christ (2 Nephi 25:24-30).

Book of Mormon teachers interpret the law christologically. It contains signs, types, and shadows (Mosiah 3:14-15); it points souls toward Christ (Jacob 4:5); teachers must explain that it is a shadow of things to come (Mosiah 16:14-15); and its sacrificial order anticipates a great and last sacrifice (Alma 34:9-15). These are explicit claims of the Book of Mormon text. Whether every original Israelite lawgiver or worshiper understood the system in this fully developed way is a separate historical question.

The resurrected Christ declares that the law given to Moses is fulfilled in him (3 Nephi 15:4-10). The surrounding teaching does not equate fulfillment with moral permissiveness: [[Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)]] intensifies demands concerning reconciliation, purity, truthful speech, nonretaliation, and love (3 Nephi 12:17-48). Christ also distinguishes the fulfilled Mosaic law from covenantal and prophetic words not yet completely fulfilled.

The New Testament presents a related but internally varied transition. Galatians 3:17-25 calls the law a temporary custodian and denies that it opposes God’s promises. Hebrews 7:11-19 associates change of priesthood with change of law. At the [[Jerusalem conference]], the apostles reject imposing circumcision and the whole Mosaic obligation on Gentile converts (Acts 15:1-29), while Acts 21:20-26 still depicts Jewish believers as zealous for the law. These passages support freedom of Gentile Christians from conversion to Mosaic covenant identity, but they also caution against imagining that every Jewish Christian immediately abandoned ancestral practice.

Historical sermons and compilations are evidence for Latter-day Saint interpretive history, not automatically binding doctrinal definitions. [[Conference Report, April 1923 (General Conference (full report), April 1923)]] describes the Mosaic order as a lesser law superseded by Christ’s gospel. [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]] similarly criticizes reliance on outward forms without righteousness. Such formulations should be evaluated beneath the controlling canonical texts, not substituted for them.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The canonical story places the lawgiving after [[The Exodus from Egypt]], at [[Mount Sinai]] or Horeb, as the covenantal constitution of Israel. Exodus through Deuteronomy develops worship, priesthood, purity, ethics, festivals, property rules, courts, and covenant sanctions. The narrative also depicts pre-Mosaic practices such as sacrifice, circumcision, and tithing, which means not every obligation later included in Torah necessarily originated for the first time with Moses.

Israel’s later history repeatedly interprets fidelity through this law. Royal and prophetic narratives measure rulers and people against it; Josiah’s reform follows discovery and public reading of a book of the law (2 Kings 22:8-13; 23:1-3). Daniel explains exile through Mosaic covenant sanctions (Daniel 9:11-14). After the [[Return from exile]], the people request that [[Ezra (scribe)]] read the law publicly and receive explanation of it (Nehemiah 8:1-8). These narratives establish the law’s authority in the authors’ worlds, although they do not by themselves settle the date or composition of the Pentateuch.

Luke places [[Jesus Christ]] within observant Jewish family life: circumcision, purification, temple presentation, and Passover pilgrimage appear in [[Luke 2]]. During his ministry, Jesus sometimes directs compliance with Mosaic procedure, as with the healed leper (Matthew 8:2-4), while also appealing behind particular Mosaic accommodations to creation, as in the divorce controversy (Mark 10:2-9). His followers subsequently debate circumcision and Gentile covenant obligations in Acts 15 and Acts 21.

The Book of Mormon’s internal history depicts Lehi’s descendants keeping the law while awaiting Christ (2 Nephi 25:24-30; Jarom 1:5, 11). Jacob and Sherem dispute whether the law testifies of Christ ([[Jacob 7]]). Abinadi confronts priests who profess the law while, according to his accusation, violating its demands ([[Mosiah 12]]-[[Mosiah 16]]). Amulek predicts the end of blood sacrifice ([[Alma 34]]), and the risen Christ declares fulfillment at Bountiful ([[3 Nephi 15]]). These are clear features of the Book of Mormon narrative; their value as evidence for an ancient Nephite community depends on the separate historical-authenticity question.

Restoration scripture further interprets the Mosaic order through priesthood and preparatory-gospel categories (D&C 84:23-27) and discusses circumcision in D&C 74. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Latter-day Saint writers often described Israel as receiving a “lesser” or “carnal” law after rejecting a higher order; examples occur in [[Key to the Science of Theology]], [[Conference Report, October 1912 (General Conference (full report), October 1912)]], and [[Conference Report, April 1923 (General Conference (full report), April 1923)]]. These sources document development in Latter-day Saint explanation but vary in authority and should not be read as a single, formally defined statement.

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong textual findings

The strongest evidence concerns what the canon actually teaches. Across 2 Nephi 25:24-30, Jacob 4:5, Mosiah 3:14-15, Mosiah 13:27-35, Mosiah 16:14-15, Alma 34:9-15, and 3 Nephi 15:4-10, the Book of Mormon consistently combines temporary observance, inability of law alone to save, typological direction toward Christ, and fulfillment in his sacrifice. This is strong evidence for a sustained internal theology. It does **not** independently establish ancient authorship; common authorship, editing, translation, or shared source traditions can also explain coherence.

Galatians 3, Hebrews 7, Acts 15, and Romans 10 provide strong textual evidence that early Christian authors reread Mosaic institutions and language around Christ. They do not all answer the same question or use “law” with precisely the same scope. Treating them as a single undifferentiated theory would erase real differences among promise, circumcision, priesthood, Gentile identity, and scriptural interpretation.

### Moderate inferences

Luke 2:22-24 names the bird offering permitted by Leviticus 12:6-8 when a lamb could not be afforded. Modest economic circumstances are a well-grounded inference, but Luke never supplies an income figure or a complete social profile.

[[King Benjamin's address]] occurs at the temple amid offerings, thanksgiving, family encampment, and royal succession ([[Mosiah 1]]-[[Mosiah 2]]). This strongly supports a literary connection between Mosaic worship and Nephite public order. It is not archaeological confirmation of the event.

### Evidence requiring restraint

The biblical record’s repeated appeal to Mosaic authority demonstrates reception and attribution. Josiah’s reform, Ezra’s reading, and Daniel’s prayer show that later communities understood Torah as governing Israel. They cannot alone prove that Moses personally wrote every passage or that the Pentateuch had its final form in the second millennium BCE.

The timeline entry in [[3 Nephi 25]] places Christ’s quotation of Malachi at Bountiful in AD 34 according to Book of Mormon internal dating. That is a narrative chronology, not an independently established external date. It also intersects the separate question [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Malachi before Malachi wrote]], which requires attention to exactly who quotes Malachi and when.

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does “fulfilled” mean in each context: completed, brought to its intended goal, transformed, rendered nonbinding, or some combination? Compare Matthew 5:17-20, Galatians 3:19-25, Hebrews 7:11-19, and 3 Nephi 15:4-10.
- Which obligations express enduring moral demands, and which marked Israel’s particular covenant administration? The canon supplies examples but no single exhaustive classification table.
- Does freedom from the Mosaic covenant mean freedom from divine commandments? [[Sermon on the Mount]], [[Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)]], Romans 6, and Galatians 5 strongly resist that inference.
- How should Jewish observance by Jesus and early Jewish believers be related to the decision not to circumcise Gentiles? Study [[Luke 2]], Acts 15, Acts 21, and Galatians 2-5 without making one community’s situation universal.
- To what extent were Israelite sacrifices consciously understood as predictions of Christ before Christianity? The Book of Mormon answers this strongly within its narrative; historical-critical reconstruction is less certain.
- Why did a good God command animal sacrifice, purity restrictions, severe penalties, and social regulations that trouble modern readers? See [[Why did God command animal sacrifice]], [[Justice and Mercy]], and [[Does the Bible endorse slavery]].
- Did Moses author the whole Pentateuch, mediate an older legal core later expanded by scribes, or function principally as the authoritative figure under whose name Israel’s Torah was transmitted?
- How much of the historical Latter-day Saint “higher law/lower law” scheme is explicit in scripture, and how much is a later synthesis of Exodus 32-34, JST-related readings, D&C 84, and Pauline language?
- How would Nephite communities have administered sacrifices, priesthood, festivals, and purity rules outside Jerusalem for centuries? The text asserts observance but supplies no ritual manual or continuous institutional record.
- Alma 34:13 declares a permanent “stop to the shedding of blood” through the last sacrifice, yet the priesthood restoration recorded in Joseph Smith’s history quotes Malachi’s expectation that “the sons of Levi” will “offer again an offering unto the Lord in righteousness” (Malachi 3:3) — a passage some Latter-day Saint teachers have read as anticipating a future, non-atoning ceremonial offering. How should that expectation be reconciled with the Book of Mormon’s language of the law’s complete and final fulfillment?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest historical-critical objection is that “law of Moses” need not mean that Moses personally composed the complete Pentateuch. Linguistic variation, duplicated narratives, differing legal collections, tensions among provisions, and signs of later social settings lead much contemporary scholarship to models of composition and editing over centuries. Traditional attribution can still preserve a Mosaic nucleus or founding authority, but the biblical title alone does not resolve authorship or dating.

A second objection targets Christian caricatures of Judaism. Paul’s criticisms have often been read as though Jews pursued salvation by mechanical merit under a spiritually barren law. E. P. Sanders’s *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* challenged that picture through “covenantal nomism”: obedience normally functioned within an already given covenant rather than earning initial election. Whatever one concludes about Paul, the law should not be portrayed as a failed attempt by ancient Jews to invent grace-free salvation.

A third objection concerns retrospective typology. Christian and Book of Mormon readings identify Christ as the law’s intended referent, but many laws address immediate concerns of worship, health, land, courts, kinship, and communal identity without explicitly predicting a Messiah. A believer may regard Christological meaning as divinely intended and progressively disclosed; a historian may instead describe it as later rereading. The textual existence of the rereading is certain, while its divine or original authorial status is a theological conclusion.

A fourth objection concerns the Book of Mormon’s unusually explicit pre-Christian Christianity. Nephi, Jacob, Abinadi, and Amulek articulate fulfillment and atonement in language readily intelligible to later Christians. Believers explain this through revelation and prophetic foresight; critics regard it as evidence of nineteenth-century Christian composition. Internal coherence alone cannot adjudicate between those models.

Finally, supersessionist readings can turn “fulfillment” into dismissal of Jews, Judaism, or the Hebrew Bible. The canon does depict a changed covenantal situation for Christ’s followers, especially Gentiles, but Paul also denies that the law is contrary to God’s promises (Galatians 3:21), and the New Testament continues to use Israel’s scriptures as revelation. Theological claims about Christ do not justify contempt for Jewish observance or the assertion that Torah possessed no grace, wisdom, or spiritual value.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Historical-critical Pentateuchal scholarship generally treats Torah as a layered collection rather than a transcript written entirely by one hand. Particular source and redaction models remain disputed, but broad recognition of compositional development is common. Scholarship therefore distinguishes the historical Moses question, the origins of individual laws, the formation of legal collections, and the final literary shape of the Pentateuch.

Jewish scholarship emphasizes Torah as covenantal instruction, identity, worship, and a gift rather than merely a burden awaiting abolition. Jacob Milgrom’s work on Leviticus, especially his Anchor Bible commentary, illustrates how ritual, holiness, ethics, and communal order interlock. Such work challenges readings that classify ritual provisions as meaningless externals.

E. P. Sanders’s *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* and the subsequent “New Perspective on Paul,” associated especially with James D. G. Dunn, dispute the older portrait of Judaism as straightforward works-righteousness. Scholars continue to disagree about the meaning of “works of the law,” Paul’s relation to Torah, and whether his strongest statements concern all believers, Gentile boundary markers, participation in Christ, or several issues at once. Galatians 3-5 and Romans 2-11 should consequently be read in their separate arguments rather than collapsed into a slogan.

Christian scholarship commonly understands Jesus and Paul through both continuity and discontinuity: Israel’s scriptures remain authoritative witnesses, while circumcision, sacrifice, priesthood, and Gentile covenant membership are reread through Christ. The balance differs widely among Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox, Messianic Jewish, and secular scholars.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation adds Book of Mormon and Restoration scripture to this discussion. It usually emphasizes a pre-Christian gospel, the law’s typological purpose, a lesser/preparatory order associated with the [[Aaronic Priesthood]], and fulfillment through the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. [[Conference Report, October 1912 (General Conference (full report), October 1912)]] and [[Conference Report, April 1923 (General Conference (full report), April 1923)]] document influential historical formulations. Reported sermons, compendia, and later compilations such as [[Discourses of Brigham Young]] remain secondary witnesses to tradition, not equivalents of canon or a present institutional declaration.

The principal divide is therefore not over whether the texts contain laws or whether later Christians interpreted them through Christ. It is over historical composition, the extent of continuity, the legitimacy and antiquity of Christological typology, and whether Restoration claims disclose ancient reality or represent later Christian reinterpretation.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with the covenant setting: read [[Exodus 19]]-[[Exodus 24]] and [[Deuteronomy 4]]-[[Deuteronomy 6]]. Identify grace or deliverance, covenant, commandments, ratification, and promised consequences before debating “law versus grace.”

2. Sample the law’s actual breadth rather than treating it as only sacrifice: [[Leviticus 4]], [[Leviticus 17]], [[Leviticus 19]], [[Leviticus 23]], Numbers 3-4, and Deuteronomy 16-19. Track worship, holiness, ethics, priesthood, festivals, courts, and witnesses.

3. Study reception within Israel: 2 Kings 22-23, [[Nehemiah 8]], [[Daniel 9]], and [[Malachi 4]]. Ask what each author means by Mosaic authority and what historical claims the passage does or does not establish.

4. Read Jesus within Jewish observance before reading fulfillment debates: [[Luke 2]], Matthew 8:1-4, Mark 10:2-9, and Luke 24:25-27, 44-47.

5. Follow the apostolic transition in sequence: Acts 15, [[Galatians 3]], Galatians 5, [[Romans 10]], and [[Hebrews 7]]. Keep circumcision, Gentile identity, justification, priesthood, and scriptural interpretation analytically distinct.

6. Trace the Book of Mormon argument chronologically: [[2 Nephi 25]], [[Jacob 4]], [[Jacob 7]], [[Mosiah 3]], [[Mosiah 12]]-[[Mosiah 16]], [[Alma 34]], and [[3 Nephi 15]]. Mark every passage as observation, interpretation, or claim of fulfillment.

7. Compare ethical continuity in [[Sermon on the Mount]] and [[Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)]]. Ask whether Christ merely removes commands or redirects and intensifies covenant life.

8. Only then examine Restoration synthesis in D&C 74 and D&C 84:23-27, followed by [[Key to the Science of Theology]], [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]], and the linked conference reports. Distinguish canon, historical preaching, compilation, and modern scholarly interpretation at every stage.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The law of Moses is best understood as Israel’s covenantal way of life, not as a single ritual rule or a primitive opposite of grace. It orders worship, priesthood, ethics, festivals, courts, households, purity, sacrifice, and national memory. Canonical narratives treat it as divine instruction and repeatedly criticize people who possess or teach it while violating its demands.

The Book of Mormon’s central synthesis is unusually explicit: the law is valid during its appointed period, cannot save independently of Christ’s atonement, and points through types and shadows to his sacrifice. Nephi observes it while believing in Christ; Jacob reads it as directional; Abinadi rejects confidence in law alone; Amulek connects fulfillment to the last sacrifice; and the risen Christ declares the Mosaic law fulfilled in him. [[Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)]] then frames life after fulfillment as deeper discipleship rather than commandlessness.

The New Testament similarly records both continuity and change. Jesus lives within Jewish practice and interprets Israel’s scriptures as witnesses to his mission. Paul denies that the law opposes God’s promises but rejects making Mosaic observance, especially circumcision, the basis of Gentile covenant membership. Hebrews links a new priesthood to a changed law. Acts preserves evidence that Jewish believers continued ancestral observance even while the Church declined to impose the whole Mosaic order on Gentiles.

Three conclusions require different confidence levels. It is **textually established** that biblical and Book of Mormon writers attribute authority to Mosaic law and that Christian texts reread it through Christ. It is a **theological interpretation** that its entire divinely intended meaning culminates in Christ. It is a **historically disputed larger conclusion** that the full Pentateuchal corpus came directly from Moses in its present form or that every ancient Israelite understood its rites christologically.

A trustworthy reading therefore rejects two caricatures at once: the law was neither an evil, grace-free system nor an independently saving mechanism. Within the canon it is holy instruction situated in a particular covenantal administration, exposing sin, ordering a people, preserving worship, and, in Christian and Restoration readings, preparing for and witnessing of [[Jesus Christ]].
