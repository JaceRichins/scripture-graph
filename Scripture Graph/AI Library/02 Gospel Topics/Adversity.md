# Adversity

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, **adversity** is an umbrella term rather than a single technical doctrine. Its vocabulary includes affliction, tribulation, trouble, persecution, bondage, chastening, weakness, sorrow, pain, and trial. These words can describe natural mortality, oppression, consequences of sin, covenant judgment, persecution for righteousness, divine testing, or suffering borne because of another's wrongdoing. Revelation 2:22, for example, presents tribulation in a judgment setting, while Revelation 2:9-10 presents faithful Smyrna suffering without rebuke. Alma 4:3 reports calamity that the people interpret as judgment, whereas Alma 4:13-15 depicts humble disciples suffering persecution while serving the needy.

Latter-day Saint instructional resources preserve this plurality. [[Adversity (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Adversity (True to the Faith)]] teach that hardship may arise from personal disobedience, ordinary mortal conditions, other people's agency, or experiences that become occasions for growth. That classification is pastoral guidance, not authority to diagnose why a particular person suffers. The wider doctrinal setting includes [[Agency]], [[Plan of Salvation]], [[Faith]], [[Hope]], and [[Endurance]].
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon supplies several teachings about adversity without giving one complete explanation for every instance.

First, [[2 Nephi 2]] places opposition within the conditions required for meaningful agency and moral choice (2 Nephi 2:11-16, 26-27). That general framework does not say that God individually designed or directly inflicted every tragedy.

Second, suffering appears in materially different settings. Alma 4:3 reports that the people interpreted communal calamity as judgment for wickedness; Alma 4:13-15 separately depicts humble believers suffering persecution while caring for the needy. Revelation 2:9-10 anticipates suffering for commended disciples, while Revelation 2:22 threatens tribulation as judgment. These texts do not authorize readers to infer another person's guilt from that person's pain.

Third, divine aid is not limited to immediate removal. In [[Mosiah 24]], burdens are eased and the people are strengthened while bondage continues, with deliverance following later. [[Alma 36]] promises support in trials and final lifting up while recounting prison, bonds, and danger. [[D&C 121]] gives peace and eternal perspective amid imprisonment rather than denying the magnitude of present loss.

Fourth, affliction can be redeemed without the harm becoming good. God promises to consecrate Jacob's afflictions for his gain, but the text still attributes those afflictions to his brethren's rudeness (2 Nephi 2:1-2). Romans 5:3-5 presents tribulation as capable of contributing to patience, experience, and hope; it does not guarantee that every trial produces visible growth.

Fifth, the Christian center is [[Jesus Christ]]. Mosiah 3:7-10 and D&C 19:15-19 describe his suffering in bodily and spiritual terms. [[Mosiah 14]] and [[Mosiah 15]] present the servant as bearing the griefs and sins of others, strongly supporting a representative reading while leaving a complete theory of atonement unstated.

Finally, the canonical response is both personal and communal: lament, prayer, trust, repentance where actually needed, patient endurance, resistance to coercion, and practical succor. D&C 121:36-44 restricts priesthood influence to persuasion, long-suffering, gentleness, meekness, and love; Alma 4 joins faithful endurance to feeding and assisting people in need. [[Adversity (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Adversity (True to the Faith)]] offer useful pastoral summaries, but neither is a revealed diagnosis of any particular sufferer's circumstances.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The scriptural witness develops by accumulation rather than by replacing one explanation with another.

The Exodus tradition presents adversity as bondage from which God hears and delivers a covenant people; [[The Exodus from Egypt]] becomes a recurring language of later hope. Israel's covenant texts also connect communal fidelity, judgment, exile, and restoration, while Lamentations 3 preserves hope spoken from inside unresolved catastrophe.

Isaiah's servant tradition gives suffering a representative dimension. [[Mosiah 14]] reproduces the servant song, and [[Mosiah 15]] applies its suffering imagery to the Messiah. Christian and Latter-day Saint canonical readings connect the servant with [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], while historical-critical interpretations dispute the servant's original historical identity.

New Testament texts portray suffering as compatible with divine sonship, mission, and love. Romans 5 links tribulation with patience and hope; [[Romans 8]] joins suffering with Christ to future glory and inseparable divine love; Hebrews 11 honors both miraculous deliverance and faithful death; Revelation 2 promises Smyrna final life rather than immediate escape.

The Book of Mormon repeatedly places deliverance beside prolonged adversity. [[Mosiah 21]] traces anger, failed self-deliverance, humility, and delay. [[Mosiah 23]] and [[Mosiah 24]] portray righteous converts entering bondage, receiving strength, and eventually escaping. [[Alma 4]] joins persecution with concrete care for the poor, while [[Alma 31]] asks for strength to bear affliction.

Restoration teaching on adversity is concentrated in the setting of [[Liberty Jail imprisonment]]. [[D&C 121]] preserves lament, reassurance, and an ethic opposed to coercion; [[D&C 122]] answers an escalating catalogue of possible disasters by placing them beneath Christ's prior descent below all. Modern Church resources continue to apply these themes pastorally. [[Mountains to Climb (President Henry B. Eyring, April 2012)]] declines to promise an end to mortal adversity, while [[Swallowed Up in the Joy of Christ (Elder Brian K. Taylor, April 2024)]] emphasizes divine sustaining amid it.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong findings

The clearest finding is negative but important: scripture does not support an exceptionless prosperity or rescue rule. Hebrews 11:32-40 honors both the delivered and those tortured, imprisoned, afflicted, or killed in faith. Smyrna receives no rebuke, yet faces prison and death (Revelation 2:8-11). Romans 8:35-39 promises inseparable divine love in persecution, famine, peril, and death—not exemption from them. This is strong canonical evidence against blaming every continuing trial on deficient faith.

A second strong finding is that scripture differentiates forms of divine help. In [[Mosiah 24]], comfort and strengthened burden-bearing precede escape (Mosiah 24:13-21). Alma asks for endurance rather than immunity, and the resulting afflictions are described as swallowed up in Christ's joy (Alma 31:31-38). These narratives support the claim that spiritual aid may occur without immediate circumstantial change. They do not establish that every faithful sufferer will consciously feel such aid.

A third strong finding is the canon's refusal to give suffering one moral cause. The transition from prosperity to chastening in [[Mosiah 23]] challenges a simple prosperity equation (Mosiah 23:19-22). Alma 4 distinguishes perceived judgment from persecution of humble believers. Second Nephi 2:1-2 preserves both human wrongdoing and God's capacity to consecrate its effects.

### Moderate findings

Romans 5:3-5, Ether 12:27, and D&C 122:7 suggest that adversity can become formative. This is meaningful canonical support for growth through hardship, but it does not demonstrate that every instance of suffering is necessary, proportionate, or intentionally assigned. Growth may be a redeemed outcome rather than the original cause.

The pairing of Isaiah's suffering servant with Abinadi's exposition in [[Mosiah 14]]–[[Mosiah 15]] strongly supports the Latter-day Saint reading of the servant in relation to Christ. It does not settle the servant's full historical referent in Isaiah's original setting or establish one exhaustive theory of atonement.

### Methodological caution

The supplied index marks many chapter-level keyword matches as ambiguous. The cited verses in [[Alma 36]], [[Mosiah 24]], [[Alma 4]], [[Alma 31]], [[2 Nephi 4]], [[Mosiah 21]], and [[Revelation 2]] genuinely concern affliction or tribulation, but mention counts cannot establish a chapter's central theme. The evidence is strongest where verse-level context and narrative sequence converge.

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Serious study should leave several questions open rather than answering them with a single slogan.

- How much suffering is inherent in embodied, law-governed mortality, how much follows from [[Agency]], and what—if anything—is directly imposed as chastening? Scripture supplies examples of each category but no reliable diagnostic procedure for individual cases.

- What does it mean for God to permit suffering while remaining loving and powerful? [[Why does God allow suffering]] requires attention to moral evil, natural suffering, unequal vulnerability, animal suffering, hidden divine action, and promised resurrection—not only the language of growth.

- Why are some prayers followed by deliverance while others meet delay, silence, or death? Compare [[Mosiah 24]], Hebrews 11:32-40, and [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]].

- When is adversity spiritually formative, and when does talk of growth become a way to minimize trauma or excuse preventable injustice? Second Nephi 2:2 permits confidence in divine consecration but does not call the brothers' cruelty good.

- How should chastening texts be used without victim-blaming? Mosiah 23:21 offers a narrator's interpretation of that community's bondage; it does not grant readers authority to pronounce the cause of another person's illness, abuse, poverty, or grief.

- What kind of final repair would be adequate for apparently irreparable loss? The canon answers chiefly with [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Resurrection]], [[Final Judgment]], and [[Eternal Life]], but the experiential and philosophical details remain incomplete.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objection is the evidential and moral problem of evil: the scale, intensity, randomness, and unequal distribution of suffering can appear incompatible with a perfectly loving and powerful God. Appeals to agency explain some human cruelty but not earthquakes, disease, congenital disorders, or animal pain. Appeals to growth face cases where suffering destroys agency, personality, relationships, or life before observable growth occurs. Appeals to a future life can seem unfalsifiable and morally inadequate if they are used merely to postpone the demand for justice.

A related objection targets scriptural explanations themselves. Language about God chastening or trying people (Mosiah 23:21) may portray a deity who inflicts or permits disproportionate pain. D&C 122:7's claim that suffering can give experience may sound detached when applied to abuse, genocide, or a child's death. The honest response begins by limiting the claim: these texts testify that God can redeem affliction and that some adversity may test faith; they do not establish that every horror was individually designed, needed, or deserved.

Critics also object that deliverance narratives encourage survivorship bias. [[Mosiah 24]] remembers a community that escaped, while countless sufferers do not receive comparable temporal rescue. Hebrews 11:35-40 is the canon's own strongest corrective: it honors faithful people who were not delivered in mortality. That correction protects against a false guarantee but transfers much of the claim to resurrection and final judgment, which remain matters of faith rather than publicly demonstrable resolution.

Psychological and sociological explanations provide an alternative account of spiritual endurance: prayer, communal belonging, narrative meaning, and expectation can reduce distress without requiring supernatural intervention. Such mechanisms can explain some reported consolation. They do not logically disprove divine participation, but neither does the experience of consolation by itself establish its divine cause.

Finally, adversity teaching can be misused institutionally—to demand silence, obedience, or endurance of preventable harm. D&C 121:36-44 directly challenges that misuse by denying legitimate priesthood influence through control or compulsion. A doctrine of endurance is ethically incomplete unless joined to protection, truthful lament, repentance by perpetrators, justice, and concrete [[Service]].
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship commonly treats the canon as preserving several voices about suffering rather than one systematic theodicy. Retributive strands connect conduct and consequence; Job disputes the friends' attempt to infer guilt from suffering; lament traditions protest divine absence; prophetic texts interpret communal catastrophe covenantally; apocalyptic texts defer vindication to resurrection and judgment. James L. Crenshaw's *Defending God: Biblical Responses to the Problem of Evil* surveys this plurality. Historical-critical interpretation also debates whether Isaiah's servant was originally Israel, an idealized remnant, a prophet, or another historical figure, even while Christian canonical interpretation applies the servant to Jesus.

Philosophical theology likewise offers competing models. John Hick's *Evil and the God of Love* develops a soul-making account in which a challenging world permits moral development. Eleonore Stump's *Wandering in Darkness* offers a relational, narrative theodicy attentive to suffering and union with God. These approaches can illuminate Romans 5 and Restoration teachings about growth, but critics argue that they do not justify apparently excessive or destructive suffering.

Bart D. Ehrman's *God's Problem* presents a skeptical reading of biblical responses and argues that their tensions and the world's suffering undermined his own belief in divine providence. This position takes the empirical weight of suffering seriously, though autobiographical unbelief is not itself a logical proof that God does not exist. Naturalistic scholarship can account for adversity through biology, geology, psychology, political power, and chance without invoking divine purpose; those explanations are often strong at proximate causes while remaining philosophically neutral or skeptical about ultimate meaning.

Latter-day Saint teaching emphasizes premortal choice, embodied mortality, opposition, agency, growth, Christ's experienced compassion, and eventual resurrection. [[Plan of Salvation (Gospel Topics)]], [[Adversity (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Adversity (True to the Faith)]] summarize that framework. General conference addresses such as [[Is Faith in the Atonement of Jesus Christ Written in Our Hearts (Linda K. Burton, October 2012)]], [[Tested, Proved, and Polished (President Henry B. Eyring, October 2020)]], and [[Remember Thy Suffering Saints, O Our God (Elder Anthony D. Perkins, October 2021)]] apply it pastorally. These sources are authoritative Church teaching in their respective genres, but individual explanations and illustrations are not equivalent to new canon.

Believing and non-believing scholarship often agree on textual plurality, the inadequacy of simplistic victim-blaming, and the real psychological effects of meaning and community. They part principally over whether divine purpose, revelation, Christ's atoning solidarity, resurrection, and final redress are warranted realities or faith interpretations extending beyond publicly available evidence.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with categories rather than a single explanation. Read [[Alma 4]] and [[Revelation 2]], distinguishing reported judgment, persecution, poverty, inequality, and faithful suffering.

2. Study opposition and agency in [[2 Nephi 2]], especially 2 Nephi 2:1-2, 11-16, and 26-27. Ask what the text requires for meaningful agency and what it does not claim about the cause of each tragedy. Continue through [[Agency]] and [[Plan of Salvation]].

3. Compare the bondage narratives. Read [[Mosiah 21]], then [[Mosiah 23]] and [[Mosiah 24]]. Track anger, humility, prayer, strengthening, delay, and deliverance, noting that the narratives interpret the communities differently.

4. Study inward support through [[2 Nephi 4]], [[Alma 31]], and [[Alma 36]]. Compare Nephi's remembered preservation, Alma's prayer for patient strength, and the promise of support under trials.

5. Read [[D&C 121]] and [[D&C 122]] in sequence with [[Liberty Jail imprisonment]] in view. Observe lament before reassurance, ethical restrictions on authority, and Christ's descent below all.

6. Test rescue assumptions through Romans 5:3-5, [[Romans 8]], and Hebrews 11:32-40. List separately what these texts promise within mortality and what they locate in future resurrection, glory, and life.

7. Center the study on Christ. Read [[Mosiah 14]], [[Mosiah 15]], Mosiah 3:7-10, and D&C 19:15-19 alongside [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Suffering in Gethsemane]], and [[Crucifixion of Jesus Christ]]. Keep textual observation, Christian interpretation, and theories of atonement distinct.

8. Move from explanation to discipleship. Study Alma 4:12-15 and D&C 121:36-44 with [[Service]], [[Charity]], and [[Justice and Mercy]]. Ask both why suffering occurs and what love requires of witnesses, communities, and institutions.

9. Finish with [[Why does God allow suffering]] and [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]]. Use [[Mountains to Climb (President Henry B. Eyring, April 2012)]] as a safeguard against promising easy or immediate relief.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scripture does not reduce adversity to one cause or one outcome. Some suffering follows sin; some belongs to embodied mortality; some is imposed through another person's agency; some texts interpret particular communal trials as chastening; and Christ suffers voluntarily for others. Because these categories coexist, another person's pain is not reliable evidence of personal wickedness, weak faith, or divine rejection.

The canon's most consistent promise is not uninterrupted safety. [[Mosiah 24]] distinguishes strength under burdens from later escape. [[Alma 36]] joins trust with support through imprisonment and danger. Revelation 2:8-11 and Hebrews 11:32-40 honor faithfulness that may end in death. [[Romans 8]] promises that suffering cannot sever God's love and that present suffering does not cancel future glory.

Adversity may become an occasion for patience, experience, humility, hope, and deeper reliance on grace (Romans 5:3-5; Ether 12:27). Yet redeemed outcomes must not be confused with moral approval of the harm. God can consecrate Jacob's afflictions without making his brothers' rudeness good (2 Nephi 2:1-2). Growth language is truthful only when it leaves room for lament, trauma, delayed healing, and mysteries that mortality does not resolve.

The Christian center is [[Jesus Christ]], not suffering considered valuable in itself. [[Mosiah 14]]–[[Mosiah 15]] and D&C 19:15-19 portray him as bearing suffering for others, breaking death's bands, and offering repentance, mercy, and resurrection. D&C 122:8 places every disciple's descent in relation to his deeper descent. This supplies a faith-grounded claim of divine solidarity and final redemption; it does not provide a publicly demonstrable explanation for every tragedy.

The faithful response is therefore larger than endurance. It includes honest lament, prayer, repentance where actually needed, hope, resistance to coercion, care for bodies and minds, protection from abuse, and material succor. Alma 4:13-15 and D&C 121:36-44 prevent adversity doctrine from becoming passive submission to injustice. The strongest Latter-day Saint portrait is neither 'everything happens for a reason' nor 'faith removes every trial.' It is that mortal opposition is real, causes differ, God can sustain and redeem without calling evil good, Christ has entered suffering, disciples must relieve it where they can, and complete vindication rests finally on [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Resurrection]], and [[Eternal Life]].
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
