# Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
“Jesus” designates Jesus of Nazareth; “Christ” is the Greek title meaning “anointed one,” corresponding to Messiah. In the canon, the subject is therefore both a person and a cluster of claims about that person. He is called Son of God and Mary's son (Mosiah 3:8), Lamb of God (1 Nephi 11:21), Savior and Redeemer, Creator (3 Nephi 9:15), bread of life (John 6:35), good shepherd (John 10:11), and the way to the Father (John 14:6). Each title contributes something distinct: identity, relationship, vocation, sacrifice, nourishment, guidance, or saving mediation.

In Latter-day Saint tradition, [[Jesus Christ]] is the premortal divine Son, identified with Jehovah, the agent of [[Creation]], the incarnate and risen Messiah, and the only Savior through whom humanity can be reconciled to the Father. [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topics)]] organizes this teaching through [[Premortal Life]], [[Birth of Jesus Christ]], the mortal ministry, [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Resurrection]], and [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]]. The traditional identification of premortal Jesus with the LORD of Israel is doctrinally important, but students should distinguish that synthesis from what an isolated Old Testament passage explicitly says.

The similarly titled pages [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] and [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topics)]] should not be mistaken for additional persons. They are topic records about the same Jesus. By contrast, titles such as “savior” can occasionally describe subordinate human instruments; context must determine whether the ultimate divine Savior or a commissioned deliverer is intended.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon presents Jesus Christ as premortal, divine, creative, incarnate, crucified, risen, and living. John 1:1-3, 14 places the Word with God, calls the Word divine, attributes creation to him, and describes his becoming flesh. Mosiah 3:8 names Jesus Christ as Son of God, Creator, and Mary's son. First John makes his coming in the flesh central to faithful confession (1 John 4:2-3). Restoration scripture reports the risen Son at God's right hand (D&C 76:22-24) and distinguishes the embodied Father and Son (D&C 130:22).

His saving work is the center of the [[Gospel of Jesus Christ]]. First Corinthians 15:3-8 summarizes his death for sins, burial, resurrection, and appearances. In [[3 Nephi 27]], Christ defines his gospel through his coming in obedience to the Father, being lifted upon the cross, drawing humanity to judgment, and calling all people to repentance, [[Baptism]], sanctification, and endurance (3 Nephi 27:13-21). John depicts him as sacrificial shepherd and life-giving bread (John 10:11; John 6:35). These images illuminate the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] without reducing it to a single theory.

The canon also requires response: faith in Christ, repentance, baptism, reception of the Holy Ghost, covenant fidelity, love, and endurance (Mosiah 3:12; 2 Nephi 31:5-21; John 13:34-35; 3 Nephi 27:19-21). Salvation is attributed ultimately to God and Christ, even when ministers are called instruments in saving others.

Official Latter-day Saint teaching, represented by [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topics)]], [[Premortal Life (Gospel Topics)]], [[Birth of Jesus Christ (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Atonement of Jesus Christ (Gospel Topics)]], affirms that the Father chose Christ premortally, that Christ created under the Father's direction, and that his Atonement and Resurrection make redemption and immortality possible. Those are confessional teachings. Historical arguments can examine their textual witnesses and origins but cannot independently verify every metaphysical assertion.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
A careful history must distinguish the canon's narrated dispensational sequence from conclusions recoverable by ordinary historical method.

In Israel's scriptures, the LORD is Israel's unique God and Savior (Isaiah 43:10-11; Hosea 13:4), while royal, servant, shepherd, and messianic patterns supply later Christians with language for interpreting Jesus. Latter-day Saint teaching identifies this LORD with the premortal Christ; historical-critical scholarship more often studies how Jewish divine, messianic, and wisdom traditions were reapplied to Jesus.

Book of Mormon narrative places unusually explicit knowledge of Christ before his birth. Lehi predicts a Messiah and Savior among the Jews six hundred years after his departure (1 Nephi 10:4); Nephi sees the Lamb's birth, baptism, ministry, and rejection ([[1 Nephi 11]]); Benjamin names Jesus Christ and Mary ([[Mosiah 3]]). Believers read these as prophetic revelation. Critics regard their explicit Christian vocabulary and King James idiom as evidence of nineteenth-century composition or mediation. That disagreement cannot be resolved merely by noting the passages' clarity.

The New Testament moves from Jesus's Jewish ministry and crucifixion to resurrection proclamation and increasingly explicit reflection on his identity. Paul's letters attest developed devotion to Christ before the written Gospels; this weighs against the simple claim that late Gospel writers invented Christianity, while leaving room for real doctrinal development. John presents especially concentrated “I am” sayings ([[John 6]], [[John 10]], [[John 14]]). The relationship between these literary formulations and Jesus's exact historical words remains disputed.

Third Nephi reports the risen Christ's ministry at Bountiful and preserves a concentrated account of his gospel, covenant teaching, sacraments, and church ([[3 Nephi 27]]; [[Christ's appearance at the temple in Bountiful]]). Restoration revelations then report additional appearances and visions, especially [[D&C 76]] and [[D&C 110]]. The historical occurrence of these experiences rests chiefly on participant testimony; their doctrinal content is directly observable in the records.

Post-apostolic Christians debated how to speak of the Father, Son, humanity, and divinity of Christ. [[Nicaea]] and [[Chalcedon]] formulated answers that became normative for much of Christianity. Latter-day Saint teaching agrees that Christ is truly divine and truly incarnate but rejects important elements of classical Trinitarian metaphysics; see [[How did the doctrine of the Trinity develop]] and [[The Godhead]].

Modern Latter-day Saint discourse repeatedly centers faith, hope, redemption, and discipleship on Christ. This trajectory appears in [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]], [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[Conference Report, April 1924 (General Conference (full report), April 1924)]], [[Conference Report, October 1924 (General Conference (full report), October 1924)]], [[Conference Report, October 1926 (General Conference (full report), October 1926)]], [[Conference Report, April 1928 (General Conference (full report), April 1928)]], and [[Conference Report, October 1929 (General Conference (full report), October 1929)]]. More recent applications join Christ's sacrifice to discipleship and family or temple hope in [[To Be a Follower of Christ (Elder Alfred Kyungu, October 2021)]], [[See Yourself in the Temple (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2016)]], and [[Be Peaceable Followers of Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, October 2023)]]. These sermons witness to institutional and personal faith; repetition alone is not independent historical proof of their supernatural claims.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The evidence is strongest when the proposition is defined narrowly.

**Very strong:** Jesus existed as a first-century Jewish person and was executed under Pontius Pilate. This is the broad judgment of specialists across confessional lines; see [[Did Jesus exist as a historical person]]. Paul's letters, the Gospel traditions, Josephus, and Tacitus differ in date and independence, but a wholly mythical Jesus explains the surviving evidence less economically. This conclusion does not establish divinity or miracle claims.

**Strong:** Resurrection belief and reported appearances arose early, not only in late Christian legend. First Corinthians 15:3-8 preserves a received list of appearances, and Paul's letters predate the written Gospels. This firmly establishes early proclamation and claimed experience. Whether bodily resurrection, visions, bereavement experiences, communal interpretation, or some combination best explains the data remains disputed; see [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]]. Historical method can compare explanations but cannot make metaphysical assumptions disappear.

**Strong as textual observation:** Multiple canonical collections present Christ as Savior, Creator, sacrificial Redeemer, risen Lord, and mediator. Compare John 1:1-3; John 10:11; Mosiah 3:8; 3 Nephi 9:15; 3 Nephi 27:13-16; D&C 76:22-24. This coherence is doctrinally substantial.

**Weak to moderate as independent authentication:** Intercanonical agreement does not by itself prove multiple ancient witnesses. Shared Christian tradition, biblical dependence, King James translation language, Joseph Smith's religious environment, and editorial shaping can produce coherence. Consequently, these parallels should not be inflated into proof that the Book of Mormon or Restoration visions are ancient or supernatural.

**Direct evidence of recorded claims, but not independent corroboration:** [[D&C 76]] and [[D&C 110]] say that witnesses saw the living Christ. The texts establish what is claimed and permit study of the testimony's setting and transmission. They do not provide repeatable public observation. Similarly, [[1 Nephi 11]] and [[Mosiah 3]] securely establish the Book of Mormon's explicit Christology, while the historical provenance of that Christology belongs to the wider assessment of [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]].

**Important internal pattern:** Christ's titles connect identity with function. The Lamb suffers, the shepherd gives his life, the bread gives life, the way mediates access, and the Creator claims power to redeem. The pattern supports a coherent literary-theological reading. It does not establish that every title originated as Jesus's own exact wording.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Significant questions remain open at several levels:

- Which sayings and actions in the Gospels can be traced to the historical Jesus, and which bear the evangelists' literary and theological shaping? [[Why do the Gospels differ from one another]] is essential here.
- Did Jesus understand himself in the terms later applied to him—Messiah, divine Son, preexistent Word—or did some formulations develop after Easter? John and Paul must both be weighed; neither may simply be ignored.
- How should imminent-kingdom sayings be interpreted? Did Jesus expect the end within his generation, speak of Jerusalem's fall, describe vindication symbolically, or combine horizons? See [[Did Jesus expect the end within his generation]].
- What best explains the resurrection testimony: bodily resurrection, visionary experiences, developing tradition, or a mixed process? See [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]].
- How should exclusive claims such as John 14:6 be reconciled with justice and mercy toward people who never knowingly encounter Christ? See [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]].
- How do titles such as “Father of heaven and earth” relate to passages distinguishing the Father and Son (Mosiah 3:8; D&C 130:22)? The canon supplies data, while systematic reconciliation remains interpretive.
- Is the Book of Mormon's explicit pre-Christian Christology best explained by ancient prophecy, inspired translation into Christian language, modern composition, or some combination? Related evidence must be weighed cumulatively, not by proof-text.
- How much continuity exists between Jesus's proclamation and Paul's Christ-centered gospel? The crude claim that Paul simply invented Christianity is historically inadequate, but [[Did Paul invent Christianity]] addresses genuine questions of development.
- Which model or combination of models best accounts for the Atonement—victory, sacrifice, reconciliation, substitution, moral transformation, covenant, or representative participation? Scripture uses multiple images and does not reduce them to one later theory.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objections should be stated without dilution.

**The sources are theological and comparatively late.** The canonical Gospels were written by committed communities decades after Jesus's death, are literarily related, and differ in chronology, wording, and emphasis. They are not four modern independent transcripts. A responsible response is not to deny this but to assess traditions individually and compare them with the earlier Pauline letters. See [[Why do the Gospels differ from one another]] and [[How reliable is the biblical text]].

**High Christology may reflect development.** John's preexistent Word and “I am” discourses may express mature community theology rather than Jesus's verbatim self-description. Paul's early letters show that exalted devotion was already present relatively early, but that fact does not prove that every later formulation came directly from the historical Jesus.

**Miracles and resurrection exceed ordinary historical demonstration.** Testimony can establish that people reported experiences and became convinced. It cannot make supernatural causation a methodologically compulsory conclusion. Naturalistic explanations face difficulties of their own, but gaps in them do not automatically prove a miracle. See [[Do miracles violate the laws of nature]] and [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]].

**Exclusive salvation claims raise moral problems.** If explicit belief in Christ is required without a genuinely available opportunity, billions appear disadvantaged by birth and history. Latter-day Saint teachings about postmortal evangelization and ordinances answer part of this objection, but they are themselves revealed claims rather than publicly demonstrated facts; see [[Death and the Spirit World]], [[Baptism for the Dead]], and [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]].

**Book of Mormon Christology can appear anachronously Christian.** Its pre-Christian prophets use explicit names, doctrines, and language closely familiar from the New Testament and King James Bible. Believers appeal to prophecy and revelatory translation; critics appeal to modern composition or biblical dependence. The clarity of the Christology is therefore the datum under dispute, not proof of either explanation. See [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Isaiah and the King James Bible]], [[Why does the Book of Mormon reproduce King James italics]], and [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]].

**Latter-day Saint Godhead teaching conflicts with Nicene orthodoxy.** Classical Christians may argue that distinct embodied divine personages compromise monotheism; Latter-day Saints answer that the Father and Son are perfectly one in glory, will, and saving work while personally distinct. Both sides appeal to scripture through developed metaphysical frameworks. See [[How did the doctrine of the Trinity develop]], [[Nicaea]], [[Chalcedon]], and [[Are Latter-day Saints Christians]].

**Atonement theories can create moral objections.** Crude descriptions can make the Father appear to punish an innocent third party. Stronger Christian accounts stress the Son's voluntary self-giving, divine participation rather than external victimization, resurrection victory, reconciliation, and transformed covenant relationship. Even so, questions about justice, violence, and necessity remain live within [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]].
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing and non-believing scholarship substantially overlap on several historical judgments: Jesus was a first-century Jew; he proclaimed God's kingdom; he gathered disciples; he was crucified under Pontius Pilate; and his followers very early believed he had appeared alive. E. P. Sanders's *The Historical Figure of Jesus* represents a major historical reconstruction, while Bart Ehrman's *Did Jesus Exist?* defends the broad consensus on existence against mythicism.

The parting point is usually not whether sources exist but what can legitimately be inferred from them. Methodologically naturalist historians typically stop at reconstructed events, reported experiences, and developing beliefs. Confessional scholars may argue that resurrection or revelation is the best total explanation. N. T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* argues strongly for bodily resurrection; Dale Allison's *The Resurrection of Jesus: Apologetics, Polemics, History* treats the evidence as significant while emphasizing unresolved historical and epistemic difficulties. [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]] should preserve this distinction between data, explanation, and faith commitment.

Historical-Jesus scholarship also disputes Jesus's self-understanding and eschatology. Apocalyptic readings give substantial weight to imminent judgment and kingdom sayings; other readings emphasize inaugurated kingdom, symbolic prophetic language, or multiple temporal horizons. [[Did Jesus expect the end within his generation]] is therefore not a peripheral question.

On Christology, scholarship commonly studies development from Jewish messianic, wisdom, apocalyptic, and divine-agency traditions into New Testament and patristic formulations. There is no scholarly warrant for pretending that [[Nicaea]] simply invented Christ's divinity, since high devotion appears earlier; neither is there warrant for treating the fourth-century creeds as verbatim New Testament formulas. [[How did the doctrine of the Trinity develop]] should distinguish early textual ingredients from later conceptual synthesis.

Book of Mormon scholarship divides more sharply because prior judgments about provenance matter. Believing scholarship reads [[1 Nephi 11]], [[Mosiah 3]], and [[3 Nephi 27]] as ancient prophetic witnesses transmitted through inspired translation. Critical scholarship sees their explicit Christology, New Testament parallels, and King James diction as consistent with nineteenth-century production. Literary coherence is observable; ancient provenance and revelation are larger conclusions requiring additional evidence.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
A disciplined study should move from primary texts to synthesis rather than beginning with isolated titles.

1. **Begin with the earliest Christian proclamation.** Read 1 Corinthians 15:1-28 and Philippians 2:5-11. Separate what Paul says he received, what he argues, and what later readers infer. Pair this with [[Did Paul invent Christianity]].

2. **Read the Gospel portraits comparatively.** Study Mark's passion-centered narrative, Matthew 5-7, Luke 4 and 15, and [[John 6]], [[John 10]], and [[John 14]]. Record agreements and differences before harmonizing them. Use [[Why do the Gospels differ from one another]].

3. **Trace the mortal ministry's decisive events.** Move through [[Birth of Jesus Christ]], [[Baptism of Jesus]], [[Parables of Jesus]], [[The Last Supper]], [[Gethsemane]], [[Crucifixion of Jesus]], and [[Resurrection of Jesus]]. Keep historical-event questions distinct from each narrative's theological meaning.

4. **Study Book of Mormon prophecy and fulfillment.** Read 1 Nephi 10, [[1 Nephi 11]], [[Mosiah 3]], 2 Nephi 31, 3 Nephi 9-11, and [[3 Nephi 27]]. Compare the Lamb, Son, Creator, baptismal exemplar, crucified Redeemer, and risen covenant Lord. Then examine the provenance questions rather than assuming that doctrinal clarity proves historicity.

5. **Study Restoration witnesses.** Read [[D&C 76]], [[D&C 110]], [[D&C 130]], and the related history of the [[Kirtland Temple dedication]]. Mark precisely which propositions are textual reports, participant interpretations, and later doctrinal syntheses.

6. **Trace major doctrines outward.** Follow [[Premortal Life]], [[Creation]], [[The Godhead]], [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Gospel of Jesus Christ]], [[Salvation]], [[Resurrection]], and [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]]. Compare official summaries in [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topics)]] with the cited primary passages.

7. **Test the conclusions against the hard questions.** Read [[Did Jesus exist as a historical person]], [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]], [[Did Jesus expect the end within his generation]], [[How did the doctrine of the Trinity develop]], and [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]]. A conclusion is stronger when it states exactly what the evidence establishes and what remains a faith judgment.

Index matches should not all receive equal weight. Exact naming passages such as John 14:6, John 6:35, John 10:11, 1 Nephi 11:21, and Mosiah 3:8 should anchor the study; keyword-only chapter matches require examination in context before being treated as evidence.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The canonical portrait of [[Jesus Christ]] is cumulative. Israel's scriptures proclaim the LORD as unique Savior. The New Testament identifies Jesus as Messiah, Son, crucified and risen Lord, shepherd, bread, way, and divine agent of creation. The Book of Mormon presents explicit prophecy of his name and mission before his birth and reports the risen Christ ministering at Bountiful. Restoration scripture adds reported visions of the living Son and a theology that strongly distinguishes Father and Son while affirming their complete unity in saving work.

At the doctrinal center is not a title alone but a narrated saving movement: premortal divine commission, creation, incarnation, obedient ministry, voluntary suffering and death, resurrection, judgment, reconciliation, covenant discipleship, and promised return. [[3 Nephi 27]] gives one of the clearest canonical summaries. Christ's followers are called not only to confess him but to repent, receive ordinances, love, sacrifice, and become like him.

The historical evidence should be graded rather than flattened. Jesus's existence and crucifixion are very strongly supported. The early rise of resurrection belief and claimed appearances is also strongly supported. The supernatural explanation of those claims is not compelled by ordinary historical method, although believers judge it the best explanation in a broader theological framework. Restoration appearances and Book of Mormon prophecy are firmly attested as claims in their respective records; their divine and ancient provenance remains contested.

The central intellectual tensions are real: Gospel diversity, development in Christology, the historical limits on miracle claims, apparently exclusive salvation language, Book of Mormon dependence questions, and differences between Latter-day Saint and Nicene accounts of God. None is answered responsibly by suppressing evidence. Nor do these questions erase the substantial and early evidence that devotion to the crucified and risen Christ stood near Christianity's beginning.

The most defensible conclusion has layers. Textually, the canon unmistakably makes Jesus Christ its unique saving center. Historically, Jesus lived, was crucified, and became the object of very early resurrection faith. Theologically, identifying him as the premortal Creator, Only Begotten Son, universal Redeemer, and living Lord is a coherent canonical and Latter-day Saint confession. Whether that confession corresponds to ultimate divine reality remains a conclusion of reasoned faith rather than a result mechanically proved by the surviving evidence.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
