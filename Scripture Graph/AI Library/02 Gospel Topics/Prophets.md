# Prophets

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, a prophet is a person called or commissioned to receive and communicate a message from God. The role includes warning, covenant accusation, instruction, consolation, testimony of Christ, symbolic action, and sometimes prediction; reducing prophecy to forecasting misses much of [[Amos 7]], [[Jeremiah 23]], and [[Micah 6]]. Amos grounds his ministry in God's call rather than ancestry or occupation (Amos 7:14–15).

Related terms overlap without always being interchangeable. Mosiah 8:15 records [[Limhi]] saying that a seer is greater than a prophet; Ammon then explains that a seer is also a revelator and prophet and may disclose hidden, past, and future things through a divine gift (Mosiah 8:16–17). This is a hierarchy voiced and clarified within one narrative, not a universal lexical rule imposed on every biblical occurrence.

The canon also speaks of prophecy as a [[Spiritual Gifts|spiritual gift]] exercised in a congregation (1 Corinthians 12:10; 14:29–33), of named prophetic figures such as [[Moses (prophet)]], [[Jeremiah (prophet)]], and [[Anna (prophetess)]], and of false prophets who claim authorization they do not possess. In contemporary Latter-day Saint usage, “living prophet” particularly denotes the President of the Church, while the First Presidency and Quorum of the Twelve Apostles are sustained as prophets, seers, and revelators; that institutional usage should be distinguished from every broader scriptural use of “prophet.”
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon teaches that God reveals his purposes through prophets (Amos 3:7; Hosea 12:10), calls them rather than merely recognizing a profession (Amos 7:14–15), and sends them to summon communities to repentance (Jeremiah 26:4–6; Ether 11:1–2). Their central work is not self-display but witness: the law and prophets witness God's righteousness in Christ (Romans 3:21–22), Jesus identifies himself as the one foretold by the prophets (3 Nephi 11:10), and Abinadi calls Christ the founder of the peace prophets proclaim (Mosiah 15:13–18). [[Jesus Christ]] remains the cornerstone rather than a subordinate part of the prophetic foundation (Ephesians 2:20).

Scripture simultaneously requires discernment. Deuteronomy rejects a wonder-worker who draws Israel after other gods and condemns presumptuous speech (Deuteronomy 13:1–5; 18:20–22). Jeremiah and Ezekiel oppose speakers whom the Lord did not send (Jeremiah 23:21–22; Ezekiel 13:2–9). Paul instructs that prophetic speech be judged rather than merely accepted because of its label (1 Corinthians 14:29). These passages make uncritical title-based obedience a poorer account of the canon than tested trust.

Restoration scripture teaches continuing divine speech and commands attention to the Lord's servants (D&C 1:14, 37–38). It also says revelation is delivered through servants in weakness and in their language (D&C 1:24–28), while D&C 68:3–4 identifies speech as scripture when servants speak as moved by the Holy Ghost. Official contemporary teaching in [[Prophets (Gospel Topics)]], [[The Role of Prophets (Gospel Topics)]], and [[The Blessing of Continuing Revelation to Prophets and Personal Revelation to Guide Our Lives (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2020)]] affirms living prophets. This establishes the Church's doctrinal position; it does not amount to a canonical declaration that every opinion, policy, prediction, or historical judgment uttered by a leader is infallible.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The Torah's governing paradigm is [[Moses (prophet)]]: called at Horeb, sent against Pharaoh, mediator of covenant, and remembered as knowing the Lord face to face (Exodus 3:1–15; Deuteronomy 34:10–12). Deuteronomy 18:15–19 promises a prophet like Moses, a passage Acts 3:20–23 applies to [[Jesus Christ]].

In Israel's monarchic period, prophets could authorize rulers yet also confront them. [[Elijah]] opposed Ahab's Baal establishment ([[1 Kings 18]]), Micaiah contradicted a royal prophetic consensus ([[1 Kings 22]]), and Amos resisted expulsion from the royal sanctuary ([[Amos 7]]). Prophetic authority was therefore neither simply royal nor reliably identified by majority agreement.

Around the Babylonian crisis, rival claims became especially acute. [[Jeremiah 23]], [[Jeremiah 27]], and [[Jeremiah 28]] contrast Jeremiah's warnings with reassuring speakers said not to have been sent. [[Ezekiel 13]] similarly condemns prophets who follow their own spirits. After exile, figures such as Haggai and Zechariah connected prophetic exhortation with restoration, while Zechariah 13:2–5 envisioned the exposure of deceptive prophecy.

The New Testament presents John the Baptist and Jesus within Israel's prophetic story, identifies Jesus as the promised prophet like Moses, and depicts apostles and prophets as foundational witnesses under Christ the cornerstone (Acts 3:22–26; Ephesians 2:20). Prophecy also appears as a distributed congregational gift subject to evaluation (1 Corinthians 14:29–33). Women participate in this history: Miriam and Anna are called prophetesses, and Philip's daughters prophesy (Exodus 15:20; Luke 2:36–38; Acts 21:9).

The Book of Mormon extends the pattern through [[Lehi (father of Nephi)]], [[Abinadi]], [[Samuel the Lamanite]], [[Mormon (prophet-historian)]], and others. It emphasizes testimony of Christ, rejection of warnings, narrated fulfillment, preservation of records, and the seer-prophet distinction ([[Mosiah 8]]; [[Mosiah 15]]; [[Helaman 14]]; [[Ether 11]]).

The Restoration claims renewed prophetic authority through [[Joseph Smith Jr]] and a continuing succession of Church Presidents. [[Come, Listen to a Prophet’s Voice (Bishop Dean M. Davies, October 2018)]] states that Joseph Smith was a prophet and that living prophets remain on earth. [[A Living Prophet for the Latter Days (Elder Allen D. Haynie, April 2023)]] emphasizes present prophetic counsel. The institutional history of succession, including [[How was Brigham Young chosen to succeed Joseph Smith]], is distinct from the theological claim that God guided that succession.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
Several different kinds of evidence should not be collapsed.

**Strong textual evidence:** Across widely separated canonical collections, prophets are depicted as commissioned messengers, covenant critics, witnesses of Christ, and targets of rejection. The recurrence is direct and extensive: Exodus 3:10–15; Amos 7:14–15; Jeremiah 26:4–11; Matthew 23:29–37; Acts 3:18–24; Mosiah 15:11–18; Ether 11:1–5. This strongly establishes the canonical portrait, though it does not by itself prove every prophet's historical claims.

**Strong evidence of contested authority:** [[Jeremiah 28]] calls both Jeremiah and Hananiah prophets while narrating their incompatible messages. Deuteronomy 13:1–5 and 18:20–22, Jeremiah 23:21–22, Ezekiel 13:2–9, Matthew 7:15–20, and 1 Corinthians 14:29 provide overlapping but not mechanically identical tests. This strongly establishes that scripture itself recognizes false claims and requires discernment. It does not provide an effortless test for every modern dispute; rival groups can disagree about fidelity, fruit, fulfillment, and who was genuinely sent.

**Moderate internal narrative evidence:** Helaman 14 announces signs, Helaman 16:13–14 anticipates their fulfillment, and 3 Nephi 1:4–21 narrates their occurrence. This is a coherent prophecy-fulfillment sequence within the record. It supports the narrator's theological presentation of prophetic reliability, but a critic can explain the same correspondence through unified authorship, editorial shaping, or retrospective composition. It is not independent proof of supernatural prediction.

**Strong evidence of canonical importance, limited as historical proof:** Moses recurs throughout the Torah, later prophets, Gospels, Acts, Pauline letters, Book of Mormon, and Pearl of Great Price. That distribution strongly demonstrates his importance in the received canon and later religious memory. It cannot alone resolve the historical formation of Mosaic traditions.

**Experiential evidence in the tradition:** [[The Role of Prophets (Gospel Topics)]] invites believers to act on prophetic counsel and observe promised blessings. Such an experiment may be spiritually meaningful and can support personal warranted trust. Outcomes are difficult to isolate from expectation, selection effects, ordinary prudence, and differing reports of harm or nonfulfillment, so this is not public proof of a leader's universal divine authority.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Significant questions remain open:

- How should Deuteronomy's tests be applied when a prophecy is conditional, metaphorical, distant, or only partly fulfilled?
- How can hearers distinguish revelation from a prophet's judgment, cultural inheritance, administrative policy, or private opinion? D&C 1:24–28 and 68:3–4 establish both divine commission and human mediation but do not supply a complete classification procedure.
- Does Ephesians 2:20 refer to Old Testament prophets, New Testament Christian prophets, or both? The grammar and wider letter permit scholarly disagreement.
- How does the congregational gift of prophecy in 1 Corinthians 14 relate to an enduring institutional office of prophet?
- What should believers do when statements by past and present leaders appear to conflict? [[A Living Prophet for the Latter Days (Elder Allen D. Haynie, April 2023)]] offers a rule for present guidance, while [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]] raises the historical and epistemic difficulty.
- Are apparent fulfillments independently documented, or are prophecy and fulfillment preserved by the same narrator or editor?
- How do women designated as prophetesses or shown prophesying relate to modern institutional offices and [[Why do women not hold the priesthood]]?
- When communities disagree over a claimant, what evidence could be shared across faith commitments rather than depending entirely on prior acceptance of the community's authority?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest critical objection is evidentiary: prophetic authority is usually attested by the texts and communities that already recognize it. A narrated call, miracle, or fulfillment establishes what a source claims, but does not independently establish divine causation. Prophecy-and-fulfillment sequences can reflect genuine prediction, conditional fulfillment, retrospective narration, editorial coordination, or composition after the described events.

That last possibility is central in historical-critical scholarship. Many scholars date the final form of Daniel's apocalyptic visions to the second-century BC Maccabean period and distinguish Isaiah 40–55, and often Isaiah 56–66, from the eighth-century setting of Isaiah 1–39. Conservative scholars defend earlier or more unified composition. These disputes matter because a text written after an event has different evidentiary force from an independently documented prediction. Mosiah 15's use of Isaiah 52 also intersects with [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Isaiah and the King James Bible]]. See [[When was the book of Daniel written]] for the corresponding Daniel debate.

A second objection is historical fallibility. Leaders recognized as prophets have made statements later revised, abandoned, or contradicted. This challenges maximal claims that every authoritative-sounding statement is God's timeless word. Distinguishing canonized revelation, official teaching, policy, judgment, and opinion may be necessary, but becomes ad hoc if applied only after difficulties arise. [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]] is therefore central to the subject.

A third objection is circular discernment: true prophets are said to agree with true doctrine, while the community may know true doctrine chiefly through those prophets. Deuteronomy places loyalty to God above signs, Jeremiah and Ezekiel distinguish sent from unsent speakers, Jesus points to fruits, and Paul requires prophetic speech to be judged. These tests resist pure title-based obedience, but communities can still disagree about doctrine, mixed fruits, conditional predictions, and genuine commission.

Sociological accounts can interpret prophets as charismatic critics, court or cultic specialists, literary personae, or institutions whose authority was negotiated within communities. Such accounts explain substantial textual and social evidence without affirming revelation. They do not disprove revelation; they show that divine causation is an additional theological conclusion.

Finally, exhortations to obey living prophets can preserve communal continuity but can magnify harm when leaders err or members suppress conscience and evidence. The canon's own false-prophet warnings prevent obedience from replacing moral responsibility, humility, and discernment.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Historical-critical scholarship studies prophecy within ancient Near Eastern and Mediterranean social, political, and literary settings. Abraham J. Heschel's *The Prophets* emphasizes prophetic participation in divine concern and the moral force of the message. Robert R. Wilson's *Prophecy and Society in Ancient Israel* analyzes prophetic social roles; Joseph Blenkinsopp's *A History of Prophecy in Israel* traces historical development; and David E. Aune's *Prophecy in Early Christianity and the Ancient Mediterranean World* situates early Christian prophecy in a wider environment. These works differ substantially, but none reduces prophets chiefly to predictors of remote events.

Many scholars distinguish historical prophetic figures from the final literary books bearing their names, attending to collection, transmission, and redaction. A major example is the widespread critical dating of Daniel's final apocalyptic form to the Maccabean era, contrasted with conservative defenses of earlier composition. Likewise, many scholars distinguish Isaiah 40–55 and 56–66 from the eighth-century prophet, while traditional scholarship defends substantial unity or early authorship. These positions materially affect whether apparent predictions are evaluated as prior forecasts, later theological interpretations, or layered compositions. See [[When was the book of Daniel written]] and [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Isaiah and the King James Bible]].

Believing readers may accept editorial development while regarding the received scripture as inspired; stricter traditional models attribute much more directly to the named prophet. Neither compositional analysis nor canonical reception by itself adjudicates whether God inspired the resulting text.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation adds claims that ordinary historical method cannot establish: restored prophetic authority through [[Joseph Smith Jr]], prophetic keys, and continuing revelation through Church Presidents. [[Prophets (Gospel Topics)]] and [[The Role of Prophets (Gospel Topics)]] state that confessional position. [[Eternal Truth (Elder John C. Pingree Jr., October 2023)]] describes truth coming through scriptural and living prophets.

The principal divide is methodological as well as factual. Critical scholarship can investigate texts, genres, dates, institutions, reception, and literary relationships; it does not normally certify divine speech. Believing scholarship interprets some texts and experiences as revelation. Responsible work on either side should acknowledge rival claimants, editorial development, conditional prophecy, human error, and the distinction between evidence for a community's belief and evidence for divine causation.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with the Mosaic paradigm in [[Exodus 3]] and Deuteronomy 18 and 34. Ask what commissions Moses, what authority he receives, and what limits or tests appear.

2. Study prophetic confrontation in [[1 Kings 18]], [[1 Kings 22]], and [[Amos 7]]. Compare solitary dissent, institutional pressure, royal power, and divine calling.

3. Read the false-prophecy dossier as a unit: [[Jeremiah 23]], [[Jeremiah 27]], [[Jeremiah 28]], and [[Ezekiel 13]]. List every stated test, then note what remains unresolved when rival speakers both invoke God.

4. Examine prophetic ethics in [[Micah 3]] and [[Micah 6]]. This guards against reducing prophecy to prediction.

5. Follow the Christological line through Deuteronomy 18:15–19, Acts 3:20–26, Romans 3:21–22, and Ephesians 2:19–22. Keep [[Jesus Christ]] as cornerstone and fulfillment, not merely one prophet among equals.

6. Study New Testament prophetic participation and discernment in 1 Corinthians 12–14, 1 John 4:1, Luke 2:36–38, and Acts 21:8–9. Compare institutional ministry with the spiritual gift of prophecy.

7. Read the Book of Mormon sequence [[Mosiah 7]], [[Mosiah 8]], and [[Mosiah 15]] for [[Abinadi]], seership, and prophets as publishers of Christ's peace. Then compare [[Helaman 14]], [[Helaman 16]], and [[3 Nephi 1]] as an internal prophecy-fulfillment sequence, and [[Ether 11]] as a rejection narrative.

8. Read D&C 1:14, 24–28, 37–38 together with D&C 68:3–4. A sound theology must preserve both divine authority and the text's explicit acknowledgment of human language and weakness.

9. Compare current official presentations in [[Prophets (Gospel Topics)]], [[The Role of Prophets (Gospel Topics)]], [[Come, Listen to a Prophet’s Voice (Bishop Dean M. Davies, October 2018)]], and [[A Living Prophet for the Latter Days (Elder Allen D. Haynie, April 2023)]]. Conclude with the historical challenge posed by [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]] and formulate criteria that are principled rather than retrospective.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The canon's strongest portrait of prophets is neither “infallible predictors” nor merely “inspired teachers.” Prophets are commissioned witnesses who disclose divine counsel, prosecute covenant unfaithfulness, call people to repentance, preserve and interpret sacred memory, and testify of [[Jesus Christ]]. [[Moses (prophet)]] supplies the foundational pattern; the prophets of Israel repeatedly confront rulers and religious institutions; the New Testament locates apostles and prophets beneath Christ the cornerstone; and the Book of Mormon emphasizes Christ-centered witness, rejection, records, seership, and fulfillment.

The same canon that commands attention to prophets also makes discernment unavoidable. Deuteronomy tests allegiance and presumptuous prediction; Jeremiah and Ezekiel expose speakers who were not sent; Jesus warns about fruits; Paul says prophetic speech should be judged. [[Jeremiah 28]] is especially sobering because incompatible speakers both occupy the public category of prophet. A title, charisma, institutional position, majority, sign, or comforting message is not self-validating.

Latter-day Saint doctrine adds a continuing institutional claim: God restored prophetic authority through [[Joseph Smith Jr]] and guides the Church through living prophets. That is the explicit position of [[Prophets (Gospel Topics)]] and current general conference teaching. Restoration scripture also describes revelation as given through servants in weakness and human language (D&C 1:24–28), and describes their words as scripture when moved by the Holy Ghost (D&C 68:3–4). A defensible Latter-day Saint synthesis therefore combines serious attention to authorized prophetic counsel with Christ-centered testing, recognition of human limitation, and refusal to turn trust into a claim of universal prophetic inerrancy.

Textual recurrence strongly establishes the importance of prophets within the canon. Internal prophecy-fulfillment narratives support the theological claims their narrators make, but do not independently prove supernatural foreknowledge. Historical and experiential arguments may rationally strengthen faith, yet alternative literary, social, and psychological explanations remain possible. The larger conclusion—that God truly calls and speaks through prophets—is a coherent faith judgment grounded in scripture, tradition, experience, and claimed revelation; it is not a conclusion historical method alone can compel.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
