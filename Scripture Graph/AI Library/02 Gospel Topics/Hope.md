# Hope

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In ordinary speech, *hope* can mean a wish whose outcome is uncertain. Scripture uses a wider range of related senses, so no single English definition should be forced onto every occurrence. Hope may designate trust in the LORD (Jeremiah 17:7, 13, 17), waiting upon his word (Psalm 119:49, 81, 114), expectation amid lament (Lamentations 3:18–26), or orientation toward a promised good not yet seen (Romans 8:24–25).

Christian and Restoration texts give hope a particularly Christ-centered and eschatological content: resurrection, bodily redemption, glory, and eternal life through the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] and resurrection of [[Jesus Christ]] (1 Peter 1:3; Romans 8:23–25; Moroni 7:41). In [[Ether 12]], faith-generated hope anchors disciples in steadfastness and good works. In [[2 Nephi 31]], a perfect brightness of hope belongs to a life of steadfastness, love, nourishment by Christ's word, and endurance.

Hope is therefore more than positive emotion, but it is not present possession or a guarantee of every desired temporal outcome. Contemporary Latter-day Saint teaching, including [[Hope (Gospel Topics)]] and [[The Infinite Power of Hope (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, October 2008)]], interprets gospel hope as Christ-centered, active, and sufficiently confident to sustain faithful action while fulfillment remains unseen.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon teaches that the ultimate ground of Christian hope is God as a truthful promiser (Hebrews 6:17–18; Titus 1:2), and its decisive historical ground is the resurrection of Jesus Christ (1 Peter 1:3; Moroni 7:41). Its principal future objects include resurrection, bodily redemption, freedom from corruption, divine glory, and eternal life (Romans 5:2; Romans 8:21–23; Moroni 7:41).

Hope relates closely to [[Faith]] and [[Charity]]. Moroni asks how faith can be attained without hope and then says that faith in Christ gives hope through his atonement and resurrection (Moroni 7:40–42). This apparent reciprocity is better treated as a mutually reinforcing relationship than forced into a single mechanical order. Ether 12:4 adds the ethical consequence: hope anchors the soul, makes disciples steadfast, and leads to abundant good works. [[Moroni 7]] therefore refuses both inactive belief and moral effort detached from Christ.

Hope also belongs to sanctification in the present. Romans 5:3–5 links affliction, patience, tested experience, hope, divine love, and the Holy Ghost. Romans 15:4, 13 connects hope with scripture, patient endurance, comfort, joy, peace, belief, and the Spirit. [[Moroni 8]] similarly places hope and perfect love within a progression involving repentance, baptism, remission, meekness, the Holy Ghost, prayer, and endurance (Moroni 8:25–26).

The Church's contemporary teaching, represented by [[Hope (Gospel Topics)]], [[The Triumph of Hope (Elder Neil L. Andersen, October 2024)]], and [[Spiritually Whole in Him (President Camille N. Johnson, April 2025)]], emphasizes Christ-centered confidence and the mutually supporting roles of faith, hope, and charity. These teachings interpret and apply the canon; they do not turn every personal desire for healing, reconciliation, or deliverance into an unconditional revealed promise.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
A responsible history distinguishes the canon's narrative sequence from a demonstrated history of doctrinal development. The Hebrew Bible contains varied expressions: lament can say that hope has perished and then recover hope through remembered mercy (Lamentations 3:18–26); psalms locate hope in God's word (Psalm 119:49, 81, 114, 116); Jeremiah names the Lord as Israel's hope (Jeremiah 17:7, 13, 17); and Zechariah addresses covenant captives as prisoners of hope (Zechariah 9:11–12). These passages sustain trust in God but do not alone present the full later Christian or Latter-day Saint account of resurrection and the spirit world.

In the New Testament, Israel's promised future and Jesus's resurrection become central. Paul identifies his contested message with the hope of the ancestral promise and immediately raises the question of resurrection (Acts 26:6–8). [[Romans 5]] relates hope to justification, grace, suffering, divine love, and the Spirit. [[Romans 8]] expands its horizon to bodily and cosmic redemption. Hebrews presents hope as access-oriented confidence anchored beyond the veil (Hebrews 6:18–20), while [[1 Peter 1]] calls resurrection-grounded hope living and directs it toward future grace.

The Book of Mormon presents this Christ-centered formulation within its own pre-Christian prophetic history. Jacob claims that earlier prophets hoped in Christ's glory (Jacob 4:4–11); Nephi places a perfect brightness of hope on the enduring covenant path (2 Nephi 31:20); Ether joins hope to faith, steadiness, and good works (Ether 12:4); and Mormon defines its object through Christ's atonement and resurrection (Moroni 7:41). Whether these formulations represent ancient Israelite-Christian continuity, translation into familiar Christian idiom, modern composition, or a combination belongs to the larger historical debate about the book's origin; the texts themselves clearly present the continuity.

[[Doctrine and Covenants 138]] extends the picture into [[Death and the Spirit World]], portraying the righteous dead as still awaiting a glorious resurrection (Doctrine and Covenants 138:14–16). Modern discourse increasingly applies this eschatological hope to addiction, grief, illness, family fracture, and social crisis, as in [[O That Cunning Plan of the Evil One (Elder M. Russell Ballard, October 2010)]], [[A Perfect Brightness of Hope (Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, April 2020)]], and [[Follow Jesus Christ with Footsteps of Faith (President M. Russell Ballard, October 2022)]].
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong textual findings

The strongest finding is canonical convergence. Lamentations, Paul, Hebrews, First Peter, the Book of Mormon, and latter-day revelation repeatedly combine trust in God, an unseen future, endurance, resurrection, and action (Lamentations 3:21–26; Romans 5:2–5; Romans 8:23–25; Hebrews 6:18–19; 1 Peter 1:3; Ether 12:4; Moroni 7:41; Doctrine and Covenants 138:14). This strongly establishes a coherent theme within the received canon. It does not prove that the promises are historically or metaphysically true.

The canon also gives strong counterevidence to prosperity-style claims. Hebrews honors both dramatic deliverance and faithful sufferers who were tortured or killed while awaiting a better resurrection (Hebrews 11:33–39). In [[Mosiah 24]], God strengthens burdened people before releasing them (Mosiah 24:14–16). Romans 8 defines hope by patient waiting for what is unseen. Thus immediate rescue is not a canonical test of sufficient faith.

### Meaningful but limited literary findings

[[Hebrews 6]] and [[Ether 12]] share the memorable anchor image (Hebrews 6:19; Ether 12:4). The parallel is certain and useful for interpretation: both associate hope with stability, while each develops the image differently. Its historical significance is weak by itself. Common metaphor, English biblical influence, deliberate intertextuality, translation vocabulary, or independent prophetic usage can each account for resemblance; the parallel cannot decide among them.

[[Moroni 8]] integrates hope into a sustained theological progression rather than mentioning it decoratively (Moroni 8:25–26). That is meaningful evidence of literary coherence. It is weak evidence for any particular origin theory because ancient composition, modern composition, translation, and editing can all produce coherence.

The index labels several chapter matches as ambiguous, but this is an indexing caution rather than evidence that the word is absent. [[Hebrews 6]], [[Romans 5]], [[Lamentations 3]], [[1 Peter 1]], [[Colossians 1]], [[Jacob 4]], [[Romans 15]], [[Acts 26]], [[Jeremiah 17]], and [[Psalm 119]] contain direct, relevant uses. Their functions nevertheless differ: resurrection hope, covenant trust, apostolic vocation, comfort from scripture, and even an agricultural-labor analogy should not be flattened into one sense.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Several questions remain productive rather than merely troublesome:

- What exactly is the relationship between faith and hope? Moroni 7:40 asks how faith is attained without hope, yet Moroni 7:41 and Ether 12:4 ground hope in faith. Is this reciprocal growth, a rhetorical circle, or a distinction between preliminary desire and mature Christian hope?

- How certain may hope be while remaining hope? Romans 8:24 says seen fulfillment is no longer hope, whereas Hebrews 6:11 speaks of full assurance. The likely distinction is between confidence in God's promise and present possession of its outcome, but the texts do not reduce that confidence to a measurable psychological state.

- Which hoped-for outcomes are promised to everyone? Resurrection and God's final redemption occupy a different doctrinal category from desired marriages, cures, employment, or immediate safety. [[A Perfect Brightness of Hope (Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, April 2020)]] applies hope amid such desires without supplying a timetable or guaranteeing each specific result.

- Does hope require a particular emotional feeling? Lamentations 3 moves through exhaustion and the reported loss of hope before renewed trust. This suggests that covenantal hope can coexist with grief, but questions about depression, trauma, and diminished emotional capacity require pastoral and clinical care beyond lexical study.

- How should readers relate Israel's historical hopes to Christian claims about Christ? Acts 26:6–8 presents resurrection faith as fulfillment of Israel's ancestral promise, but Jewish and Christian interpreters differ over whether that identification is warranted.

- What historical significance, if any, belongs to the shared anchor imagery in Hebrews 6:19 and Ether 12:4? The parallel is real; its direction and explanation remain underdetermined.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objection is experiential: religious hope can look unfalsifiable because disappointments are deferred to an unseen future. If rescue occurs, it may be counted as fulfillment; if it does not, fulfillment may be relocated to resurrection or eternity. That structure can protect belief from evidence and, handled badly, silence grief. The canonical response is not a demonstration that every promise will be fulfilled. It is narrower: scripture openly includes lament, delay, persecution, death, and unreceived temporal promises (Lamentations 3:18; Romans 8:24–25; Hebrews 11:35–39). Honest hope must therefore permit lament and must not invent promises the text does not make.

A second objection is ethical: exhortations to hope can encourage passivity or preserve abusive conditions. Ether 12:4 pushes against that use by linking hope with abundant good works, and 2 Nephi 31:20 joins it to love and persevering action. Still, these verses do not automatically identify the wisest action in a dangerous circumstance. Appeals to endurance must never substitute for safety, justice, treatment, or responsible intervention.

A historical-critical objection is that the canon's apparent unity may result from literary borrowing, translation, editorial selection, or later communities reading diverse texts through a Christian lens. That is a serious alternative. The convergence strongly supports a canonical theological pattern, but it cannot independently prove common revelation or uniform original meanings. The anchor parallel between [[Hebrews 6]] and [[Ether 12]] is especially compatible with more than one origin model.

A philosophical objection holds that hope may be psychologically useful without its object being true. Romans 5:3–5 and contemporary talks describe hope's fruits, but beneficial effects do not verify resurrection or divine promises. Christian conviction ultimately depends on broader claims about [[Jesus Christ]], revelation, covenant, and especially [[Resurrection of Jesus]], not on hope's therapeutic value alone.

Finally, hope can become cruel when believers imply that despair, depression, or fear proves moral failure. The texts command and commend hope, but Lamentations preserves the voice of someone whose hope felt destroyed. Nothing in these passages warrants diagnosing every emotional inability to feel hopeful as sin.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship generally treats hope according to each text's literary and historical setting rather than assuming every English occurrence has an identical sense. Standard lexical works such as *A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament and Other Early Christian Literature* (BDAG) and the *Hebrew and Aramaic Lexicon of the Old Testament* distinguish the source-language terms hidden by the single English word. This cautions against building doctrine from an English concordance alone.

On Paul, N. T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* emphasizes the bodily and Jewish-apocalyptic character of resurrection hope, a reading strongly compatible with Romans 8:11, 23 and 1 Corinthians 15. Scholars disagree over Wright's larger historical and theological conclusions, but the observation that Pauline hope includes transformed embodiment is textually strong. Jürgen Moltmann's *Theology of Hope* influentially makes promised future and resurrection central to Christian theology and ethics; readers need not accept his whole system to see its resistance to reducing hope to private consolation.

Historical-critical interpreters commonly stress diversity across the Bible: lament, national restoration, wisdom reflection, apocalyptic resurrection, and early Christian expectation arise in different contexts. Confessional interpreters more readily read these as stages or mutually illuminating witnesses within one divine economy. The divide is chiefly over historical and theological explanation, not over whether the texts use different forms of hope.

For the Book of Mormon, Grant Hardy's *Understanding the Book of Mormon* models close literary analysis that can identify coherent narration and theology without first resolving historicity. Believing scholarship may read Jacob 4, 2 Nephi 31, Ether 12, and Moroni 7 as witnesses of ancient prophetic continuity centered in Christ. Critical scholarship may instead attribute their Christian language and biblical resonances to nineteenth-century composition or translation. The textual observations—recurring linkage of faith, hope, charity, resurrection, and works—survive either model; claims about provenance do not follow from that coherence alone.

Latter-day Saint doctrinal exposition is represented by [[Hope (Gospel Topics)]], [[The Infinite Power of Hope (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, October 2008)]], and [[The Triumph of Hope (Elder Neil L. Andersen, October 2024)]]. These are best used as authoritative tradition-specific interpretation and pastoral application, kept distinct from conclusions established solely by historical or lexical scholarship.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with lament and covenant trust: read [[Lamentations 3]], [[Jeremiah 17]], and the hope verses in [[Psalm 119]]. Track what has and has not changed when the speaker renews hope.

2. Study the Christian object of hope: read [[1 Peter 1]], Acts 26:6–8, and 1 Corinthians 15:12–22. Ask why resurrection is indispensable rather than decorative.

3. Read Paul's experiential sequence in [[Romans 5]] and his bodily, cosmic horizon in [[Romans 8]]. Distinguish present peace and the Spirit's presence from future redemption still awaited.

4. Examine the community's means of sustaining hope in [[Romans 15]]: scripture, patience, comfort, joy, peace, belief, and the Holy Ghost.

5. Compare the anchor passages, Hebrews 6:11–20 and Ether 12:4–9. Record the shared image, each passage's distinct setting, and several possible explanations for the parallel before drawing historical conclusions.

6. Read [[Jacob 4]], [[2 Nephi 31]], and [[Moroni 7]] in sequence. Trace hope's relationships to prophetic witness, Christ's atonement and resurrection, faith, charity, love, good works, and endurance.

7. Add [[Moroni 8]] and [[Doctrine and Covenants 138]] to see hope located respectively within covenant formation and among departed spirits awaiting resurrection.

8. Test the doctrine against suffering rather than only success: compare Hebrews 11:33–40, Mosiah 24:13–16, and Moroni 9:22–26. Ask what hope promises when catastrophe is not prevented.

9. Finish with modern applications in [[The Infinite Power of Hope (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, October 2008)]], [[A Perfect Brightness of Hope (Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, April 2020)]], and [[Spiritually Whole in Him (President Camille N. Johnson, April 2025)]]. Mark carefully where speakers quote canon, interpret it, or offer pastoral application.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scriptural hope is disciplined trust in God and expectant orientation toward a promised good not yet seen. Its Christian center is not optimism about circumstances but [[Jesus Christ]]: his [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], resurrection, promised grace, and power to raise humanity to [[Eternal Life]] (1 Peter 1:3; Moroni 7:41). Because its object remains future, hope waits; because its ground is God's promise, it can be confident without pretending fulfillment is already possessed (Romans 8:24–25; Hebrews 6:17–19).

The canon portrays hope as relational and active. It grows with [[Faith]], matures with [[Charity]], anchors conduct, generates good works, and sustains [[Endurance]] (Ether 12:4; Moroni 7:40–48; 2 Nephi 31:20). Scripture, the Holy Ghost, remembered mercy, and tested experience nourish it (Lamentations 3:21–26; Romans 5:3–5; Romans 15:4, 13). Its horizon includes not only private survival but bodily redemption and creation's liberation (Romans 8:19–23).

This portrait does not authorize easy promises. Faithful people may be delivered, strengthened while still burdened, or die without receiving the anticipated temporal outcome (Mosiah 24:14–16; Hebrews 11:35–40). Hope therefore cannot honestly mean that illness will always heal now, danger will always pass, or every desired relationship will be restored. Modern teachings such as [[A Perfect Brightness of Hope (Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, April 2020)]] apply hope amid such desires, but the canon locates its final security in God rather than in a preferred timetable.

The evidence for this canonical pattern is strong: diverse books repeatedly join God, promise, waiting, resurrection, endurance, and action. The evidence does not by itself prove divine inspiration or the historical truth of resurrection. Literary parallels such as the anchors in [[Hebrews 6]] and [[Ether 12]] deserve study but cannot settle provenance. A believer may receive the convergence as mutually confirming revelation; a critical reader may explain it through inheritance, borrowing, translation, editing, and common human responses to mortality. Intellectual integrity requires keeping that explanatory dispute open while stating the textual result plainly: biblical, Book of Mormon, and latter-day texts make hope a resilient form of allegiance to God that faces suffering without calling suffering good and acts toward promised life without claiming already to see it.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
