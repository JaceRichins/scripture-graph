# Charity

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In the King James New Testament, **charity** in [[1 Corinthians 13]] translates Greek *agapē*, ordinarily rendered "love" in modern English. It does not mean only donations or almsgiving. Paul defines it operationally: patience, kindness, humility, resistance to selfishness and provocation, joy in truth, and endurance (1 Corinthians 13:4-8).

Restoration scripture makes the definition more explicit. [[2 Nephi 26]] says that "charity is love" and connects it with refusing to let the laborer in Zion perish (2 Nephi 26:30). [[Moroni 7]] calls charity "the pure love of Christ" (Moroni 7:47), while [[Ether 12]] identifies Christ's love for the children of men as charity (Ether 12:34).

"Love of Christ" can denote Christ's own love, love directed toward Christ, or Christlike love bestowed upon and practiced by disciples. Ether 12:34 clearly supports the first sense; Moroni 7:48 and current Church teaching support participation in that love as a gift. [[Charity (Gospel Topics)]] consequently defines charity as both the love Heavenly Father and Jesus Christ have for all people and the love people should have for one another. That official summary is a contemporary doctrinal synthesis, not a replacement for the distinct wording of each passage.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon presents charity as indispensable rather than ornamental. Without it, tongues, prophecy, knowledge, extraordinary faith, generosity, and sacrifice do not profit the disciple (1 Corinthians 13:1-3). Mormon says that without charity a person is "nothing" and cannot be acceptable before God (Moroni 7:44); Ether connects it with inheriting the place prepared by the Father (Ether 12:34).

Charity originates in God and takes Christ as its pattern. [[1 John 4]] grounds love in God's prior sending of the Son (1 John 4:7-11), and [[John 13]] commands disciples to love one another as Jesus loved them (John 13:34-35). The doctrine therefore includes more than affection: it entails truthfulness, patience, mercy, endurance, material care, and refusal to make spiritual gifts or authority serve self-exaltation.

The canon also holds command and gift together. All people are commanded to have charity (2 Nephi 26:30), yet disciples must pray "with all the energy of heart" that the Father will fill them with it (Moroni 7:48). Charity is thus both practiced through [[Agency]] and received through [[Grace]]. It belongs with [[Faith]], [[Hope]], [[Humility]], and [[Endurance]], but is not reducible to any of them.

Current Church applications include family ministry, peacemaking, bearing burdens, and resisting racism; see [[Knowing, Loving, and Growing (Elder Hans T. Boom, October 2019)]], [[Peacemakers Needed (President Russell M. Nelson, April 2023)]], and [[Race and The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]]. These applications carry institutional and pastoral authority within the Latter-day Saint tradition, but they should remain distinguishable from canonized propositions.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The doctrine grows from older covenant commands to love God and neighbor (Deuteronomy 6:5; Leviticus 19:18). Jesus joins those commands as the great summary of the law (Matthew 22:37-40), extends love toward enemies (Matthew 5:43-48), and gives his disciples the Christ-patterned command of John 13:34-35.

In the apostolic writings, Paul places love at the center of congregational life. [[1 Corinthians 13]] belongs within Paul's treatment of competing spiritual gifts in 1 Corinthians 12-14, while Romans 13:8-10 says love fulfills the law. [[1 John 4]] grounds mutual love in God's prior action in sending the Son.

Within Book of Mormon narrative chronology, Nephi commands charity and applies it across divisions among his people, Jews, and Gentiles (2 Nephi 26:30; 2 Nephi 33:7-9). Mormon's sermon integrates moral discernment, [[Faith]], [[Hope]], and charity, and Moroni preserves that sermon in [[Moroni 7]]. [[Ether 12]] describes Christ's love as charity and presents charity as dependent upon grace.

The close wording shared by Moroni 7:45 and 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 creates a historical-source question. Latter-day Saint readings can understand the parallel as prophetic convergence or revealed translation into biblical language; critical readings can treat it as dependence on the King James Bible. The parallel itself is certain, but it does not adjudicate those explanations.

Later Restoration teaching broadened the doctrine's institutional application. Doctrine and Covenants 121:41-46 joins charity with noncoercive priesthood leadership. Modern instruction—including [[The Great Commandment (Elder Joseph B. Wirthlin, October 2007)]], [[Charity Never Faileth (Silvia H. Allred, October 2011)]], [[One in Christ (Elder Ulisses Soares, October 2018)]], and [[Cheering Each Other On (Sister J. Anette Dennis, October 2025)]]—regularly interprets charity through Christlike ministry, unity, burden-bearing, and conduct under opposition.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence is textual rather than apologetic.

- **Very strong:** 1 Corinthians 13:1-13, Moroni 7:44-48, 2 Nephi 26:30, Ether 12:28-36, John 13:34-35, and 1 John 4:7-11 independently establish that charity or love is central to the received canon. This demonstrates canonical centrality, not the historical independence or divine truth of every text.

- **Very strong observation, disputed significance:** Moroni 7:45 follows 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 closely in sequence and King James diction. A literary relationship is unmistakable. Competing accounts include ancient shared teaching, revealed translation into familiar biblical language, mixed translation and editing, or modern dependence on the KJV. The parallel alone cannot choose among them.

- **Strong:** Paul treats love as a criterion by which religious gifts and sacrifices are judged (1 Corinthians 13:1-3). This strongly supports an internal critique of impressive religious performance without transformed character. It does not establish that sincere love makes a doctrine true or an institution just.

- **Strong:** The reading's wider findings consistently connect love with [[Hope]], [[Faith]], [[Truth]], [[Prayer]], family duty, marriage, peacemaking, and righteous governance. Examples include 2 Nephi 31:20; Romans 5:3-5; Romans 13:8-10; Ephesians 4:15,25; and Doctrine and Covenants 121:45. These connections show a coherent ethical network, though some convergence results from later texts reusing earlier language.

- **Moderate as evidence of reception:** Modern talks and [[Charity (Gospel Topics)]] demonstrate how the contemporary Church interprets and applies the doctrine. They are good evidence for current Latter-day Saint teaching, but not independent evidence for ancient meanings or historical claims.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Does "the pure love of Christ" primarily mean Christ's love for humanity, a disciple's love for Christ, or Christlike love granted to disciples? Ether 12:34 clarifies one sense, but the full phrase remains multivalent.

2. How do command and gift relate? If all people must have charity (2 Nephi 26:30), why must disciples ask the Father to bestow it (Moroni 7:48)? A productive study should hold [[Agency]] and [[Grace]] together rather than using either to erase the other.

3. What does charity "believeth all things" mean (1 Corinthians 13:7; Moroni 7:45)? It cannot safely be made a blanket command for credulity without addressing the same description's commitment to [[Truth]].

4. Does charity require approval, unrestricted trust, or the absence of boundaries? None of the principal passages states that equation. How love governs accountability, protection of vulnerable people, and institutional discipline requires additional texts on [[Justice and Mercy]], repentance, and judgment.

5. How should personal love relate to structural injustice? Second Nephi 26:30 gives charity an economic consequence, and [[Race and The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]] applies it to racism. Yet warm feeling alone does not specify adequate institutional remedies.

6. What historical explanation best accounts for the close wording of Moroni 7 and 1 Corinthians 13? The textual fact is clearer than the provenance inference.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
A strong objection is that "charity" is archaic and now suggests optional donations rather than the demanding love intended by *agapē*. That criticism is linguistically sound for many modern readers. Retaining the traditional term can preserve scriptural and Latter-day Saint associations, but teaching should explicitly define it as love expressed in character and action.

A second objection is that praise of charity can become a tool for suppressing justified anger, disagreement, reporting of abuse, or demands for institutional reform. Nothing in 1 Corinthians 13 requires indifference to wrongdoing: charity "rejoiceth not in iniquity" and "rejoiceth in the truth" (1 Corinthians 13:6). Even so, those phrases do not automatically settle difficult disputes over boundaries, discipline, protest, or public accountability. Appeals to charity should be tested by their effects on truth and vulnerable people.

A third objection is that the standard is psychologically impossible and can produce guilt or hypocrisy. Moroni 7 partly answers this by treating charity as a gift sought in prayer rather than a performance generated by willpower alone. That theological answer does not eliminate the pastoral risk of using the ideal harshly.

A fourth objection concerns Book of Mormon provenance: Moroni 7:45 sounds strikingly like the KJV of 1 Corinthians 13. Critical readers reasonably regard this as evidence of biblical literary dependence. Believing models appeal to shared revelation or a translation delivered in familiar biblical language. Neither model should pretend the verbal parallel is absent; the parallel has much greater evidentiary force than any single explanation of it.

Finally, canonical love language does not by itself provide a complete social program. It supplies motives, constraints, and tests of character, but disagreements can remain about which policies, boundaries, or remedies best embody love. Calling one preferred solution "charity" without further argument risks begging the question.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Lexical scholarship broadly agrees that the Greek *agapē* in 1 Corinthians 13 means love, not merely charitable giving; see Frederick William Danker's *A Greek-English Lexicon of the New Testament and Other Early Christian Literature* (2000). Scholars also caution against assuming that the word itself always denotes a uniquely divine or selfless kind of love. Its content is shaped by context—in this case, Paul's predicates and his argument about communal gifts.

Gordon D. Fee's *The First Epistle to the Corinthians* and Anthony C. Thiselton's commentary of the same title interpret chapter 13 within 1 Corinthians 12-14. On this reading, it is not originally a detached wedding poem but Paul's correction of status competition and misuse of spiritual gifts in [[Corinth]]. That historical setting strengthens rather than diminishes its critique of giftedness without love.

Raymond E. Brown's *The Epistles of John* (1982) treats Johannine love as grounded in God's prior action and expressed in mutual obligation. Scholarship debates the composition and community behind 1 John; consequently, the chapter should not be used to make confident biographical claims about [[John the Apostle]] without separate argument.

Latter-day Saint interpretation makes Moroni 7:47's "pure love of Christ" a doctrinal synthesis of Christ's love and Christlike love received and practiced by disciples; [[Charity (Gospel Topics)]] is a clear official example. Talks such as [[His Yoke Is Easy and His Burden Is Light (Sister J. Anette Dennis, October 2022)]] and [[The Character of Christ (President D. Todd Christofferson, April 2026)]] place the Savior's character and atoning self-offering at the center.

Believing and critical scholarship can agree on the literary shape of the passages, their ethical demands, and the close Paul–Moroni parallel. They part chiefly over theological authority and provenance. Believing interpretation permits revelation, ancient continuity, and a Bible-shaped translation process; historical-critical interpretation ordinarily explains the English parallel through literary dependence and nineteenth-century biblical culture. The textual observation is strong; conclusions about revelation or authorship depend on broader premises and evidence.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
Begin with the vocabulary and setting of [[1 Corinthians 13]], but read chapters 12-14 together. Track what Paul says gifts can and cannot accomplish, then list each verb or disposition attributed to charity.

Next read [[John 13]] and [[1 John 4]]. Ask what initiates love, whose love supplies the pattern, and how love becomes evidence of discipleship. Add Matthew 5:43-48 and Matthew 22:37-40 to connect mutual love, enemy-love, and the great commandments.

Then read [[2 Nephi 26]] and [[2 Nephi 33]]. Observe how charity affects labor, priestcraft, group boundaries, reconciliation to Christ, and endurance. Continue with 2 Nephi 31:20 to place love within the covenant path toward [[Eternal Life]].

Read all of [[Moroni 7]], not only verses 44-48. Trace the movement from judging good and evil to [[Faith]], [[Hope]], meekness, charity, and prayer. Compare Moroni 7:45 clause by clause with 1 Corinthians 13:4-7, keeping the textual observation separate from theories explaining it.

Read [[Ether 12]] for the relation among weakness, [[Grace]], faith, hope, Christ's love, and Moroni's prayer for the Gentiles. Then study Doctrine and Covenants 121:34-46 to see charity applied to power and [[Priesthood]].

Finally compare canonical teaching with current reception in [[Charity (Gospel Topics)]], [[Peacemakers Needed (President Russell M. Nelson, April 2023)]], and [[Race and The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]]. For each application ask: What does the text require, what is a reasonable development, and what additional ethical or historical argument is still needed?
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scriptural charity is love disciplined into Christlike character and action. It is patient and kind, refuses vanity and selfishness, rejoices in [[Truth]], bears burdens, and endures. It is not merely emotion, politeness, tolerance, or almsgiving. [[1 Corinthians 13]] makes it the criterion without which gifts, knowledge, faith, generosity, and sacrifice fail to profit the disciple.

The canon grounds this love in God before placing it upon human beings as a duty. [[1 John 4]] points to God's sending of the Son; [[John 13]] makes Jesus' love the pattern of discipleship; [[Ether 12]] explicitly calls Christ's love for humanity charity. At the same time, [[2 Nephi 26]] commands all people to have charity, and [[Moroni 7]] directs them to seek it from the Father. Charity is therefore both gift and practiced obligation, joining [[Grace]] and [[Agency]].

Its reach is spiritual, interpersonal, and material. It belongs with [[Faith]], [[Hope]], [[Humility]], and [[Endurance]]; it marks discipleship; it restrains coercive spiritual power; and it refuses to let the laborer in Zion perish. Modern Church teaching applies it to ministry, family life, peacemaking, burden-bearing, and racism. Those applications matter, but charity language cannot substitute for truthful diagnosis, accountability, or careful decisions about remedies.

Two cautions protect the doctrine from overreading. First, "pure love of Christ" carries more than one possible grammatical emphasis, although Ether 12 clearly includes Christ's own love. Second, Moroni 7:45 closely parallels the KJV of 1 Corinthians 13. That fact is certain; its explanation is disputed. Honest study neither hides the dependence question nor pretends that the parallel alone resolves Book of Mormon historicity.

The canon's larger conclusion is demanding but limited: love is not an optional ornament on otherwise successful religion. It is a governing test of discipleship. The texts do not, however, prove that every action labeled loving is wise, true, or just. Charity must remain joined to truth, discernment, humility, and concrete care.
