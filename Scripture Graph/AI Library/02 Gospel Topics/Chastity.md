# Chastity

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In the canon, chastity is not defined by one technical term. The subject emerges through commands against adultery (Exodus 20:14), warnings against fornication and lust (Matthew 5:27–28; Galatians 5:16–21), fidelity within marriage (Hebrews 13:4), bodily holiness (1 Corinthians 6:13–20), and condemnation of exploitation and betrayal ([[Jacob 2]]; [[Moroni 9]]).

The words must be distinguished carefully. Adultery ordinarily concerns violation of marriage; biblical fornication translates terms with a wider and context-dependent semantic range; lust in Matthew 5:28 describes a directed, covetous gaze rather than every involuntary attraction. Virtue is broader still. It can mean moral excellence in [[2 Peter 1]], healing power in [[Luke 6]], or authority in the phrase “by virtue of” in [[D&C 121]]. Later Latter-day Saint discourse sometimes specializes virtue to mean sexual purity, as in [[Chastity (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Come Let Us Go Up to the Mountain of the Lord (Elaine S. Dalton, April 2009)]]. That devotional usage is legitimate within its setting but should not be projected onto every canonical occurrence.

Current official Church teaching defines the law of chastity as no sexual relations outside a legal marriage between a man and a woman; see [[Temples of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]]. It also presents chastity as a temple covenant. This is a modern authoritative formulation of [[Chastity]], not a verbatim sentence drawn from a single ancient passage.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The clearest canonical core is sexual fidelity to covenant and refusal to use another person as an object. The Decalogue prohibits adultery (Exodus 20:14). Jesus carries accountability inward to deliberate lust (Matthew 5:27–28; 3 Nephi 12:27–28). Paul grounds sexual restraint in belonging to Christ and in the holiness of the body (1 Corinthians 6:13–20). Hebrews holds together an affirmative view of [[Marriage]] and divine judgment of sexual misconduct (Hebrews 13:4).

The Book of Mormon adds strong attention to relational and communal harm. Jacob condemns men whose conduct wounds wives and children and says God delights in women's chastity ([[Jacob 2]]). Alma connects Corianton's conduct with abandoned responsibility, impaired missionary credibility, and the need for repentance ([[Alma 39]]). [[Moroni 9]] depicts sexual violence as the perpetrators' atrocity, not the victims' sin. Chastity teaching that stigmatizes an assault survivor is therefore a moral inversion of that passage.

Restoration scripture also places thought and character under discipleship. D&C 121:45–46 connects charity and virtue in thought with confidence before God, although the passage itself does not reduce virtue to sexuality. [[Chastity (Gospel Topics)]] makes that application explicitly. Official contemporary teaching further connects the law with [[Covenants]], [[Temples]], and [[Eternal Marriage]].

Serious sexual wrongdoing is not presented as beyond redemption. Alma calls Corianton to repent and continues teaching him ([[Alma 39]]–[[Alma 42]]). The doctrine must therefore be taught together with [[Agency]], accountability, [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], and the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]].
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
At Sinai, adultery appears as a concise covenant prohibition within the [[Ten Commandments]] (Exodus 20:14). Israel's prophets broaden sexual imagery: Hosea can describe actual misconduct while also using marital infidelity to illuminate covenant apostasy ([[Hosea 4]]). This double usage warns against treating every occurrence as a modern sexual-behavior rule.

Jesus reaffirms the adultery command, addresses desire, and places the teaching amid disputes about divorce ([[Matthew 5]]; [[Matthew 19]]). The early Christian writings connect sexual conduct with life in the Spirit, the body, marriage, and membership in Christ ([[Galatians 5]]; [[1 Corinthians 6]]; [[Hebrews 13]]). These texts arose in ancient Jewish and Greco-Roman societies whose marriage law, household authority, and gender assumptions were not identical to modern ones.

In the Book of Mormon, [[Jacob 2]] says the Nephites had already received relevant commandments through [[Lehi (father of Nephi)]]. Jacob emphasizes injury to wives and children. [[Alma 39]] treats Corianton's offense within a father-son discourse about ministry, repentance, resurrection, justice, and mercy. [[Moroni 9]] later portrays sexual violence amid social collapse.

Restoration-era teaching introduces a historical complication: [[D&C 132]] treats some plural marriages as authorized while also defining and condemning adultery. Thus chastity cannot historically be equated without qualification with monogamy. Questions about authorization, secrecy, consent, legality, and implementation belong with [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]] and cannot be settled merely by repeating the general law.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century Church discourse increasingly uses law of chastity as a named doctrinal category and often uses virtue as a sexual-purity term. Examples include [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[Chastity (Gospel Topics)]], [[Guardians of Virtue (Elaine S. Dalton, April 2011)]], [[We Believe in Being Chaste (Elder David A. Bednar, April 2013)]], and [[Two Great Commandments (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2019)]]. These sources witness contemporary institutional teaching; they should not be mistaken for proof that every earlier writer used the vocabulary identically.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence is direct and cumulative. Exodus 20:14 prohibits adultery; Matthew 5:27–28 and 3 Nephi 12:27–28 address lustful intention; 1 Corinthians 6:13–20 gives a theology of bodily holiness; Hebrews 13:4 honors marriage while warning against misconduct; [[Jacob 2]] and [[Alma 39]] depict relational and religious consequences. Together these establish that sexual fidelity and restraint are substantial canonical concerns.

A second strong finding concerns harm. Jacob's rebuke centers wounded wives and children, while Moroni 9:9–10 — part of a letter from Mormon to his son Moroni, preserved in the record that bears Moroni's name — attributes the deprivation of captive women's chastity and virtue to violent captors. This strongly supports victim-sensitive teaching. It does not establish a complete modern theology of consent, since the text retains ancient social assumptions and does not formulate contemporary legal categories.

The lexical evidence also imposes limits. Virtue is polysemous: the index match in [[2 Peter 1]] concerns a broad chain of moral qualities, Luke 6:19 uses the older English sense of power, and D&C 121:41 means authority or warrant. Accordingly, raw keyword counts substantially overstate how many chapters directly discuss chastity. The application of D&C 121:45 to sexual purity is an official devotional interpretation, not a definition supplied by the verse itself.

The figurative reading of [[Revelation 17]]–[[Revelation 18]] is plausible but not conclusively established: Babylon is a symbolic woman with whom kings commit fornication, and the surrounding imagery points substantially toward idolatrous, imperial, and economic complicity. This should be held as a strong possibility rather than settled consensus, and it should not be flattened together with [[Hosea 4]]:13-14, which many commentators read as describing actually-practiced cultic prostitution rather than pure covenant metaphor — the two chapters sit at different points on a literal-to-figurative spectrum, not one uniform idiom.

The reading surfaced no controlled historical or social-scientific evidence establishing every promised temporal outcome attached to chastity. Claims about increased happiness in [[We Believe in Being Chaste (Elder David A. Bednar, April 2013)]] are authoritative religious teaching and pastoral testimony; they should not be misreported as experimentally demonstrated universal results.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What conduct did the ancient authors include within the terms translated as fornication, uncleanness, and lust, and where do modern definitions extend beyond their original social settings?
- Does Matthew 5:28 condemn any sexual thought, or specifically a cultivated gaze aimed at desire or possession? How should the distinction protect people troubled by involuntary thoughts?
- How should fidelity to official teaching be joined with careful pastoral treatment of shame, compulsion, divorce, infertility, disability, orientation, and unequal power?
- How can communities teach [[Moroni 9]] without implying that assault removes a victim's moral worth or spiritual standing?
- How do consent and coercion, which modern ethics foreground, relate to ancient texts organized around marriage, household, and male authority?
- How should [[D&C 132]] and historical plural marriage affect claims that the practical definition of chastity has never changed?
- When Revelation uses sexual language for imperial and covenant corruption, what does that metaphor reveal, and what gendered stereotypes might it reinforce?
- Which consequences of chastity are explicit promises in scripture, which are pastoral generalizations, and which depend upon testimony rather than publicly testable evidence?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
One strong objection is that scriptural sexual rules reflect patriarchal societies in which adultery, virginity, and marriage were often framed through male lineage, household control, and women's status. The gendered language of [[Jacob 2]], Hosea, and Revelation does not by itself escape that history. A faithful reading can emphasize covenant and protection from exploitation, but it should not pretend that all ancient legal assumptions are already equivalent to modern ideals of equal agency and consent.

A second objection argues that harm and consent are better ethical standards than marital status alone. This view notes that a legal marriage can contain coercion or abuse, while some relationships outside a tradition's recognized boundaries may exhibit loyalty and care. The canonical response is that discipleship makes covenant and divine command morally relevant in addition to measurable harm. That response explains the religious claim but does not eliminate disputes about revelation, interpretation, or the adequacy of the institutional boundary.

A third objection is that Matthew 5:28 and D&C 121:45 can encourage thought policing, scrupulosity, or shame. This concern is strongest when involuntary attraction is treated as chosen lust. The texts do address inward formation, but neither requires the claim that temptation, an intrusive thought, and deliberate mental cultivation are identical.

A fourth objection concerns victim-blaming. Traditional phrases about losing or being robbed of virtue can suggest that assault changes a victim's purity. [[Moroni 9]] grammatically condemns the captors, yet its idiom can still be heard harmfully. Responsible teaching must state explicitly that culpability belongs to the perpetrator and that a victim's worth and moral agency are not erased.

A fifth objection alleges historical inconsistency: plural marriage authorized in [[D&C 132]] appears difficult to reconcile with simple claims that chastity always meant monogamous marriage. The strongest honest response distinguishes fidelity to the marital order believed authorized in a given period from one unchanging marital form. That model is internally coherent, but it depends on accepting disputed prophetic authorization and does not by itself answer historical questions about consent, secrecy, or practice.

Finally, several indexed passages are false or misleading positives. Virtue in [[2 Peter 1]] is broad moral excellence, virtue in Luke 6:19 is power, and fornication in [[Revelation 18]] is predominantly symbolic. Treating keyword recurrence as doctrinal uniformity would manufacture evidence.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Lexical scholarship supports caution. Standard reference works such as BDAG for New Testament Greek and HALOT for Biblical Hebrew distinguish adultery terms from broader and context-sensitive vocabulary for illicit sex, prostitution, impurity, desire, and figurative covenant infidelity. No single ancient term maps perfectly onto the complete modern Latter-day Saint expression law of chastity.

Historical-critical scholarship commonly reads biblical sexual ethics within kinship, inheritance, honor, household, and covenant systems. Jennifer Wright Knust's *Unprotected Texts* (2011) emphasizes the Bible's diverse and sometimes resistant sexual texts rather than a seamless modern code. Dale B. Martin's *Sex and the Single Savior* (2006) likewise questions anachronistic harmonization of early Christian categories. Such scholarship is valuable for historical description, though its theological conclusions are not binding on believers.

Believing Latter-day Saint teaching generally reads the canon as a coherent divine demand for sexual relations within authorized marriage, developed through continuing revelation and temple covenant. [[Chastity (Gospel Topics)]], [[Temples of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]], and [[We Believe in Being Chaste (Elder David A. Bednar, April 2013)]] exemplify this synthesis.

The principal divide is therefore not whether the texts contain serious sexual norms—they plainly do—but how diverse ancient commands should be harmonized, whether contemporary prophetic teaching authoritatively defines their application, and how historical change should be understood. Critical scholarship tends to explain variation through social history and competing authors; believing scholarship may acknowledge context while treating later revelation as authorized clarification. Neither approach licenses careless lexical conflation.
<!-- SG:END scholarship -->

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with the explicit covenant command in [[Exodus 20]] and place it within the [[Sinai covenant and Ten Commandments]]. Ask what adultery meant in that setting before generalizing.

2. Read Jesus's treatment of act, desire, marriage, and divorce in [[Matthew 5]] and [[Matthew 19]], then compare [[3 Nephi 12]]. Distinguish deliberate lust from involuntary temptation and note the gendered form of the sayings.

3. Study early Christian bodily and communal reasoning in [[1 Corinthians 6]], [[Galatians 5]], and [[Hebrews 13]]. Track the distinct roles of body, Spirit, marriage, and judgment.

4. Read [[Genesis 39]] as a narrative of resisted sexual solicitation and accountability to God. Then read [[Jacob 2]] for covenant betrayal and harm to family members.

5. Read [[Alma 39]] through [[Alma 42]] as one continuous address to [[Corianton]]. This prevents Alma 39:5 from being isolated from repentance, resurrection, justice, mercy, and renewed ministry.

6. Read [[Moroni 9]] with explicit attention to agency and victimization. Keep the captors' guilt separate from the captive women's worth.

7. Audit vocabulary through [[2 Peter 1]], [[D&C 121]], and [[Revelation 17]]–[[Revelation 18]]. Mark each occurrence of virtue or fornication as moral, sexual, instrumental, or figurative before drawing conclusions.

8. Study the Restoration complication in [[D&C 132]] alongside [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]]. Separate the canon's claim of authorization from historical evidence about implementation.

9. Only then compare modern formulations in [[Chastity (Gospel Topics)]], [[Temples of The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]], [[Pornography (Gospel Topics)]], and [[We Believe in Being Chaste (Elder David A. Bednar, April 2013)]]. Label clearly what comes from ancient text and what comes from continuing institutional interpretation.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The canon gives strong support to a doctrine of sexual fidelity, disciplined desire, bodily holiness, and accountability before God. The direct textual base is substantial: Exodus prohibits adultery; Jesus addresses lustful intention; Paul relates sexual conduct to Christ and the sanctity of the body; Hebrews honors marriage; Jacob exposes injury to spouses and children; Alma joins grave accountability with repentance. Modern Church teaching gathers these strands under the named law of chastity and defines its present institutional boundary through marriage and temple covenant.

Honest synthesis also requires limits. Chastity, adultery, fornication, lust, and virtue are not interchangeable words. Some indexed matches concern general excellence, healing power, or symbolic apostasy. Ancient texts reflect social assumptions that require historical interpretation, particularly concerning gender, marriage, and consent. [[D&C 132]] prevents an unqualified equation of chastity with one timeless marital form, while [[Moroni 9]] demands that victimization never be confused with personal sin.

What is established is that sexual conduct is a serious covenantal and communal concern across multiple canonical corpora. What remains interpretive is the precise scope of several ancient terms and how every modern circumstance maps onto them. What rests on faith is the claim that contemporary prophetic and temple teaching authoritatively defines the law's present application. A trustworthy doctrine of [[Chastity]] therefore combines moral seriousness with lexical care, historical honesty, protection of victims, and confidence in [[Repentance and Forgiveness]] rather than shame as a permanent identity.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
