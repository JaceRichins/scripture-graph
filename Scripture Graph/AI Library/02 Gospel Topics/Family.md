# Family

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, *family* is not one uniform technical term. It may mean a household of parents and children (Ephesians 6:1-4), an extended lineage or ancestral house ([[Numbers 27]]; [[Zechariah 12]]), a tribe or people designated as the “children of” an ancestor ([[Numbers 26]]; [[Joshua 19]]), a community constituted by discipleship ([[Matthew 12]]; [[Luke 8]]), or the whole human family descended from [[Adam]] (Mormon 3:20-22). These senses must not be collapsed. In particular, the many “children of Israel” entries in [[Ezra 2]], [[Nehemiah 7]], Numbers, Joshua, and Judges usually refer to corporate descent or national identity, not specifically to young children or a modern nuclear household.

In contemporary Latter-day Saint usage, family additionally carries covenantal and eternal meanings associated with [[Marriage]], [[Eternal Marriage]], [[Temples]], and sealing. [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]] presents sealing as binding disciples to God's family and to earthly families forever. That is a Restoration teaching; it should be identified as such rather than read without argument into every biblical use of household or kinship language. [[Family (Gospel Topics)]] also acknowledges conflict, divergent choices, and the continuing obligation to love and support family members.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon places genuine obligations within family relationships. [[Exodus 20]] commands honor toward father and mother (Exodus 20:12). [[Ephesians 6]] calls children to obey their parents “in the Lord” and commands fathers not to provoke children but to nurture them (Ephesians 6:1-4). [[Mosiah 4]] requires parents to provide food and clothing, discourage contention and sin, and teach love and service (Mosiah 4:14-15). [[D&C 68]] adds explicit Restoration duties to teach [[Repentance]], [[Faith]], [[Jesus Christ]], [[Baptism]], the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], prayer, and upright living (D&C 68:25-28).

These duties are not a scriptural promise that faithful parents can control every outcome. [[Agency]] remains individual, and Mormon 3:20-22 universalizes final accountability to every member of Adam's family. Nor does scripture make biological family loyalty absolute: Jesus defines a wider kinship among those who do God's will ([[Matthew 12]]; [[Luke 8]]) and warns that discipleship may divide households ([[Matthew 10]]; [[Luke 12]]). Those sayings predict and interpret costly allegiance; they do not command hostility toward relatives.

Current institutional teaching goes further in presenting family relationships as potentially eternal through sealing: see [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]]. Conference addresses such as [[The Family-Centered Gospel of Jesus Christ (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2025)]], [[His Arm Is Sufficient (Barbara Thompson, April 2009)]], and [[Priesthood Responsibilities (Elder Claudio R. M. Costa, April 2009)]] recommend home worship, practical instruction, shared work and recreation, and family-history participation. These are authoritative pastoral teachings within the tradition, but they are not identical in status or scope to canonized scripture.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
In the Old Testament, family is inseparable from descent, land, covenant identity, inheritance, and economic survival. [[Exodus 20]] embeds honor for parents in Israel's covenant law. [[Numbers 27]] and [[Numbers 36]] show both the force and limitations of a patrilineal, tribal inheritance system: Zelophehad's daughters receive an inheritance, while later regulation seeks to preserve tribal allotments. [[Psalm 68]] emphasizes divine care for widows, fatherless people, prisoners, the poor, and the solitary; [[Psalm 128]] presents a flourishing household as an ideal of blessing. [[Malachi 2]] makes marital covenant betrayal relevant to worship itself.

The New Testament retains household duties but places them under allegiance to Christ. [[Ephesians 6]] gives reciprocal, though not socially symmetrical, obligations to children and fathers. Jesus' call can reorder work and kinship ([[Matthew 4]]; [[Mark 1]]), create conflict within households ([[Matthew 10]]; [[Luke 12]]), and form a community described in familial terms ([[Matthew 12]]; [[Luke 8]]). The Gospels therefore add spiritual kinship without simply erasing biological bonds.

The Book of Mormon often narrates religion through households and peoples. Families face the temple together in [[Mosiah 2]], receive parental instruction in [[Mosiah 4]], flee together in [[Mosiah 18]], and bear the social costs of war in [[Mosiah 21]] and [[Mormon 6]]. [[Mormon 3]] expands family language to all humanity while preserving individual judgment.

Restoration scripture makes home instruction and age-eight ordinances explicit in [[D&C 68]]. Later Latter-day Saint teaching develops a temple-centered account of eternal family bonds. In recent decades, home evening, family prayer, scripture study, family history, shared activities, and home-centered gospel learning have been recurrent practices; [[Deep and Lasting Conversion to Heavenly Father and the Lord Jesus Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, October 2018)]] documents the modern home-centered emphasis. Development in practice should not be mistaken for proof that every current program is directly prescribed in ancient texts.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence is direct and textual. Exodus 20:12, Ephesians 6:1-4, Mosiah 4:14-15, and D&C 68:25-28 explicitly assign responsibilities within parent-child relationships. Their wording establishes that family conduct is a religious concern. It does not establish one exhaustive household model or guarantee particular outcomes.

Narrative evidence broadens the picture. [[Mosiah 2]] organizes worshippers by family; [[Mosiah 18]] portrays families fleeing danger; [[Mosiah 21]] and [[Mormon 6]] measure political violence in widows, fatherless children, and severed relationships. [[Zechariah 12]] depicts communal mourning enacted family by family. These observations strongly support the conclusion that scripture treats households as important religious and social units. They do not by themselves establish modern sealing doctrine.

The canon also supplies corrective evidence against idealization. [[Matthew 10]], [[Mark 13]], [[Luke 12]], and [[Luke 21]] anticipate betrayal and conflict within families. [[Matthew 12]] and [[Luke 8]] define kinship through obedience to God. [[Psalm 68]] centers people whose ordinary household support is missing. Together these passages make it difficult to equate righteousness simply with a conventional, harmonious household.

The keyword index requires aggressive disambiguation. The numerous hits in [[Ezra 2]], [[Nehemiah 7]], [[Numbers 26]], and Joshua largely arise from formulas such as “children of” and “according to their families.” These passages matter for descent, tribal identity, and inheritance, but raw frequency is weak evidence for the prominence of domestic-family instruction.

Modern addresses supply evidence for what Church leaders recommend, not controlled proof of causal effectiveness. The claims that prayer, scripture study, home evening, recreation, and family history strengthen families are pastorally plausible and experientially meaningful, but the provided materials do not quantify effects or show that the practices overcome every difference in safety, resources, health, or belief.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. What does honoring parents require when a parent is abusive, manipulative, or demands conduct contrary to conscience? Exodus 20:12 states the duty but does not map its limits; Ephesians 6:1's phrase “in the Lord” and the prohibition on provoking children are relevant but not a complete safeguarding policy.

2. How should disciples balance family loyalty with loyalty to Christ? [[Matthew 10]] and [[Luke 12]] refuse the assumption that discipleship always preserves household peace, while [[Mosiah 4]] and [[Ephesians 6]] strongly affirm family duties.

3. How much of the doctrine of eternal families is explicit in ancient scripture, and how much depends on Restoration revelation and temple interpretation? [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], [[Eternal Marriage]], and [[Covenants]] are essential to the Latter-day Saint answer; the highlighted biblical household texts alone do not state the full doctrine.

4. How should readers relate Exodus 20:5's intergenerational consequences to Mormon 3:20-22's individual judgment? The texts may address different dimensions—historical consequences and final accountability—but the relationship requires interpretation.

5. How should an ideal such as [[Psalm 128]] be taught without treating marriage, fertility, or household prosperity as a reliable measure of righteousness? [[Psalm 68]] and [[Remember What Matters Most (President M. Russell Ballard, April 2023)]] widen attention to solitary people, widows, the fatherless, friends, extended kin, and ward communities.

6. Which features of biblical and Book of Mormon households are enduring moral teaching, and which reflect ancient patriarchal, tribal, and economic arrangements? [[Numbers 27]], [[Numbers 36]], [[Malachi 2]], and [[Ephesians 6]] make this question unavoidable.

7. How far does parental accountability in D&C 68:25 extend when children exercise contrary [[Agency]] despite serious teaching and care? The revelation assigns duty and warns against neglect; it does not say that parents inherit guilt for every later choice of a child.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
A strong historical objection is that many family texts arise from patriarchal societies in which land, authority, and identity commonly passed through male lines. [[Numbers 27]] improves the position of daughters within a particular inheritance crisis, but [[Numbers 36]] still regulates their marriages for tribal property purposes. Such texts cannot simply be transferred into modern family ethics without asking whose interests the structures served.

A pastoral objection is that family-centered religious rhetoric can marginalize people who are single, infertile, divorced, widowed, estranged, LGBTQ, childless, orphaned, or unsafe at home. An idealized use of [[Psalm 128]] can intensify that harm. [[Psalm 68]] and [[Remember What Matters Most (President M. Russell Ballard, April 2023)]] offer broader resources, but they do not erase the lived disparity between the ideal and many members' circumstances.

A doctrinal objection is that eternal-family teaching is more explicit in Restoration ordinances and institutional interpretation than in the highlighted Bible passages. Appeals to biblical honor, household codes, or ancestral language do not independently prove that marriages and parent-child relations are sealed eternally. The honest Latter-day Saint case must identify its reliance on Restoration claims rather than suggesting that the doctrine follows unambiguously from the Bible alone.

A moral objection is that exhortations to obedience, unity, or forgiveness can be weaponized to conceal abuse and compel continued access to victims. Ephesians 6:4 restrains paternal conduct, and Malachi 2 condemns marital betrayal, but neither passage supplies a modern safeguarding framework. Protection, accountability, and lawful intervention cannot be replaced by slogans about family unity.

Finally, Jesus' conflict sayings resist sentimental accounts of family. [[Matthew 10]] and [[Luke 12]] acknowledge that truth claims and religious allegiance can fracture households. A critic may see this as sectarian pressure that elevates the religious group over intimate bonds; a believing reader may see unavoidable conflict produced by costly conscience. The text clearly anticipates division, but deciding when a rupture reflects fidelity, coercion, or human failure requires evidence beyond the mere fact of conflict.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
The supplied research corpus is weighted toward scripture and Latter-day Saint institutional or devotional sources; it does not provide a balanced bibliography of peer-reviewed historical, biblical, sociological, or family-studies scholarship. It would therefore be misleading to claim a detailed scholarly consensus from this material alone.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation normally reads the canon cumulatively: ancient commandments concerning parents, spouses, covenant peoples, and spiritual kinship are integrated with Restoration revelation concerning [[Baptism]], [[Temples]], [[Covenants]], [[Eternal Marriage]], and sealing. [[Family (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]] represent that institutional synthesis. Modern addresses then translate it into practices such as prayer, scripture study, home evening, practical instruction, recreation, family history, and mutual support.

Historical-critical scholarship typically begins by distinguishing the settings and social functions of individual texts: tribal inheritance in Numbers, postexilic genealogical identity in Ezra-Nehemiah, wisdom ideals in Psalms, prophetic marriage criticism in Malachi, Jesus-movement conflict in the Gospels, and Greco-Roman household instruction in Ephesians. That approach cautions against treating every occurrence of “family,” “father,” or “children” as one timeless system. The principal divergence is therefore not over whether the passages exist, but over whether later Restoration teachings are authorized fulfillment of earlier covenant patterns or a subsequent theological development.

[[Seeing God’s Family through the Overview Lens (Sister Tamara W. Runia, October 2023)]] cites James D. MacArthur's “The Functional Family,” but the underlying work was not supplied for assessment. It should not be used here as evidence for empirical claims without direct examination. A stronger future scholarship section would require primary academic sources representing biblical history, sociology of kinship, family systems, trauma and safeguarding, and Latter-day Saint temple theology.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
Begin with direct household commands: read [[Exodus 20]] with attention to Exodus 20:5 and 20:12, then [[Ephesians 6]] and [[Mosiah 4]]. Compare what each text asks of children and what it asks of parents. Add [[D&C 68]] to identify what is specifically Restoration teaching about instruction and ordinances.

Next study family as covenant, descent, and material organization through [[Numbers 27]], [[Numbers 36]], [[Joshua 19]], and [[Zechariah 12]]. Keep household, clan, tribe, and nation distinct. Sample [[Ezra 2]] and [[Nehemiah 7]] to see why “children of” is often genealogical or corporate rather than a reference to minors.

Then study Jesus' reordering of kinship in [[Matthew 4]], [[Matthew 10]], [[Matthew 12]], [[Matthew 19]], [[Luke 8]], [[Luke 12]], and [[Luke 18]]. Compare Matthew 10:35-36 and Luke 12:53 with Micah 7:6. Ask both what biological obligations remain and what allegiance to Christ can cost.

Study vulnerability and disruption through [[Psalm 68]], [[Mosiah 18]], [[Mosiah 21]], [[Mark 13]], [[Luke 21]], and [[Mormon 6]]. This prevents the household ideal of [[Psalm 128]] from becoming the only scriptural picture of family.

Finally, distinguish canon from later institutional application. Read [[Family (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], followed by [[His Arm Is Sufficient (Barbara Thompson, April 2009)]], [[Deep and Lasting Conversion to Heavenly Father and the Lord Jesus Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, October 2018)]], [[Remember What Matters Most (President M. Russell Ballard, April 2023)]], and [[The Family-Centered Gospel of Jesus Christ (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2025)]]. Mark which propositions are explicit scripture, which are official Restoration interpretation, and which are pastoral recommendations.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scripture treats family as important but never simple. Parents and children owe one another serious duties: honor, nonprovocation, nourishment, religious teaching, prayer, upright conduct, love, and service ([[Exodus 20]]; [[Mosiah 4]]; [[Ephesians 6]]; [[D&C 68]]). Marriage and family conduct bear directly on covenant faithfulness and worship ([[Malachi 2]]). Families can organize sacred assemblies, preserve identity, transmit records, and sustain life through crisis ([[Mosiah 2]]; [[Mosiah 18]]; [[Mormon 6]]).

Yet the canon does not identify righteousness with an untroubled conventional household. It attends to widows, fatherless people, prisoners, the poor, and the solitary ([[Psalm 68]]). It depicts families devastated by war ([[Mosiah 21]]; [[Mormon 6]]) and divided by responses to Christ ([[Matthew 10]]; [[Luke 12]]). Jesus forms spiritual kinship among those who hear and do God's word ([[Matthew 12]]; [[Luke 8]]), while Mormon speaks to the entire family of Adam and retains individual judgment (Mormon 3:20-22).

Latter-day Saint tradition integrates these themes with Restoration teachings about [[Covenants]], [[Temples]], [[Eternal Marriage]], and sealing. [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]] states the resulting institutional doctrine of potentially eternal family bonds. Modern leaders recommend shared worship, work, recreation, practical learning, and family history. Those practices may nurture connection, but neither scripture nor the supplied sources justify promising that faithful effort will eliminate conflict, estrangement, disability, poverty, abuse, or differences of belief.

The most defensible conclusion is therefore both affirmative and bounded: family relationships are major arenas of covenant responsibility, care, formation, and hope, but they are subject to God's claims, individual [[Agency]], moral accountability, and protection of the vulnerable. Eternal sealing is a distinct Restoration claim grounded in Restoration authority, not a conclusion established solely by keyword counts or ancient household language.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
