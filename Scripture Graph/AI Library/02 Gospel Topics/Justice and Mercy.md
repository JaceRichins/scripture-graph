# Justice and Mercy

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, **justice** is God's unwavering rightness in keeping his word, upholding moral law, judging persons and communities, and restoring consequences according to works and desires (2 Nephi 9:17; Alma 41:2-15). **Mercy** is God's compassionate action toward the undeserving, distressed, sinful, or estranged—especially forgiveness, deliverance, resurrection, and redemption through [[Jesus Christ]] (Psalm 86:5, 15; 2 Nephi 9:6-19; Alma 34:15-18).

The words are related but not interchangeable. Mercy is not merely leniency, and justice is not merely punishment. Micah 6:8 makes both human virtues: disciples must “do justly” and “love mercy.” Alma 41:14 similarly commands people to be merciful, deal justly, and judge righteously.

Translation matters. The King James Version's *mercy* represents more than one Hebrew or Greek term and can denote covenant loyalty, compassion, pity, or merciful action. “Mercy seat” in [[Exodus 25]] and [[Exodus 37]] names a sanctuary object and should not be counted as though every occurrence were an abstract treatment of mercy. The Psalms' repeated mercy language is genuinely important, but raw keyword totals overstate how often a passage analyzes the justice-mercy relationship.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon does not portray justice and mercy as rival deities or as defects that God must balance. Both belong to God's character and work. God fulfills his law and word (2 Nephi 9:17), yet is compassionate, gracious, forgiving, and abundant in mercy (Psalm 86:5, 15; [[Psalm 103]]).

The central Latter-day Saint canonical account appears in [[Alma 34]] and [[Alma 42]]. The Fall leaves humanity subject to death, separation, and moral accountability. Mercy cannot simply erase the work of justice; the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] makes the plan of mercy possible, and mercy claims those who exercise faith unto [[Repentance]] (Alma 34:14-16; Alma 42:13-15, 22-24). Alma's closing invitation preserves [[Agency]]: all may come, while refusal has consequences (Alma 42:27-30).

Other passages enlarge rather than replace this account. [[2 Nephi 9]] includes universal resurrection within the merciful plan. Mosiah 15:8-9 and Moroni 7:27-28 portray Christ as intercessor and advocate who answers the law's ends. Romans 3:24-26 presents God as both just and the justifier through Christ. Romans 11:30-32 places Jews and Gentiles alike under mercy, while James 2:13 and Alma 41:14 connect receiving mercy with showing mercy.

Modern Latter-day Saint teaching continues this canonical emphasis. [[Free Forever, to Act for Themselves (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2014)]] relates mercy to justice and moral agency. [[The Plan of Mercy (Elder James R. Rasband, April 2025)]] explicitly teaches from Alma 34:16 and Alma 42:15 that loving mercy does not make atonement or justice unnecessary. These talks are authoritative contemporary teaching, but they are not additional canon and should be distinguished from the scriptural texts they interpret.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
A responsible history begins with the texts rather than assuming that every era used one systematic theory. Exodus 34:6-7 supplies a recurring confession of a compassionate and gracious God who is also morally serious about guilt. Leviticus 19:15-18 joins impartial judgment with neighbor-love, while Micah 6:8 commands worshippers to do justice, love mercy, and walk humbly.

The Psalms develop especially the mercy side of this history. [[Psalm 25]], [[Psalm 86]], [[Psalm 103]], [[Psalm 118]], and [[Psalm 136]] praise or petition a God who forgives, hears, delivers, and maintains covenant loyalty. They do not ordinarily construct an Alma-like theory of how justice and mercy relate, so their importance should not be inferred from keyword totals alone.

New Testament writings place several aspects of the question around Christ and covenant history. Romans 3:24-26 joins redemption, justification, and God's being just. [[Romans 9]] raises objections about mercy, hardening, and accountability; [[Romans 11]] places Jews and Gentiles in common dependence on mercy while ending in humility before God's unsearchable judgments.

The Book of Mormon gives the relationship unusually sustained exposition. Mosiah 15:8-9 portrays the Messiah as interceding, bearing transgression, and satisfying justice. [[King Benjamin's address]] makes received mercy a reason for generosity to the poor. [[Alma 34]] connects Christ's sacrifice with faith unto repentance, while [[Alma 41]] and [[Alma 42]] answer Corianton's concern through restoration, Fall, probation, atonement, repentance, resurrection, judgment, justice, and mercy. [[2 Nephi 9]] adds universal resurrection and, in verse 26, special treatment for those without law.

Nineteenth-century Latter-day Saint writers systematized these themes; John Taylor's [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]] is a substantial example. Modern teachings such as [[Free Forever, to Act for Themselves (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2014)]] and [[The Plan of Mercy (Elder James R. Rasband, April 2025)]] continue to expound Alma's language. These later works document interpretation within the tradition; they are not independent evidence for the scriptures' provenance.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence is direct and textual.

1. **Explicit synthesis — very strong.** Alma 42:13-25 repeatedly names justice and mercy and relates them through repentance and atonement. Alma 42:15 states that the atonement permits God to remain both just and merciful. Alma 34:15-16 uses closely related language, saying that mercy can satisfy justice's demands.

2. **Related Book of Mormon formulations — strong.** Mosiah 15:8-9 portrays the Messiah standing between humanity and justice, bearing transgression, and satisfying justice. Moroni 7:27-28 connects Christ's advocacy and rights of mercy with answering the ends of the law. 2 Nephi 9:6-26 joins Fall, resurrection, judgment, justice, grace, and mercy; verse 26 explicitly says the atonement satisfies justice's demands for those without law. These passages establish literary and doctrinal coherence within the book, not independent authorship, historicity, or revelation.

3. **Biblical convergence — moderate to strong theological relevance.** Romans 3:24-26 holds redemption and gracious justification together with God's being just. This is a meaningful conceptual parallel to Alma 42, but it does not establish literary dependence, independent ancient origin, or one exclusive atonement theory.

4. **Ethical consequences — strong.** Micah 6:8, Mosiah 4:16-26, Alma 34:28-29, Alma 41:14, and James 2:13 connect receiving or seeking mercy with just and merciful treatment of others.

5. **Old Testament mercy formula — strong observation, limited inference.** Psalm 86:15 closely echoes Exodus 34:6 and related passages. This establishes a durable tradition concerning God's character, while leaving specific literary dependence, date, and authorship unresolved.

6. **Index concentrations — useful but weak doctrinal evidence.** [[Psalm 136]] strongly emphasizes enduring mercy, while [[Exodus 25]] and [[Exodus 37]] use 'mercy seat' as an object's name. Index counts can identify passages for inspection but cannot establish thematic importance or equivalence among different uses of 'mercy.'
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What exactly are the “demands of justice”: consequences intrinsic to moral agency, a divinely established law, God's own character, or some combination of these?
- Does atonement satisfy justice by substitution, representation, healing, victory, covenant restoration, transformed relationship, or several models at once? Alma's language constrains interpretation but does not supply a complete metaphysical mechanism.
- Why is repentance the stated condition of mercy in Alma 34 and 42, and how should that condition be related to unearned [[Grace]]?
- How do divine foreknowledge and hardening in [[Romans 9]] coexist with meaningful agency and accountability? Paul's argument asserts divine freedom more clearly than it resolves the philosophical problem.
- Does Romans 11:32 anticipate mercy offered to all, mercy encompassing both Jews and Gentiles, or universal final salvation? The verse supports the first two readings strongly but does not settle the third by itself.
- Are punishment and restorative consequences primarily retributive, educative, protective, or self-chosen? Different passages emphasize different aspects.
- How should statements that mercy can have “no claim” be read alongside invitations extended to all? Context usually limits such statements to persistent, knowing refusal, but questions about postmortal opportunity and [[Is hell eternal]] require a wider canonical study.
- How should communities combine mercy toward offenders with protection, restitution, truth-telling, and care for victims? Forgiveness does not automatically answer questions of trust, office, legal accountability, or safety.
- What does perfect justice mean amid apparently unequal mortal circumstances, disability, ignorance, trauma, and suffering? [[Infuriating Unfairness (Elder Dale G. Renlund, April 2021)]] addresses the pastoral problem, but many details remain matters of hope rather than disclosed mechanism.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objections should not be softened.

**The satisfaction problem.** If God establishes moral law, why can he not simply forgive? Requiring suffering before forgiveness can appear to subordinate God to an external rule or to portray punishment as valuable in itself. Alma's answer is that destroying justice would compromise God's own identity and the moral order (Alma 42:13, 25), but the chapter does not fully explain the metaphysics of that necessity.

**The substitution problem.** Punishing an innocent Christ in place of guilty people may appear unjust rather than just. The canon also speaks of voluntary sacrifice, representation, intercession, resurrection, healing, liberation, and Christ's power to claim believers (Mosiah 15:8-9; Moroni 7:27-28), so a crude model of involuntary third-party punishment is not the only reading. Nevertheless, atonement theories disagree about how these images cohere, and no brief formula eliminates the moral difficulty.

**Disproportionate punishment.** Phrases about endless misery or mercy having no further claim can seem incompatible with finite wrongdoing and perfect love. Contextual readings stress willful persistence, genuine invitation, resurrection, differing accountability, and refusal of offered mercy (Mosiah 16:12; Alma 42:27). That response reduces the charge of arbitrary exclusion but does not settle every question about duration or proportionality; see [[Is hell eternal]].

**Divine partiality.** Romans 9's mercy and hardening language can sound arbitrary, particularly when Paul responds with the potter's authority over clay. Reading the chapter in its corporate, covenantal, and historical setting broadens the issue beyond individual predestination, but individual implications remain debated. The objection is real because Paul raises it himself and does not provide a modern philosophical defense of freedom.

**Mercy can perpetuate injustice.** Religious appeals to forgive may silence victims, avoid restitution, or preserve unsafe power. Scriptural mercy does not require calling evil good: Micah 6:8 pairs mercy with justice, Alma 41:14 pairs mercy with righteous judgment, and Moroni 9 condemns brutality within the writer's own people. Personal release of vengeance, institutional accountability, reconciliation, restored trust, and legal consequence are distinct questions.

**The system may be internally constructed rather than revealed.** A critical reader may interpret Alma's justice-mercy scheme as a theological solution created by its author within a nineteenth-century Christian environment. The internal coherence and canonical parallels establish that the text has a serious theology; they do not independently prove its antiquity or divine origin.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship generally treats “mercy” as a translation cluster rather than one uniform technical term. Studies of Hebrew *hesed*, including Katherine Doob Sakenfeld's *The Meaning of Hesed in the Hebrew Bible*, emphasize relational loyalty and action as well as emotion. That work cautions against reading every King James occurrence as the same abstraction. Scholars also widely recognize Exodus 34:6-7 as a recurring scriptural formula echoed in texts such as Psalm 86:15 and Jonah 4:2.

Pauline scholarship agrees that Romans 9-11 must be read as a sustained argument about Israel, Gentiles, promise, election, unbelief, and mercy. It divides over whether election is primarily corporate or individual, historical or eternal, and conditional or unconditional. It also divides over the scope of Romans 11:26 and 11:32. Calvinist, Arminian, New Perspective, apocalyptic, and universalist readings illuminate different features but none should be presented as the uncontested meaning of the chapters.

Christian atonement scholarship offers multiple models: satisfaction or penal substitution, Christus Victor, moral transformation, representation, covenant restoration, and healing. The scriptural data used here contain elements compatible with several models. Alma's “demands of justice” language fits satisfaction readings particularly well, while resurrection, advocacy, liberation, and transformation prevent the dossier from reducing atonement to punishment transfer alone.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation usually reads [[Alma 34]] and [[Alma 42]] as revealed clarification of the [[Plan of Salvation]] and the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. John Taylor's [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]], [[Free Forever, to Act for Themselves (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2014)]], and [[The Plan of Mercy (Elder James R. Rasband, April 2025)]] represent this confessional trajectory. Literary scholarship, including Grant Hardy's work on Book of Mormon narrative, is more likely to emphasize speakers, audiences, argument, and narrative setting before constructing a systematic doctrine.

Believing and non-believing scholarship part most sharply over provenance and authority, not over the observable fact that the Book of Mormon develops a sustained justice-mercy argument. Literary coherence can demonstrate a serious and integrated theology; it cannot by itself prove ancient authorship, historical occurrence, or revelation.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with the Old Testament character formula: Exodus 34:6-7; then compare [[Psalm 86]], [[Psalm 103]], [[Psalm 136]], and Jonah 4:2. Ask what mercy means in prayer, covenant, forgiveness, and judgment.
2. Read Leviticus 19:15-18 and Micah 6:8. Establish that justice and mercy are obligations for worshippers, not merely attributes to discuss.
3. Read [[2 Nephi 9]] from verses 6-26. Trace Fall, death, resurrection, judgment, justice, grace, and mercy before isolating individual phrases.
4. Read Mosiah 15:1-12 and Moroni 7:24-28 together. Compare intercession, the ends of the law, Christ's claim, and the demands of justice.
5. Read [[Alma 34]] in sequence: sacrifice (vv. 8-16), prayer for mercy (vv. 17-27), care for the needy (vv. 28-29), and the urgency of repentance (vv. 30-35).
6. Read [[Alma 41]] before [[Alma 42]]. The restoration teaching supplies the immediate background to Corianton's concern about punishment. Diagram Alma 42's sequence: Fall → law → accountability → probation → repentance → atonement → mercy and justice → invitation.
7. Compare Romans 3:21-31, [[Romans 9]], and [[Romans 11]]. Keep Paul's Jew-Gentile and covenant argument visible when considering individual salvation.
8. Read Mosiah 4:16-26, Alma 34:28-29, Alma 41:14, and James 2:13 as an ethical test: does receiving mercy produce just and merciful treatment of others?
9. Only after the canonical work, compare modern interpretations in [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]], [[Free Forever, to Act for Themselves (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2014)]], [[Infuriating Unfairness (Elder Dale G. Renlund, April 2021)]], and [[The Plan of Mercy (Elder James R. Rasband, April 2025)]]. Mark clearly where each source quotes scripture, interprets it, or extends it pastorally.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scripture's strongest portrait is not “justice versus mercy” but justice and mercy united in the character and redemptive work of God. Justice means that moral agency, God's word, human harm, and judgment are real; mercy means that fallenness and sin need not have the final word. The Psalms ground hope in God's compassion and covenant faithfulness. Micah makes justice and mercy inseparable duties. Paul presents Christ as the means by which God remains just while justifying and places Jews and Gentiles together under mercy. The Book of Mormon gives the relationship its most explicit sustained exposition: through Christ's atonement, mercy does not destroy justice but claims the repentant and opens salvation to all who will come ([[Alma 34]]; [[Alma 42]]).

That synthesis has practical force. A person who pleads for divine mercy cannot consistently despise the poor, refuse compassion, or hide wrongdoing behind religious language (Mosiah 4:16-26; Alma 34:28-29). Mercy is not denial of harm, and justice is not permission for vengeance. Scriptural discipleship requires truthful judgment, protection of the vulnerable, repentance, restitution where possible, forgiveness, and humility about one's own dependence upon grace.

Several conclusions remain open. The canon supplies multiple atonement images without one exhaustive mechanism. Romans 9 does not dissolve every problem of hardening and agency. Romans 11:32 does not alone decide universalism. Mortal experience does not disclose how every unequal circumstance will be judged. Claims beyond those limits belong to theological interpretation or faith, not demonstrated observation.

What is well established is narrower and stronger: the canon treats God as both just and merciful; centers reconciliation in [[Jesus Christ]]; makes mercy universally needed but not morally trivial; repeatedly invites repentance rather than fatalism; and requires recipients of mercy to practice justice and mercy themselves.
