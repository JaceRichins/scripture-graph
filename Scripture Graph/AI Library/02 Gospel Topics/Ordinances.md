# Ordinances

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In the Bible’s King James English, *ordinance* is a flexible word. It may mean a divine statute (Leviticus 18:4), the prescribed observance of Passover (Exodus 12:43; Numbers 9:14), altar and temple procedures (Ezekiel 43:11, 18), or even a human institution (1 Peter 2:13). Consequently, not every keyword occurrence means a saving rite. The matches in [[Leviticus 18]], [[Numbers 9]], and [[Ezekiel 43]] are relevant but semantically different from modern Latter-day Saint usage.

In official Latter-day Saint teaching, an ordinance is “a sacred, formal act performed by the authority of the priesthood”; [[Ordinances (True to the Faith)]] further distinguishes saving ordinances as baptism, confirmation, Melchizedek Priesthood ordination for men, the temple endowment, and marriage sealing. This is an institutional theological classification, not a definition contained in any single biblical verse.

An ordinance is not identical to a [[Covenants|covenant]]. The ordinance is an authorized enacted form; a covenant is the relationship of promise and obligation with God that an ordinance may establish, signify, or renew. Nor is performance identical to divine ratification: D&C 76:51-53 distinguishes receiving ordinances from overcoming by faith and being sealed by the Holy Spirit of Promise.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canonical center is Christ. Articles of Faith 1:3 places obedience to gospel ordinances under the saving [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], while Articles of Faith 1:4 identifies baptism and laying on of hands for the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]] as the first ordinances. At Bountiful, Christ prescribes baptismal authority and form (3 Nephi 11:21-28). D&C 84:19-22 joins [[Priesthood]], ordinances, knowledge of God, and the manifestation of godliness.

The canon presents three distinguishable elements:

1. **Divine gift and command:** God appoints the ordinance and its covenantal meaning.
2. **Authorized administration:** Articles of Faith 1:5 requires calling and authority; authorized form also matters in [[3 Nephi 11]].
3. **Faithful reception and ratification:** outward administration does not substitute for repentance, enduring faith, covenant loyalty, or sealing by the Holy Spirit of Promise (D&C 76:51-53).

[[Alma 13]] adds a Christ-centered interpretation: priestly ordinances are types directing people to the Son of God, remission, and the Lord’s rest (Alma 13:16). [[Isaiah 58]] and Ezekiel 11:19-20 prevent ritualism by tying worship to justice and obedient transformation.

Official modern teaching extends the ordinance-covenant pattern through the temple. [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]] calls endowment and sealing essential parts of the covenant path, while [[Vicarious Work (Gospel Topics)]] teaches that ordinances for the dead remain ineffective unless freely accepted and covenantally embraced. These official formulations interpret and systematize several canonical passages; they should not be presented as quotations from one proof-text.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The canon portrays ordinances across changing dispensational settings. In the Restoration account of primeval history, Noah preaches faith in Christ, repentance, baptism, and reception of the Holy Ghost (Moses 8:24). In Israel’s exodus, Passover becomes an intergenerational memorial ordinance ([[Exodus 12]]; Numbers 9:12-14). The tabernacle system includes priestly consecration, sacrifice, washing, clothing, anointing, and ordered sacred space ([[Exodus 29]], [[Exodus 40]], and [[Leviticus 4]]). Ezekiel’s restoration vision again joins temple form, altar service, holiness, and covenant obedience ([[Ezekiel 43]]).

The New Testament presents both fulfillment and discontinuity. Ephesians 2:14-16 and Colossians 2:14 describe Christ overcoming a divisive or hostile legal order called “ordinances” in the KJV. These passages should not be generalized into the abolition of all Christian ritual: baptism, laying on of hands, and communal remembrance continue elsewhere. Likewise, 4 Nephi 1:12 says Christ’s people ceased Mosaic performances while maintaining Christ-centered worship.

Restoration scripture develops a more explicit ordinance system. [[Articles of Faith 1]] summarizes initiation and authority; [[D&C 84]] connects ordinances with priesthood and godliness; [[D&C 124]] locates washings, anointings, proxy baptism, solemn assemblies, and other sacred acts in the temple; and [[D&C 128]] connects proxy baptism with records, binding, and resurrection symbolism. [[Chapter 8 The Everlasting Priesthood]] preserves Joseph Smith’s insistence that divinely appointed ordinances must be kept in the appointed manner, though the historical record also shows development in how temple ordinances were administered.

Institutional access has not been socially constant. Before 1978, Church policy denied priesthood ordination and associated temple access to worthy members of Black African descent. Official Declaration 2 removed the racial restriction. The change is established; whether the prior restriction originated in revelation, human prejudice, or an interaction of institutional inheritance and fallible interpretation remains disputed. The subject should therefore be studied with [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]], not narrated as an uncomplicated history of universal access.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
**Strong textual findings:** Articles of Faith 1:3-5 explicitly coordinates Atonement, ordinances, authority, baptism, and confirmation. D&C 84:19-22 explicitly connects priesthood ordinances with the manifestation of godliness. Alma 13:16 explicitly interprets ordinances as directing worshipers toward Christ. These conclusions require little inferential reconstruction.

**Strong canonical pattern:** Exodus, Ezekiel, the New Testament, the Book of Mormon, and Restoration revelations repeatedly connect appointed actions, covenant identity, communal membership, sacred authority, remembrance, and transformation. This strongly establishes ordinance as a major canonical category, though the rites and terminology are not uniform across periods.

**Strong literary but historically limited evidence:** Moses 8:24 depicts Noah preaching recognizably Christian ordinances. This strongly establishes the Restoration text’s claim of gospel continuity before the Flood. It does not independently verify that the historical Noah used this vocabulary; a critical model can explain it as nineteenth-century Christian restorationism written into the primeval story.

**Important lexical correction:** “Ordinances” in Ephesians 2:15, Colossians 2:14, Leviticus 18, and 1 Peter 2:13 does not carry one technical meaning. The KJV word covers commandments, decrees, statutes, ritual prescriptions, and institutions. Arguments based on the English keyword alone are weak.

**Strong historical evidence with unresolved theological causation:** Official Declaration 2 documents the 1978 extension of priesthood ordination, and consequently temple ordinance access, without racial restriction. The documentary fact is firm. Competing explanations for the origin of the prior restriction remain materially different and should not be blurred.

Modern devotional claims—such as President Russell M. Nelson’s teaching in [[Christ Is Risen; Faith in Him Will Move Mountains (President Russell M. Nelson, April 2021)]] that ordinances unlock God’s power, or Tad R. Callister’s account in [[The Atonement of Jesus Christ (Tad R. Callister, April 2019)]]—are authoritative pastoral interpretations within the tradition, not independent empirical demonstrations of supernatural effects.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Which ordinance elements are permanent, and which forms may change by dispensation or prophetic administration? Exodus, 4 Nephi 1:12, and D&C 124 display both continuity and change.
- What exactly does it mean for divine power to be “manifest” in ordinances (D&C 84:20-21): experienced grace, covenant authority, revealed knowledge, ecclesial recognition, transformation, or some combination?
- What distinguishes valid administration, worthy reception, and divine ratification? D&C 76:51-53 suggests that these are related but nonidentical.
- How should one understand people who die without access to authorized ordinances? Mosiah 15:24 promises inclusion without specifying the mechanism; D&C 138:33-34, 58 supplies a later vicarious framework. See [[Death and the Spirit World]], [[Baptism for the Dead]], and [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]].
- How are women situated in an ordinance system whose public priesthood offices are restricted by sex but whose temple rites confer covenantal participation and sacred responsibilities? See [[Why do women not hold the priesthood]].
- How should believers evaluate changes in wording, sequence, access, and administration while also affirming revealed authority? This belongs with [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]].
- What standards make sacred privacy legitimate, and when can secrecy prevent accountability or informed consent? See [[Why do Latter-day Saints have temples and ceremonies they do not discuss]].
- How should the Church remember and repair the harms created when race restricted access to priesthood and temple ordinances before 1978?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
A strong Reformed or evangelical objection is soteriological: requiring authorized ordinances can appear to compromise salvation by grace and make institutional access or human performance a condition of divine acceptance. Ephesians 2:8-10 excludes boasting in works, while Ephesians 2:15 and Colossians 2:14 describe decrees, commandments, or a hostile written record being overcome through Christ. The strongest Latter-day Saint response is limited rather than conclusive: Articles of Faith 1:3 makes Christ's Atonement the saving ground; Ephesians 2:10 still expects transformed action; and the KJV word *ordinances* in Ephesians and Colossians does not contextually denote every Christian rite. This supports the coherence of the Latter-day Saint model but does not compel acceptance of it. Other Protestant traditions, as well as Catholic and Orthodox Christianity, maintain stronger sacramental accounts, so the objection should not be attributed to Christianity or Protestantism as a whole.

A historical objection holds that the breadth and changing administration of Restoration temple rites look more like developing liturgy than recovery of one invariant ancient ceremony. Documentary history establishes changes in wording, presentation, participation, and access. Believers may interpret development as continuing revelation governing stable covenantal purposes; critics may interpret it as human construction and adaptation. Historical evidence establishes development but cannot by itself adjudicate divine authorization.

An institutional objection is that exclusive authority claims can enable gatekeeping, social control, or exploitation. Korihor voices this concern in Alma 30:23-28. The narrative opposes his larger position, but that does not make the structural risk unreal. The pre-1978 racial restriction demonstrates that control of priesthood and temple access caused profound injustice within a community claiming divine authority. The documentary fact of the restriction and its consequences is firm; whether its origin was revelation, human prejudice, or an interaction of institutional inheritance and fallible interpretation remains disputed.

Proxy ordinances can also appear presumptuous toward people who rejected or never knew the faith. [[Vicarious Work (Gospel Topics)]] answers that proxy performance does not compel the dead: efficacy depends on acceptance, covenant faithfulness, and sealing by the Holy Spirit of Promise. This preserves agency within Latter-day Saint theology, although outsiders may still object to symbolic representation or to the underlying claim that authorized ordinances remain necessary.

Finally, ritual can become a checklist detached from conversion and justice. [[Isaiah 58]] presents that objection from within scripture itself: outward religious diligence can coexist with exploitation, conflict, and violence. D&C 84's high view of ordinances must therefore be read alongside prophetic demands for repentance, holiness, and care for others.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship generally treats the KJV category “ordinance” as lexically imprecise. Hebrew terms rendered as *ordinance* can refer to statutes and prescribed practices, while Greek terms in Ephesians 2:15 and Colossians 2:14 concern decrees or regulations; 1 Peter 2:13 uses language commonly understood as a human institution or created order. Standard lexical works such as HALOT and BDAG, and commentaries on Leviticus, Ephesians, and Colossians, therefore resist building one sacramental theology from the English keyword.

Ritual scholars and biblical historians commonly understand Israelite rites as embodied practices that form communal identity, order sacred space, preserve memory, and negotiate purity and atonement. Jacob Milgrom’s *Leviticus* commentary and Gordon Wenham’s *The Book of Leviticus* differ in theological commitments but both treat ritual details as integral to Israel’s symbolic and moral world rather than meaningless ceremonial residue.

Latter-day Saint scholarship emphasizes restoration, priesthood authority, temple theology, and covenantal continuity. Jonathan Stapley’s *The Power of Godliness: Mormon Liturgy and Cosmology* traces how ritual practice and cosmological understanding developed together. Devery S. Anderson’s *The Development of LDS Temple Worship, 1846–2000* documents administrative and textual change, while David John Buerger’s *The Mysteries of Godliness* offers a more critical institutional history. These works agree that Mormon liturgy developed historically; they differ over framing, theological significance, and how readily development can be harmonized with restoration claims.

Believing interpretation typically sees development as continuing revelation that preserves or unfolds divinely authorized covenant purposes. Non-believing scholarship normally brackets revelation as a method and explains change through leadership, social conditions, borrowing, adaptation, and institutional needs. Historical method can document dates, texts, influences, and practices; it cannot directly verify or falsify divine authorization. Conversely, an appeal to revelation cannot erase documentary evidence of change or the moral consequences of restricted access.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with [[Articles of Faith 1]] 1:3-5. Mark separately the saving ground, human response, named ordinances, and authority claim.
2. Read [[3 Nephi 11]] 11:21-40 with [[Baptism]] and the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]]. Ask what Christ specifies about authority, form, doctrine, and disputation.
3. Read [[D&C 84]] 84:19-22 beside D&C 76:50-53. Distinguish ordinance administration, divine power, faithful overcoming, and ratification by the Holy Spirit of Promise.
4. Read [[Alma 13]] 13:1-16. Trace how calling, ordination, typology, sanctification, Christ, and the Lord’s rest relate.
5. Study the biblical background: [[Exodus 12]] for [[Passover]], [[Exodus 29]] and [[Exodus 40]] for tabernacle consecration, [[Leviticus 4]] and [[Leviticus 18]] for ritual and ethical statutes, and [[Ezekiel 43]] for temple pattern and obedience.
6. Test ritual against ethics in Isaiah 58:2-7 and Ezekiel 11:19-20. This guards against treating performance as self-validating.
7. Examine transition after Christ in Ephesians 2:8-16, Colossians 2:14-23, and 4 Nephi 1:12. Check each use of “ordinance” contextually rather than assuming one definition.
8. Follow Restoration temple development through [[D&C 124]] 124:28-41 and [[D&C 128]] 128:1-18, then read [[Temples (Gospel Topics)]], [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Holy Temples, Sacred Covenants (Silvia H. Allred, October 2008)]].
9. Study proxy work through D&C 138:28-35, 57-59 and [[Vicarious Work (Gospel Topics)]]. Ask how teaching, mortal performance, agency, covenant, and ratification fit together.
10. Conclude with the historical tests posed by Official Declarations 1 and 2, especially access, authority, institutional preservation, and change. Pair this with [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]] and [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]].

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scripture does not use *ordinance* with one invariant meaning. Sometimes it names statutes, sometimes ritual prescriptions, sometimes human institutions, and in Restoration usage it becomes a defined category of sacred acts administered by priesthood authority. Careful study begins by classifying each occurrence rather than importing the modern definition everywhere.

Within Latter-day Saint doctrine, ordinances belong inside the gospel of [[Jesus Christ]]. The Atonement saves; faith and repentance orient the person to Christ; authorized ordinances enact covenantal belonging; obedience expresses continuing fidelity; and the Holy Spirit of Promise signifies divine ratification. These are related but not interchangeable. Neither authority without conversion nor private sincerity without the claimed authority exhausts the canonical model.

The strongest positive canonical portrait is embodied and Christ-centered. Passover makes deliverance communal memory. Baptism enacts death, cleansing, and new life. Laying on of hands joins initiation to the promised Spirit. Alma’s holy order points beyond itself to the Son of God and the Lord’s rest. Temple ordinances extend covenant worship across generations, including vicarious service for the dead. In this theology, ordinances do not compete with grace; they are appointed forms through which disciples receive, remember, and live within grace.

The cautions are equally canonical. Isaiah shows that religious observance can coexist with oppression. Korihor articulates the danger that leaders can use ordinances to preserve power. Historical changes demonstrate that ordinance administration is mediated through developing institutions. The pre-1978 racial restriction shows that claims of sacred authority do not make every policy or explanation immune from moral scrutiny.

What is established is that ordinances are a major organizing feature of Restoration scripture and present Church teaching, closely joined to [[Covenants]], [[Priesthood]], [[Temples]], and the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. What remains interpretive is whether claimed ancient and modern continuities reflect genuine restoration, inspired development, human construction, or some combination. Faith may affirm divine appointment, but intellectual integrity requires preserving the difference between textual observation, historical evidence, theological interpretation, and spiritual conviction.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
