# Testimony

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, **testimony** is broader than a feeling of religious certainty. It can mean a statement by a witness (Deuteronomy 19:15-18), evidence or a record that stands for or against someone (Deuteronomy 31:19-26), prophetic proclamation (Alma 5:44-46), an enacted covenant witness (Mosiah 18:8-10), divine attestation to Christ (John 5:31-39; 1 John 5:9-10), or a personal assurance attributed to the Holy Ghost (Moroni 10:4-5).

This range matters. In [[Numbers 17]], “the testimony” and “the tabernacle of witness” concern the covenant sanctuary and its sacred deposit; those keyword matches are not repeated examples of modern testimony-bearing. Likewise, “witness against” in [[Alma 5]], [[Job 16]], and [[Deuteronomy 31]] is judicial language, not necessarily a declaration of faith.

In current Latter-day Saint usage, testimony often means a person's belief or knowledge of gospel truth received as a spiritual witness. [[Witness (Gospel Topics)]] preserves both senses: testimony may be evidence or a statement, and a witness may speak from personal knowledge. [[Nourishing and Bearing Your Testimony (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, October 2022)]] emphasizes confirmation through the Holy Ghost and resulting changes in conduct. That modern emphasis is important, but it should not be projected onto every biblical occurrence of “testimony.”
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon presents testimony as both divine gift and human responsibility.

- God, Christ, the Spirit, prophetic words, scripture, works, ordinances, records, and human beings can all function as witnesses. [[John 5]] presents an especially cumulative pattern: John the Baptist, Jesus' works, the Father, and scripture bear witness concerning Christ (John 5:31-39).
- Personal spiritual knowledge is sought rather than merely inherited. Alma says that he learned through fasting, prayer, and manifestation by the Holy Spirit (Alma 5:45-46). Moroni directs readers to remember, ponder, ask sincerely with real intent, and exercise faith in Christ (Moroni 10:3-5). [[Knowing That We Know (Elder Douglas L. Callister, October 2007)]] expresses the modern teaching that another person's testimony may nourish faith but cannot permanently substitute for one's own.
- Faith can precede fuller witness. [[Ether 12]] says that witness comes after a trial of faith (Ether 12:6); [[D&C 17]] places the Three Witnesses' faith before their promised view and their public commission to testify (D&C 17:1-5).
- Testimony is meant to be lived. At the [[Waters of Mormon]], baptism witnesses a covenant expressed through service, commandment keeping, burden-bearing, mourning, comforting, and public fidelity (Mosiah 18:8-10). [[Stand as a Witness (Susan W. Tanner, April 2008)]] applies that pattern to home and community life.
- Testimony can be costly. Abinadi refuses to deny his words (Mosiah 17:20), while [[Revelation 12]] and [[Revelation 20]] connect testimony with endurance under lethal persecution. Revelation 12:11 grounds victory in the Lamb's blood and joins it with human testimony and fidelity; testimony does not replace the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]].

Official contemporary Church teaching identifies the Holy Ghost as the source of spiritual confirmation; that is a doctrinal claim of the tradition. A further interpretation—that any strong religious feeling is therefore revelation—is neither required by these sources nor safe. Deuteronomy's investigation of false witnesses, Proverbs' contrast between true and deceitful witnesses, and the canon's commands to discern all resist such automatic identification.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The canonical history of testimony develops without abandoning its earlier senses.

**Israelite covenant and law.** In [[Deuteronomy 19]], witnesses participate in public adjudication and can be investigated for falsehood. In [[Deuteronomy 31]], a song and the law itself become enduring covenant witnesses. In [[Numbers 17]], Aaron's preserved rod functions as a token against rebellion. These texts supply the legal and covenantal background for later religious witness language.

**Prophetic witness.** Prophets do more than report private conviction: their commissioned words call communities to repentance and may stand in judgment. The Book of Mormon explicitly casts Abinadi's death as sealing his words (Mosiah 17:20) and Alma's preaching as both inherited prophetic teaching and personally claimed knowledge (Alma 5:44-46). See [[Abinadi]], [[Alma the Younger]], and [[Prophets]].

**New Testament witness to Christ.** The Gospel of John organizes much of its christological argument around witnesses—John the Baptist, works, the Father, scripture, and disciples. [[1 John 5]] adds the Spirit, water, blood, and inward witness. Revelation then depicts Jesus as the faithful witness and his followers as maintaining testimony amid persecution (Revelation 1:5; 12:11, 17; 20:4).

**Book of Mormon covenant and experiential witness.** [[Mosiah 18]] makes baptism an outward covenant witness with communal duties. [[Mosiah 24]] makes remembered deliverance a basis for later witness. [[Ether 12]] orders trial before witness, and [[Moroni 10]] extends an invitation to seek personal manifestation by the Holy Ghost.

**Restoration witness.** [[D&C 5]] and [[D&C 17]] apply the multiple-witness pattern to the coming forth of the Book of Mormon. Their theological framing should be distinguished from the historical question evaluated in [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]], [[Witnesses of the Book of Mormon]], and [[How did the Three Witnesses live out their lives]]. [[D&C 62]] treats missionary testimony as accountable before heaven.

**Modern Latter-day Saint emphasis.** Sources such as [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[Conference Report, October 1925 (General Conference (full report), October 1925)]], [[The Weak and the Simple of the Church (President Boyd K. Packer, October 2007)]], [[Knowing That We Know (Elder Douglas L. Callister, October 2007)]], and [[Nourishing and Bearing Your Testimony (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, October 2022)]] increasingly foreground personal spiritual assurance and authorized witness of Christ. This is development in emphasis, not evidence that every earlier use carried the same meaning.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
**Strong textual findings.** The canonical texts unquestionably use witness language in legal, covenantal, christological, experiential, and eschatological settings. The convergence is especially clear across [[Deuteronomy 19]], [[John 5]], [[Mosiah 18]], [[Alma 5]], [[1 John 5]], and [[Revelation 12]]. It strongly supports a coherent family of ideas: truth claims carry accountability, may appeal to more than one kind of attestation, and ought to produce faithful conduct.

*A caution on method:* this convergence should not be overread. The passages above were located by searching for testimony/witness vocabulary, so finding that they share witness-related content is partly a function of the search itself. The more informative, less circular finding is the recurring *structure* — a truth-claim paired with a stated basis (perception, calling, covenant, manifestation) — recurring across independent literary traditions (Torah, Wisdom literature, the Gospels, Revelation, the Book of Mormon, and Restoration revelation) that did not have to converge on that particular structure.

The text also strongly supports the observation that testimony is personal but not merely private. It is taught, recorded, tested in community, enacted through [[Baptism]] and [[Covenants]], proclaimed through [[Missionary Work]], and evaluated by endurance and ethical fruit. The Waters of Mormon pattern is particularly concrete (Mosiah 18:8-10).

**Strong evidence for what sources claim; limited evidence for the claimed transcendent events.** Alma 5:45-46 securely establishes that Alma's narrated voice attributes its knowledge to fasting, prayer, and the Holy Spirit. D&C 76:22-23 securely establishes that Joseph Smith and Sidney Rigdon claim to have seen Jesus Christ and heard testimony concerning him — a vision, not a recorded two-way conversation. These texts alone do not independently establish the supernatural origin of either experience.

**Historically important but separately disputed.** [[D&C 5]], [[D&C 17]], and [[Ether 5]] deliberately invoke multiple witnesses in connection with the Book of Mormon. That literary and theological structure is clear. Whether the Three and Eight Witnesses (two distinct groups, reporting different kinds of experience) provide compelling historical evidence depends upon the wording, independence, circumstances, later recollections, and competing interpretations of their experiences. Those issues belong with [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]] rather than being settled by counting canonical uses of "witness."

**Interesting but inconclusive.** Parallels between biblical legal rules and Restoration witness structures show scriptural patterning. They do not decide whether the later accounts reflect divine fulfillment, sincere imitation of biblical precedent, literary construction, or some mixture. Likewise, coherence among spiritual-witness passages can establish the tradition's epistemology without publicly proving its metaphysical claims.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- When a speaker says "I know," what kind of knowledge is intended: direct perception, spiritual assurance, justified confidence, covenantal loyalty, or emphatic belief?
- John 5:31 says Jesus's unsupported self-witness "is not true," yet John 8:14 has Jesus affirm that his own record of himself is true — how does the Fourth Gospel's treatment of self-testimony hold together, and what does that imply for an ordinary believer's own first-person "I know" claim, which asks for exactly the kind of unsupported self-witness John 5:31 calls untrue?
- How can a seeker distinguish the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]] from emotion, expectancy, group influence, memory reconstruction, or confirmation bias?
- What should be concluded when a sincere person follows Moroni 10:3-5 or John 7:17 and perceives no answer? See [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]].
- How should conflicting spiritual testimonies across religions be evaluated? Sincerity alone cannot establish mutually incompatible propositions. See [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]].
- Does the two-or-three-witness rule establish truth, or only a responsible threshold for communal judgment? Deuteronomy 19:16-19 itself anticipates false testimony.
- How independent were the Book of Mormon witnesses, and how should visionary, spiritual, and ordinary sensory language in their reports be related? See [[Witnesses of the Book of Mormon]], [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]], and [[How did the Three Witnesses live out their lives]].
- What does Revelation's "testimony of Jesus" mean grammatically: testimony about Jesus, testimony from Jesus, or both (Revelation 19:10)?
- Can conduct corroborate testimony without proving every proposition it contains? The canon expects fruit, but morally admirable behavior occurs among people with incompatible beliefs.
- When is public testimony pastorally helpful, and when might strong certainty language pressure doubters or reward conformity?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
**Religious experience is not self-authenticating.** People in incompatible traditions report peace, certainty, voices, visions, and transformed lives. Naturalistic accounts appeal to expectation, emotion, social learning, cognitive bias, and memory. Because similar phenomenology supports contradictory conclusions, the experience alone cannot publicly determine which interpretation is true. [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]] states the challenge directly.

**The prescribed test may appear circular.** Moroni 10 asks for faith in Christ and John 7:17 asks for willingness to do God's will before confirmation. A critic can argue that these conditions prime the expected conclusion and allow nonconfirmation to be blamed on insufficient sincerity or faith. A fair reading should not use the conditions to dismiss every negative result; the passages state conditions but do not authorize outsiders to judge another seeker's heart.

**Multiple witnesses may not be independent.** Deuteronomy's rule is a legal safeguard, not a mathematical proof. Witnesses can share expectations, influence one another, rely on a common narrator, or describe different kinds of experience with harmonized language. This objection is particularly important for [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]], where the Three Witnesses (a visionary experience) and the Eight Witnesses (a reported handling of the plates) should not be treated as one uniform claim. Continued affirmation may count against a simple recantation theory, but it does not eliminate visionary, psychological, social, or literary explanations.

**An internal tension in self-testimony.** John 5:31 has Jesus say that unsupported self-testimony "is not true," while elsewhere in the same Gospel he affirms that his own record of himself is true (John 8:14). Interpreters resolve this differently — a provisional concession to his opponents' legal standards in chapter 5 versus a claim to unique self-knowledge in chapter 8 — but the tension is real and unresolved by either verse alone. It is worth naming because personal testimony ("I know these things are true") asks for exactly the kind of unsupported self-witness John 5:31 calls untrue, without the corroborating structure Jesus invokes there.

**Canonical testimony can be internally persuasive but externally underdetermined.** A scripture's assertion that God bore witness supports a description of that scripture's theology. Without independent access to the claimed divine act, citing the scripture cannot compel someone who disputes its authority.

**Strong certainty language can exceed the evidence.** Speakers may use "know" devotionally where "believe deeply" would be epistemically more precise. Such usage can unintentionally marginalize honest uncertainty. The counterpoint is that ordinary language permits knowledge claims based on personal experience and testimony, not only laboratory replication; the real dispute concerns whether the experience has been interpreted reliably.

**Good fruit is corroborative, not decisive.** Courage, charity, endurance, and reform make a witness morally serious, but comparable virtues appear in incompatible communities. Ethical fruit may support confidence in sincerity and practical value without proving every historical or metaphysical claim.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship broadly recognizes the legal and forensic background of witness language. Raymond E. Brown's *The Gospel According to John I–XII* treats the Fourth Gospel's network of witnesses as part of its sustained presentation of Jesus' identity. Scholars can agree that [[John 5]] constructs a cumulative testimony while disagreeing over the sources' historical independence and the theological conclusions readers should draw.

On early Christian testimony, Richard Bauckham's *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* argues that the Gospels remain closely connected to named eyewitness testimony. That thesis has been influential and contested; historical-critical scholars dispute both the identification of eyewitness markers and how reliably oral memory preserves events. The existence of testimony and the reliability assigned to it are separate judgments.

Believing Latter-day Saint scholarship commonly treats the Book of Mormon witnesses as mutually reinforcing historical evidence. Richard Lloyd Anderson's *Investigating the Book of Mormon Witnesses* is a major example, emphasizing their later lives and continued affirmations. Critical scholarship gives greater weight to visionary vocabulary, nineteenth-century religious culture, dependence among participants, and variations in recollection. Ann Taves's *Revelatory Events* offers a naturalistic, event-centered approach to the plates and other revelatory claims without reducing the analysis to a simple accusation of fraud.

These approaches overlap more than polemics sometimes suggest. Both can examine documents, chronology, relationships, wording, and later conduct. They part over background assumptions and explanatory standards: believing readings allow divine manifestation as a live explanation, while methodological naturalism restricts historical explanation to publicly analyzable human processes. Neither textual coherence nor psychological explanation alone proves or disproves divine action.

Within official Latter-day Saint teaching, [[Witness (Gospel Topics)]], [[Knowing That We Know (Elder Douglas L. Callister, October 2007)]], and [[Nourishing and Bearing Your Testimony (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, October 2022)]] emphasize personal spiritual confirmation and lived discipleship. These are authoritative witnesses to what the tradition teaches; they are not substitutes for historical or philosophical argument when the reliability of spiritual knowledge itself is the question.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with **legal and covenantal witness**: read [[Deuteronomy 19]], then [[Deuteronomy 31]] and [[Numbers 17]]. Classify human testimony, false testimony, written witness, and symbolic objects separately.
2. Study **the witnesses concerning Christ** in [[John 1]], [[John 3]], and [[John 5]]. List each witness and ask whether it is a person, work, text, divine voice, or first-person claim. Then compare [[1 John 5]].
3. Study **personal spiritual knowledge** in [[Alma 5]], especially Alma 5:44-46; [[John 7]], especially John 7:17; and [[Moroni 10]], especially Moroni 10:3-5. Note each passage's conditions and claimed source of knowledge.
4. Study **faith before manifestation** in [[Ether 12]] and [[D&C 17]]. Distinguish trust, action, sight, and the later duty to testify.
5. Study **testimony as covenant practice** in [[Mosiah 18]], [[Mosiah 21]], and [[Mosiah 24]]. Connect the outward witness of baptism with [[Service]], [[Charity]], [[Covenants]], and remembered deliverance.
6. Study **testimony under judgment and persecution** in [[Mosiah 17]], [[Revelation 6]], [[Revelation 12]], [[Revelation 19]], and [[Revelation 20]]. Ask what belongs to Christ's saving work and what belongs to the believer's response.
7. Study **Restoration witnesses** in [[D&C 5]], [[D&C 17]], and [[Ether 5]], then turn to [[Witnesses of the Book of Mormon]], [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]], and [[How did the Three Witnesses live out their lives]]. Keep canonical prediction, witness statements, and later historical recollections in separate columns.
8. Compare the scriptural range with modern usage in [[Witness (Gospel Topics)]], [[Knowing That We Know (Elder Douglas L. Callister, October 2007)]], and [[Nourishing and Bearing Your Testimony (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, October 2022)]].
9. Finish with the two major epistemic challenges: [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]] and [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]]. A mature theology of testimony must address both without questioning a person's sincerity merely because the result differs from expectation.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scriptural testimony is not one thing. It is truthful speech under accountability, evidence preserved in records and symbols, prophetic proclamation, divine attestation, personal spiritual assurance, enacted covenant, and faithful conduct under pressure. [[Deuteronomy 19]] supplies both the value of multiple witnesses and the warning that witnesses can lie. [[John 5]] gathers several witnesses around Jesus. [[Alma 5]] describes personally sought spiritual knowledge. [[Mosiah 18]] turns witness into covenantal community life. [[Ether 12]] places trial before witness, while [[Revelation 12]] joins human testimony to endurance grounded in the Lamb's blood.

The modern Latter-day Saint meaning—belief or knowledge confirmed by the Holy Ghost—is therefore genuinely scriptural but narrower than the canon's complete vocabulary. [[Witness (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Nourishing and Bearing Your Testimony (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, October 2022)]] express that official contemporary emphasis. The strongest canonical safeguard against a shallow account is that testimony must not remain mere assertion: it is linked with truthfulness, discernment, covenant loyalty, service, repentance, and endurance.

The evidence must nevertheless be graded. It is well established that these texts and modern teachers make the claims described here. It is also well established that religious testimony can shape lives and communities. Whether a particular spiritual impression, vision, miracle, or historical witness is correctly attributed to God requires additional judgment. Private certainty is meaningful evidence for the experiencer but normally weaker public evidence for someone without access to the experience. Multiple attestation can strengthen a case, yet dependence, shared expectations, and differing kinds of perception must be investigated.

A faithful approach need not pretend that testimony is laboratory proof; a skeptical approach need not assume that every witness is dishonest. The central questions are whether the witness is accurately reported, responsibly interpreted, independently corroborated where possible, ethically embodied, and open to correction. On the canon's own terms, testimony is a gift and a duty—but also a claim for which the witness remains accountable.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
