# Priesthood

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, “priesthood” does not always denote one uniform institution. The Torah presents a hereditary priestly ministry centered in [[Aaron (brother of Moses)]] and his descendants (Exodus 28:1; 29:9; 40:15), while Exodus 19:6 and 1 Peter 2:9 can apply priestly language to an entire covenant people. Hebrews uses priesthood chiefly to explain representation before God and the unique, permanent high priesthood of [[Jesus Christ]] (Hebrews 5:1-10; 7:11-28). [[Alma 13]] speaks of a holy order patterned after the Son, whose priests teach redemption and invite people into God's rest (Alma 13:1-12).

Restoration scripture gives the term a more systematic institutional meaning. [[D&C 107]] names Aaronic and Melchizedek priesthoods, assigns them differentiated powers, and treats Church offices as appendages to priesthood (D&C 107:1-20). Current official teaching defines priesthood as God's power and authority and distinguishes four related ideas: priesthood itself, ordination to an office, keys that direct its authorized exercise, and spiritual power associated with righteousness. See [[Priesthood (Gospel Topics)]], [[Aaronic Priesthood (Gospel Topics)]], [[Melchizedek Priesthood (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Priesthood Keys (Gospel Topics)]]. This four-part formulation is a Latter-day Saint synthesis; the biblical books do not present it in this exact vocabulary.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon's center is not institutional rank but divine service. Priests are called rather than self-appointed (Hebrews 5:4); they act in matters pertaining to God (Hebrews 5:1); and their ministry points ultimately to [[Jesus Christ]], whose priesthood and intercession are permanent (Hebrews 7:23-25). [[Alma 13]] similarly makes the order's purpose Christological and pastoral: its priests teach so that hearers may look forward to redemption and enter God's rest (Alma 13:1-12).

Restoration scripture teaches two priesthoods in the Church. The Aaronic is associated with preparatory ordinances and outward administration, while the Melchizedek holds the right of presidency and the keys of spiritual blessings (D&C 107:1-20). [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] narrates the conferral of Aaronic authority by [[John the Baptist]] in May 1829 (Joseph Smith—History 1:68-72). [[D&C 84]] joins priesthood reception to an oath and covenant (D&C 84:33-42), and [[D&C 110]] connects restored keys with gathering, the dispensation of the gospel of Abraham, and sealing (D&C 110:11-16).

Authority and divine approval must not be conflated. D&C 121:36-42 says rights may be conferred while heavenly power is lost through pride, concealed sin, ambition, or unrighteous compulsion. Its positive model is persuasion, long-suffering, gentleness, meekness, love, kindness, and pure knowledge (D&C 121:41-45). Thus ordination is neither proof of personal righteousness nor permission for domination.

Official contemporary teaching further says that people may exercise priesthood authority through authorized callings and assignments under priesthood keys. [[The Keys and Authority of the Priesthood (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2014)]] explicitly applies this principle to women's Church service, although the Church currently ordains only men to priesthood offices. That institutional distinction should not be represented as a sentence stated verbatim in ancient scripture.

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The Torah's dominant model is hereditary: Aaron and his sons are consecrated for priestly office, Levites receive assigned sanctuary duties, and Phinehas receives an enduring priesthood covenant for his descendants (Exodus 28:1; Numbers 3:5-10; Numbers 25:11-13). Ezra 2:61-63 shows that genealogical proof remained institutionally decisive after the exile. Exodus 19:22, however, refers to “priests” before Aaron's formal investiture; the text leaves their identity unresolved.

Genesis 14:18-20 introduces [[Melchizedek]] as king of Salem and priest of the most high God. Psalm 110:4 later joins royal and priestly imagery. Hebrews builds on those passages to argue that Christ's priesthood is not Levitical: Jesus comes from Judah, is appointed through a divine oath, continues forever, and supersedes a mortal succession (Hebrews 7:11-28). Academic and confessional interpreters agree on this textual movement while disagreeing about its implications for later church ministry.

The Book of Mormon presents priesthood language in more than one setting. [[Alma 13]] expounds a holy order patterned after the Son, while [[Moroni 3]] preserves a concise procedure for elders to ordain priests and teachers. Those passages should not automatically be forced into every later office distinction without argument.

The Latter-day Saint historical claim is one of restoration after apostasy. [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] narrates Aaronic conferral in 1829; later revelations articulate higher priesthood, quorums, offices, keys, covenants, and sealing authority. See [[Restoration of the Priesthood (Gospel Topics)]], [[Restoration of the Aaronic Priesthood]], [[Restoration of the Melchizedek Priesthood]], and [[Priesthood keys restored in the Kirtland Temple]]. Historical scholarship notes that this vocabulary and institutional structure became progressively more explicit during the early Church. Believing interpretation calls that progressive revelation; critical interpretation may regard it as institutional development later projected onto foundational events.

A grave later history concerns race. Before June 1978, Church policy withheld priesthood ordination and associated temple blessings from Black members of African descent. [[Official Declaration 2]] records the revelation ending that restriction for all faithful, worthy men without regard to race or color, but it does not explain the restriction's origin. The fuller historical and moral question belongs at [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]].
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
Evidence should be graded according to the proposition it can actually support.

**Strong textual evidence:** Hebrews 5 and 7 explicitly teach divine calling, distinguish Levitical succession from Christ's enduring priesthood, and anchor the latter in Psalm 110:4. D&C 121:36-46 explicitly subordinates priesthood power to righteous principles. These conclusions require little reconstruction.

**Strong evidence for canonical Latter-day Saint claims:** Joseph Smith—History 1:68-72 plainly records an angelic Aaronic conferral, and D&C 110:11-16 plainly records restoration of keys in the Kirtland Temple. These texts establish what the canon claims. Because they are sacred historical narratives rather than independent external corroboration, they do not by themselves prove the supernatural events to a reader who does not already accept their witnesses.

**Strong institutional evidence, limited historical explanation:** [[Official Declaration 2]] is primary evidence for the 1978 announcement, its revelatory framing, and the extension of ordination and temple blessings. It is not an account of how the restriction began and cannot carry historical explanations it never gives.

**Accepted observation, disputed interpretation:** Alma 13:3 speaks of preparation from the foundation of the world, divine foreknowledge, moral choice, faith, and good works. It fits Latter-day Saint teaching about [[Premortal Life]] and foreordination, but its syntax does not make every detail of that later synthesis indisputable.

**Useful but insufficient connections:** Luke 9:1-2 shows that Jesus delegated authority to the Twelve. Exodus 19:6 and 1 Peter 2:9 show communal priesthood imagery. Neither connection alone establishes modern offices, succession rules, priesthood keys, or a nineteenth-century restoration.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Major questions remain even after the canonical claims are mapped:

- How should Christ's unique priesthood in [[Hebrews 7]] relate to continuing ordained ministries? Does it authorize them, relativize them, or principally describe Christ alone?

- Does Alma 13:3 describe premortal choices, mortal qualifications foreknown from eternity, or a compressed combination of both? The passage supports foreordination language but does not eliminate grammatical debate.

- Who are the “priests” in Exodus 19:22, 24 before Aaron's formal consecration? The narrative does not decide among the leading proposals.

- How do communal descriptions such as a “royal priesthood” in 1 Peter 2:9 relate to specialized offices? A universal Christian vocation and ordained ministry need not be mutually exclusive, but the relationship is interpreted differently across traditions.

- What is revealed doctrine and what is later administrative synthesis in distinctions among priesthood, office, keys, authority, and power?

- How should believers morally and historically understand the racial restriction that [[Official Declaration 2]] ended but did not explain? See [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]].

- Why are women not ordained to priesthood offices even though current official teaching says women exercise priesthood authority in authorized callings? The relevant distinction and its contested adequacy require full treatment at [[Why do women not hold the priesthood]].

- What evidence could distinguish progressive restoration from ordinary institutional development in early Latter-day Saint history? Canonical testimony and historical reconstruction answer different kinds of questions.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest Christian objection is Christological: Hebrews emphasizes Christ's singular, permanent, untransferable priesthood, so a modern hierarchy called "Melchizedek Priesthood" may appear to appropriate what the text reserves to Christ. A Latter-day Saint response is that mortal ministers act only by delegated authority under Christ, not as rivals or successors who replace him. That response is coherent within Restoration theology, but Hebrews itself does not lay out the modern Latter-day Saint office system.

A second, more basic objection challenges the restoration premise itself: major Christian traditions (Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and in a qualified form Anglican) claim an unbroken chain of apostolic succession and priestly authority that never lapsed, making the entire concept of a needed "restoration" moot on their own terms. A reader from such a tradition would not concede that authority was ever lost; Latter-day Saint theology rejects this claim by pointing to what it identifies as a general apostasy, but a serious dossier must name the rival claim rather than assume the apostasy premise as given.

The strongest historical objection is developmental: early Latter-day Saint terminology and organization became more elaborate over time, while the canonical account of the 1829 angelic conferral was written retrospectively. Critics therefore interpret restoration narratives as later institutional legitimation. Believers answer that experiences can precede their complete vocabulary and that progressive revelation predicts growing articulation. Neither development alone nor retrospective narration logically settles whether the underlying encounters occurred.

The strongest ethical objection concerns exclusion. The pre-1978 racial restriction denied ordination and temple access on ancestry-based grounds, and the Church's canonized declaration ending it does not explain why it existed. Past speculative rationales cannot simply be treated as revelation because leaders taught them. Likewise, limiting ordination to men raises questions about equality, representation, and whether delegated authority under male-held keys answers the concern. Appeals to present practice establish institutional position, not a complete revealed rationale.

The strongest institutional objection invokes D&C 121 itself: a claim to valid conferral can coexist with unrighteous conduct, and religious authority can become a vehicle for coercion. The text's own answer is unusually severe—heaven withdraws when dominion is exercised unrighteously (D&C 121:36-42)—but identifying and remedying abuse still requires accountability rather than assuming officeholders will self-correct.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship broadly reads Israelite priesthood as historically bound to sanctuary, sacrifice, purity, genealogy, and the Aaronide-Levitical order. It also recognizes that the Bible preserves more than one priesthood idiom: hereditary cultic office, royal-priestly symbolism, communal priestly vocation, and Hebrews's christological use of Melchizedek. Harold W. Attridge's *The Epistle to the Hebrews* (Hermeneia, 1989) is a major critical treatment of Hebrews's scriptural and priestly argument. Confessional readings and historical-critical readings usually agree that Psalm 110:4 drives Hebrews 5-7; they divide over theological authority and how the argument should govern later ministry.

A specific critical model bears directly on the Aaronic/Levitical texts many claims here rely on: the Documentary Hypothesis attributes most of the priestly legislation (Exodus 25-31, 35-40; Leviticus; much of Numbers, including the Phinehas covenant) to a Priestly (P) source composed or heavily redacted in the exilic or post-exilic period, centuries after Moses. On this view, texts like Exodus 40:15's 'everlasting priesthood' reflect a later priestly caste's self-legitimating claims projected back onto the wilderness narrative, rather than a straightforward record of Mosaic-era institution. This is a mainstream, not fringe, position in biblical studies and should be weighed alongside the confessional reading that treats these texts as reporting Mosaic-era events directly.

Latter-day Saint scholarship treats [[Alma 13]], [[D&C 84]], [[D&C 107]], and restoration narratives as mutually illuminating components of restored doctrine. Official summaries include [[Priesthood (Gospel Topics)]], [[Aaronic Priesthood (Gospel Topics)]], [[Melchizedek Priesthood (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Restoration of the Priesthood (Gospel Topics)]]. These sources speak normatively for the tradition; they are not neutral histories.

Historical scholarship on early Mormon priesthood emphasizes development. Gregory A. Prince's *Power from on High: The Development of Mormon Priesthood* (1995) argues that offices, terminology, and retrospective accounts developed through the early years. Richard Lyman Bushman's *Joseph Smith: Rough Stone Rolling* (2005) places priesthood restoration within Joseph Smith's broader revelatory development. Believing historians can accept development as progressive revelation; critical historians may explain the same sequence through institutionalization, memory, and legitimation. The historical data demonstrate development more readily than they adjudicate revelation.

Recent Latter-day Saint teaching also broadens discussion beyond male ordination by emphasizing authorized participation under keys. [[The Keys and Authority of the Priesthood (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2014)]] and [[The Melchizedek Priesthood and the Keys (President Dallin H. Oaks, April 2020)]] are important official articulations. Critics question whether delegated authority without access to ordination and final governing keys constitutes equality; the official sources explain the current structure but do not eliminate that normative disagreement.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with Israel's institutions: read Exodus 19, 28-29, and 40; Leviticus 8; Numbers 3-4 and 25; then Ezra 2. Track calling, consecration, lineage, sanctuary duties, and the unresolved reference to priests before Aaron's investiture.

2. Study [[Melchizedek]] in sequence: Genesis 14:18-20, Psalm 110:4, Hebrews 5, and [[Hebrews 7]]. Mark which claims come from Genesis, which from the psalm, and which are Hebrews's typological inferences.

3. Read Hebrews 8-10 to see why priesthood cannot be isolated from covenant, sanctuary, sacrifice, and the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. Note the repeated contrast between recurring rites and Christ's single offering.

4. Read [[Alma 13]] slowly with [[Agency]], [[Premortal Life]], [[Repentance]], and [[Salvation]] in view. Separate its explicit statements from later conclusions about the chronology of foreordination.

5. Read [[Moroni 3]] alongside Moroni 2, 4, 5, and 6. Observe how ordination, the Holy Ghost, sacrament, baptismal community, and discipline function together.

6. Study Restoration claims in historical order: [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] at verses 68-72, D&C 13, D&C 27:8-13, [[D&C 84]], [[D&C 107]], and [[D&C 110]]. Then compare the official synthesis in [[Restoration of the Priesthood (Gospel Topics)]].

7. Study authority ethically through [[D&C 121]], especially verses 34-46. Compare its restrictions on compulsion with modern treatments in [[The Priesthood and the Savior’s Atoning Power (Elder Dale G. Renlund, October 2017)]] and [[Life’s Lessons Learned (Elder Joseph B. Wirthlin, April 2007)]].

8. End with the difficult institutional history: read [[Official Declaration 2]], then examine [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]] and [[Why do women not hold the priesthood]]. Keep canonized declarations, historical reconstruction, official policy, and moral evaluation distinct.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Priesthood across the canon is a family of related concepts rather than a single unchanged administrative chart. Israel's priesthood is chiefly hereditary and sanctuary-centered. Psalm 110 and Hebrews use [[Melchizedek]] to explain a priesthood grounded not in Levi but in divine oath and Christ's endless life. The Book of Mormon emphasizes a holy order that points people toward the Son, while Restoration scripture describes Aaronic and Melchizedek priesthoods, offices, keys, ordinances, covenants, and restored commissions.

The unifying theological claim is that legitimate authority originates with God and exists to serve, teach, administer sacred acts, and bring people to Christ. Hebrews 5:4 rejects self-appointment. D&C 121:36-46 rejects the more subtle counterfeit of valid office exercised through pride or compulsion. Conferral and heavenly power are therefore related but not identical.

The historical claims deserve calibrated treatment. The Restoration canon clearly testifies of angelic conferral and restored keys; that establishes the tradition's claim but does not independently prove supernatural events. Early priesthood organization demonstrably developed; progressive revelation and institutional construction are competing larger interpretations of that development. [[Official Declaration 2]] clearly ends the racial restriction but does not explain its origin, and current explanations of women's authority under keys do not dissolve disagreement over male-only ordination.

A faithful account can therefore be confident about what the texts say while modest about what those texts alone establish. Priesthood, in its best canonical expression, is authority under Christ disciplined by covenant, service, and righteousness. Its institutional history also contains development, exclusion, and abuse serious enough that claims of authority must be tested by the canon's own ethical standard.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
