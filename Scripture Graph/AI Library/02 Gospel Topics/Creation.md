# Creation

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, **creation** can mean several related things that should not be conflated: God's bringing the heaven and earth into an ordered world ([[Genesis 1]]; [[Moses 2]]; [[Abraham 4]]), the production or ordering of living creatures and humanity, God's larger work of making innumerable worlds ([[Moses 1]]; [[D&C 76]]), and the future renewal of creation ([[Isaiah 65]]; [[Revelation 21]]).

The texts emphasize divine agency, order, goodness, purpose, and relationship more consistently than physical mechanism. In Latter-day Saint tradition, creation is ordinarily understood as the organization of preexistent materials rather than classical creation out of absolute nothing (see [[Becoming Like God (Essay)]]). Abraham 3:24 and 4:1 describe "the Gods" organizing and forming existing materials; other texts (Moses 2:1; John 1) identify the Father as directing creation through [[Jesus Christ]] — a synthesis of these separate passages taught institutionally rather than stated verbatim in any one of them. D&C 93:29 also denies that intelligence was created or made. These passages still leave important metaphysical questions unanswered.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon presents God as Creator and creation as intentional and good (Genesis 1:1, 31). Humanity, male and female, is made in the divine image and receives responsibility within the earthly order (Genesis 1:26–28). The completed creation grounds sacred rest and the [[Sabbath Day]] (Genesis 2:2–3; Exodus 20:11).

The Son's role is explicit in Restoration scripture and the New Testament. God creates by the Only Begotten (Moses 1:33; 2:1), all things are made through the Word who becomes flesh (John 1:1–3, 14), and worlds were created through Christ (D&C 76:24). Current Latter-day Saint teaching synthesizes these texts as the Father directing creation through the Son; that synthesis belongs with [[The Godhead]], but no single creation passage gives the entire later formulation.

Creation also belongs to the [[Plan of Salvation]]. [[2 Nephi 2]] connects created opposition, [[Agency]], the [[Fall of Adam and Eve]], posterity, redemption, and joy. Romans 8:19–23 extends redemption beyond individual souls to a creation awaiting liberty from corruption. [[Isaiah 65]] and [[Revelation 21]] then use new-creation imagery for a world in which divine presence, joy, and life replace estrangement and death.

Official Latter-day Saint teaching commonly rejects creation ex nihilo and speaks of divine organization. [[Becoming Like God (Essay)]] places that position within the history of Christian doctrine. That institutional teaching should be distinguished from the narrower observation that Genesis 1:1 itself says God created the heaven and earth without defining the prior state or specifying a material process.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The canonical trajectory begins with creation as the ordering of heaven, earth, life, and sacred time in [[Genesis 1]]–[[Genesis 2]]. Later biblical writers reuse creation language rather than merely repeat the opening narrative: Isaiah grounds divine sovereignty in God's founding of earth and heavens (Isaiah 48:12–13), Psalm 24 grounds divine ownership in creation, and [[Isaiah 65]] turns the language toward promised renewal.

The New Testament identifies [[Jesus Christ]] with the divine creative work (John 1:1–3; Colossians 1:16) and connects creation with redemption. Jesus appeals to creation in teaching about [[Marriage]] (Matthew 19:4–8; Mark 10:5–9); Paul treats creation as both witness to divine power (Romans 1:20) and participant in future liberation (Romans 8:19–23); Revelation concludes with renewed creation and God's presence ([[Revelation 21]]).

Restoration texts expand this framework. [[Moses 1]] describes worlds without number, [[Moses 3]] states that things were created spiritually before naturally, [[Abraham 3]]–[[Abraham 4]] speaks of preexistent materials and organizing Gods, and [[D&C 76]] attributes the worlds to Christ. D&C 93:29 adds the assertion that intelligence was not created or made.

The later theological question of *creatio ex nihilo* must not be projected backward without argument. Gerhard May's *Creatio Ex Nihilo* traces its mature Christian formulation to second-century controversy, although scholars debate whether Jewish and New Testament texts contain earlier conceptual antecedents. Modern Latter-day Saint sermons add devotional applications: [[Pray Always (Elder David A. Bednar, October 2008)]] uses spiritual-before-temporal creation as an analogy for planning a day, while [[A Plea to My Sisters (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2015)]] calls woman's formation creation's culminating act. Those are later applications or interpretations, not additional wording found in Genesis.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence here is textual, and its reach should be stated precisely.

- **Very strong:** Genesis, Moses, Abraham, John, and D&C independently within the accepted Latter-day Saint canon make creation a major theological claim and assign Christ a creative role. This establishes what the canon teaches, not whether revelation or divine creation can be demonstrated by historical method.

- **Strong:** The repeated sequence and close correspondence among [[Genesis 1]], [[Moses 2]], and [[Abraham 4]] make comparison indispensable. Their differences—especially Abraham's organizing Gods and Moses's explicit Only Begotten—are real theological data. Literary correspondence alone does not decide whether those differences represent restoration, reinterpretation, or nineteenth-century composition.

- **Strong internal evidence:** [[2 Nephi 2]] sustains one argument through law, opposition, creation, enticement, Fall, redemption, and consequential choice. This supports deliberate theological integration; it does not independently verify Adamic history or a scientific model.

- **Moderate historical evidence:** The documented development of technical creation-ex-nihilo doctrine complicates claims that the mature formula is simply explicit in Genesis. Historical development does not make a doctrine false, and possible earlier antecedents remain disputed. [[Becoming Like God (Essay)]] points to Gerhard May's study for this question.

- **Weak as scientific evidence:** Attempts to map each creative period directly onto modern cosmology, geology, or evolution are highly model-dependent. Flexible correlations can be suggestive, but the texts provide no measurements by which such correlations could be tested decisively. The dossier should route that problem to [[How old is the earth according to the Bible]] and [[How do faith and science fit together]].
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean to be created in God's image: bodily resemblance, royal vocation, moral capacity, relationality, divine potential, or some combination? See Genesis 1:26–28, [[Adam]], [[Eve]], and [[Becoming Like God (Essay)]].

- Do the creative "days" describe ordinary days, long periods, visionary presentations, a literary framework, temple-patterned ordering, or something else? The texts contain sequence and sacred-time language but no modern physical measurements; see [[How old is the earth according to the Bible]].

- How should Abraham's "organized and formed" language relate to Genesis's "created"? Does it describe the same act in different terms, clarify it, or offer a distinct cosmological model?

- What are the relationships among uncreated intelligence (D&C 93:29), spirit creation (Moses 3:5), premortal intelligences (Abraham 3:22), and embodied mortal life? The relevant texts constrain answers but do not provide a complete metaphysical system; see [[Premortal Life]].

- How should believers relate Adam and Eve, Eden, death, and the Fall to evolutionary history? See [[Were Adam and Eve real people]], [[Garden of Eden]], and [[Fall of Adam and Eve]].

- Does Romans 1:20 support a successful philosophical proof of God, or only Paul's theological judgment about accountability? See [[Does God exist]].

- Is the new creation of Isaiah 65 and Revelation 21 restoration, transformation, replacement, or symbolic political-theological language? See [[Hope]], [[Resurrection]], and [[Millennium]].

- How do divine goodness and purpose relate to predation, extinction, natural disaster, and suffering within the created order? See [[Why does God allow suffering]].
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objections concern genre, history, science, and metaphysics.

**Scientific conflict:** A recent, six-ordinary-day creation and a biological origin of all humans from one first couple conflict with mainstream cosmology, geology, genetics, and evolutionary biology. A response that simply lengthens the days does not by itself resolve the order of events, common ancestry, death before humanity, or population-genetic questions. Less literal readings reduce some conflicts but raise questions about how Adam, the Fall, and scriptural genealogies function.

**Ancient literary setting:** Genesis shares motifs and concerns with ancient Near Eastern creation literature. Critical scholars may therefore explain it as Israelite theological literature shaped within that environment rather than as an eyewitness or scientifically revealed account. Believing readings can accept cultural setting while seeing inspired transformation, but cultural parallels alone establish neither revelation nor dependence in every detail.

**Multiple narratives:** Genesis 1:1–2:3 and Genesis 2:4–25 differ in style, divine naming, focus, and apparent narrative sequence. Source-critical scholarship commonly treats them as distinct traditions placed together. Harmonizing readings are possible, but they must address the wording rather than assert without argument that no tension exists.

**Creation out of nothing:** Classical Christians can argue that God's absolute sovereignty and several later biblical passages support creation ex nihilo even if Genesis 1:1 is not a technical philosophical formula. The historical lateness of a precise formulation does not refute it. Conversely, Latter-day Saint organization language raises its own questions about God's sovereignty, the status of eternal matter, and whether unexplained preexistent realities merely relocate the problem of origins.

**Natural evil:** Calling creation "very good" can appear difficult to reconcile with immense animal suffering, extinction, disease, and disaster, especially if these preceded human sin. Appeals to the Fall, mortal development, stable natural law, or eventual redemption offer theological frameworks, but none removes the emotional and philosophical force of the objection.

**Overextension of application:** Jesus' appeal to creation strongly governs his answer about divorce, but it does not directly answer every modern question about intersex conditions, same-sex relationships, abuse, civil marriage, or pastoral separation. Likewise, calling woman's creation the "culminating act" is modern sermonic interpretation, not the text's explicit ranking.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing and non-believing scholars often agree on several observations: Genesis 1 is carefully structured; it belongs in an ancient Near Eastern cultural and literary setting; it makes theological claims rather than speaking in the categories of modern science; and Genesis 1 and 2 differ in form and emphasis. They part over inspiration, historical reference, compositional history, and whether later scripture restores an original account or reinterprets an inherited one.

Gerhard May's *Creatio Ex Nihilo: The Doctrine of ‘Creation out of Nothing’ in Early Christian Thought* argues for the second-century crystallization of the technical doctrine. [[Becoming Like God (Essay)]] cites this work. The historical thesis is important but not self-interpreting: other scholars argue for earlier Jewish or New Testament antecedents, and an implicit idea can precede formal terminology.

John H. Walton's *The Lost World of Genesis One* emphasizes functional ordering and cosmic-temple context. That approach has influenced many believing readers seeking to avoid treating Genesis as modern material science, but important specialists dispute aspects of Walton's reconstruction and do not regard functional and material concerns as mutually exclusive.

Latter-day Saint scholarship gives special weight to [[Moses 1]]–[[Moses 3]], [[Abraham 3]]–[[Abraham 5]], and D&C 93. Believing scholars may interpret their organization language and premortal framework as revealed restoration. Critical scholars generally evaluate the same features in the setting of Joseph Smith's nineteenth-century intellectual and scriptural production. The textual observations are shared; the claims of ancient provenance and revelation are not established by literary comparison alone.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Read [[Genesis 1]] and [[Genesis 2]] separately. Mark sequence, repeated formulas, evaluations of goodness, human vocation, and Sabbath. Do not harmonize their differences before observing them.

2. Compare [[Moses 2]]–[[Moses 3]] verse by verse with Genesis. Note additions concerning the Only Begotten and spiritual creation.

3. Compare [[Abraham 3]], [[Abraham 4]], and [[Abraham 5]], especially Abraham 3:22–25 and 4:1. Track "create," "organize," "form," "materials," and "Gods" without assuming those terms answer more than they say.

4. Read [[Moses 1]] and [[D&C 76]] for the expansion from one earth to worlds without number and for the Son's role. Pair these with John 1:1–17 and D&C 93:9–10.

5. Place creation within the [[Plan of Salvation]] by studying [[2 Nephi 2]] alongside [[Fall of Adam and Eve]], [[Agency]], and [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. Separate the chapter's theological argument from questions of scientific mechanism.

6. Trace creation's ethical uses: Exodus 20:8–11 for [[Sabbath Day]], Matthew 19:3–9 and Mark 10:2–9 for [[Marriage]], Psalm 24:1 and 1 Corinthians 10:26 for divine ownership, and Romans 1:19–20 for natural revelation.

7. Trace creation's future: Romans 8:19–23, [[Isaiah 65]], and [[Revelation 21]]. Ask whether each passage depicts restoration, transformation, or symbolic renewal.

8. Only after the textual work, study competing historical and scientific models through [[How old is the earth according to the Bible]], [[How do faith and science fit together]], and [[Were Adam and Eve real people]]. Use [[January 12–18. "In the Beginning God Created the Heaven and the Earth" Genesis 1–2; Moses 2–3; Abraham 4–5]] as a curriculum companion, not as a substitute for comparing the texts.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Creation in the Latter-day Saint canon is not merely an account of beginnings. It establishes a world ordered by God, repeatedly pronounced good, inhabited by image-bearing women and men, and oriented toward sacred rest. It identifies [[Jesus Christ]] as the agent through whom the Father creates and expands the horizon to worlds without number. Restoration texts add distinctive claims about spiritual creation, preexistent intelligences, materials, and divine organization.

The subject then opens outward. Creation grounds the [[Sabbath Day]], informs Jesus' teaching on [[Marriage]], supplies Paul with a language of accountability, and becomes part of the integrated movement from [[Agency]] and the [[Fall of Adam and Eve]] to redemption and joy. Romans 8 and [[Revelation 21]] refuse to leave creation behind: the created order groans, awaits liberty, and is finally envisioned as renewed around God's presence.

What is established is chiefly theological and textual: God is Creator; creation is purposeful and good; humanity bears a distinctive divine relation and vocation; the Son participates in creation; and redemption has cosmic reach. What remains open includes the physical mechanism and chronology, the relationship of the narratives to ancient Near Eastern literature, the historical and biological setting of Adam and Eve, and the precise metaphysics of intelligence, spirit, and matter.

Faithful study need not hide those limits. Scientific concordances, historical arguments, and source-critical models should be assessed on their own evidence. The canon's most secure contribution is not a substitute physics but a theological vision: existence is gift and stewardship, mortal agency belongs within a larger redemptive purpose, and the end of the story is not abandonment of creation but its liberation and renewal.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
