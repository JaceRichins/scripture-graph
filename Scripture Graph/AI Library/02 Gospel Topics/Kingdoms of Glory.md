# Kingdoms of Glory

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In Latter-day Saint usage, **kingdoms of glory** are differentiated post-resurrection inheritances made possible through [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. The three named kingdoms are celestial, terrestrial, and telestial; the celestial is highest and is compared to the sun, the terrestrial to the moon, and the telestial to the stars ([[D&C 76]]; [[Kingdoms of Glory (Gospel Topics)]]).

Two distinctions prevent common conflations. First, the three kingdoms described in D&C 76:50-113 are not the same thing as the three “heavens or degrees” **within celestial glory** in D&C 131:1-4. Second, the biblical words *celestial* and *terrestrial* can simply mean heavenly and earthly. In 1 Corinthians 15:40-42 they occur in Paul's argument about resurrection bodies; Paul does not there name three destinations or use the term *telestial*. Restoration scripture adopts and expands Paul's imagery.

John 14:2 promises “many mansions,” meaning dwellings or abiding places in the Father's house. It is thematically compatible with differentiated heavenly reward, but it is not by itself a definition or enumeration of the three kingdoms. Official summaries such as [[Celestial Kingdom (Gospel Topics)]], [[Kingdoms of Glory (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Kingdoms of Glory (True to the Faith)]] state the mature Latter-day Saint doctrine more fully than this Johannine verse does.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canonical center is [[D&C 76]]. Christ's atoning death and universal saving intent are stated in D&C 76:40-43; the wider description of Christ's creative and redemptive role continues through D&C 76:22-24. Differentiated glory is therefore presented within, not in place of, salvation through Christ.

Celestial heirs receive Jesus' testimony, baptism, the Holy Spirit, and the Father's fulness; they overcome by faith and dwell with the Father and Son (D&C 76:51-70). Terrestrial heirs include honorable persons blinded by human craftiness, those who received Jesus' testimony only after mortality, and those not valiant in it (D&C 76:71-80). Telestial heirs did not receive the gospel or testimony of Jesus and suffer divine wrath before their eventual redemption; their glory nevertheless surpasses understanding (D&C 76:81-90, 98-112). The sons of perdition are treated separately (D&C 76:30-49).

[[D&C 88]] adds that each glory has a corresponding law; resurrected persons are quickened by the portion of glory they are able to receive (D&C 88:21-32). This makes character, desire, obedience, and capacity integral to judgment. The same revelation elsewhere speaks of a divine 'decree' governing the ordered timing of God's dealings with his kingdom (D&C 88:61), but that passage occurs within a distinct parable about visitation, so its bearing on how final kingdom-assignment itself is decided is not settled by the text (see Questions).

[[D&C 137]] expands the account of celestial inheritance: God judges the unevangelized by works and heart-desires, and children who die before accountability are saved in the celestial kingdom (D&C 137:7-10). Notably, D&C 76:72-74 assigns a similar unevangelized population to the terrestrial, not celestial, kingdom — a tension the text does not itself resolve (see Objections). Later teachings about [[Baptism for the Dead]] supply an ordinance framework, but that mechanism is not explained in D&C 137 itself.

Finally, [[D&C 131]] teaches three degrees within celestial glory and relates the highest to [[Eternal Marriage]] (D&C 131:1-4). The text does not license treating every use of 'saved,' 'celestial,' 'eternal life,' and 'exaltation' as automatically interchangeable; careful study should track each passage's own wording.

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The New Testament provides important antecedents rather than the complete later system. Paul contrasts celestial and terrestrial bodies and uses sun, moon, and stars to illustrate differing glory in 1 Corinthians 15:35-44. John 14:1-3 promises prepared dwellings with the Father. Other texts, including John 5:28-29, express judgment more simply as life versus damnation.

The decisive Restoration development came in the 1832 vision now recorded as [[D&C 76]], received by [[Joseph Smith Jr]] and [[Sidney Rigdon]]. It supplied the name *telestial*, detailed the inhabitants of three glories, and separated the sons of perdition from those kingdoms. Later in 1832 and early 1833, [[D&C 88]] linked differentiated glory to differentiated law and resurrected capacity.

In 1836, [[D&C 137]] addressed the unevangelized and children who die before accountability, widening the explicit account of who may inherit celestial glory. In 1843, [[D&C 131]] introduced or recorded the distinction of three degrees within celestial glory and associated the highest with the covenant of marriage.

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century publications systematized these revelations. [[A Compendium of the Doctrines of the Gospel]] reproduced the D&C 131 formulation; [[Discourses of Brigham Young]] preserved speculation that additional divine kingdoms might be innumerable, explicitly presented as uncertainty rather than canon. [[Conference Report, April 1922 (General Conference (full report), April 1922)]] illustrates later teaching that nearly all humanity eventually receives some glory while perdition remains exceptional. Current institutional summaries appear in [[Kingdoms of Glory (Gospel Topics)]], [[Celestial Kingdom (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Kingdoms of Glory]]. These summaries are authoritative expositions within the tradition, but they should remain distinguishable from the wording and historical setting of the canon itself.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong textual evidence

[[D&C 76]] and [[D&C 88]] explicitly teach celestial, terrestrial, and telestial outcomes — direct evidence within the Latter-day Saint canon. D&C 76:70-90 uses the sun/moon/stars pattern; D&C 88:21-32 supplies the law-and-capacity framework. [[D&C 131]] directly teaches degrees within celestial glory, and [[D&C 137]] directly teaches celestial inheritance for certain unevangelized persons and for children who die before accountability.

### Strong parallel, limited conclusion

1 Corinthians 15:40-42 supplies celestial-terrestrial terminology and differing sun-moon-stars glory. The parallel with D&C 76 is strong enough to support deliberate scriptural development, but it does not show that Paul articulated D&C 76's full taxonomy — his immediate argument concerns bodily resurrection, not a ranked destination scheme.

### Compatible but weak proof texts

John 14:2's 'many mansions' is compatible with differentiated heavenly places but neither counts nor names them. 1 Corinthians 3:15 distinguishes personal salvation from loss of reward but does not teach three kingdoms. These can participate in a cumulative reading but should not be advertised as independent proof.

### Canon-wide tension requiring interpretation

John 5:28-29 and Mosiah 16:10-11 use twofold life-versus-damnation language that D&C 76 elaborates rather than states.

### An internal Restoration-canon tension

A more concrete tension sits inside the canon itself: D&C 76:72-74 assigns 'those who died without law' and spirits in prison who received Christ's testimony only after mortality to the *terrestrial* kingdom, while D&C 137:7-9, given four years later, promises *celestial* inheritance to a closely similar population — the unevangelized who would have received the gospel 'with all their hearts.' This is a checkable tension between two canonized revelations, not merely a Bible-versus-Restoration gap. Plausible harmonizations exist (a stricter threshold of heartfelt desire in D&C 137; D&C 137 as a later clarification narrowing part of the D&C 76 population; or genuine development in revealed understanding across Joseph Smith's ministry), but the text does not perform that harmonization itself, and a careful student should hold the tension open rather than assume it is resolved.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does 'saved in the celestial kingdom' in D&C 137:10 specify a particular degree within celestial glory, or only celestial inheritance generally? [[D&C 137]] predates the formulation recorded in [[D&C 131]], and neither passage explicitly reconciles the terminology.

- How does D&C 137:7-9's celestial promise to the righteous unevangelized dead relate to D&C 76:72-74, which assigns 'those who died without law' and spirit-prison recipients of Christ's post-mortal testimony to the terrestrial, not celestial, kingdom? The two revelations describe closely similar populations differently, four years apart, and the text does not reconcile them itself.

- How are children who die before accountability related to the marriage requirement for the highest celestial degree? Later teachings propose postmortal or millennial opportunities, but D&C 137:10 does not explain them.

- What precisely constitutes being 'valiant in the testimony of Jesus' in D&C 76:79? The canon provides patterns of [[Conversion]], [[Covenants]], and [[Obedience]], but no mechanical threshold.

- Are D&C 88's kingdom-specific laws best understood as divine commands, intrinsic laws of a transformed being, or both? D&C 88:21-40 grounds glory in willingness and capacity to abide a law; a 'decree' mentioned later in the same revelation (D&C 88:61) occurs within a distinct parable about the timing of God's visitations, so it is not clear that verse bears on how final kingdom-assignment is decided, and it should not be treated as settling the law-versus-decree question.

- How should binary judgment passages such as John 5:28-29 and Mosiah 16:10-11 be related to the threefold scheme? The canon places both forms of description before the reader without an explicit hermeneutical rule.

- Does telestial redemption mean scriptural descriptions of hell are temporary for most people? D&C 76 supports that reading for telestial heirs, but the scope of phrases like 'everlasting punishment' requires separate study under [[Is hell eternal]].

- How much doctrinal weight should be placed on later speculation about innumerable kingdoms, as preserved in [[Discourses of Brigham Young]]? Historically interesting but neither clearly canonical nor a settled official extension.

- What is the exact relationship among [[Salvation]], [[Eternal Life]], celestial inheritance, and exaltation? Usage varies by passage, so assuming fixed synonyms can create artificial contradictions.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest biblical objection is that the detailed three-kingdom scheme is not plainly taught in the Bible. In 1 Corinthians 15, celestial and terrestrial can denote heavenly and earthly bodies, and sun, moon, and stars can illustrate differing splendor without constituting a map of three destinations. John 14's many dwellings likewise says nothing about three ranked kingdoms. On historical-critical premises, D&C 76 is a nineteenth-century theological development using biblical vocabulary, not the recovery of an unambiguous apostolic doctrine.

A second, more concrete objection is internal to the Restoration canon itself rather than external to it: [[D&C 76]] (76:72-74) places 'those who died without law' and spirits in prison who received Christ's testimony only after mortality in the terrestrial kingdom, while [[D&C 137]] (137:7-9), given four years later, promises celestial inheritance to a closely similar population — the unevangelized who would have received the gospel 'with all their hearts.' This is a checkable tension between two canonized revelations. It can be harmonized (for example, by reading D&C 137's heartfelt-desire standard as stricter than D&C 76's general 'without law' language), but the text does not perform that harmonization itself, and it should be presented as an open question rather than resolved silently.

A third objection concerns tension with texts presenting only salvation and condemnation. John 5:28-29 and Mosiah 16:10-11 appear binary, and some passages about hell sound final. The Latter-day Saint response — that these are broad summaries and D&C 76 supplies additional resolution — is coherent within an open canon but cannot be derived from those binary passages alone.

A fourth objection concerns grace and merit. Detailed population criteria can sound like graded salvation earned by moral performance. D&C 76 resists a purely merit-based account by grounding redemption in Christ (D&C 76:40-43), but it also assigns real importance to testimony, ordinances, works, and faithfulness.

A fifth objection concerns proportionality: D&C 76 assigns permanent differences of glory based on finite mortal choices, even though even telestial glory 'surpasses understanding.' D&C 88's capacity-and-law framework answers partly, but that response depends on accepting its revealed anthropology rather than independently demonstrating it.

Finally, naturalistic historians can situate D&C 76 amid early American disputes over universalism, heaven, hell, and postmortal reward. Such context can explain the revelation's questions without proving either divine origin or conscious borrowing; internal coherence and biblical resonance can support faith in revelation but do not, by themselves, establish it.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation reads [[D&C 76]] as revelation that expands biblical glimpses rather than merely repeating them. Official resources—[[Kingdoms of Glory (Gospel Topics)]], [[Celestial Kingdom (Gospel Topics)]], [[Kingdoms of Glory (True to the Faith)]], and President Oaks's [[Kingdoms of Glory]]—treat the three kingdoms as settled doctrine and stress Christ's Atonement, conversion, obedience, and reception of Jesus' testimony. Historical studies by believing scholars generally situate the revelation in 1832 while allowing its nineteenth-century questions to be the setting for genuine revelation.

Mainstream New Testament scholarship ordinarily reads 1 Corinthians 15:35-49 as Paul's defense of transformed bodily resurrection. Major treatments such as Gordon D. Fee's *The First Epistle to the Corinthians* and N. T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* do not treat verses 40-42 as an explicit statement of the later Latter-day Saint three-kingdom taxonomy. Raymond E. Brown's *The Gospel According to John XIII-XXI* reads John 14 within Jesus' farewell discourse and the disciples' promised communion with him, not as an enumeration of celestial, terrestrial, and telestial worlds.

Historians such as Grant Underwood in *The Millenarian World of Early Mormonism* and Richard Lyman Bushman in *Joseph Smith: Rough Stone Rolling* place early Restoration teachings in their antebellum religious environment. A critical model sees D&C 76 as creative development amid contemporary arguments about universal salvation and punishment. A believing model can accept the same historical setting while understanding it as the occasion rather than the sufficient cause of revelation.

The principal divide is therefore not over whether D&C 76 uses and expands biblical imagery—that is observable—but over the authority and origin of that expansion. Historical and literary methods can describe sequence, vocabulary, context, and conceptual relationships. They cannot by themselves adjudicate the divine-revelation claim.
<!-- SG:END scholarship -->

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with resurrection rather than a chart of destinations. Read 1 Corinthians 15:12-49 and [[Resurrection]]; identify exactly what Paul says about transformed bodies and differing glory.

2. Read John 14:1-3 and John 5:28-29. Keep the “many mansions” promise distinct from John's twofold judgment language.

3. Study all of [[D&C 76]], not only D&C 76:70-81. Trace the sequence from the vision of Christ and his saving work through perdition, celestial, terrestrial, and telestial outcomes. Note repeated criteria concerning testimony, reception, and works.

4. Read [[D&C 88]], especially D&C 88:14-40 and 88:61. Ask how resurrection, sanctification, law, willingness, and capacity relate.

5. Read [[D&C 137]] beside [[Final Judgment]], [[Death and the Spirit World]], and [[Baptism for the Dead]]. Separate what D&C 137 states from later explanations of how ordinances reach the dead.

6. Read [[D&C 131]] with [[Eternal Marriage]]. Keep its degrees within celestial glory distinct from the three kingdoms in D&C 76.

7. Test the synthesis against apparently binary texts: John 5:28-29 and Mosiah 16:10-11. Record whether a proposed reconciliation is explicit, plausible, or assumed.

8. Compare the canon with current official summaries: [[Kingdoms of Glory (Gospel Topics)]], [[Celestial Kingdom (Gospel Topics)]], [[Eternal Life (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Kingdoms of Glory]]. Then consult historical witnesses such as [[A Compendium of the Doctrines of the Gospel]] and [[Conference Report, April 1922 (General Conference (full report), April 1922)]] to see how exposition developed.

9. Finish with the adjacent questions [[Is hell eternal]], [[Justice and Mercy]], [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], and [[Plan of Salvation]]. These expose the doctrine's stakes more clearly than memorizing kingdom labels alone.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The three kingdoms of glory are explicit Restoration doctrine and only partially anticipated in earlier scripture. [[D&C 76]] is the controlling text: through [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], resurrected humanity receives differentiated glory described as celestial, terrestrial, or telestial, while perdition is treated separately. [[D&C 88]] interprets these outcomes through law, sanctification, willingness, and capacity; [[D&C 137]] extends celestial hope to the unevangelized whom God knows would have received the gospel and to children who die before accountability; [[D&C 131]] distinguishes degrees within celestial glory and connects the highest with [[Eternal Marriage]].

The doctrine's biblical roots are real but limited. Paul supplies celestial-terrestrial terminology and sun-moon-stars imagery in 1 Corinthians 15:40-42, yet his immediate subject is resurrection bodies, not a fully named three-kingdom system. John 14:2 offers the compatible image of many dwellings but does not enumerate them.

The canon also preserves unresolved tensions that a careful reader should hold open rather than assume are settled. Some passages describe judgment as life versus damnation; D&C 76 presents greater differentiation. More concretely, D&C 76:72-74 places the unevangelized and spirit-prison recipients of Christ's testimony in the terrestrial kingdom, while D&C 137:7-9 places a closely similar population in the celestial kingdom — a genuine tension between two Restoration revelations, not merely between the Bible and the Restoration. D&C 137 also promises celestial salvation to children without explaining its relation to D&C 131's highest-degree marriage requirement, and D&C 88 speaks both of capacity under law and, elsewhere in the same revelation, of divine decree.

A careful conclusion is therefore two-layered. Textually, the detailed doctrine is strongly established in the Latter-day Saint canon and accurately summarized in [[Kingdoms of Glory (Gospel Topics)]]. Historically, its full form develops through nineteenth-century revelations that expand earlier Christian language and, in places, develop unevenly even within themselves. Whether that development is divine revelation is a larger faith judgment; biblical parallels and doctrinal coherence can support that judgment but cannot compel it.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
