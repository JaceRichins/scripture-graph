# Book of Mormon Geography

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
Book of Mormon geography is the study of spatial relationships within the narrative and, more controversially, attempts to correlate them with modern American locations. The internal question asks where places stand relative to one another: [[Zarahemla]], [[Land of Nephi]], [[Land of Bountiful]], [[Desolation]], [[Sidon]], seas, wildernesses, and the narrow corridor between lands northward and southward. The external question asks whether that network can be identified on a present-day map.

These questions must remain distinct. [[Alma 22]] and [[Ether 10]] give substantial relative information, but no modern coordinates. The American [[Land of Bountiful]] must also be distinguished from [[Bountiful (Old World)]], the coastal place reached during Lehi's Arabian journey.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
Geographic reconstruction is not itself a doctrine of the Church. The Book of Mormon presents Lehi's destination as a promised land (1 Nephi 18:23; 2 Nephi 1:5), but those verses do not name a modern nation or hemisphere. The official [[Book of Mormon Geography (Gospel Topics)]] statement locates the narrative generally in the ancient Americas while expressly declining to endorse any specific model.

Accordingly, belief in the Book of Mormon's divine origin does not require accepting Mesoamerican, Heartland, hemispheric, or any other privately proposed map. Its stated religious purposes concern Jesus Christ, covenants, repentance, and the gathering of Israel; [[The Power of the Book of Mormon in Conversion (Elder Benjamin M. Z. Tai, April 2020)]] emphasizes living its precepts, while [[This Day (Elder Ronald A. Rasband, October 2022)]] treats geography as one possible reading lens rather than the book's governing message.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The ancient narrators use geography for practical purposes: journeys in [[Alma 8]], political boundaries in [[Alma 22]], defensive strategy in [[Alma 50]] and [[Alma 52]], migration in [[Helaman 3]], and the terminal division of territory in [[Mormon 2]]. Ether adds an earlier Jaredite layer in [[Ether 10]] and [[Ether 15]].

Since publication, readers have tried to place this landscape in the Americas. The early association of the New York hill from which Joseph Smith obtained the plates with [[Cumorah]] became deeply rooted in Latter-day Saint tradition, but the canonical passages do not give that hill a modern coordinate. By 1929, Church discourse already acknowledged substantial debate; [[Conference Report, April 1929 (General Conference (full report), April 1929)]] notes the recurring questions about Zarahemla and other locations.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century proposals increasingly divided between large continental readings and limited-geography models. John L. Sorenson's An Ancient American Setting for the Book of Mormon (1985) developed the most influential scholarly Mesoamerican model among believers; Brant A. Gardner later advanced a related historical reading in Traditions of the Fathers (2015). Other believers advocate Great Lakes, North American Heartland, Central American, South American, or hybrid models. None has become official. The present institutional standard is the caution stated in [[Book of Mormon Geography (Gospel Topics)]].
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence is internal. [[Alma 22]] distinguishes Bountiful and Desolation, lands northward and southward, seas, wildernesses, and a small neck. [[Alma 50]] and [[Alma 52]] show the corridor's military importance. [[Helaman 3]] describes northward migration, cement construction, scarce timber, and shipping. [[Ether 10]] places a Jaredite city near a narrow neck where the sea divides the land. [[Mormon 2]] later uses a narrow passage as a territorial boundary. Together these passages support an internally connected narrative landscape.

The internal map is not metrically precise. Alma 22:32's day-and-a-half journey is a real constraint, but speed, terrain, route, and endpoints are unknown. Helaman 3:8's four-sea language may be regional totality rhetoric rather than a survey of four separate coastlines. Terms such as small neck, narrow neck, narrow pass, and narrow passage probably concern the same strategic zone, but exact synonymy is not stated.

External correlation is substantially weaker. Sorenson's Mesoamerican work assembles ecological, settlement, warfare, and spatial parallels; Gardner develops a compatible limited setting. Such correspondences can make a model plausible, but flexible directional readings and the large number of possible comparisons create risks of selection bias. Mainstream archaeology has not accepted any Book of Mormon map or identified a site through an inscription securely naming a Book of Mormon person or polity. Geographic fit therefore supports a hypothesis only modestly and does not establish antiquity or revelation.

Population language also requires restraint. Alma 28:2, 10-11 uses large casualty totals, but those figures do not by themselves determine territorial scale. The same caution applies to the Ramah/Cumorah connection in Ether 15:11: it links Jaredite and Nephite narrative geography but does not locate the hill in modern New York or elsewhere.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Major open questions include:

- Do small neck, narrow neck, narrow pass, and narrow passage denote one feature, several routes through one region, or different features?
- What distance did a day-and-a-half journey represent under the conditions assumed in Alma 22:32?
- Are directional labels based on cardinal directions, coast orientation, culturally defined directions, or translation conventions?
- Does Helaman 3:8 describe literal coastlines, regional seas, or rhetorical totality?
- Is the narrative [[Cumorah]] the New York hill, or did the traditional name transfer to the recovery site?
- How should demographic claims in [[Alma 28]] and military narratives constrain a map?
- Can any proposal explain the entire internal network without repeatedly redefining terms or selecting only favorable passages?
- What external discovery would distinguish a genuine identification from a plausible resemblance?

These questions are examined most directly alongside [[Where did the Book of Mormon take place]], [[How large were Book of Mormon populations]], and [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]].
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest critical objection is not merely that one proposed map has difficulties. It is that, after extensive searching, no proposed geography has produced an identification broadly accepted by archaeologists, historians, linguists, and geographers outside confessional scholarship. Michael D. Coe's “Mormons and Archaeology: An Outside View” argues that the expected archaeological confirmation has not appeared. Critics also contend that candidate maps often adjust directional terms, distances, coastlines, and cultural correspondences after the fact, making the models difficult to falsify.

A second objection is literary: coherent relative geography does not prove historicity. Authors of fiction can create consistent maps, and Mormon's editorial narration could impose consistency on diverse episodes. Therefore internal coherence has evidentiary value but cannot bear the larger historical conclusion by itself.

A strong believing response is that older hemispheric expectations may ask the text to describe every Indigenous population and the entire American continent when it may depict a limited regional society. Names can disappear, archaeology is incomplete, and modest migrant populations may not produce an obvious genetic or material signature. This response weakens some sweeping objections, especially those addressed by [[Does DNA evidence disprove the Book of Mormon]] and [[What is the Book of Mormon's view of Native American origins]]. It does not, however, positively identify a location.

The New York Cumorah objection cuts both ways. A single-Cumorah reading preserves traditional simplicity but makes many limited Mesoamerican reconstructions geographically difficult. A two-Cumorah reading relieves that distance problem but requires an unstated transfer of the name and therefore remains hypothetical. Neither model should be represented as explicit scripture.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing scholarship commonly begins by reconstructing the internal map and then testing limited American settings. John L. Sorenson's An Ancient American Setting for the Book of Mormon and Mormon's Codex argue extensively for Mesoamerica; Brant A. Gardner's Traditions of the Fathers uses a similar setting while emphasizing cultural context. Their strongest contribution is methodological: individual parallels must fit a connected system of travel, ecology, settlement, chronology, and political geography.

Other believing writers defend North American Heartland or Great Lakes models, often giving greater weight to the New York [[Cumorah]], nineteenth-century statements, or North American archaeological features. These schools disagree not only about locations but about which textual statements are rigid constraints and how much authority later tradition carries.

Critical and mainstream archaeological assessments generally do not treat the Book of Mormon as an established ancient American source. They emphasize the absence of securely deciphered Book of Mormon names, the lack of an agreed archaeological horizon, and broader questions collected under [[What about horses, steel and other anachronisms in the Book of Mormon]]. Believing scholars answer that loan shifts, preservation limits, limited geography, and incomplete excavation can reduce expected visibility. Those answers are possible in varying degrees, but possibility is not independent confirmation.

The central divide is therefore evidentiary rather than merely cartographic. Believing models often ask whether a setting is compatible with the text and known cultures; critical scholarship asks whether evidence independently selects the Book of Mormon explanation over ordinary alternatives. Compatibility can make a model worth studying, but independent selection is the higher historical standard.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
Begin with the internal text before viewing any proposed map.

1. Read [[Alma 22]], especially Alma 22:27-34, and list only explicit relationships. Separate the narrator's statements from distances inferred by a modern mapmaker.
2. Read [[Alma 50]] and [[Alma 52]] for east/west seas, Sidon, Bountiful, and the military function of the narrow pass.
3. Read [[Alma 8]] for the Zarahemla-Melek-Ammonihah route, then test whether a proposed map preserves every stated relationship.
4. Read [[Helaman 3]] and [[Alma 63]] for migration, resources, shipping, and the land northward. Do not turn Hagoth's missing voyages into a named modern destination the text never supplies.
5. Read [[Ether 10]] and [[Ether 15]] for Jaredite geography, followed by [[Mormon 2]] and [[Mormon 6]] for the late Nephite boundary and [[Cumorah]].
6. Consult [[Book of Mormon Geography (Gospel Topics)]] before treating any private model as a Church position.
7. Compare at least one developed Mesoamerican proposal, one developed North American proposal, and a critical archaeological assessment. Record where each model follows the text directly, where it interprets ambiguity, and what evidence could falsify it.
8. Continue through [[Where did the Book of Mormon take place]], [[How large were Book of Mormon populations]], and [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]] so geography is not asked to settle questions it cannot settle alone.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The Book of Mormon contains a meaningful internal geography. Lands northward and southward, Bountiful, Desolation, Sidon, seas, wildernesses, routes, and a narrow strategic corridor recur across different narratives. This is observation. Reading all narrow-feature terms as one corridor and constructing a detailed map from them is interpretation. Treating the map's coherence as evidence of careful composition is reasonable; treating it as proof of ancient American history is an overreach.

No current external model is established. Mesoamerican limited-geography scholarship offers the most extensively developed correlation within believing academic work, while North American models preserve other textual or traditional priorities. Each depends on contestable decisions about directions, distances, terminology, population scale, and [[Cumorah]]. Mainstream archaeology has not independently confirmed any of them.

The most responsible conclusion matches [[Book of Mormon Geography (Gospel Topics)]]: the Church affirms an ancient American setting in general but does not identify specific locations. A believer may regard the record as historical without claiming a revealed map. A skeptic may reasonably find present external corroboration inadequate without pretending that every conceivable limited setting has been disproved. What is established is the text's relative geographic network; what remains open is its modern location; what exceeds the evidence is certainty about a favored map.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
