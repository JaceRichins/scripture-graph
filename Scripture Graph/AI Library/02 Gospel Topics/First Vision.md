# First Vision

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
The **First Vision** is the traditional Latter-day Saint name for [[Joseph Smith Jr]]’s reported encounter with God the Father and Jesus Christ while praying in woods in the spring of 1820. The event-title is traditional rather than language used inside the narrative. The canonized account is Joseph Smith—History 1:5-26, with the vision itself concentrated in Joseph Smith—History 1:14-20.

The wooded setting is traditionally called the [[Sacred Grove]], although that proper name does not appear in the canonical verses. In Church teaching, the event is both a personal answer to prayer and the opening revelation of the [[Restoration of the Gospel]]. Those two meanings should remain distinguishable: the text first presents a young person seeking wisdom and forgiveness, while later institutional interpretation identifies the event as the beginning of a new dispensation.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
### What the canon directly says

In [[Joseph Smith—History 1]], Joseph confronts competing interpretations of Christianity, reads James 1:5, and decides to ask God (Joseph Smith—History 1:5-14). He reports hostile opposition as he begins praying, followed by deliverance and a pillar of light (Joseph Smith—History 1:15-17). He then sees two personages; one introduces the other as his Beloved Son and commands Joseph to hear him (Joseph Smith—History 1:17). In response to Joseph’s denominational question, he is told to join none of the existing sects (Joseph Smith—History 1:18-20).

### Official Latter-day Saint teaching

Official teaching draws several doctrines from the account: the Father and Son are personally distinguishable; they know and answer individuals; revelation did not end with the Bible; and the existing religious world required a divine restoration. See [[The Godhead]], [[Prayer]], [[Revelation]], [[Apostasy]], [[Chapter 1 The First Vision The Father and the Son Appear to Joseph Smith]], and [[Chapter 10 The Divine Calling of the Prophet Joseph Smith]].

Some customary conclusions go beyond what this passage alone states. Joseph Smith—History 1:17 visually and verbally distinguishes the Father and Son, but it does not by itself define every aspect of their embodiment, unity, or relationship. Likewise, calling the experience the formal beginning of the last dispensation is an authoritative Restoration interpretation, not wording found in the vision narrative.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
Ancient scripture supplies patterns through which Latter-day Saints understand the event: God grants wisdom in [[James 1]], the Father identifies the Son in [[Matthew 3]], prophetic callings include overwhelming manifestations, and witnesses such as Paul defend a heavenly encounter in [[Acts 9]]. These are illuminating parallels, not historical evidence that Joseph Smith’s report occurred.

For the modern event, the canonical account retrospectively locates the prayer in spring 1820. The earliest extant firsthand narration is the 1832 history. Further primary accounts followed in 1835, 1838, and 1842; [[Chapter 38 The Wentworth Letter]] represents the 1842 public summary. The 1838-era history eventually became [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] and thus the Church’s authoritative scriptural narration. [[First Vision Accounts (Gospel Topics)]], [[First Vision Accounts (Essay)]], and [[02 Hear Him]] provide routes into these documents.

The vision’s prominence also developed historically. Its comparatively limited documentation and public circulation in the earliest years is acknowledged across much modern scholarship; later canonization, missionary teaching, art, and prophetic exposition made it a defining Restoration narrative. That development can be understood as increasing public articulation of an earlier private experience, as institutional elevation of a founding memory, or—critically—as evidence of retrospective narrative construction. Documentary history alone does not choose conclusively among those interpretations.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest documentary conclusions are limited but important:

- **Very strong:** Joseph Smith produced or supervised four primary accounts dated 1832, 1835, 1838, and 1842. The 1838-era account became canonical. See [[First Vision Accounts (Gospel Topics)]] and the Joseph Smith Papers collection *Joseph Smith’s Accounts of the First Vision*.
- **Strong:** The accounts share a core of religious distress, prayer, a reported heavenly manifestation, and a consequential divine answer. This establishes continuity in Joseph’s claim across the surviving firsthand narratives.
- **Strong:** The accounts also contain real differences of selection and emphasis. Their descriptions of the divine figures, forgiveness, churches, and angels are not verbally identical. Audience, genre, compression, and ordinary memory are plausible explanations for many differences; critical developmental explanations remain plausible as well.
- **Strong but limited in scope:** Joseph Smith—History 1:17 establishes how authoritative Latter-day Saint scripture narrates the Father and Son. It is not an independent 1820 record.
- **Moderate and disputed:** Broad religious ferment in upstate New York fits the narrative’s world, but the precise timing and local profile of the revival language have been debated since Wesley P. Walters’s 1969 critique and Milton V. Backman Jr.’s historical response.

The primary accounts all ultimately depend on Joseph Smith; they are not four independent eyewitnesses. No known contemporaneous 1820 record documents the manifestation. Conversely, the absence of such a record does not demonstrate invention: a private experience of an obscure adolescent might leave no immediate paper trail. Historical evidence can assess when and how the story was recorded, but not directly test the divine identity of beings reported in a private vision.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What is the earliest recoverable form of Joseph Smith’s memory, and how much can the 1832 manuscript tell us about an experience reported twelve years earlier?
- Does the 1832 account’s explicit focus on “the Lord,” compared with later accounts’ clearer descriptions of two personages, reflect compression, different narrative purpose, developing memory, developing theology, or some combination?
- How much of the language in Joseph Smith—History 1 records exact remembered speech, and how much expresses the experience through later King James scriptural language such as 2 Timothy 3:5?
- What local religious activity best explains Joseph’s description of unusual denominational excitement around 1820?
- Why was the vision less prominent in the earliest surviving public discourse than it became later?
- What can historical methods responsibly conclude about a private supernatural claim, and what requires philosophical or spiritual judgment?
- How should a seeker evaluate claimed spiritual confirmation when sincere people in different religions report incompatible experiences? See [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]].
- How can the story inspire [[Prayer]] without implying that every sincere petitioner will receive a visible manifestation? See [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]].
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
### Late documentation

The earliest extant firsthand account is from 1832, about twelve years after the reported event, and no known contemporaneous 1820 record documents the manifestation. A critic can reasonably argue that this gap permits substantial memory development or invention. A private adolescent experience could also have gone unrecorded. Documentary silence is therefore a real limitation on historical access, not proof of either occurrence or fabrication.

### Differences among the accounts

The 1832 narrative explicitly describes “the Lord,” while later accounts more clearly foreground two personages. The accounts also differ in their emphasis on forgiveness, institutional apostasy, and angels. Critics read this trajectory as possible development in memory, theology, or institutional purpose. Believing historians answer that autobiographical retellings select details for different audiences and that omission is not contradiction. That response can explain many differences, but it cannot make the accounts verbally identical or independently establish what Joseph saw. See [[Why are there multiple First Vision accounts]].

### Revival chronology

Wesley P. Walters argued that surviving records better support major Palmyra revival activity in 1824–1825 than in 1820. Milton V. Backman Jr. and later researchers identified broader regional religious activity compatible with Joseph's setting. Broad ferment is well supported, while the precise local timing, denominational sequence, and relationship to Joseph's retrospective wording remain disputed.

### Growth in prominence

The vision was not as prominent in surviving early Church discourse as it became later. A critical model sees increasing detail and institutional importance as retrospective construction. A believing model sees a private experience gradually disclosed and later recognized for its doctrinal significance. Later prominence is compatible with both models and does not settle historicity.

### Visionary genre and naturalistic explanations

Critics also place the account within an early nineteenth-century culture of conversion narratives and published visionary reports. On this model, familiar religious genres could have shaped how Joseph perceived, remembered, or narrated an experience, consciously or unconsciously. Other naturalistic possibilities include visionary psychology, dreamlike perception, and memory reconstruction. Comparable experiences in incompatible religions show that sincerity alone does not validate a particular theological interpretation; see [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]]. These models do not by themselves establish conscious fraud, and they must still explain Joseph's sustained presentation of the experience as real.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing scholarship is not monolithic, but James B. Allen, Richard Lloyd Anderson, Milton V. Backman Jr., Steven C. Harper, and Richard Lyman Bushman generally treat the accounts as historically serious recollections whose variations can be studied through genre, audience, memory, and context. Harper’s *Joseph Smith’s First Vision: A Guide to the Historical Accounts* emphasizes reading each account on its own terms. [[First Vision Accounts (Essay)]] supplies a useful bibliography, while [[First Vision Accounts (Gospel Topics)]] gives the Church’s official synthesis.

Critical scholarship represented by Fawn M. Brodie’s *No Man Knows My History*, Wesley P. Walters’s revival research, and Dan Vogel’s *Joseph Smith: The Making of a Prophet* places greater weight on the absence of an 1820 record, the account’s changing emphases, its later institutional usefulness, and naturalistic explanations for visionary experience. Critical writers differ over whether Joseph consciously fabricated, gradually reconstructed, or sincerely interpreted an experience.

The broadest agreement is documentary: several firsthand accounts exist; the first surviving one is from 1832; they overlap substantially but differ materially; and the canonical version is later than the reported event. The major divide concerns evidentiary significance. Believing scholars see complementary testimony and contextualized memory; critical scholars more often see development that weakens confidence in the 1838 account as a transparent recollection. Neither position can convert the surviving documents into independent eyewitness corroboration of a private supernatural event.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Read [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] as a complete argument: historical setting (verses 1-4), denominational conflict (5-10), James 1:5 and the decision to pray (11-13), the vision (14-20), and reception of the claim (21-26).
2. Read [[James 1]] independently before returning to Joseph Smith—History 1:11-13. Distinguish James’s general invitation to seek wisdom from the later reception history attached to it.
3. Compare the Father’s introduction of the Son in Joseph Smith—History 1:17 with Matthew 3:16-17. Note the parallel without claiming literary or historical dependence merely from similarity.
4. Compare all four primary accounts in chronological order through [[First Vision Accounts (Gospel Topics)]], [[First Vision Accounts (Essay)]], and the Joseph Smith Papers. Make a table separating shared elements, omissions, additions, audience, genre, and purpose.
5. Study the 1842 retelling through [[Chapter 38 The Wentworth Letter]] and compare its public purpose with the private 1832 history and institutional 1838 history.
6. Examine the principal historical dispute by reading Wesley P. Walters alongside Milton V. Backman Jr.; avoid treating either regional religious activity or missing local records as a single decisive test.
7. Trace doctrinal reception through [[The Godhead]], [[Apostasy]], [[Restoration of the Gospel]], [[Chapter 1 Our Father in Heaven]], and [[Chapter 4 The Prophet Joseph Smith, God’s Instrument in Restoring the Truth]]. Mark which conclusions are explicit in the canon and which arise from later authoritative interpretation.
8. Finally, consider the Church’s proposed spiritual mode of evaluation in Moroni 10:4-6 and Alma 5:45-46, as cited by [[First Vision (Gospel Topics)]]. Keep personal spiritual conviction distinct from publicly reproducible historical evidence.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The First Vision is simultaneously a canonical narrative, a historical claim, a theological foundation, and a personal-testimony question. [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] securely establishes what the Church’s authoritative scripture teaches: amid denominational confusion, Joseph Smith read James 1:5, prayed, reported deliverance from hostile opposition, saw two personages, heard the Father identify the Son, and was told not to join the existing sects. This text strongly grounds Latter-day Saint teaching about [[Prayer]], [[Revelation]], [[The Godhead]], [[Apostasy]], and the [[Restoration of the Gospel]].

Historical study adds both support and restraint. Four primary accounts show that Joseph Smith repeatedly claimed a foundational heavenly experience. Their common core matters, but because all depend on Joseph they are repeated self-testimony rather than independent eyewitness corroboration. Their differences are genuine. Audience and genre offer plausible explanations for selective details, while memory development and retrospective theological construction remain serious alternatives. The twelve-year gap before the earliest extant account and the disputed revival chronology lower historical certainty without proving fabrication.

A careful conclusion therefore has layers. It is **established** that Joseph Smith made and sustained the claim, that several firsthand narratives survive, and that the 1838-era version became canonical. It is **well supported within the text** that the vision distinguishes the Father and Son and frames revelation as an answer to prayer. It is **historically open** how accurately each later narration reproduces the 1820 experience. It is **not established by documentary evidence alone** that divine beings appeared—or that they did not. Accepting the event as a revelation remains a reasoned act of faith informed by texts, context, spiritual experience, and one’s broader judgments about God and revelation, rather than a conclusion history can compel.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
