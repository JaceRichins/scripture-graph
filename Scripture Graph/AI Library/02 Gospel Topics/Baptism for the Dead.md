# Baptism for the Dead

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In [[1 Corinthians 15]], “baptized for the dead” names an activity familiar enough to serve Paul's resurrection argument, but Paul does not define its form. Within the Latter-day Saint canon and tradition, **baptism for the dead** means that a living person is immersed as a proxy for a deceased person who lacked the ordinance in mortality. The deceased person is not treated as automatically converted: [[D&C 138]] makes postmortal teaching, repentance, and obedience integral to redemption (D&C 138:31-35, 57-59).

The expressions **redemption of the dead** and **work for the dead** are broader. They encompass postmortal [[Missionary Work]], proxy ordinances performed in [[Temples]], record keeping, and the intergenerational sealing work associated with [[Elijah]] and [[Family]]. Thus baptism for the dead is one ordinance within a larger theology of [[Death and the Spirit World]], [[Agency]], [[Ordinances]], and [[Salvation]].
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The Latter-day Saint canonical model has four connected movements:

1. **The dead can hear and respond.** [[1 Peter 3]] and [[1 Peter 4]] supply the brief biblical language of preaching to spirits and to the dead. [[D&C 138]] interprets those passages as an organized mission among deceased spirits (D&C 138:6-11, 25-37).
2. **Necessary ordinances are performed in mortality.** The dead are taught faith, repentance, and vicarious baptism, while temple ordinances are performed on their behalf by the living (D&C 138:31-34, 54).
3. **The dead retain agency.** D&C 138:34, 58-59 predicates redemption on repentance and obedience. Official Latter-day Saint teaching therefore distinguishes offering an ordinance from forcing its acceptance; see [[Participating in Temple and Family History Work (Gospel Topics)]].
4. **The work is authorized, witnessed, recorded, and temple-centered.** [[D&C 124]] assigns the enduring practice to the Lord's house, with a temporary historical exception (D&C 124:29-36). [[D&C 127]] requires eyewitness records (D&C 127:5-9), and [[D&C 128]] relates earthly records to heavenly judgment (D&C 128:2-9).

[[D&C 128]] further interprets immersion as a likeness of death and [[Resurrection]] (D&C 128:12-13) and baptism for the dead as an intergenerational welding link (D&C 128:15, 17-18). These are explicit claims of the Latter-day Saint canon. Whether the revelations are divinely authoritative is a faith judgment; textual analysis can identify the claims but cannot prove their postmortal efficacy.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
No canonical text says ancient Israel practiced proxy baptism. Malachi's promise concerning fathers and children does not itself name an ordinance; baptism for the dead is a later canonical application of that language.

In the New Testament, Paul refers to people being baptized for the dead while defending resurrection ([[1 Corinthians 15]]). The reference makes an associated practice intelligible but does not identify its practitioners, procedure, or normative status. [[1 Peter 3]] and [[1 Peter 4]] later became important to Christian discussion of Christ's descent and postmortem salvation, though neither mentions proxy baptism. Later patristic polemics report practices associated with baptism on behalf of the dead among groups their authors opposed. Those reports show that proxy interpretations existed in later antiquity, but their lateness and polemical setting do not securely reconstruct first-century Corinth or establish continuous mainstream practice.

In the Restoration sequence, [[D&C 137]] teaches that some who died without gospel knowledge may inherit the celestial kingdom, but it does not mention proxy baptism. [[D&C 110]] reports [[Elijah]] committing keys and repeats the heart-turning promise, yet it does not name baptism for the dead, sealing, or temple work for deceased persons.

According to [[Historical Summary]] and [[Participating in Temple and Family History Work (Gospel Topics)]], [[Joseph Smith Jr]] publicly announced baptism for the dead at an August 15, 1840 funeral, after which Saints began performing proxy baptisms in rivers and streams near [[Nauvoo]]. These are the earliest documented public introduction and early practice represented in the supplied sources; they do not prove that Joseph had never considered the idea earlier.

[[D&C 124]] subsequently directed the ordinance toward an appointed temple setting while allowing a temporary exception. The 1842 letters later canonized as [[D&C 127]] and [[D&C 128]] established witnesses, records, resurrection symbolism, Pauline precedent, and welding-link theology. In 1918, [[Joseph F. Smith]]'s [[Vision of the redemption of the dead]], now [[D&C 138]], supplied the most developed canonical account of organized preaching among the dead and its relationship to ordinances performed in temples. Later teaching made temple and family-history service a sustained institutional program. The sequence can be read as progressive revelation or as human theological and institutional development; chronology alone cannot decide between them.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The evidence is strongest when each proposition is kept within its proper scope.

**Strong textual evidence:** [[1 Corinthians 15]] shows that Paul expected his audience to recognize an activity described as baptism for the dead. It does not disclose the rite's exact form, practitioners, rationale, or Paul's approval. [[D&C 124]], [[D&C 127]], and [[D&C 128]] strongly establish that Joseph Smith taught and regulated a literal proxy ordinance. D&C 127:5-9 is especially strong evidence of a formally administered communal practice because it requires eyewitnesses and durable records.

**Strong evidence of canonical interpretation and development:** D&C 128 explicitly quotes 1 Corinthians 15:29 and Revelation 20:12 and applies Malachi's heart-turning prophecy to baptism for the dead. [[D&C 138]] explicitly engages [[1 Peter 3]] and [[1 Peter 4]] and expands their brief language into an organized mission among deceased spirits. These literary relationships are verifiable; they show how Latter-day Saint scripture constructs the doctrine but do not prove that the biblical passages originally carried the full later system.

**Moderate historical evidence:** The supplied institutional histories report the August 1840 Nauvoo announcement and rapid commencement of river baptisms. Their agreement should not be treated as fully independent corroboration without examining their underlying records. Later reports document recurring institutional practice, not by themselves uninterrupted continuity in every place and period.

**Moderate and disputed evidence for ancient proxy practice:** A literal proxy sense of 1 Corinthians 15:29 is grammatically serious, but one sentence cannot securely recover the practice or connect it institutionally to modern temple ritual. Later patristic reports show that some ancient Christians interpreted or practiced baptism on behalf of the dead, while their polemical and chronologically later character limits what they establish about Paul's Corinth.

**Faith-dependent claims:** That deceased persons accept ordinances, that earthly records correspond to heavenly records, or that the work has postmortal efficacy rests on acceptance of revelation and religious conviction. Documentary evidence establishes that these claims were taught and enacted; historical methods do not independently verify them.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What exactly did the Corinthian group do, and why does Paul call its practitioners “they” rather than “we” (1 Corinthians 15:29)?
- Is Paul approving the practice, tolerating it for argument's sake, or exposing an inconsistency in people whose conduct presupposed resurrection?
- Do the “spirits in prison” of 1 Peter 3:19 mean deceased human beings, rebellious supernatural beings, or another group? Does 1 Peter 4:6 describe postmortal evangelization or people who heard while alive and later died?
- How should a reader distinguish the original meanings of Malachi 4:5-6, Hebrews 11:40, and Revelation 20:12 from their expanded applications in [[D&C 128]]?
- Why does [[D&C 137]] promise celestial inheritance to some unbaptized dead without naming proxy baptism, and what does that sequence imply about progressive revelation?
- How do [[Agency]] and proxy representation fit together? [[D&C 138]] answers that ordinances do not bypass repentance, but the metaphysics of accepting a proxy act remain largely undescribed.
- What, precisely, was committed by [[Elijah]] in [[D&C 110]], and how should later applications to [[Eternal Marriage]] and work for the dead be related to a text that does not name those practices?
- What does it mean that the living and dead cannot be made perfect without one another (D&C 128:15)? Is this chiefly ordinance dependency, covenant solidarity, family sealing, or all three?
- Which modern safeguards best honor both the religious convictions of descendants and the identities of deceased persons from other faith traditions?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest biblical objection is underdetermination. A substantial doctrine is connected to one cryptic Pauline sentence and two Petrine passages that never mention proxy baptism. Paul neither institutes nor explains the Corinthian practice, and his third-person wording permits, without proving, distance from its practitioners. The spirits of 1 Peter 3 may be rebellious supernatural beings rather than ordinary deceased humans, while 1 Peter 4 may concern believers evangelized before death. The Bible alone therefore does not clearly teach the complete Latter-day Saint system.

A second objection concerns interpretive expansion. Malachi speaks of turned hearts, Hebrews of earlier faithful people not being perfected apart from later believers, and Revelation of books opened at judgment. [[D&C 128]] applies those passages to proxy ordinances, records, and intergenerational welding, but those applications are not explicit in the earlier wording. Likewise, [[D&C 110]] names Elijah's keys and turned hearts but not baptism for the dead. A careful defense must not project the later synthesis backward as the explicit meaning of every earlier text.

Patristic reports complicate rather than settle the ancient question. They indicate that later Christian groups were associated with proxy baptism, which makes a literal ritual reading historically possible. Yet the reports are later, often polemical, and commonly concern groups classified as heretical by their opponents. They do not demonstrate continuous apostolic or mainstream practice.

A historical-critical account sees a developing theology: an inclusive vision in [[D&C 137]], a documented public introduction in 1840, administrative elaboration in 1841-42, and a fuller spirit-world account in 1918. Bereavement, kinship, and institutional temple development can explain that sequence without revelation. The believing account understands the same sequence as progressive revelation; chronology alone does not adjudicate between them.

A moral objection holds that performing a rite in another person's name can symbolically appropriate the identity of the deceased or disregard descendants and faith communities. Latter-day Saint teaching responds that the rite does not compel conversion because the dead retain [[Agency]]. That answers coercion within the theology but does not erase external concerns about representation, submission practices, or consent.

Finally, records establish practice, sincerity, and doctrinal development—not what deceased persons experience. The ordinance's claimed postmortal efficacy remains a claim of revelation and faith.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation commonly reads 1 Corinthians 15:29 as referring to a genuine ancient proxy ordinance and regards Paul's use as compatible with approval; [[Chapter 13 The Sacred Importance of Temples]] represents that approach. This is a theological reading informed by later revelation, not a conclusion compelled by the isolated Pauline verse.

Critical commentators including Gordon D. Fee and Anthony C. Thiselton treat the verse within Paul's resurrection argument and emphasize how little it reveals about the Corinthian practice. Many scholars regard some proxy sense as grammatically natural while disagreeing over practitioners, motivation, and Paul's stance. Other proposals include baptism motivated by deceased believers, funerary or graveside contexts, and metaphorical readings. Richard E. DeMaris's study of Corinthian religion uses archaeology and anthropology to make a funerary setting more intelligible, but such contextual work does not demonstrate continuity with modern temple ritual.

Later patristic testimony associates forms of baptism on behalf of the dead with groups opposed by the reporting authors. It is relevant evidence that literal proxy practices or interpretations existed in antiquity, but it is chronologically later than Paul and filtered through heresiological controversy. It therefore neither proves apostolic authorization nor justifies dismissing the ritual reading as a modern invention.

Scholarship on [[1 Peter 3]] and [[1 Peter 4]] remains divided. The imprisoned spirits have been identified as deceased humans, rebellious angels, or defeated supernatural powers; the dead of 1 Peter 4:6 may be postmortal hearers or people evangelized in life who later died. Jeffrey A. Trumbower documents the wider diversity of early Christian ideas about posthumous salvation. [[D&C 138]] resolves these ambiguities within Latter-day Saint theology but does not create academic consensus about the Petrine texts' original meanings.

Historians such as Samuel Morris Brown place the ordinance within Joseph Smith's larger responses to death, kinship, priesthood, and temple ritual. Believing history may accept those contexts while affirming revelation; naturalistic history may regard them and the documentary sequence as sufficient explanation. The principal divide concerns not whether development occurred, but whether it is best understood as progressive revelation or human religious innovation.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with [[1 Corinthians 15]], reading the whole resurrection argument before isolating verse 29. Mark what Paul actually says and every question he leaves unanswered.
2. Read [[1 Peter 3]] and [[1 Peter 4]]. Compare the competing referents for “spirits in prison” and “the dead”; notice that neither text names an ordinance.
3. Read [[D&C 137]] for the earlier principle governing people who die without gospel opportunity.
4. Read [[D&C 110]], then record both what Elijah commits and what the chapter does not say. This prevents later interpretations from being projected backward as explicit wording.
5. Read [[D&C 124]] for the transition from temporary outdoor performance to an appointed temple setting and for the broader complex of [[Ordinances]].
6. Read [[D&C 127]] closely for witnesses, recorders, corroboration, and archives.
7. Read [[D&C 128]] alongside 1 Corinthians 15:29, Hebrews 11:39-40, Revelation 20:12, and Malachi 4:5-6. Separate quotation, interpretation, and larger doctrinal conclusion.
8. Read [[D&C 138]] alongside its cited Petrine passages. Trace Christ's ministry, the commissioned messengers, the dead persons' repentance, and the living persons' ordinance work.
9. Place the sequence on a timeline using [[Historical Summary]] and [[Participating in Temple and Family History Work (Gospel Topics)]]. Then compare devotional treatments in [[Gospel Doctrine]] and [[Chapter 46 Redeeming Our Dead through Temple Service]] with Fee, Thiselton, DeMaris, Trumbower, and Samuel Morris Brown.
10. Finish with [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]], asking separately what the texts establish, what the Latter-day Saint canon interprets, and what remains a matter of faith.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Baptism for the dead is the Latter-day Saint answer to a genuine theological problem: if baptism and conscious response to Christ matter, what becomes of those who die without a meaningful opportunity to receive them? The doctrine preserves both universal divine invitation and human [[Agency]] by dividing the work between realms. The dead hear, choose, repent, and obey; the living perform bodily ordinances in [[Temples]] on their behalf.

The biblical foundation is real but limited. Paul unquestionably mentions people baptized for the dead in 1 Corinthians 15:29 and uses their practice in defense of [[Resurrection]]. First Peter speaks of preaching to spirits and to the dead. Yet these passages do not independently provide the modern ordinance's temple setting, records, missionary organization, or theology of acceptance. Their exact ancient meanings remain disputed.

The full system emerges through Latter-day Saint canonical development. [[D&C 137]] states the inclusive principle. [[D&C 124]] gives the temple setting; [[D&C 127]] gives the documentary discipline; [[D&C 128]] joins resurrection symbolism, heavenly and earthly records, Pauline precedent, and Malachi's intergenerational promise; [[D&C 138]] supplies the spirit-world mission and explicitly preserves repentance and choice. Later teaching links this work to [[Elijah]], [[Family]], and the gathering of generations.

Historically, the evidence strongly establishes an ordinance publicly introduced in 1840 and progressively elaborated afterward. Textually, it strongly establishes the later revelations' dependence on and interpretation of biblical passages. Neither kind of evidence independently proves postmortal efficacy. Believers receive the development as revelation; critics can coherently read it as innovative theology shaped by death, kinship, and institutional growth. The most defensible conclusion is therefore layered: ancient baptism associated with the dead is textually attested but poorly understood; the integrated Latter-day Saint doctrine is clearly documented and internally coherent; its divine authority and effect remain claims of revelation and faith.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
