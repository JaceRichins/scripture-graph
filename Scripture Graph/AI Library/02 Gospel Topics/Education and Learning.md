# Education and Learning

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, **education and learning** are broader than schooling. Proverbs treats wisdom as teachable skill in choosing a right path (Proverbs 4:5-11). Deuteronomy and Psalm 78 treat learning as intergenerational transmission of covenant words and remembered acts. Romans 15:4 treats earlier scripture as a source of learning, endurance, comfort, and hope. [[D&C 88]] adds disciplined inquiry, mutual teaching, good books, study, faith, history, law, nations, and current affairs (D&C 88:77-80, 118-119).

The associated words are not one technical vocabulary. **Knowledge** can mean information, acquaintance with God's will, moral awareness, or truth disclosed by revelation. **Wisdom** commonly concerns sound judgment and faithful conduct. **Learning** is an activity of receiving, investigating, remembering, and teaching. **Intelligence** in D&C 93:29, 36 is identified with the light of truth and divine glory; in D&C 130:18-19 it is something attained through diligence and obedience. Neither passage simply defines intelligence as IQ, academic rank, or a degree.

Modern Church teaching applies these principles to formal education and lifelong learning. [[Education (True to the Faith)]] includes reading, cultural participation, historical study, and observation of the world. [[Seek Learning You Have a Work to Do (Mary N. Cook, April 2012)]] and [[Meeting the Challenges of Today’s World (Elder Robert D. Hales, October 2015)]] connect education with opportunity, service, employment, and resistance to ignorance and error. These are authoritative Church teachings in their respective genres, but they are not additional canonical definitions.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon supports several propositions with differing scope:

- Learning is commanded. Proverbs 4:5-11 calls for wisdom and understanding; D&C 88:118 commands learning by study and faith; D&C 90:15 names books, languages, tongues, and peoples.
- Learning is both received and pursued. Scripture attributes knowledge to teaching, records, observation, disciplined study, faithful practice, and revelation (Deuteronomy 6:6-9; Moses 6:5-6; Matthew 11:27-29; Mosiah 8:13-18; D&C 88:77-80).
- Intellectual and spiritual formation belong together. D&C 88:118-119 places study and a house of learning alongside faith, prayer, fasting, order, and worship.
- Learning is morally conditioned without being condemned. 2 Nephi 9:28 rejects the move from being learned to supposing oneself independently wise; 2 Nephi 9:29 immediately affirms learning when the learner heeds God's counsel.
- Knowledge creates responsibilities. Mosiah 3:11, 20-22 and Mosiah 15:24-26 distinguish ignorance from knowingly resisting received truth, though they do not give humans a complete formula for judging another person's culpability.
- Some aspect of intelligence acquired in mortality endures. D&C 130:18-19 expressly connects attained intelligence with resurrection and future advantage. The text does not specify the survival of every memory, credential, or earthly status.
- Truth and intelligence have cosmic significance in Restoration scripture. D&C 93:24, 29-36 relates truth, intelligence, light, divine glory, and agency. Competing metaphysical elaborations should not be mistaken for everything the verses themselves establish.

Modern applications in [[Chapter 27 Learning by Study and by Faith]], [[Education (True to the Faith)]], and general conference commend education and lifelong learning. Such applications interpret and extend the canonical principles; their genre and authority should remain distinct from the scriptural text.

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
In the Hebrew Bible, education appears principally through household covenant instruction, public reading, communal memory, and wisdom formation. Deuteronomy 6:6-9 commands parents to teach covenant words throughout daily life; Deuteronomy 31:12-13 calls the assembled community, including children, to hear and learn; Joshua 4:21-24 turns a memorial into an occasion for children's questions; Psalm 78:5-7 links teaching with hope and faithful conduct. Proverbs 1-4 depicts instruction through the voice of parent or teacher. These texts show educational practices, but they do not by themselves prove the existence or form of a modern-style school system.

The return-from-exile narrative adds public textual interpretation: Nehemiah 8:1-8 depicts the law being read and its sense given to an assembled people. Ecclesiastes 12:12-13 both warns of the weariness of endless bookmaking and study and concludes with reverence and obedience; this is a caution about limits, not a blanket rejection of learning.

The New Testament places learning within discipleship and scriptural interpretation. Jesus invites hearers to learn from him and attributes knowledge of the Father to the Son's revelation (Matthew 11:25-29). Paul says earlier scripture was written for learning and hope (Romans 15:4), while 1 Corinthians 2:10-14 argues that divine things require spiritual discernment. That argument should not be enlarged into a rejection of all ordinary reasoning.

The Book of Mormon emphasizes records as carriers of language, identity, and commandments. [[Mosiah 1]] credits written records with preserving sacred knowledge; [[Omni 1]] contrasts that preservation with Zarahemla's loss of language and remembered genealogy. This contrast is part of the Nephite record's own presentation, not independent anthropology. [[Mosiah 8]] adds seership as disclosure of otherwise hidden knowledge, and [[2 Nephi 9]] distinguishes good learning from intellectual pride.

Restoration revelations broaden the expressed curriculum. [[D&C 88]] and [[D&C 90]] call for study of doctrine, history, law, nations, languages, and peoples. [[D&C 93]] embeds intelligence in a theology of light, truth, and agency; [[D&C 130]] connects mortal attainment with resurrection. Later Latter-day Saint discourse increasingly applies these passages to schools, employment, culture, service, and lifelong learning. [[Discourses of Brigham Young]], [[Conference Report, April 1930 (General Conference (full report), April 1930)]], [[Two Principles for Any Economy (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, October 2009)]], and [[Education (True to the Faith)]] document that continuing application. Reported sermons in the *Journal of Discourses* require source criticism and should not automatically be treated as canon or as precise transcripts.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong textual findings

The strongest finding is convergence rather than a single proof-text. Proverbs 4:5-11 commands acquisition of wisdom; 2 Nephi 9:28-29 distinguishes learning from self-sufficient pride; D&C 88:77-80, 118-119 joins broad inquiry with faith, mutual teaching, and worship; D&C 90:15 adds books, languages, and peoples; D&C 130:18-19 assigns enduring consequence to attained intelligence. Together these strongly support a canonically positive but morally accountable view of learning.

A second strong pattern connects records with learning. Moses 6:5-6 joins a book of remembrance with literacy. [[Mosiah 1]] says records preserve language and commandments. [[Omni 1]] associates the absence of records with loss of language and confused tradition. Romans 15:4 treats earlier writings as resources for learning and hope. The thematic pattern is clear; the historical and sociological generalizations sometimes drawn from it are less secure.

A third pattern concerns the limits and consequences of knowledge. [[Mosiah 3]] and [[Mosiah 15]] distinguish ignorance from informed rebellion; 2 Nephi 9:14 anticipates perfect moral self-knowledge at judgment; Matthew 24:32-36 combines recognition of signs with ignorance of the exact time. These passages resist both contempt for knowledge and claims to unlimited human certainty.

### Necessary cautions

The index's large counts for [[Proverbs 2]] and [[1 Corinthians 2]] are ambiguous semantic matches, not measurements of doctrinal importance. Proverbs 2 concerns wisdom instruction; 1 Corinthians 2 contrasts human wisdom with knowledge disclosed through the Spirit. Comparison is fruitful only if those distinct concerns remain visible.

D&C 93:29 does not settle every later debate about the ontology of intelligence. D&C 130:18-19 does not identify schooling with exaltation or promise preservation of every acquired fact. The record narratives demonstrate what their texts claim and value; without independent corroboration, they do not prove a universal theory of literacy and cultural survival.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What precisely is the relationship among intelligence, light, truth, agency, and individual identity in D&C 93:24-36? The passage establishes relationships but leaves room for more than one metaphysical account.
- What is the "advantage" in the world to come promised in D&C 130:19, and which kinds of knowledge or developed capacity does it include?
- What does learning "by faith" add to careful study in D&C 88:118: revelation, faithful action, moral formation, trust in divine testimony, or some combination?
- How should a learner proceed when a well-supported scholarly conclusion appears to conflict with an inherited scriptural interpretation? The canon commands both learning and fidelity but gives no simple adjudication procedure for every case.
- How do ignorance, opportunity, intention, and willful rejection interact in divine accountability? [[Mosiah 3]] and [[Mosiah 15]] provide principles but not an exhaustive human calculus; see also [[Justice and Mercy]] and [[Final Judgment]].
- How reliable is the contrast [[Mosiah 1]] draws between Nephite record keeping and Lamanite tradition? It is important evidence for Nephite self-understanding, but it is also an interested account from a Nephite king.
- How should apparent tensions between human limits and claims of revealed knowledge be managed? Matthew 24:36 limits knowledge of the hour, while [[Mosiah 8]] and [[Revelation]] affirm divinely enabled disclosure.
- Which educational applications are canonical requirements, which are prudent modern counsel, and which reflect the economic and institutional circumstances of a particular era?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
A strong secular objection is that these texts commend religious formation, not open-ended inquiry. Their approved learning is commonly directed toward covenant loyalty, obedience, and acceptance of revelation; 2 Nephi 9:28 and 1 Corinthians 2 can be used to discount conclusions that challenge religious authority. On this reading, "study and also by faith" does not establish equal freedom for critical investigation because faith supplies the permitted boundary.

A strong textual response is limited but real: D&C 88:77-80 and D&C 90:15 expressly widen study to history, law, nations, current events, books, languages, and peoples. The curriculum is still religiously framed, yet it is not confined to devotional propositions. Moreover, 2 Nephi 9:29 says learning itself is good; the target in verse 28 is the proud refusal of counsel. This response shows breadth within a theological framework, not modern academic autonomy without limits.

Another objection is that D&C 130:18-19 can turn educational privilege into eternal hierarchy. Access to time, books, health, literacy, and formal schooling is radically unequal, so a promise of future "advantage" may appear to eternalize mortal inequality. The verse attributes gain to diligence and obedience but does not discuss unequal access. Appeals to perfectly informed divine judgment may reconcile the concern theologically, but they are an inference from wider doctrine, not an explanation supplied in these two verses.

A historical objection concerns records. [[Mosiah 1]] and [[Omni 1]] may reflect a literate elite's preference for texts and an oversimplified portrayal of oral transmission. Oral cultures can preserve complex history, law, and identity; loss of writing does not necessarily entail cultural degeneration. The passages remain evidence for the narrators' theology of records, but their broader social claims require independent evaluation.

Finally, canonical and modern language can be harmonized too quickly. "Intelligence" in [[D&C 93]], practical education in [[Education (True to the Faith)]], and employment preparation in [[Meeting the Challenges of Today’s World (Elder Robert D. Hales, October 2015)]] overlap but are not identical. Treating them as a single timeless definition obscures semantic and historical development.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Critical scholarship commonly studies biblical education through wisdom literature, scribal culture, household transmission, public reading, and the social setting of texts. James L. Crenshaw's *Education in Ancient Israel: Across the Deadening Silence* argues from limited and indirect evidence; Michael V. Fox's commentary *Proverbs 1-9* treats Proverbs as wisdom instruction while attending to its literary voices and settings. Such work can clarify genre and historical context without accepting the texts' revelatory claims. Scholars differ over how much formal schooling can be reconstructed from the evidence, so household or wisdom instruction should not automatically be labeled a modern school.

A specific and widely discussed comparative datum concerns Proverbs 22:17-24:22, whose sayings share notable form and content with the Egyptian 'Instruction of Amenemope.' Mainstream biblical scholarship treats this parallel as well established at the level of literary form; what remains genuinely debated is the direction and nature of dependence (direct borrowing, a shared regional scribal convention, or indirect transmission), and whether shared form says anything about the inspired status of the Israelite text's content. This parallel does not by itself decide the question of inspiration in either direction — a shared genre convention is compatible with a distinctly reframed theological content, but it does complicate claims that the wisdom material's content is wholly sui generis.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation normally reads [[D&C 88]], [[D&C 93]], and [[D&C 130]] as revealed doctrine and integrates intellectual development with premortal identity, mortal discipleship, resurrection, and divine glory. [[Chapter 27 Learning by Study and by Faith]], [[Education (True to the Faith)]], and [[Seek Learning You Have a Work to Do (Mary N. Cook, April 2012)]] represent this constructive, devotional use. [[The Father (Brian K. Ashton, October 2018)]] illustrates a modern explanatory use of ordinary dictionary definitions, but a contemporary dictionary definition of intelligence cannot control the meaning of a nineteenth-century revelation.

The principal divide is therefore not whether the texts value learning; that observation is well supported. The divide concerns source authority and inference. Believing readings accept revelation as a genuine source of knowledge and may treat D&C 93:29 or D&C 130:18-19 as disclosures about premortal and postmortal reality. Non-believing or methodologically naturalistic scholarship can describe those claims, their language, and their historical development but does not treat revelation as independently verifiable evidence of that reality.

The supplied library is weighted toward scripture, Church manuals, and devotional addresses. It does not constitute a representative survey of educational history, cognitive science, philosophy of education, or critical Book of Mormon scholarship. Accordingly, claims of scholarly consensus beyond the narrow textual observations, and beyond the Amenemope parallel noted above, would be unwarranted.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with the Hebrew Bible's foundational models: read [[Deuteronomy 6]], [[Deuteronomy 31]], [[Joshua 4]], and [[Psalm 78]] for household teaching, public hearing, memorial, and intergenerational memory.
2. Read [[Proverbs 1]], [[Proverbs 2]], [[Proverbs 3]], and [[Proverbs 4]] as wisdom instruction. Track how knowledge is tied to conduct, discipline, and the choice of a path.
3. Add limits and textual interpretation through [[Ecclesiastes 12]] and [[Nehemiah 8]]. Ask what each passage cautions against and what it continues to affirm.
4. Study New Testament epistemology through [[Matthew 11]], Romans 15:4, and [[1 Corinthians 2]]. Distinguish learning from Christ, learning from earlier scripture, and Spirit-mediated discernment.
5. Follow the Book of Mormon's record theme through [[Omni 1]], [[Mosiah 1]], and [[Mosiah 8]]. Separate what the narrative observes from larger historical claims about literacy and culture.
6. Read [[Mosiah 3]], [[Mosiah 15]], and 2 Nephi 9:13-14 for the relation between knowledge, ignorance, accountability, resurrection, and judgment.
7. Read [[2 Nephi 9]] before the Restoration revelations. Hold verses 28 and 29 together so criticism of intellectual pride is not misquoted as criticism of learning.
8. Study [[D&C 88]] and [[D&C 90]] for curriculum and method, then [[D&C 93]] for truth and intelligence, and finally [[D&C 130]] for postmortal continuity. Do not assume that "intelligence" has exactly the same force in every passage.
9. Compare canonical teaching with later application in [[Chapter 27 Learning by Study and by Faith]], [[Education (True to the Faith)]], [[Two Principles for Any Economy (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, October 2009)]], and [[Meeting the Challenges of Today’s World (Elder Robert D. Hales, October 2015)]]. Mark explicitly where each source quotes, interprets, or extends scripture.
10. Conclude by studying adjacent doctrines: [[Truth]], [[Faith]], [[Revelation]], [[Agency]], [[Records and Sacred Writings]], [[Resurrection]], and [[Final Judgment]].

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The canon presents learning as a lifelong, morally consequential pursuit encompassing scripture, history, languages, peoples, law, the created world, practical judgment, and knowledge received through revelation. Its most balanced formulation is D&C 88:118: learning is sought diligently, shared with others, drawn from good books, and pursued by both study and faith. [[2 Nephi 9]] supplies the necessary warning: education is good, but being learned does not make a person self-sufficiently wise.

Scriptural education is therefore not merely information transfer. Deuteronomy and Psalm 78 aim at covenant memory and faithful action; Proverbs aims at wise conduct; Matthew 11 places learning within discipleship to [[Jesus Christ]]; Romans 15 connects scriptural learning with hope; [[Mosiah 1]] and [[Omni 1]] present records as supports for language, identity, and sacred memory. [[D&C 88]] and [[D&C 90]] broaden the field of inquiry, while [[D&C 93]] and [[D&C 130]] give intelligence theological and eternal importance.

The boundaries of these teachings matter. "Intelligence" should not be flattened into IQ or academic credentials. D&C 130 does not explain how unequal educational opportunity affects future advantage. Record narratives express a strong theology of literacy but do not disprove the power of oral tradition. Learning by faith does not guarantee that sincere believers reach identical conclusions, and the canon gives no automatic procedure for resolving every conflict between scholarship and inherited interpretation.

Modern Church sources such as [[Education (True to the Faith)]], [[Seek Learning You Have a Work to Do (Mary N. Cook, April 2012)]], and [[Meeting the Challenges of Today’s World (Elder Robert D. Hales, October 2015)]] reasonably apply the canon to schooling, employment, service, and lifelong growth. Those applications are best understood as faithful development rather than as the sole meaning of ancient or Restoration texts.

The strongest honest conclusion is modest but substantial: scripture consistently commends serious learning, preserves a place for broad study, and opposes ignorance, while insisting that knowledge be joined to humility, faith, obedience, service, and openness to correction. Claims about the precise metaphysics of intelligence, the mechanics of learning after death, or the guaranteed harmony of faith and scholarship remain matters of interpretation and, in part, faith.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
