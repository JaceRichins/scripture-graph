# Gathering of Israel

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, the **gathering of Israel** is God's recovery, reunification, and covenant renewal of people previously scattered or estranged. Its referent and mechanism vary by passage. Prophetic texts can describe return to ancestral land, reunion of Judah and Israel, cleansing, renewed obedience, divine protection, and restored worship ([[Isaiah 11]], [[Jeremiah 16]], [[Ezekiel 11]], [[Ezekiel 36]], [[Ezekiel 37]]). The Gospels add gathering to Christ and the final gathering of the elect ([[Matthew 23]], [[Matthew 24]], [[John 11]]). The Book of Mormon includes geographic restoration, coming to know Christ, baptism, covenant remembrance, and the recovery of Israel's records ([[1 Nephi 22]], [[2 Nephi 29]], [[3 Nephi 20]], [[3 Nephi 21]]).

"House of Israel" is likewise contextual rather than mechanically uniform. It may denote an ancient national or exilic audience, Judah and the northern tribes, a dispersed covenant people, or a particular remnant. Thus occurrences in [[Ezekiel 18]] are moral addresses, not automatically gathering predictions; by contrast, 3 Nephi 16:5 and 3 Nephi 21:1 explicitly promise gathering.

In contemporary Latter-day Saint teaching, the term commonly emphasizes bringing people to [[Jesus Christ]], receiving the gospel and its ordinances, keeping covenants, and participating in missionary and temple work. [[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]] describes gathered Israel as people willing to let God prevail. That is an authoritative modern application within the tradition, but it should not erase the land, justice, communal restoration, and eschatological dimensions of the ancient texts. See also [[Gathering of Israel (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Chapter 44 The Kingdom of God and the Gathering of Israel]].
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canonical doctrine begins with [[Abrahamic Covenant]]: Abraham's seed is chosen not merely for privilege but so that the families of the earth may be blessed (Genesis 12:3; 1 Nephi 22:9). Scattering follows rebellion, conquest, exile, and dispossession; gathering is God's covenant response, involving remembrance, deliverance, and renewed belonging.

The prophets keep outward and inward restoration together. Isaiah foresees an ensign and recovery of dispersed Israel (Isaiah 11:11–12). Jeremiah joins the houses of Israel and Judah and compares return with the Exodus (Jeremiah 3:18; 16:14–15). Ezekiel's pattern is especially complete: gathering from the nations, return to land, cleansing, a new heart and spirit, obedience, unity, covenant peace, and God's sanctuary among the people (Ezekiel 11:17–20; 36:24–28; 37:21–28). Ancestry does not guarantee the promised outcome; continued idolatry still brings judgment (Ezekiel 11:21), and 3 Nephi 10:6 conditions protective gathering on repentance.

The New Testament centers gathering in [[Jesus Christ]]. His mortal ministry gives priority to Israel (Matthew 10:5–6; 15:24), yet mercy reaches beyond that boundary and the risen Lord commissions disciples to all nations (Matthew 15:28; 28:19). John connects Christ's death with gathering God's scattered children into one (John 11:51–52). Matthew and Mark also portray a climactic gathering of the elect at the coming of the Son of Man (Matthew 24:29–31; Mark 13:24–27).

The Book of Mormon presents itself as an instrument and sign of covenant remembrance. Israel's records will come together (2 Nephi 29:12–14); a remnant will begin to know the covenant and Christ (3 Nephi 20:29–33; 21:6–7); and Gentiles may assist but are warned against pride, unbelief, and oppression ([[Jews and Gentiles]]; 3 Nephi 16:7–15). It expressly prohibits contempt for Jews or any remnant of Israel (3 Nephi 29:8–9).

Official Latter-day Saint doctrine adds the claim that priesthood authority for this work was restored when Moses committed gathering keys in the Kirtland Temple (D&C 110:11). [[He Goes before Us (President Henry B. Eyring, April 2020)]] and [[Gathering of Israel (Gospel Topics)]] connect those keys with missionary, covenant, and temple work. The historical occurrence of the revelatory report is documentable; its supernatural origin remains a faith claim rather than an independently demonstrated conclusion.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The doctrine develops through successive settings rather than appearing as one unchanged program.

1. **Patriarchal promise:** The covenants associated with [[Abraham (patriarch)]], Isaac, and [[Jacob (Israel)]] join posterity, land, divine relationship, and blessing to the nations ([[Genesis 12]], [[Genesis 17]], [[Genesis 28]]).
2. **Exodus and covenant nation:** [[The Exodus from Egypt]] becomes Israel's foundational deliverance. Jeremiah later uses it as the comparison for restoration from northern and other lands (Jeremiah 16:14-15).
3. **Division, conquest, and exile:** The [[Assyrian captivity of Israel]] and [[Babylonian captivity of Judah]] give gathering language concrete historical force. Prophetic texts promise return, reunion, purification, just rule, and renewed divine presence ([[Jeremiah 3]], [[Ezekiel 11]], [[Ezekiel 36]], [[Ezekiel 37]], [[Micah 4]]).
4. **Return and continuing hope:** The Persian-period [[Return from exile]] realizes part of the return motif. Whether unfulfilled features indicate future fulfillment, prophetic idealization, canonical reapplication, or nonfulfillment remains disputed; the text does not force one model.
5. **Jesus and the early mission:** Jesus prioritizes Israel in his mortal mission, laments refused gathering, extends mercy across that boundary, and commissions disciples to all nations ([[Matthew 10]], [[Matthew 15]], [[Matthew 23]], [[Matthew 28]]). John interprets Jesus's death as gathering scattered children into one ([[John 11]]).
6. **Book of Mormon expansion:** Nephi treats Isaiah's subject as temporal and spiritual, connects restoration with Gentile nations and Abrahamic blessing, and anticipates convergence of records ([[1 Nephi 22]], [[2 Nephi 29]]). The risen Christ applies covenant promises to a remnant while holding Israel and Gentiles accountable ([[3 Nephi 16]], [[3 Nephi 20]], [[3 Nephi 21]], [[3 Nephi 29]]).
7. **Latter-day Saint restoration:** [[D&C 110]] reports Moses committing gathering keys in the Kirtland Temple. Nineteenth-century preaching and practice often gave gathering strongly geographic, community-building, and literal-lineage expressions; [[Key to the Science of Theology]], [[36 Incline Them to Gather]], and period sermons witness this setting. Reported sermons must be cited by speaker, date, and page and treated as historical evidence rather than canon. The available Journal of Discourses index also flags 'The Blood of Israel,' showing that lineage rhetoric deserves explicit investigation rather than being silently reduced to geography.
8. **Twentieth- and twenty-first-century emphasis:** [[Conference Report, April 1910 (General Conference (full report), April 1910)]] preserves early twentieth-century gathering rhetoric. Contemporary official discourse emphasizes worldwide gathering to Christ and covenants; [[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]] makes willing submission to God central. This is demonstrably a change in emphasis and application. Whether it represents doctrinal continuity, substantive development, or both requires more primary-source history than the supplied excerpts provide, especially concerning older literal-lineage teachings.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong textual findings

The strongest evidence is the repeated canonical pattern itself. [[Ezekiel 11]], [[Ezekiel 36]], and [[Ezekiel 37]] independently display a dense sequence of regathering, land, cleansing or inner renewal, obedience, covenant belonging, and divine presence. Jeremiah 16:14–15 explicitly constructs a new-Exodus analogy. [[3 Nephi 20]] and [[3 Nephi 21]] explicitly join remnant restoration, knowledge of Christ, baptism, covenant fulfillment, and the coming forth of records. These conclusions describe what the texts say; they do not depend on identifying a modern fulfillment.

The reading also establishes several safeguards. Gathering is conditional and ethical rather than ancestry alone (Ezekiel 11:21; 3 Nephi 10:6–7). Gentile agency is paired with judgment for exploitation and unbelief (3 Nephi 16:7–15). Contempt for Jews and Israel's remnants is directly forbidden (3 Nephi 29:8–9). Micah's inclusion of the lame and driven out makes restoration a claim about vulnerable people, not only institutional expansion (Micah 4:6–7).

### Accepted observation, disputed significance

The Book of Mormon's many correspondences with biblical restoration language are undeniable at the level of text: ensigns, remnants, covenant remembrance, a second recovery, records, land, and nations recur throughout [[1 Nephi 22]], [[2 Nephi 29]], and 3 Nephi. Believing interpretation sees revealed continuity and fulfillment. A critical interpretation can explain much of the same coherence through the Book of Mormon's use of the King James Bible and nineteenth-century restorationist discourse. The correspondence therefore supports thematic integration but does not, by itself, establish ancient authorship or supernatural translation.

D&C 110 is strong evidence that Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery reported a vision assigning gathering authority to Moses. Within the record, it integrates [[Temples]], [[Abrahamic Covenant]], gathering, and sealing. The record does not independently verify the supernatural event; accepting that explanation depends on broader judgments about the witnesses and revelation.

### Weak or indeterminate inferences

Keyword counts are weak evidence. Several indexed "house of Israel" matches, especially in [[Ezekiel 18]], concern moral address rather than gathering. The location and present identity of all "lost tribes" are not established by the cited texts. Nor does the canon supply a universally agreed timetable aligning every return-from-exile, missionary, national, temple, and end-time passage. Revelation 14's harvest imagery should not be used as unambiguous evidence for gathering the righteous: the chapter does not explicitly label its grain harvest positive, and interpreters dispute whether both harvests portray judgment.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- When a passage says "Israel," does it mean an ancient political community, genealogical descendants, covenant participants, faithful remnants, the Church, or some combination? The answer must be argued passage by passage.
- How much of Isaiah, Jeremiah, and Ezekiel was fulfilled in the [[Return from exile]], and what textual grounds justify additional fulfillment rather than simple reapplication?
- How should geographic promises concerning land and Jerusalem relate to spiritual gathering to Christ without either dimension swallowing the other?
- Does the New Testament enlarge Israel, incorporate Gentiles into Israel's blessings, create a multiethnic people alongside Israel, or anticipate distinct but related futures? Romans 9–11 should be studied before answering.
- Who are the "other tribes" and "lost tribes" of 2 Nephi 29:12–13 and D&C 110:11? The texts affirm their place in God's purposes but do not provide a verifiable modern map of their location.
- What exactly counts as the commencement, continuation, and completion of gathering in 3 Nephi 21:1–7? A sign that work has begun is not identical to completed fulfillment.
- How should claims about Book of Mormon remnants be made without turning a theological promise into an unsupported genetic classification of Indigenous peoples? Compare [[What is the Book of Mormon's view of Native American origins]] and [[Why did the Lamanite introduction change in 2006]].
- How can Christians affirm their participation in Abrahamic blessing without supersessionism, contempt for Jews, or appropriation of another people's history? Third Nephi 29:8–9 makes this an internal scriptural concern.
- Does a modern nation-state fulfill any particular oracle, partially instantiate an older pattern, or remain distinct from the covenant category? Scripture does not authorize a simple one-to-one equation.
- How do missionary, humanitarian, temple, genealogical, communal, geographic, and eschatological dimensions relate? The canon contains all of these trajectories but no single verse that systematizes them completely.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest historical-critical objection is that prophetic gathering oracles first address concrete ancient crises—Assyrian conquest, Babylonian exile, diaspora, and hopes of return. Reading them primarily as detailed predictions of a nineteenth- or twenty-first-century church can displace their original audiences and turn later resemblance into proof of exclusive fulfillment.

A second objection concerns incomplete fulfillment. The return under Persian rule did occur, yet it did not obviously produce every feature of Ezekiel's ideal restoration: complete national unity, universal inner renewal, enduring security, and permanent divine kingship. Believers may infer an ongoing or future fulfillment; critics may instead see prophetic idealization, theological hope, or promises whose full expectations were not historically realized. The text alone does not force one resolution.

A third objection is literary dependence. The Book of Mormon's gathering discourse is saturated with King James biblical language. A believing model attributes this to prophetic intertextuality, translation, and covenant continuity. A critical model locates it within nineteenth-century American biblical culture and restorationism. The thematic parallels cannot adjudicate authorship without additional evidence.

A fourth objection targets identity claims. Broadly labeling Indigenous peoples as "Lamanites" or assigning them a single Israelite ancestry can exceed the Book of Mormon's own limited remnant claims and conflict with the complexity of population history. It can also subordinate living peoples to an externally imposed sacred narrative. [[What is the Book of Mormon's view of Native American origins]] and [[Why did the Lamanite introduction change in 2006]] are necessary controls.

A fifth objection is ethical and political: gathering language can legitimate displacement, colonial entitlement, nationalism, or institutional triumphalism when land promises are transferred without attention to existing populations. The canon itself complicates such uses by condemning Gentile oppression (3 Nephi 16:8–15), requiring repentance (3 Nephi 10:6), centering afflicted people (Micah 4:6–7), and forbidding contempt for Jews (3 Nephi 29:8–9).

Finally, "spiritual gathering" can become unfalsifiably broad: nearly any conversion, migration, institutional growth, political development, or technological change can be counted as fulfillment after the fact. A responsible claim should specify the passage, its original setting, the proposed correspondence, what remains unfulfilled, and what alternative explanation fits the same facts.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship broadly agrees that gathering language is rooted in displacement, exile, return, restoration of community, and renewed relationship with Israel's God. It also emphasizes that "Israel" changes referent across monarchic, exilic, postexilic, Jewish, and Christian texts. Joseph Blenkinsopp's *Isaiah 1–39*, Daniel I. Block's *The Book of Ezekiel*, and Paul M. Joyce's *Ezekiel: A Commentary* represent differing confessional locations but all treat prophetic restoration within literary and ancient historical contexts. They do not reduce the material to a coded schedule of modern events.

Scholars divide over composition, predictive prophecy, and fulfillment. Historical-critical approaches commonly treat many restoration texts as responses to ancient imperial crises and later editorial development. Confessional readers can accept those historical settings while also seeing typology, divine foreknowledge, and multiple fulfillment. The important divide is not whether the texts speak of restoration—they plainly do—but whether later applications are divinely intended fulfillment, legitimate reception, or retrospective appropriation.

New Testament scholarship generally recognizes John 11:51–52 as the evangelist's theological reinterpretation of Caiaphas's words. Raymond E. Brown's *The Gospel According to John XIII–XXI* is a major reference point. Debate remains over whether the scattered children are chiefly Diaspora Jews, Gentile believers, or the eschatological people of God. Matthew's Israel-first mission and mission to all nations likewise generate competing accounts of continuity between Israel and the Church.

Latter-day Saint official interpretation is represented by [[Gathering of Israel (Gospel Topics)]], [[Chapter 44 The Kingdom of God and the Gathering of Israel]], [[He Goes before Us (President Henry B. Eyring, April 2020)]], and [[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]]. These sources accept the Book of Mormon and D&C as revealed extensions of the biblical story and connect gathering with missionary and temple work. They are authoritative sources for what the tradition teaches, but they are not independent demonstrations of the historical or supernatural claims.

For the wider intellectual setting, Philip L. Barlow's *Mormons and the Bible: The Place of the Latter-day Saints in American Religion* situates Latter-day Saint scriptural interpretation within strong King James and restorationist currents. Terryl L. Givens's *By the Hand of Mormon* presents the Book of Mormon's claims and reception from a more sympathetic perspective. Critical and believing scholarship part most sharply over the Book of Mormon's antiquity, Joseph Smith's revelatory claims, and whether D&C 110 describes an actual heavenly commission.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. **Begin with covenant and scattering.** Read [[Genesis 12]], [[Genesis 17]], and [[Genesis 28]] for promise; [[Deuteronomy 28]] and [[Deuteronomy 30]] for dispersion, return, and heart renewal. Use [[Abrahamic Covenant]] and [[Scattering of Israel]] as conceptual guides.
2. **Study the prophetic restoration cluster.** Read [[Isaiah 11]], [[Isaiah 49]], [[Jeremiah 3]], [[Jeremiah 16]], [[Jeremiah 31]], [[Ezekiel 11]], [[Ezekiel 36]], [[Ezekiel 37]], and [[Micah 4]]. Track five columns: people gathered, place, divine action, required moral change, and remaining promise.
3. **Follow Jesus's Israel mission and its expansion.** Read [[Matthew 10]], [[Matthew 15]], [[Matthew 23]], [[Matthew 24]], [[Matthew 28]], and [[John 11]]. Distinguish mortal mission priority, protective gathering, worldwide discipleship, unity through Christ's death, and eschatological gathering.
4. **Read Nephi's interpretive framework.** Study [[1 Nephi 10]], [[1 Nephi 15]], [[1 Nephi 19]], [[1 Nephi 21]], and [[1 Nephi 22]]. Mark every place Nephi distinguishes scattering, temporal restoration, spiritual knowledge, Gentile assistance, and Abrahamic blessing.
5. **Study records as gathering instruments.** Read [[2 Nephi 29]] and compare Ezekiel 37:15–23. Do not assume that shared "stick" imagery resolves every question about the passages' original meanings.
6. **Read the risen Christ's covenant discourse as a unit.** Study [[3 Nephi 16]], [[3 Nephi 20]], [[3 Nephi 21]], and [[3 Nephi 29]]. Track remnant, Gentiles, land, repentance, baptism, records, Zion, judgment, and the prohibition against mocking Jews.
7. **Add Mormon's remnant perspective.** Read [[Mormon 5]] and [[Mormon 7]] for the sequence of Gentile possession, remnant scattering, covenant remembrance, and Mormon's direct address to a future remnant.
8. **Examine the restoration claim.** Read [[D&C 110]] in the setting of the [[Kirtland Temple dedication]] and [[Priesthood keys restored in the Kirtland Temple]]. Then compare [[He Goes before Us (President Henry B. Eyring, April 2020)]] with the wording of D&C 110 itself.
9. **Study modern application with historical controls.** Compare [[Key to the Science of Theology]], [[Conference Report, April 1910 (General Conference (full report), April 1910)]], [[Gathering of Israel (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]]. Note where emphasis moves from physical concentration toward worldwide covenant participation.
10. **Test every conclusion.** Ask whether it is an observation, interpretation, claimed fulfillment, or faith conclusion; whether the original audience has been honored; and what competing model explains the same evidence.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The gathering of Israel is not one migration, one ethnicity test, one missionary slogan, or one end-time scene. It is the canon's many-sided answer to scattering: God remembers covenant, recovers displaced people, reunites what has been divided, renews hearts, establishes justice and worship, gathers people to Christ, and ultimately gathers the elect.

The strongest textual portrait keeps dimensions together that are often separated. Gathering can be geographic, but return without cleansing is incomplete ([[Ezekiel 36]]). It can be spiritual, but inward language must not erase land, community, and the afflicted ([[Micah 4]]). It serves Israel, yet Abraham's covenant aims to bless all families (1 Nephi 22:9). Gentiles may assist, yet they remain answerable for unbelief and oppression ([[3 Nephi 16]]). Israelite descent matters within the story, yet ancestry does not substitute for repentance (Ezekiel 11:21; 3 Nephi 10:6).

Jesus stands at the center of the Christian and Book of Mormon synthesis. His death gathers scattered children into one (John 11:52); his gospel crosses national boundaries (Matthew 28:19); and his return includes a final gathering of the elect (Matthew 24:31). The Book of Mormon claims that restored records, knowledge of Christ, baptism, and covenant remembrance mark the latter-day commencement of this work ([[2 Nephi 29]], [[3 Nephi 21]]). D&C 110 adds the distinct Latter-day Saint claim that Moses restored priesthood keys for gathering in the Kirtland Temple.

What is established is the richness and persistence of the canonical theme, its covenant structure, and its moral demands. What remains disputed is how ancient promises map onto later communities and events, who precisely constitutes every promised remnant, how geographic and ecclesial fulfillments relate, and whether modern Latter-day Saint revelatory claims are accepted as divine. A faithful reading need not pretend those questions are settled. It can act on what the texts make clear: come to Christ, repent, honor covenant, gather rather than despise, resist oppression, preserve and share sacred witness, and make room for those who have been scattered.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
