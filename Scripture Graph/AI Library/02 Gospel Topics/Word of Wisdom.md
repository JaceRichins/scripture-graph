# Word of Wisdom

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, **the Word of Wisdom** most precisely means the revelation now canonized as [[D&C 89]]. It calls itself “a word of wisdom,” concerns the Saints’ “temporal salvation,” warns against wine, strong drink, tobacco, and “hot drinks,” commends wholesome plants, fruit, and grain, counsels sparing use of animal flesh, and attaches promises to faithful observance (D&C 89:1–21).

In present Latter-day Saint usage, the term also denotes the Church’s interpreted standard of abstinence, especially from alcoholic drinks, tobacco, coffee, tea, and harmful or illicit drugs; see [[Word of Wisdom (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Chapter 29 Living the Word of Wisdom]]. This contemporary standard should not simply be read backward as if every detail were explicit in the 1833 text. The text leaves “hot drinks” undefined, mentions sacramental wine, and assigns barley to “mild drinks” (D&C 89:5–9, 17).

A separate biblical and Restoration use must be kept distinct: the “word of wisdom” in 1 Corinthians 12:8, D&C 46:17, and Moroni 10:9 is a [[Spiritual Gifts|spiritual gift]], not the dietary revelation. Both [[Spiritual Gifts (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Spiritual Gifts (True to the Faith)]] explicitly warn against this conflation.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canonical core is broader than a short prohibited-substances list. [[D&C 89]] presents care of embodied life as part of discipleship and “temporal salvation” (D&C 89:2). It contains:

- a warning about exploitative “evils and designs” (D&C 89:4);
- restrictions concerning wine, strong drink, tobacco, and hot drinks (D&C 89:5–9);
- affirmative counsel about wholesome plants, fruit, grain, gratitude, prudence, and restrained use of meat (D&C 89:10–17); and
- promises of health, wisdom, knowledge, endurance, and divine preservation to those who keep the sayings and walk in [[Obedience]] (D&C 89:18–21).

The text itself initially says “not by commandment or constraint” and calls the revelation a “principle with promise” adapted to the weak (D&C 89:2–3). Current official Church teaching nevertheless treats the interpreted abstinence standard as binding religious practice. [[How did the Word of Wisdom become a commandment]] is therefore essential to a precise account: the original wording and the current standard are related but not identical historical objects.

The promises are covenantal, not a simple health equation. Neither D&C 89 nor responsible experience warrants the inference that every observant person will be free from disease, disability, fatigue, or early death. “Run and not be weary” echoes Isaiah 40:31, while the destroying angel evokes [[Passover]] (D&C 89:20–21; Exodus 12:12–13, 21–23). Those allusions enlarge the doctrine beyond nutrition to endurance, wisdom, belonging, and divine care.

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
Ancient scripture supplies comparisons, not an earlier version of the complete Latter-day Saint code. [[Judges 13]] restricts wine and strong drink in connection with Samson’s Nazirite consecration. [[Isaiah 28]] condemns intoxication among priests and prophets. [[Daniel 1]] portrays [[Daniel (prophet)]] refusing royal food and wine because of defilement and successfully undertaking a trial diet. These texts illuminate abstinence, consecration, and disciplined fidelity, but none names the Word of Wisdom or contains all of D&C 89.

A separate revelation, [[D&C 27]], addressed the source and ritual significance of sacramental wine before D&C 89. It should not be conflated with the later, broader health revelation merely because both mention wine and the [[Sacrament]].

D&C 89 was received in the [[Kirtland]] setting in 1833. The Joseph Smith Papers’ “Historical Introduction, Revelation, 27 February 1833 [D&C 89],” referenced through [[15 Holy Places]], places it amid contemporary discussion of temperance, tobacco, diet, and hot beverages. Historical context explains the questions and vocabulary surrounding the revelation; it does not, by itself, decide whether its origin was divine, naturalistic, or both culturally situated and revelatory.

Practice changed gradually. Nineteenth-century observance was uneven, including among leaders, and the text’s advisory framing remained visible. Historical work cited in [[The Word of Wisdom]], including Paul H. Peterson’s *An Historical Analysis of the Word of Wisdom*, and Thomas G. Alexander’s “The Word of Wisdom: From Principle to Requirement,” traces the move toward stricter observance. By the early twentieth century, Church leaders increasingly presented the Word of Wisdom as a binding standard connected with worthiness. [[Conference Report, October 1923 (General Conference (full report), October 1923)]] and [[Conference Report, April 1926 (General Conference (full report), April 1926)]] are useful primary witnesses to that established emphasis.

Current practice is therefore best described as an authoritative institutional interpretation and development of the 1833 principle—not as a verbatim restatement of every dietary distinction found in the original revelation.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong textual findings

The textual foundation is secure: [[D&C 89]] calls itself a revelation about temporal salvation, specifies substances and foods, and states promises (D&C 89:1–21). Two intertexts are especially strong. D&C 89:20 closely echoes Isaiah 40:31, and D&C 89:21 explicitly compares promised preservation to the children of Israel, invoking [[Passover]] and [[Exodus 12]]. These observations are strong; conclusions about guaranteed health outcomes are not.

The text also supplies strong evidence that its initial presentation and modern administration should be distinguished. “Not by commandment or constraint” and “a principle with promise” occur in D&C 89:2–3. Conversely, the contemporary worthiness standard is evident in official Church teaching and twentieth-century institutional practice. This establishes development; it does not settle whether development constitutes legitimate continuing [[Revelation]] or human policy change.

### Useful but limited comparisons

[[Daniel 1]] is a meaningful devotional parallel involving wine, diet, fidelity, and health under social pressure. Its relevance is analogical, not predictive or genealogical. [[1 Corinthians 3]] teaches that the Saints are God’s temple (1 Corinthians 3:16–17), but it neither names the Word of Wisdom nor teaches a dietary code. [[Judges 13]] and [[Isaiah 28]] are likewise thematic parallels and were correctly flagged as ambiguous keyword matches.

### Historically significant complexity

D&C 89:5–6 permits wine in a sacramental setting, and D&C 89:17 mentions barley for mild drinks. D&C 89:12–13 gives conspicuous counsel to use meat sparingly, while that provision is not policed as a comparable modern minimum worthiness test. These are not trivial loopholes; they are evidence that the original document’s categories and the current administrative standard do not map onto each other one-for-one.

Health benefits associated with avoiding tobacco and excessive alcohol can make portions of the standard prudentially compelling, but modern medical corroboration should not be overstated. It cannot empirically verify the spiritual gifts, covenantal protection, or universal fulfillment of D&C 89:18–21.

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How did language explicitly introduced as “not by commandment or constraint” become a binding worthiness standard? Begin with [[How did the Word of Wisdom become a commandment]], [[The Word of Wisdom]], and the 1923 and 1926 conference reports.

2. What did “hot drinks” mean to the revelation’s first hearers, and how much interpretive authority should later prophetic usage carry? Compare D&C 89:9 with [[Word of Wisdom (Gospel Topics)]].

3. What are the “mild drinks” made from barley in D&C 89:17, and how should that wording be related to modern total abstinence from alcohol?

4. Why does modern worthiness practice emphasize alcohol, tobacco, coffee, and tea but not enforce the instruction to eat meat sparingly in the same manner (D&C 89:12–13)? Is that a principled distinction, an administrative choice, or historical selectivity?

5. Are the promises in D&C 89:18–21 primarily physiological, spiritual, covenantal, communal, or some combination? How should they be read when faithful observers experience serious illness?

6. Does the presence of familiar nineteenth-century health-reform ideas count against revelation, demonstrate divine accommodation to contemporary language, or remain compatible with either model?

7. How should D&C 89’s sacramental-wine exception be read alongside [[D&C 27]] and present use of water in the [[Sacrament]]?

8. Where is the boundary between an enduring revealed principle and a changeable administrative application? This question connects the dossier to [[Revelation]], [[Obedience]], [[Agency]], and [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]].

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest historical objection is that the modern commandment is not simply what the 1833 text says. D&C 89:2 expressly says “not by commandment or constraint”; nineteenth-century Saints practiced it unevenly; and strict worthiness enforcement emerged later. Calling the present standard unchanged since 1833 is historically inaccurate.

A second objection concerns selective application. Modern practice makes certain abstentions decisive while generally not treating the command to eat flesh sparingly as a comparable recommend question. The wine exception and reference to barley-based mild drinks also resist a flat equation between the revelation’s wording and the contemporary rule. A defender may appeal to continuing prophetic authority to specify administrable requirements, but that response concedes development rather than erasing it.

A third objection is explanatory: temperance, tobacco criticism, dietary reform, and suspicion of hot beverages were already available in Joseph Smith’s environment. A naturalistic account can therefore describe D&C 89 as a religious synthesis of contemporary reform concerns. The strongest believing response is not that no parallels exist; they plainly do. It is that revelation commonly addresses live questions through culturally available language and may select, reorder, or spiritualize existing concerns. Historical comparison alone cannot adjudicate between those larger accounts.

A fourth objection targets the promises. Observant Saints still become ill, tire, and die. Reading D&C 89:18–21 as an exceptionless medical guarantee is both textually overconfident and pastorally harmful. The Isaiah and Passover allusions favor a covenantal idiom of divine sustenance and preservation, although that reading does not eliminate every question about apparent nonfulfillment.

Finally, proof-texting [[Daniel 1]], [[1 Corinthians 3]], [[Judges 13]], or [[Isaiah 28]] as if those passages directly taught the modern Word of Wisdom overstates the canon. They offer analogies and broader principles, not independent attestations of the 1833 code.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
The broad historical outline is less disputed than its theological meaning. Documentary and institutional histories generally recognize an 1833 revelation embedded in contemporary health-reform discussion, uneven nineteenth-century compliance, and gradual movement toward a stricter twentieth-century requirement. Thomas G. Alexander’s “The Word of Wisdom: From Principle to Requirement” and Paul H. Peterson’s *An Historical Analysis of the Word of Wisdom* are central studies cited by the supplied library. The Joseph Smith Papers’ historical introduction provides primary-source context; [[15 Holy Places]] directs readers to it and to further specialist work.

Believing scholarship commonly treats cultural setting and gradual implementation as compatible with revelation: God addressed a real historical community, gave a principle adapted to weak Saints, and authorized later prophets to make its application more definite. [[Word of Wisdom (Gospel Topics)]] presents this development within a faithful institutional framework.

Critical or naturalistic scholarship can accept the same chronology while interpreting the revelation as a product of nineteenth-century reform culture and later enforcement as institutional consolidation. On that model, familiar health ideas and evolving practice reduce the need to posit supernatural disclosure.

The disagreement is therefore not chiefly over whether context and development existed. It is over what they signify. No mere catalogue of parallels proves borrowing, and no subsequent health benefit proves divine authorship. Likewise, showing that practice developed does not alone prove illegitimacy. A responsible synthesis keeps the documentary history firm and the theological verdict proportionate to premises that historical method cannot independently verify.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Read [[D&C 89]] in full before reducing it to a list. Mark audience and framing (vv. 1–4), discouraged substances (vv. 5–9), commended foods and limitations (vv. 10–17), and conditions and promises (vv. 18–21).

2. Compare the original wording with present official teaching in [[Word of Wisdom (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Chapter 29 Living the Word of Wisdom]]. List what is explicit in the revelation, what is later interpretation, and what is current administrative application.

3. Trace the principal biblical allusions: Isaiah 40:28–31 for endurance and [[Exodus 12]] for the destroying angel and [[Passover]]. Ask what these images contribute beyond physical-health language.

4. Study ancient analogies carefully: [[Judges 13]] for Nazirite restriction, [[Isaiah 28]] for intoxication and impaired judgment, and [[Daniel 1]] for fidelity through dietary discipline. Record both similarities and differences.

5. Examine [[D&C 27]] with D&C 89:5–6 to distinguish sacramental instruction from the health revelation and to understand later sacramental practice.

6. Resolve the terminological false friend by comparing 1 Corinthians 12:8, D&C 46:17, and Moroni 10:9 with [[Spiritual Gifts (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Spiritual Gifts (True to the Faith)]].

7. Study institutional development through [[How did the Word of Wisdom become a commandment]], [[The Word of Wisdom]], [[Conference Report, October 1923 (General Conference (full report), October 1923)]], and [[Conference Report, April 1926 (General Conference (full report), April 1926)]].

8. Return to the ethical and theological questions through [[Agency]], [[Obedience]], [[Revelation]], and [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]]. Distinguish documented history from judgments about prophetic authority.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The Word of Wisdom is both an 1833 text and a living Latter-day Saint practice, and trustworthy study must not collapse the two. [[D&C 89]] presents a revelation for temporal salvation, initially “not by commandment or constraint” and adapted to weak Saints. It warns against specified substances, commends wholesome foods and gratitude, calls for sparing use of meat, and promises health, wisdom, endurance, and preservation (D&C 89:1–21).

Its literary world is scriptural. The endurance promise echoes Isaiah 40:31, and the destroying-angel promise invokes [[Passover]]. [[Daniel 1]], [[Judges 13]], and [[Isaiah 28]] provide meaningful analogies involving disciplined diet or intoxicants, but none is the Word of Wisdom. Similarly, the “word of wisdom” in the spiritual-gift lists is a different concept, as [[Spiritual Gifts (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Spiritual Gifts (True to the Faith)]] make explicit.

Its institutional history is developmental. The text does not define “hot drinks,” includes sacramental wine, refers to barley-based mild drinks, and foregrounds sparing meat consumption. Later leaders identified coffee and tea, strengthened abstinence expectations, and eventually connected observance with worthiness. [[How did the Word of Wisdom become a commandment]] and the 1923 and 1926 conference reports are therefore not peripheral: they explain the transition from principle to requirement.

The historical facts support neither an easy apologetic nor an easy dismissal. Contemporary reform parallels are real, but parallels do not prove plagiarism or disprove revelation. Development is real, but its legitimacy depends partly on whether one accepts continuing prophetic authority. Some health counsel has substantial prudential value, but that does not scientifically verify every covenantal promise. The most defensible conclusion is layered: the wording and history are documentable; the modern standard is an authoritative Latter-day Saint development; its divine authority remains a theological judgment; and faithful application should avoid turning promised blessing into blame for people who become ill.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
