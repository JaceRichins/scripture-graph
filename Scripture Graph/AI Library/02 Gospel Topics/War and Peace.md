# War and Peace

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, **war** can mean organized armed conflict between peoples, civil conflict, a divinely narrated judgment, or—especially in apocalyptic writing—a symbolic or cosmic struggle. These senses must not be silently merged. Revelation 12's heavenly war is not automatically a manual for earthly violence, and the armies called “Lamanites” in [[Alma 43]] are a compound political force that includes Nephite dissenters (Alma 43:13–14), not a simple racial category.

**Peace** likewise has at least two related senses. Politically, it is the ending or avoidance of violent conflict: Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3 envision weapons transformed and nations no longer learning war. Spiritually, it is reconciliation and settled trust through [[Jesus Christ]]. [[Peace (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Peace (True to the Faith)]] explicitly distinguish gospel peace from the mere absence of war. The inward meaning should deepen, not replace, the public command to “renounce war and proclaim peace” in [[D&C 98]] (D&C 98:16).
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon's clearest presumption is toward peacemaking. Jesus blesses peacemakers, commands love of enemies, and rejects violent defense at his arrest (Matthew 5:9, 44; 26:51-52; [[3 Nephi 12]]:9, 44). [[D&C 98]] commands renunciation of war and requires repeated peace initiatives -- a standard of peace offered and refused up to three times -- before divinely authorized battle (D&C 98:16, 33-36).

The canon nevertheless permits some defensive force. In [[Alma 43]], the Nephites defend lives, families, liberty, and religion after attack (Alma 43:45-47). [[Captain Moroni]] teaches nonaggression while preparing fortifications and permitting the sword to preserve life ([[Alma 48]]:7-16). In [[3 Nephi 3]], Gidgiddoni judges that a preemptive strike would fail and instead chooses gathering and fortified defense (3 Nephi 3:20-21) -- his own reasoning in the narrative, not a cited divine command against preemption. These texts support a constrained-defense ethic; they do not provide a mechanical test declaring every war labeled "defensive" righteous.

The canon also honors a more radical response. The [[Anti-Nephi-Lehi]] bury their weapons and accept death rather than shed blood again ([[Alma 24]]:11-22). Their sons fight only because they had not entered the same covenant ([[Alma 53]]:10-17). The text thus preserves both principled nonviolence and reluctant defense without announcing that one vocation fits every person and circumstance. The Zion's Camp revelations ([[D&C 103]], [[D&C 105]]) apply a related peace-first logic to a specific 1834 crisis, though that episode's outcome -- an armed company organized but disbanded without battle -- resists being reduced to a clean confirmation of any single procedure.

Official Latter-day Saint devotional teaching adds that peace through the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] and the Holy Ghost may exist even amid war; see [[Peace (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Peace (True to the Faith)]]. That claim concerns personal reconciliation and discipleship. It must not be used to minimize casualties, displacement, widows, or fatherless children, whose suffering [[Mosiah 21]]:9-17 makes explicit.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
Israel's scriptures remember God as deliverer in battle, narrate conquest, and sometimes attribute exterminatory commands to God ([[Deuteronomy 20]]:16–18; [[Numbers 31]]:1–18). They also restrict warfare, condemn cruelty to captives ([[2 Chronicles 28]]:9–15), and envision an international order in which nations cease learning war (Isaiah 2:4; Micah 4:3). [[Zechariah 9]] places a lowly king who proclaims peace beside continuing divine-war imagery, leaving interpreters to ask how judgment and peace relate.

Jesus makes peacemaking, enemy-love, and nonretaliation central to discipleship (Matthew 5:9, 39, 44; [[3 Nephi 12]]:9, 39, 44). The New Testament nevertheless retains civil “sword” language (Romans 13:4) and apocalyptic divine combat ([[Revelation 12]]; [[Revelation 19]]). Later Christians developed competing pacifist and just-war traditions; neither later system appears fully formulated in a single New Testament passage.

The Book of Mormon's history is saturated with war but rarely treats it as costless. Zeniff's account moves from reluctance to destroy an enemy through defensive killing to burial and lamentation ([[Mosiah 9]]:1–2, 16–19). [[Mosiah 21]] foregrounds bereaved families. The [[Conversion of the Anti-Nephi-Lehies]] produces weapon burial, while [[Captain Moroni]] represents fortified, limited defense. Mormon ends his own civilization's war record by urging descendants to lay down weapons and cease delighting in bloodshed ([[Mormon 7]]:4).

Restoration revelations arose amid threats and dispossession. [[D&C 98]] combines patience, constitutional redress, forgiveness, peace offers, and conditional battle; [[D&C 103]] and [[D&C 105]] belong to the setting of [[Zion's Camp]] and should be read with [[What was Zion's Camp]]. They document a developing response to actual violence rather than an abstract treatise detached from history.

Modern Latter-day Saint discourse has contained peace advocacy alongside acceptance of members' military obligations. [[Gospel Doctrine]] gathers teachings of [[Joseph F. Smith]] on war and peace. [[Conference Report, April 1898 (General Conference (full report), April 1898)]] addresses righteousness, peace, and contemporary war sentiment; [[Conference Report, October 1914 (General Conference (full report), October 1914)]] reflects the shock of European war; [[Conference Report, October 1916 (General Conference (full report), October 1916)]] declares institutional love of peace and detestation of war; and [[Conference Report, October 1918 (General Conference (full report), October 1918)]] distinguishes living in a land untouched by combat from a nation participating abroad. [[theSources Cited]] points to John Sillito's study of [[B. H. Roberts]] and his World War I peace advocacy. Earlier cultural witnesses such as [[Autobiography of Parley P. Pratt]], [[Life of Heber C. Kimball]], and [[15 Holy Places]] preserve additional Restoration-era language and settings, but reported reminiscence and sermon literature should not automatically be treated as binding doctrine.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong observations

- [[D&C 98]]:16, 33–36 expressly puts renunciation and peace initiatives before conditional battle authorization.
- [[Alma 43]]:45–47, [[Alma 48]]:11–16, and [[3 Nephi 3]]:20–21 converge on defense, reluctance, and rejection of aggression.
- [[Alma 24]]:11–22 praises a community that would die rather than repeat its bloodshed.
- [[Mosiah 21]]:9–17 and [[Mosiah 9]]:18–19 refuse to reduce war to victory statistics; grief, burial, widows, and fatherless children remain visible.
- [[Mormon 7]]:4 concludes the record with weapon renunciation and rejection of delight in bloodshed, though it retains an exception for divine command.

Together these observations strongly support a presumption for peace and strict moral constraint on force. They do **not** establish absolute pacifism, a general theory of international law, or the righteousness of any modern campaign.

### Morally adverse evidence

[[Numbers 31]]:1–18 reports divinely initiated vengeance and Moses's order to kill surviving boys and nonvirgin women. [[Deuteronomy 20]]:16–18 commands destruction of specified Canaanite populations. These are not marginal to the subject and cannot honestly be softened into ordinary self-defense. Possible theological or historical explanations—unique divine judgment, ancient war rhetoric, later ideological narration, accommodation to an ancient culture, or fallible human attribution—have sharply different implications and are disputed. None removes the wording that the received text actually contains.

### Literary and historical cautions

Nephi's distinction between ministry plates and records of kings, wars, and contentions ([[1 Nephi 9]]:2–4) shows editorial selection, not a claim that military history is spiritually neutral. Revelation's horsemen and heavenly combat are symbol-rich visions; mapping them directly onto a named modern event exceeds what the passages establish. Historical reports and conference sermons illuminate Latter-day Saint responses to particular wars, but their genre and authority must be assessed separately from canon and current official teaching.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- When does defense become retaliation, conquest, or preventive war? [[Alma 48]] and [[3 Nephi 3]] give principles but no universal modern checklist.
- Does Jesus's enemy-love require Christian pacifism, or can it coexist with limited public force under [[Government and Laws]]? Compare Matthew 5:38–48, Matthew 26:51–52, Romans 13:1–4, and [[D&C 98]].
- Is the [[Anti-Nephi-Lehi]] covenant an exceptional act of repentance, an exemplary higher law, or both? How should their sons' distinct choice in [[Alma 53]] affect the answer?
- What moral account can be given of [[Numbers 31]] and [[Deuteronomy 20]]? The connected question [[Why did God command the conquest of Canaan]] should be faced rather than bypassed.
- Who may reliably identify a genuine divine command to fight, especially when opposing communities both invoke God? [[Obedience]] cannot by itself solve the prior problem of discerning the command.
- How should civilian immunity, captivity, displacement, trauma, and responsibility to protect the vulnerable figure in a scriptural ethic centered on [[Family]], [[Justice and Mercy]], and [[Freedom and Liberty]]?
- Does Revelation 12 describe [[Premortal Life]], Christ's earthly victory, continuing spiritual persecution, an end-time conflict, or overlapping symbolic horizons?
- Can inward peace described in [[Peace (Gospel Topics)]] coexist faithfully with protest against unjust public violence, or can spiritualized language inadvertently conceal material suffering?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objection to a harmonious scriptural ethic is that the texts do not merely differ in emphasis: some command enemy-love and nonretaliation, while others attribute mass killing to God. [[Numbers 31]]:17–18 is especially difficult because children and captive women are explicitly sorted for death or preservation. [[Deuteronomy 20]]:16–18 presents destruction of Canaanite populations as commanded. A synthesis that discusses only [[Captain Moroni]] and [[D&C 98]] would evade rather than answer this evidence.

A pacifist objection argues that Jesus's teaching and example control Christian conduct: love of enemies, refusal of retaliation, and the cross exclude lethal discipleship. On this view, Romans 13 describes what states do rather than authorizing Christians to do it, and Book of Mormon defensive wars describe a pre-Christian or morally accommodated situation. The [[Anti-Nephi-Lehi]] are then the clearest Book of Mormon embodiment of Jesus's way.

A just-war response argues that refusing force can abandon victims to aggressors. [[Alma 43]]:45–47 and [[3 Nephi 3]]:20–21 distinguish protective defense from aggression, while Romans 13:4 assigns coercive responsibility to civil authority. Yet this response remains vulnerable to self-serving labels: nearly every belligerent describes its violence as necessary, defensive, or protective.

A historical-critical objection treats conquest and divine-war narratives as products of ancient political theology and literary construction rather than reliable transcripts of divine orders. This can explain moral and historical tensions but conflicts with readings that assume each narrated command directly and transparently represents God's timeless moral will.

A further objection concerns asymmetry. Scriptural narrators often belong to the victorious or covenant-claiming side; enemy motives and civilian experiences may be thinly represented. The Book of Mormon partly corrects this through mourning, captivity, dissenters, converted enemies, and the collapse at [[Cumorah]], but its wars still reach readers through interested narrators rather than neutral reporting.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Christian scholarship commonly maps several major positions: pacifism or nonviolence, classical just-war reasoning, and more permissive divine-command or crusade models. John Howard Yoder's *The Politics of Jesus* argues that Jesus's nonviolent enemy-love is politically normative for the church. Augustine and later Thomas Aquinas became foundational for just-war reasoning, which seeks legitimate authority, just cause, right intention, proportionality, and discrimination. Paul Ramsey's *The Just War* offers an influential modern Christian defense of that tradition. These frameworks are later theological constructions; identifying one with “the plain biblical position” overstates the textual simplicity.

Hebrew Bible scholarship emphasizes that biblical war texts have multiple genres and ideological functions. Susan Niditch's *War in the Hebrew Bible: A Study in the Ethics of Violence* distinguishes recurring cultural patterns rather than treating every war account identically. Lori Rowlett's *Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence* analyzes conquest rhetoric as identity-forming discourse. Historical-critical scholars frequently question whether Joshua preserves a straightforward record of rapid total conquest, while confessional scholars offer views ranging from literal judgment to limited ancient hyperbole or progressive revelation. Archaeological and literary questions do not by themselves settle the moral question of why the canonical text attributes violence to God.

Book of Mormon scholarship likewise divides over historicity but can agree on literary observations: war occupies extensive narrative space; dissent, ideology, fortification, captivity, grief, and cycles of retaliation matter; and Mormon's culminating history portrays delight in bloodshed as a sign of social ruin ([[Mormon 3]]:9–16; [[Mormon 4]]:10–12; [[Mormon 7]]:4). Believing readings often treat [[Captain Moroni]], Gidgiddoni, and the [[Anti-Nephi-Lehi]] as complementary models of faithful response. Critical readings may instead examine how the narrative legitimates Nephite power, constructs enemies, or reflects nineteenth-century political concerns. Literary observations alone do not adjudicate the book's ancient historicity.

For modern Latter-day Saint history, John Sillito's “Drawing the Sword of War against War: B. H. Roberts, World War I, and the Quest for Peace,” indexed through [[theSources Cited]], documents an internal peace tradition represented by [[B. H. Roberts]]. [[Conference Report, October 1914 (General Conference (full report), October 1914)]], [[Conference Report, October 1916 (General Conference (full report), October 1916)]], and [[Conference Report, October 1918 (General Conference (full report), October 1918)]] should be read as historically situated religious discourse, not collapsed into one timeless institutional position.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. **Begin with Jesus:** read Matthew 5:9, 38–48; Matthew 26:47–54; Luke 22:47–53; and [[3 Nephi 12]]. Record what these passages require of disciples and what questions about governments they leave unanswered.

2. **Read the Restoration rule in sequence:** study all of [[D&C 98]], especially verses 16 and 23–48. Then compare [[D&C 103]] and [[D&C 105]]:38–40 with [[Zion's Camp]] and [[What was Zion's Camp]]. Distinguish personal forgiveness, communal defense, legal redress, and claimed divine authorization.

3. **Compare three Book of Mormon responses:** flight in [[Mosiah 18]]:34–35; weapon burial in [[Alma 24]]; and constrained defense in [[Alma 43]], [[Alma 48]], and [[3 Nephi 3]]. Follow the generational distinction into [[Alma 53]] and the account of the [[Stripling warriors preserved]].

4. **Count the costs:** read [[Mosiah 9]], [[Mosiah 21]], [[Alma 54]], [[Ether 13]], [[Ether 14]], [[Mormon 3]], and the [[Final Nephite destruction at Cumorah]]. Track civilians, captives, grief, vengeance, dehumanization, and delight in bloodshed—not merely commanders and outcomes.

5. **Face the hardest Old Testament material:** read [[Deuteronomy 20]], [[Numbers 31]], Joshua 6–12, and [[2 Chronicles 28]]. Use [[Why did God command the conquest of Canaan]] to compare divine-command, rhetorical, historical-critical, accommodation, and progressive-revelation approaches.

6. **Study prophetic and apocalyptic horizons:** compare Isaiah 2:1–4, Micah 4:1–4, [[Zechariah 9]], [[Revelation 6]], [[Revelation 12]], and Revelation 19–21. Ask when imagery is earthly, symbolic, eschatological, or deliberately multivalent.

7. **Trace modern reception:** read [[Gospel Doctrine]], the four linked conference reports from 1898 through 1918, and the [[B. H. Roberts]] study listed in [[theSources Cited]]. Finish with [[Peace (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Peace (True to the Faith)]], keeping canon, historical sermon, scholarship, and present devotional teaching in separate columns.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scripture neither glorifies war without remainder nor resolves every conflict through one slogan. Its clearest moral center is peace: peacemakers are blessed, enemies are to be loved, retaliation is restrained, war is to be renounced, and prophetic hope ends the learning of war (Matthew 5:9, 44; Isaiah 2:4; [[D&C 98]]:16). Latter-day Saint teaching additionally locates enduring personal peace in [[Jesus Christ]] and the Holy Ghost; see [[Peace (Gospel Topics)]].

Yet the canon permits defensive force in some circumstances. [[Alma 43]], [[Alma 48]], and [[3 Nephi 3]] commend defense of life and liberty while rejecting aggression and delight in bloodshed. At the same time, the [[Anti-Nephi-Lehi]] embody covenantal nonviolence, and Alma the Elder's people preserve their community by flight. These are genuinely different faithful responses, not details that must be forced into a single tactic.

The canon also preserves morally disturbing violence. [[Numbers 31]] and [[Deuteronomy 20]] attribute severe killing commands to God. Believers propose divine judgment, ancient rhetorical convention, accommodation, progressive revelation, or limits in human narration; critics see ancient communities authorizing violence through divine claims. The received wording remains difficult under every approach, and no honest dossier should imply that the problem has disappeared.

The best-supported synthesis is therefore a strong presumption for peace joined to stringent, accountable limits on defense: exhaust peaceful remedies; refuse aggression, vengeance, cruelty, and delight in bloodshed; protect human life; recognize the suffering of families and enemies; and distrust easy claims that God endorses one's side. This synthesis is well supported by converging passages, but it is an interpretation rather than a complete revealed theory of modern warfare. The final scriptural horizon is not perpetual sacred combat but reconciliation under God's reign, where weapons lose their purpose and peace reaches both persons and nations.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
