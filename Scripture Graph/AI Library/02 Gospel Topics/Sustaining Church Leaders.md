# Sustaining Church Leaders

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In Restoration scripture, **common consent** is the principle that church affairs proceed through the church's voice together with order, prayer, and faith (D&C 26:2; 28:13). **Sustaining** includes public acknowledgment and continuing support through confidence, faith, prayer, patience, and conduct (D&C 21:4–5; 43:12; 107:22).

The canon distinguishes acts that should not be collapsed. D&C 41:9 speaks separately of God's call, appointment by the church's voice, and ordination. D&C 102:9 distinguishes appointment by revelation from acknowledgment in administration. Yet consent cannot be defined only as ceremonial ratification: D&C 20:65 requires a church vote before ordination in organized branches, D&C 107:22 says the Presidency is “chosen by the body” as well as appointed, ordained, and upheld, and D&C 124:144 permits approval or disapproval.

Modern practice ordinarily expresses sustaining by an uplifted hand. [[Chapter 12 Preventing Personal Apostasy]] distinguishes this from a competitive election, while [[Sustaining the Prophets (Elder Russell M. Nelson, October 2014)]] interprets it as an active commitment to support. The vote does not itself confer priesthood authority, but the cited texts leave open how constitutive the church's consent is for recognized administration.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The strongest canonical observations are:

- Church affairs are to be conducted in order and by common consent, with prayer and faith (D&C 26:2; 28:13).
- Competing private revelation does not automatically become binding on the church; D&C 28 locates churchwide commandments within an authorized ecclesiastical order.
- Calling, communal appointment or acknowledgment, and ordination are distinguishable but cooperating acts (D&C 41:9; 102:9).
- The church's role is not described uniformly as passive. D&C 20:65 requires a vote before ordination in organized branches; D&C 107:22 speaks of the Presidency being chosen by the body and upheld by confidence, faith, and prayer; D&C 124:144 allows approval or disapproval.
- Sustaining extends beyond a vote into prayerful and faithful support (D&C 21:4–5; 43:12; 107:22).
- [[D&C 104]] applies voice and common consent to treasury access within a particular economic order (D&C 104:64, 71). This demonstrates a practical consent procedure but does not by itself define general Church governance.

Modern official sources such as [[Sustaining the Prophets (Elder Russell M. Nelson, October 2014)]] and [[The Trek Continues! (Elder M. Russell Ballard, October 2017)]] teach active support and recognized ordination. Those teachings are authoritative within the tradition but should remain distinct from the narrower observations made directly by each canonical text.

These passages do not establish leader infallibility or define every disagreement as disloyalty. At the same time, D&C 21:4–5 uses exceptionally strong language about receiving prophetic words in patience and faith. [[1 Thessalonians 5]] offers a related New Testament pattern in which esteem for leaders and spiritual testing coexist.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
[[Mosiah 29]] provides a pre-Restoration civil parallel: judges are chosen through the voice of the people, partly as a restraint on concentrated rule. It is not a blueprint for priesthood appointment. [[1 Thessalonians 5]] and [[Hebrews 13]] address esteem, discernment, remembrance, and cooperation with religious leaders but do not describe common-consent voting.

In 1830, D&C 26:2 stated the common-consent principle broadly. D&C 28 applied order and consent during the controversy over [[Hiram Page]]'s competing revelations. These events must not be conflated with D&C 43's later, separate competing-revelation setting. A modern official account in [[Be One with Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2024)]] reports that the material now in [[D&C 20]] was read at the first Church conference and approved by common consent; that historical report is useful but should still be distinguished from contemporary minutes or other primary documentation.

D&C 41:9 then applied the church's voice to [[Edward Partridge]]'s appointment as bishop. D&C 102:9 distinguished revelation from acknowledgment in administration, while D&C 107:22 described the Presidency as chosen by the body, appointed, ordained, and upheld by the church. [[D&C 104]] applied common-consent language to treasury governance within the United Firm/order.

Later conference reports document continuing public explanation and performance of sustaining. The succession evidence behind [[45 An Almighty Foundation]] and [[How was Brigham Young chosen to succeed Joseph Smith]] is especially important for testing how authority claims and communal acceptance operated during conflict. The supplied solemn-assembly record should not anchor chronology until its conflicting speaker and date metadata are resolved.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong textual evidence

D&C 26:2 and 28:13 explicitly name common consent and join it with prayer, faith, and order. D&C 41:9 and 102:9 distinguish divine call or revelatory appointment from the church's appointment or acknowledgment. D&C 107:22 adds that the Presidency is chosen by the body and upheld through confidence, faith, and prayer. D&C 20:65 and 124:144 further show that voting and approval or disapproval cannot be omitted from the canonical picture.

D&C 104:64 and 71 explicitly require the voice and common consent of the order for treasury access. This is strong evidence of an intended procedural control over shared resources. Because the provisions govern a particular economic order, they do not establish democratic control throughout later Church administration.

### Moderate and limited evidence

[[Mosiah 29]] is a meaningful civil parallel but not the same institution. [[1 Thessalonians 5]] and [[Hebrews 13]] illuminate support, discernment, and accountability without supplying a common-consent procedure. The proposed developmental links from D&C 72 certificates and from D&C 28 to D&C 42 are comparatively weak; D&C 42:11's requirement that authorized ministers be known to the church is the more specific connection.

Modern talks and Church histories establish how the tradition explains common consent and document its public continuation. They do not independently prove that each calling was divinely revealed or that consent consistently constrained leaders in practice. The supplied corpus contains no systematic counts of opposition, outcomes, or perceived social pressure. It therefore cannot settle whether consent has functioned chiefly as constitutive authorization, ratification, covenantal participation, information gathering, or some combination.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What practical effect should an opposing vote have? The canon requires the church's voice but does not give a complete procedure for investigating an objection or deciding whether an appointment should be delayed.
- Is common consent constitutive of a leader's right to preside, or does it publicly acknowledge authority already received by revelation and ordination? D&C 41:9 and 102:9 distinguish the elements but do not settle every theoretical question.
- How much agreement does “common consent” require: unanimity, broad consensus, or an ordinary majority? The cited revelations do not define a numerical threshold.
- Does “all things” in D&C 26:2 and 28:13 cover every administrative act, or is it a governing ideal applied through specified procedures? [[D&C 104]] demonstrates broad temporal application but not an exhaustive jurisdiction.
- How can a member sustain a leader while conscientiously disagreeing with a particular judgment? [[1 Thessalonians 5]] suggests that esteem and discernment can coexist, but modern institutional boundaries remain a pastoral question.
- What protections allow a member to raise credible concerns about a leader without the public vote becoming either an empty ritual or an unsuitable forum for adjudicating confidential allegations?
- How should succession controversies be interpreted when more than one claimant appeals to authority and acceptance? [[How was Brigham Young chosen to succeed Joseph Smith]] and [[45 An Almighty Foundation]] provide a demanding historical test case.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objection is that a sustaining vote can function as acclamation or ceremonial ratification rather than meaningful consent. Leaders are selected before presentation, members do not choose among candidates, and an opposing vote does not have a clearly stated canonical consequence. Public voting may also impose social costs on dissent. The supplied corpus does not establish how frequent opposition is or how often it changes an outcome, so those empirical questions must remain open.

A second objection is that sustaining language can be used as a loyalty test. If patience, faith, and obedience are interpreted as agreement with every statement or administrative judgment, communal consent ceases to be reciprocal and can discourage legitimate criticism or reporting of harm. D&C 21:4–5 supplies genuinely strong authority language, while [[1 Thessalonians 5]] supplies a counterbalancing pattern of esteem joined with testing and discernment. Neither text alone defines a modern institutional procedure for disagreement.

A third objection concerns democratic analogy. [[Mosiah 29]] concerns civil government, while Restoration passages concern ecclesiastical calling and administration. Equating the systems overstates the analogy. Conversely, defining common consent only as passive acknowledgment understates D&C 20:65, D&C 107:22's “chosen by the body,” D&C 124:144's approval or disapproval, and [[D&C 104]]'s controls on common funds.

A faithful model can answer that revelation, authorized ordination, the church's voice, and covenantal support contribute different forms of legitimacy. That model fits much of the textual pattern but does not by itself resolve the practical objection: consent remains meaningful only if sincere concerns can be raised safely and reviewed seriously.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
The supplied corpus supports a clear account of **official and believing interpretation**, not a broad scholarly consensus. [[Sustaining the Prophets (Elder Russell M. Nelson, October 2014)]], [[Chapter 16 Revelation and the Living Prophet]], and [[Latter-day Saints' Southern Star 1898-12-17 (Vol. 1 No. 3)]] distinguish divine calling or priesthood authority from the church's acceptance, faith, fellowship, and support.

Documentary history asks different questions: when officers or revelations were presented, who participated, how votes were recorded, how objections were handled, and what communal acceptance did during disputed succession. The materials behind [[25 Move On to the West]], [[45 An Almighty Foundation]], and [[How was Brigham Young chosen to succeed Joseph Smith]] are more relevant to those questions than retrospective doctrinal summaries.

A critical institutional interpretation may describe sustaining as acclamation or ratification within a hierarchy, emphasizing prior selection, public visibility, and uncertain institutional consequences for opposition. A believing interpretation may understand the same observable sequence as covenantal participation in a revealed order. Both models deserve investigation, but the supplied research names no substantial non-believing monograph devoted specifically to common consent. The page therefore should not attribute either model to an established academic consensus or make unverified claims about typical historical outcomes.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with [[D&C 26]] and [[D&C 28]], especially D&C 26:2 and 28:2, 6–7, 11–13. Separate the explicit words “common consent” from interpretations of their institutional force.
2. Read [[D&C 20]], especially D&C 20:65, together with the attributed historical account in [[Be One with Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2024)]]. Distinguish voting on officers from the separate report that D&C 20 itself was approved.
3. Compare [[D&C 41]] and [[D&C 102]] (D&C 41:9; 102:9). Mark call, revelation, appointment, church voice, acknowledgment, and ordination separately.
4. Study [[D&C 21]], [[D&C 43]], and [[D&C 107]] (D&C 21:4–5; 43:12; 107:22). Include both “chosen by the body” and continuing confidence, faith, prayer, and patience.
5. Read [[D&C 104]] 104:64 and 71 for common consent in treasury governance, and [[D&C 124]] 124:144 for approval or disapproval. Compare these with D&C 42:11's requirement that authorized ministers be known to the church.
6. Compare without conflating the civil model in [[Mosiah 29]] and the New Testament leader texts in [[1 Thessalonians 5]] and [[Hebrews 13]].
7. Trace implementation through [[Organization of the Church]], [[Joseph Smith’s Teachings about Priesthood, Temples, and Women (Essay)]], and documentary historical materials.
8. Test the principle under succession pressure through [[45 An Almighty Foundation]] and [[How was Brigham Young chosen to succeed Joseph Smith]].
9. Finish with [[Sustaining the Prophets (Elder Russell M. Nelson, October 2014)]] and [[The Trek Continues! (Elder M. Russell Ballard, October 2017)]]. Ask separately what the canon says, what modern leaders teach, what historical practice demonstrates, and what remains unknown.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The canonical core is firm: D&C 26:2 and 28:13 require ordered, prayerful common consent. D&C 41:9 distinguishes God's call, appointment by the church's voice, and ordination; D&C 102:9 distinguishes revelation from acknowledgment in administration. D&C 107:22 describes the Presidency as chosen by the body, appointed, ordained, and upheld through confidence, faith, and prayer. D&C 20:65 and 124:144 add voting and approval or disapproval, while [[D&C 104]] applies common consent to shared funds within a particular economic order.

These texts support a layered model but do not produce a single simple theory. Priesthood authority is not reduced to popular preference, and sustaining is not described as competitive candidate selection. Yet the church's voice also cannot be dismissed as irrelevant or uniformly post hoc. The canon permits at least three overlapping interpretations: consent as necessary communal authorization, as acknowledgment or ratification of a revealed call, and as a covenant of continuing support. Different passages emphasize different elements.

[[Mosiah 29]] is a useful civil parallel, not an ecclesiastical blueprint. [[1 Thessalonians 5]] and [[Hebrews 13]] illuminate respect, discernment, and accountable oversight without establishing Restoration common-consent procedure. Modern official teaching strongly emphasizes active support, but it cannot by itself demonstrate how votes functioned in every historical case.

The central unresolved issue is practical force. The cited revelations do not define a voting threshold, a complete process for investigating opposition, or the circumstances in which dissent delays or defeats an appointment. The supplied evidence also cannot establish how often opposition occurs or whether public voting is experienced as free from social pressure. Critics are therefore justified in asking whether sustaining can become ceremonial or coercive; believers are justified in emphasizing its covenantal and devotional meaning. What the text establishes is an intended relationship among divine claim, authorized administration, communal voice, and continuing support. Whether particular callings are revealed by God remains a faith conclusion, not something a sustaining vote independently proves.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
