# Temples

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, a temple is first a consecrated house or precinct associated with God's name, presence, worship, sacrifice, priestly service, instruction, and covenant life. The term is not semantically uniform. It can mean the Jerusalem sanctuary ([[1 Kings 6]]), a visionary or heavenly sanctuary ([[Ezekiel 41]]; [[Revelation 15]]), Jesus's body (John 2:19-21), God's Spirit-indwelt people (1 Corinthians 3:16-17), or God and the Lamb as the immediate presence replacing a separate sanctuary in the New Jerusalem (Revelation 21:22).

In Latter-day Saint tradition, [[Temples]] are distinct from ordinary meetinghouses. Official teaching presents them as houses of the Lord where authorized ordinances and covenants—including endowment, sealing, and ordinances for the dead—are administered; see [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], [[Baptism for the Dead]], and [[Eternal Marriage]]. “Temple work” can therefore mean construction and maintenance, personal worship and covenant making, proxy ordinances, or the family-history preparation supporting those ordinances. These modern meanings should not simply be read back into every biblical occurrence.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon's most stable temple theme is divine presence ordered around holiness. Solomon acknowledges that heaven cannot contain God even while praying that God's name, attention, and mercy will be associated with the house ([[1 Kings 8]]). Prophets then use the temple both positively—as a place of teaching and gathering (Isaiah 2:2-3)—and critically, refusing to let possession of a sanctuary substitute for justice, fidelity, or repentance ([[Jeremiah 7]]).

The New Testament does not offer a single simple "replacement" formula. Jesus worships and teaches at the Jerusalem temple, confronts its misuse, and speaks of his body as a temple ([[John 2]]). Peter and John continue temple prayer after the resurrection ([[Acts 3]]). Paul applies temple identity to the Spirit-indwelt community (1 Corinthians 3:16-17). Revelation retains a heavenly temple ([[Revelation 15]]) while envisioning a final city with no separate temple because God and the Lamb are its temple ([[Revelation 21]]). The trajectory is toward unmediated divine presence, not merely toward more sacred architecture.

The Book of Mormon narrates Nephi building a temple after the manner of Solomon's (2 Nephi 5:16), Benjamin gathering families toward the temple ([[Mosiah 1]]; [[Mosiah 2]]), and the risen Christ appearing at the temple in Bountiful ([[3 Nephi 11]]; [[Christ's appearance at the temple in Bountiful]]). These passages establish temples as settings for worship, public covenant teaching, and revelation; they do not describe the full modern Latter-day Saint endowment.

Restoration scripture commands a house of prayer, fasting, faith, learning, glory, order, and God (D&C 88:119), supplies the Kirtland dedicatory prayer ([[D&C 109]]), and records heavenly manifestations and priesthood keys in that house ([[D&C 110]]; [[Priesthood keys restored in the Kirtland Temple]]). Official Latter-day Saint doctrine connects those keys and later revelation with [[Gathering of Israel]], sealing, and salvation for the dead. That is the Church's authoritative theological interpretation; the historical and supernatural claims remain matters on which believing and critical readers differ.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The biblical history begins with portable sanctuary traditions surrounding the tabernacle ([[Exodus 25]]; [[Exodus 40]]), followed by Solomon's permanent Jerusalem temple and its dedication ([[1 Kings 6]]; [[1 Kings 8]]; [[Dedication of Solomon's Temple]]). The monarchy's history includes neglect, misuse, repair, and reform: Joash (Jehoash) of Judah repairs the house ([[2 Kings 12]]; [[2 Chronicles 24]]), Hezekiah cleanses it ([[2 Chronicles 29]]), and Josiah's reform follows the discovery of a law book there ([[2 Kings 22]]). This Joash/Jehoash of Judah must not be confused with the contemporary Jehoash of Israel.

Babylon's destruction of Jerusalem created a theological crisis documented around the [[Babylonian captivity of Judah]]. Ezekiel could envision God's glory leaving the temple while also hearing that God would be "a little sanctuary" to the exiles ([[Ezekiel 10]]; Ezekiel 11:16, 22-23). The Persian-period [[Return from exile]], authorized by [[Cyrus]]'s decree, rebuilt the temple under the governor Zerubbabel and the high priest Joshua ([[Ezra 1]]; [[Ezra 3]]; [[Ezra 6]]); [[Ezra (scribe)]]'s own mission of law and reform, and [[Nehemiah (governor)]]'s rebuilding of Jerusalem's wall, both came later and concerned matters other than the temple's construction. Later expansion produced the temple complex known to Jesus's generation under [[Herod the Great]].

Jesus's relation to that temple includes family observance ([[Luke 2]]), teaching, confrontation, prophecy of destruction, and symbolic reinterpretation around his own body. Early believers still appear there in Acts, while later Christian texts increasingly apply temple language to Christ, the community, heaven, and the final divine presence. This development should not be flattened into the claim that every New Testament author held an identical temple theology.

The Book of Mormon presents temples among Nephite communities and makes the temple at Bountiful the setting for the risen Christ's ministry ([[Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)]]). Whether these accounts preserve ancient American temple practice is a larger historical question not resolved by the narrative's assertions alone.

In the Restoration, the Kirtland Temple was dedicated in 1836; [[D&C 109]] and [[D&C 110]] became foundational texts for its meaning. Nauvoo introduced and expanded rituals that became central to later Latter-day Saint temple worship. Historical development continued after Joseph Smith's death under [[Brigham Young]] and later leaders; [[Discourses of Brigham Young]] and [[Chapter 36 Receiving the Ordinances and Blessings of the Temple]] preserve important insider accounts, though neither should be treated as a neutral history.

Temple access was historically entangled with the Church's racial restriction. [[Official Declaration 2]] records the 1978 revelation extending priesthood ordination and temple blessings without regard to race or color, but does not explain the origin of the prior restriction; see [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]]. Subsequent decades brought rapid geographic expansion, documented in [[Conference Report, October 1923 (General Conference (full report), October 1923)]], [[Statistical Report, 2010 (Presented by Brook P. Hales, April 2011)]], and [[Where Are the Keys and Authority of the Priesthood (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, April 2016)]]. Growth documents institutional reach, not the truth of the associated supernatural claims.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
Strong textual evidence: temple language repeatedly coordinates divine presence, holiness, covenant, worship, authority, judgment, and communal identity across the canon. The repair accounts in [[2 Kings 12]] and [[2 Chronicles 24]], prophetic visions in [[Isaiah 2]] and [[Ezekiel 41]], Jesus's temple saying in [[John 2]], and Restoration texts in [[D&C 109]] and [[D&C 110]] are independently clear about their own literary claims.

Strong literary connection: Revelation substantially reworks Ezekiel's temple-city imagery. The measured sacred complex, tribal gates, river, fruit, and healing leaves in Ezekiel 40-48 recur in Revelation 21-22. Revelation's decisive transformation is that God and the Lamb, rather than a separate building, are the city's temple. This is strong evidence of literary dependence or shared scriptural tradition; it is not evidence that Ezekiel foresaw every detail of later Latter-day Saint temple practice.

Moderate theological continuity: ancient and modern texts share patterns of sacred space, washing or purification, anointing, priestly clothing, covenant, ascent, instruction, and divine presence. Such comparisons can illuminate how temple traditions work. They do not establish direct historical transmission, because broad ritual patterns recur across religions and because the surviving texts do not document an unbroken chain to the modern ordinances.

Historically important but evidentially limited: [[D&C 110]] strongly establishes what the canonical Restoration record claims Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery experienced. Acceptance of Moses, Elias, and Elijah as objectively present depends on judgments about testimony, revelation, and canonical authority; the text is not independent verification of its own supernatural report.

Institutional evidence: official reports reliably show construction, dedication, renovation, and wider geographic access; see [[Conference Report, October 1908 (General Conference (full report), October 1908)]], [[Closing Remarks (President Russell M. Nelson, April 2019)]], and [[“Behold I Am the Light Which Ye Shall Hold Up” (Elder Ronald A. Rasband, October 2024)]]. This evidence is strong for organizational expansion and weak as an argument for divine approval.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How do the tabernacle, Solomon's temple, the Second Temple, heavenly temple visions, Christ-as-temple, church-as-temple, and modern Latter-day Saint temples relate without being collapsed into one institution?

2. Does Revelation 21:22 describe the eventual fulfillment of temple purposes, a rejection of temple buildings, or an eschatological condition that does not determine present practice?

3. Which elements of modern endowment and sealing rites are claimed through revelation, which developed through historical adaptation, and what does legitimate ritual change mean for claims of restoration? Compare [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]] with careful historical work.

4. What can [[D&C 110]] establish to a reader who accepts Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery as witnesses, and what—if anything—can it establish independently of that prior judgment?

5. How should sacred confidentiality be balanced with informed preparation, accountability, and the needs of converts or vulnerable participants? See [[Why do Latter-day Saints have temples and ceremonies they do not discuss]].

6. How should the Church remember and repair harms associated with the former racial restriction on temple blessings? [[Official Declaration 2]] ends the restriction but does not narrate its causes; see [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]].

7. Do Book of Mormon temple accounts provide historically distinctive evidence, or are they compatible with a nineteenth-century writer drawing on the Bible? The text alone does not decide between those larger authorship models.

8. How should worthiness interviews and restricted access be related to biblical holiness boundaries, the gospel's invitation to all, pastoral care, and institutional authority?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest historical objection is that modern Latter-day Saint ordinances are not described in the extant Israelite, Jewish, or early Christian temple sources in their present form. Shared motifs are real, but similarity does not establish descent. A critic can plausibly explain much of the resemblance through Joseph Smith's biblical environment, contemporary restorationism, Freemasonry, and subsequent ritual development. Any defense that lists parallels without testing frequency, chronology, and alternative sources overstates the evidence.

A second objection concerns secrecy and informed consent. Calling ceremonies “sacred, not secret” explains believers' intended attitude but does not eliminate concerns about advance disclosure, social pressure, changing expectations, or accountability. [[Why do Latter-day Saints have temples and ceremonies they do not discuss]] should present both the religious case for reserve and the ethical case for fuller preparation.

A third objection is theological: the New Testament can be read as relocating holiness from buildings to Christ and the community, with Revelation 21:22 finally eliminating a separate temple. Modern temple construction may therefore appear to reverse that movement. A Latter-day Saint response can view present temples as preparatory places that mediate covenant participation in the divine presence anticipated by Revelation. That response is coherent, but Revelation itself does not explicitly predict the modern temple program.

A fourth objection concerns exclusion. Historic denial of temple blessings to Black members until 1978 is not a peripheral issue because temples are taught to mediate the Church's highest ordinances. [[Official Declaration 2]] changes access but leaves major causal and moral questions unanswered. Appeals to ancient restricted sacred space cannot by themselves justify a modern racial restriction, especially when the Church no longer defends earlier racial explanations.

A fifth objection concerns resources and authority. Large building programs, worthiness requirements, and centralized ordinance administration can be criticized as costly or institutionally controlling; compare [[Why is the Church so wealthy]]. Demonstrating widespread participation or rapid construction answers neither the opportunity-cost question nor the theological truth question.

Finally, visionary accounts such as [[D&C 110]] may be sincere and spiritually powerful without being externally verifiable. A skeptic is not logically compelled to accept supernatural visitors merely because the account is canonical. Conversely, lack of independent verification does not prove fraud or delusion. The evidentiary limit cuts both ways.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship broadly recognizes the temple as more than a building: it organizes sacred space, kingship, priesthood, sacrifice, creation symbolism, national identity, and divine presence. Jon D. Levenson's Sinai and Zion (1985) is a major account of Zion and temple theology. John M. Lundquist's “What Is a Temple? A Preliminary Typology” (1983) compares recurring ancient temple patterns. Such typologies are useful heuristics, but individual temples and periods differ, and a proposed pattern is not automatically a historical genealogy.

Scholarship on Ezekiel and Revelation widely recognizes Revelation's reuse of Ezekiel 40-48. The agreement concerns literary relationship; interpreters differ over whether the resulting vision predicts a structure, symbolizes a perfected people, depicts cosmic worship, or combines these possibilities.

Believing Latter-day Saint scholarship, notably Hugh Nibley's Temple and Cosmos (1992) and work influencedby Lundquist, emphasizes ancient ritual and cosmological parallels as context for Restoration temple worship. At its strongest, this work shows that temple-centered covenant, ascent, sacred clothing, instruction, and cosmic symbolism are genuinely ancient phenomena. It becomes weaker when resemblance is treated as proof that a specific modern ceremony was transmitted intact from antiquity.

Historical scholarship documents development within the Restoration. David John Buerger's Mysteries of Godliness: A History of Mormon Temple Worship (1994) emphasizes chronology, change, and the Nauvoo endowment's Masonic setting. Jonathan A. Stapley's The Power of Godliness: Mormon Liturgy and Cosmology (2018) examines how ritual practice and priesthood cosmology developed within lived Mormonism. Believing and critical scholars can agree that forms and administration changed; they part over whether development is compatible with continuing revelation or better explained as ordinary religious innovation.

Official resources such as [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], [[Chapter 36 Receiving the Ordinances and Blessings of the Temple]], and [[Generations Linked in Love (Elder Russell M. Nelson, April 2010)]] state the tradition's doctrine and purposes. They are primary sources for what the Church teaches, not independent adjudications of antiquity, supernatural origin, or historical controversy.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
Begin with the biblical sanctuary's construction and theology: [[Exodus 25]], [[Exodus 40]], [[1 Kings 6]], and [[1 Kings 8]]. Then study holiness, priestly access, and sacrifice in [[Leviticus 16]], while resisting the assumption that every Israelite rite has a modern counterpart.

Next trace loss, criticism, and restoration: [[2 Kings 12]], [[2 Kings 22]], [[Jeremiah 7]], [[Ezekiel 10]], [[Ezekiel 11]], [[Ezra 1]], and [[Ezra 3]]. Add the prophetic horizon in [[Isaiah 2]], [[Malachi 3]], [[Zechariah 6]], and [[Zechariah 14]].

Read Jesus and the early church without forcing premature harmonization: [[Luke 2]], [[John 2]], [[Matthew 27]], [[Acts 3]], and [[1 Corinthians 3]]. Finish the biblical sequence with [[Revelation 15]], [[Revelation 21]], and [[Revelation 22]], comparing them closely with Ezekiel 40-48.

Then examine the Book of Mormon's distinct scenes: Nephi's construction in [[2 Nephi 5]], the family-oriented assembly and covenant discourse in [[Mosiah 1]] through [[Mosiah 5]], and the risen Christ at Bountiful in [[3 Nephi 11]] through [[3 Nephi 18]]. Use [[King Benjamin's address]] and [[Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)]] to follow the larger settings.

For Restoration doctrine, read [[D&C 88]], [[D&C 109]], and [[D&C 110]] in sequence, then connect them with [[Kirtland Temple dedication]], [[Priesthood keys restored in the Kirtland Temple]], [[Baptism for the Dead]], [[Eternal Marriage]], and [[Vision of the redemption of the dead]]. Read [[Official Declaration 2]] alongside—not instead of—[[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]].

Finally compare official interpretation with history and critical questions: [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], [[Chapter 36 Receiving the Ordinances and Blessings of the Temple]], [[Discourses of Brigham Young]], and [[Why do Latter-day Saints have temples and ceremonies they do not discuss]]. For each stage, record separately what the source says, what an interpreter infers, and what larger conclusion the evidence can actually bear.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Across the canon, temples concentrate a larger problem: how a transcendent God can dwell with, teach, judge, forgive, and covenant with embodied communities. Israel's sanctuary locates holiness without containing God; prophets defend its sanctity while attacking confidence in the building divorced from obedience. Exile proves that divine presence is not geographically captive. Jesus both participates in temple life and redirects temple language toward his body. Paul extends it to the Spirit-indwelt community. Revelation preserves heavenly sanctuary imagery but ends with God and the Lamb as the New Jerusalem's temple.

The Book of Mormon continues temple-centered worship and covenant assembly, especially in [[King Benjamin's address]] and [[Christ's appearance at the temple in Bountiful]]. Restoration scripture then commands sacred houses and interprets the Kirtland Temple through dedication, divine appearance, priesthood keys, gathering, sealing, and endowment ([[D&C 88]]; [[D&C 109]]; [[D&C 110]]). Modern Latter-day Saint doctrine extends that system through ordinances for the living and dead, family sealing, and worldwide temple service.

The strongest conclusion is thematic, not genealogical: sacred presence, holiness, covenant, authorized service, instruction, and transformation form a durable temple complex across these texts. The canon does not by itself prove that modern ordinances reproduce one unchanged ancient ceremony. Ancient parallels can support contextual plausibility but cannot carry that larger historical claim without additional evidence.

An honest account must also include development, restricted information, institutional power, changing ritual forms, financial costs, and the exclusion of Black members from temple blessings before 1978. These facts do not logically disprove the theology, but neither can they be dismissed as irrelevant to it. [[Official Declaration 2]] is therefore part of temple history, not a footnote.

On the tradition's own terms, a temple is not finally valuable as architecture. Its stated end is a people reconciled to God and one another through Christ, covenant, holiness, service, and intergenerational belonging. Revelation's city without a separate temple supplies the controlling caution: every sacred house is provisional relative to the reality it signifies—God dwelling directly with God's people.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
