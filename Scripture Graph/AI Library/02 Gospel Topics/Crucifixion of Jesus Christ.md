# Crucifixion of Jesus Christ

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
Crucifixion was a Roman mode of public execution in which a condemned person was affixed to a cross or related wooden structure. In this dossier, the term chiefly denotes the execution and death of [[Jesus Christ]] under [[Pilate]], narrated in [[Matthew 27]], [[Mark 15]], [[Luke 23]], and [[John 19]]. The Gospels distinguish the sentence, scourging, journey to [[Golgotha]], fastening to the cross, death, and burial, even when later Christian usage calls the whole sequence “the Crucifixion.”

Theologically, scripture uses the cross for more than the mechanics of death. Paul makes “Christ crucified” central to proclamation (1 Corinthians 1:23; 2:2), Galatians 3:13 interprets the death through Deuteronomy's curse language, and Colossians 2:14 portrays a hostile record as nailed to the cross. In Latter-day Saint tradition, [[Cross (Gospel Topics)]] identifies the Crucifixion as part of the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], together with [[Suffering in Gethsemane]] and the [[Resurrection of Jesus]]. These components are connected but should not be conflated: “Crucifixion” names the execution, while “Atonement” names the larger saving work and its effects.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon consistently treats the death of Christ as purposeful without reducing it to one explanatory metaphor. He dies “for our sins” (1 Corinthians 15:3); becomes a curse to redeem others from the law's curse (Galatians 3:13); has the hostile record nailed to his cross (Colossians 2:14); and fulfills sacrificial patterns outside the camp (Hebrews 13:11-13). [[1 Nephi 11]] describes him as slain for the sins of the world (1 Nephi 11:33), while [[3 Nephi 27]] connects his being lifted up with drawing humanity to him and bringing all before him for [[Final Judgment]] (3 Nephi 27:14-15).

The cross cannot be isolated from the [[Resurrection]]. Paul's summary moves from death and burial to rising on the third day, and he says faith is vain if Christ was not raised (1 Corinthians 15:3-4, 14-22). [[Doctrine and Covenants 138]] additionally places the [[Vision of the redemption of the dead]] between Crucifixion and Resurrection, presenting Christ's apparent absence from mortality as active ministry in the world of spirits.

Official Latter-day Saint framing is represented by [[Cross (Gospel Topics)]], which places Gethsemane, Crucifixion, and Resurrection within Christ's atoning sacrifice and the [[Plan of Salvation]]. That official synthesis should remain distinct from narrower exegetical claims about what any single biblical author intended. The New Testament itself offers several complementary images rather than a fully systematized theory of how atonement works.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
Israel's scriptures predate the Gospel narratives and possess literary settings of their own. Early Christians reread passages such as Psalm 22:18, Psalm 31:5, Zechariah 12:10, and the Passover bone regulations in light of Jesus's death. [[John 19]] explicitly presents some correspondences as scriptural fulfillment. This securely demonstrates early Christian reception; it does not automatically prove that each older author consciously described Roman crucifixion or intended Jesus as the passage's only referent.

During Jesus's mortal ministry, the Synoptic narratives portray him as repeatedly anticipating rejection, death, and resurrection, eventually naming scourging and crucifixion (Matthew 20:18-19; Mark 10:33-34; Luke 18:31-33). Historically, scholars disagree over how much of the detailed wording derives from Jesus before Easter and how much reflects post-Easter formulation by the evangelists.

Paul's letters, generally dated earlier than the written Gospels, already treat “Christ crucified” as the heart of Christian proclamation (1 Corinthians 1:23; 2:2). The four Gospels subsequently supply extended narratives with a stable core and meaningful differences in chronology, sayings, and theological emphasis. The differences belong to the evidence and should be studied through [[Why do the Gospels differ from one another]].

Within Book of Mormon narrative chronology, Nephi and Jacob prophesy the Crucifixion long before the first century (1 Nephi 11:33; 2 Nephi 6:9; 10:3-5; 25:12-14). After the Resurrection, Christ interprets his own lifting upon the cross in [[3 Nephi 27]]. As external historical evidence, the predictive force of the earlier passages depends upon the separate question of the Book of Mormon's antiquity.

Restoration scripture expands the canonical account of the interval between death and Resurrection in [[Doctrine and Covenants 138]]. Later Latter-day Saint teaching, including [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]], [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[Cross (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Lifted Up upon the Cross (Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, October 2022)]], continues to place the cross within the larger Atonement rather than treating the method of execution as the whole doctrine.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest historical conclusion is narrow but important: Jesus was executed by crucifixion under Pontius Pilate. It is multiply embedded in early Christian tradition, is central rather than incidental in Paul's letters, and is reported by Tacitus in Annals 15.44. Josephus's Antiquities 18.63-64 probably retains a relevant core, but Christian interpolation makes that witness less straightforward. E. P. Sanders, John P. Meier, Raymond E. Brown, and many scholars across confessional lines regard the execution as among the most secure facts about the historical Jesus. This supports [[Did Jesus exist as a historical person]] but does not establish every Gospel detail or Christian claims about the event's saving meaning.

The narrative evidence is strong for a shared core: Roman authorization, crucifixion with other condemned men, death, and burial. It is less uniform at the level of detail. Mark 15:25 gives the third hour for crucifixion, while John 19:14-16 still places Jesus before Pilate at about the sixth hour. John's entrusted mother, “I thirst,” pierced side, blood and water, and Nicodemus's spices are not supplied by the Synoptics. Difference is not equivalent to fabrication, but neither may it be erased by an unargued harmonization.

Intertextual evidence is strongest for what early Christians believed scripture meant. John 19:37 explicitly applies Zechariah 12:10 to the pierced Jesus; the garment accounts recall Psalm 22:18; Luke 23:46 reuses Psalm 31:5; and John 19:36 may evoke Psalm 34:20, the Passover regulations, or both. These links strongly establish christological reception. They provide weaker evidence that each older text was originally an exclusive, consciously detailed prediction of the Crucifixion.

The Book of Mormon provides explicit pre-crucifixion prophecy within its own narrative world. For a believer who accepts the record's antiquity, that is significant corroboration. As a public historical argument, however, it cannot independently prove predictive prophecy unless its pre-Christian origin is established on other grounds.

No presently identified archaeological object can be securely tied to Jesus's particular cross or body. Archaeology and ancient literature illuminate Roman crucifixion generally, but historical context must not be mislabeled as direct physical evidence for this individual execution.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. What can history establish about the Crucifixion, and what belongs to revelation or theological interpretation? The execution under Pilate is historically strong; divinity, atoning efficacy, and Resurrection require additional arguments and, for believers, faith.

2. Why do the Gospel accounts differ? Compare Mark 15:25 with John 19:14-16, and compare the sayings from the cross across [[Matthew 27]], [[Mark 15]], [[Luke 23]], and [[John 19]]. [[Why do the Gospels differ from one another]] is essential background.

3. Is Luke 23:34 original to Luke? It belongs to the received Latter-day Saint KJV text, but its absence from significant early Greek witnesses makes its earliest textual status disputed.

4. In what sense did older scripture predict the cross? A responsible answer distinguishes original historical meaning, typological correspondence, and an evangelist's explicit claim of fulfillment.

5. How should divine foreordination be related to human responsibility? Acts 4:27-28 places Herod, Pilate, Gentiles, and Israel within divine counsel, while the passion narratives still hold human actors morally responsible.

6. Which model best explains the Atonement—sacrifice, victory, ransom, representation, curse-bearing, debt cancellation, substitution, or some combination? The canon uses several images and does not reduce them to one later theory.

7. How should [[Suffering in Gethsemane]] and the cross be related? Latter-day Saint doctrine assigns saving significance to both, but scripture should be examined passage by passage rather than forced into a single chronology of where or when every effect occurred.

8. What follows historically after Jesus's death? [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]] and [[Death and the Spirit World]] concern distinct claims that should not be assumed merely from the historicity of crucifixion.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest critical objection is not that Roman crucifixion was implausible; that event is broadly accepted. The sharper objections concern narrative detail and theological inference.

First, the Gospels are not four wholly independent courtroom transcripts. They are theological narratives with literary relationships, and later evangelists appear to know or share earlier traditions. Differences in hours, sayings, trial presentation, and burial details prevent a simple claim that every detail is independently confirmed. Harmonizations are possible, but some depend on assumptions not stated in the texts.

Second, critics argue that passion narratives may reflect later Christian conflict with some Jewish communities and may shift rhetorical blame toward Judean leaders or crowds while presenting Pilate more favorably than other evidence about Roman governance might suggest. Whatever one's judgment on that development, the execution remained Roman, Pilate authorized it, and the texts provide no warrant for collective or hereditary Jewish guilt.

Third, fulfillment arguments can be explained as retrospective scriptural interpretation. Followers convinced that Jesus was the Messiah could have selected, shaped, or narrated details through Israel's scriptures. This alternative is especially relevant to Psalm 22 and Zechariah 12. The strongest honest response is limited: the correspondences prove early Christian interpretation; whether they also prove supernatural prediction depends on broader judgments about revelation and the historical reliability of particular details.

Fourth, the detailed Book of Mormon prophecies may be powerful within Latter-day Saint faith but are not neutral evidence if the book's antiquity is the very point disputed. A nineteenth-century-origin model readily explains their Christian specificity. The believing response must therefore rest on the cumulative case for the Book of Mormon, not circularly use the prophecy to prove the record and the record to prove the prophecy.

Fifth, moral objections ask why forgiveness should require a violent death or why an innocent person should bear consequences associated with others' sins. Penal-substitution language can intensify this concern if treated as the canon's only model. Scripture also uses covenantal, sacrificial, representative, liberation, reconciliation, and victory motifs, but naming multiple motifs does not dissolve the philosophical problem of divine justice and suffering.

Finally, historical confirmation of Jesus's death does not prove that the death atoned for sin or that he rose. Conversely, skepticism about a theological interpretation does not make the underlying execution historically doubtful.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Across believing and non-believing historical scholarship, Jesus's crucifixion under [[Pilate]] is close to common ground. E. P. Sanders's The Historical Figure of Jesus and John P. Meier's A Marginal Jew treat it as historically secure. Martin Hengel's Crucifixion emphasizes the degradation and political terror associated with the Roman penalty. Raymond E. Brown's two-volume The Death of the Messiah compares the passion accounts in detail without pretending their differences disappear.

Scholarship parts company over reconstruction and meaning. Many critical scholars accept the execution while treating the trial speeches, fulfillment patterns, sayings from the cross, and some burial details as traditions shaped by evangelists and worshiping communities. John Dominic Crossan represents a more skeptical reconstruction of the burial tradition. Paula Fredriksen emphasizes the political and historical setting of Jesus's death. Mythicist denial that Jesus was executed because Jesus never existed remains a minority position outside the principal historical-Jesus consensus.

Believing Christian scholarship generally sees historical memory and theological shaping as compatible, though approaches range from strict harmonization to acceptance of unresolved differences. It reads the scriptural patterns as providential fulfillment or typology and treats the cross's saving meaning as disclosed by revelation, not derived from Roman history alone.

Latter-day Saint teaching shares the wider Christian affirmation that salvation depends on Christ's death and Resurrection while characteristically emphasizing [[Gethsemane]], the cross, the [[Resurrection of Jesus]], universal resurrection, and Christ's ministry among the dead. [[Cross (Gospel Topics)]] supplies an official summary; [[Lifted Up upon the Cross (Elder Jeffrey R. Holland, October 2022)]] offers modern apostolic reflection. These ecclesiastical sources establish what the tradition teaches, not what historical method alone can demonstrate.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
Begin with the narratives rather than a harmony. Read [[Mark 15]] first for its stark account, then [[Matthew 27]], [[Luke 23]], and [[John 19]]. Record the shared sequence, each distinctive saying, the named witnesses, the treatment of Pilate, and the time notices. Follow immediately with the Resurrection narratives and [[Why do the Gospels differ from one another]].

Next read the passion predictions in Matthew 16:21; 20:18-19; Mark 8:31; 10:33-34; Luke 9:22; and 18:31-33. Ask separately what each Gospel narrates and what can historically be inferred about Jesus's precise pre-Easter wording.

Then study the earliest theological interpretations: 1 Corinthians 1:18-24; 2:2; 15:3-22; Galatians 2:20; 3:13; 5:24; 6:14; Romans 6:6; Colossians 2:13-15; and Hebrews 13:11-13. List the metaphors rather than collapsing them into one theory.

Trace scriptural reception through Psalm 22:18 with the garment accounts, Psalm 31:5 with Luke 23:46, Zechariah 12:10 with John 19:34-37 and Revelation 1:7, and Exodus 12:46, Numbers 9:12, and Psalm 34:20 with John 19:36. Distinguish an older passage's contextual meaning from later christological application.

Study the Book of Mormon sequence in [[1 Nephi 11]], [[1 Nephi 19]], [[2 Nephi 6]], [[2 Nephi 10]], [[2 Nephi 25]], and [[3 Nephi 27]]. Keep internal narrative claims distinct from arguments about the record's external date. Then read [[Doctrine and Covenants 138]] alongside [[Death and the Spirit World]].

Finish with the official synthesis in [[Cross (Gospel Topics)]], then compare it with [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Suffering in Gethsemane]], [[Resurrection of Jesus]], and [[Final Judgment]]. For historical controls, consult Tacitus, Annals 15.44; Josephus, Antiquities 18.63-64 with its interpolation problem; Raymond E. Brown's The Death of the Messiah; Martin Hengel's Crucifixion; E. P. Sanders's The Historical Figure of Jesus; and John P. Meier's A Marginal Jew.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The narrow historical center is highly secure: [[Jesus Christ]] was crucified under the authority of [[Pilate]]. The four Gospel narratives, Paul's earlier proclamation, and later non-Christian testimony converge on that event even though they do not independently verify every detail. The Gospels identify several responsible actors; none licenses inherited guilt against Jews as a people.

The canon interprets the death through multiple lenses: sacrifice, curse-bearing, cancellation of debt, victory over hostile powers, covenant fidelity, representation, and the lifting up that leads to judgment. [[John 19]] rereads Israel's scriptures around the pierced and crucified Jesus. Those intertexts strongly show early Christian conviction, but by themselves they do not prove that every older passage was originally an exclusive prediction of Roman crucifixion.

The narratives also preserve real difficulties. Mark's third-hour notice and John's sixth-hour trial notice do not align easily. John's distinctive scenes and the disputed textual history of Luke 23:34 caution against treating a composite harmony as though it were a fifth Gospel. These complications limit claims about detail; they do not seriously undermine the execution itself.

Within Latter-day Saint scripture, the cross is anticipated in Nephite prophecy, interpreted by the risen Christ in [[3 Nephi 27]], and followed by ministry among the dead in [[Doctrine and Covenants 138]]. The predictive-prophecy argument is evidentially conditional on the Book of Mormon's antiquity. Official teaching in [[Cross (Gospel Topics)]] places Crucifixion within the larger [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], inseparably related to [[Suffering in Gethsemane]] and the [[Resurrection of Jesus]].

What is established historically is that Jesus died by crucifixion. What the canon teaches is that this death was voluntary, foreknown, redemptive, and overcome by Resurrection. Whether those theological claims are true cannot be settled by the bare fact of Roman execution. Christian and Latter-day Saint faith rests on the larger cumulative claims about Jesus's identity, revelation, Atonement, and Resurrection—not on suppressing the textual differences or overstating what historical evidence can prove.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
