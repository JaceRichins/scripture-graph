# The Godhead

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
“Godhead” has two related but nonidentical uses. In the King James wording of Romans 1:20 and Colossians 2:9, it means divinity or deity; neither occurrence is itself a complete three-person formula. In Latter-day Saint usage, **the Godhead** ordinarily means God the Eternal Father, Jesus Christ the Son, and the Holy Ghost, as named in Articles of Faith 1:1 and explained in [[Godhead (Gospel Topics)]].

Current Church teaching describes three distinct personages in perfect unity, with the Father presiding; see [[Chapter 2 God the Eternal Father]], [[Chapter 4 Knowing and Honoring the Godhead]], and [[The Godhead and the Plan of Salvation (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2017)]]. “Distinct” must not be confused with unrelated, and “one God” must not be silently reduced to merely cooperative agreement. Conversely, scripture's word “one” should not be assumed to carry a later technical definition without argument.

A recurrent naming hazard is the title “Father.” It normally identifies God the Father, but several Book of Mormon passages apply Father-language to [[Jesus Christ]] as Creator, covenant head, or source of spiritual life: Mosiah 3:8; Alma 11:38–39; Ether 3:14; Ether 4:12. The shared title must not be treated automatically as a shared personal referent.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon requires several affirmations to be held together:

- **Three are named and personally related.** Articles of Faith 1:1 names the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost. At Jesus's baptism, the Son is baptized, the Spirit descends, and the Father's voice speaks from heaven (Matthew 3:16–17; [[Baptism of Jesus]]). Stephen sees Jesus at God's right hand (Acts 7:55–56). The canonized [[First Vision]] account has one personage introduce the other as His Son (Joseph Smith—History 1:17).
- **The three are one God.** D&C 20:28 and Mormon 7:7 explicitly name Father, Son, and Holy Ghost as one God; 3 Nephi 11:27 likewise affirms their oneness. Any account that ignores these unity formulas is canonically incomplete.
- **The Father presides and sends the Son.** John 3:16–17, 34–35 and John 17 portray the Father as sender and giver, and the Son as the one sent who glorifies the Father. This functional ordering does not by itself establish inequality of divine nature.
- **Jesus is fully divine.** Colossians 2:9 says the fulness of the Godhead dwells bodily in Christ. Revelation 21:22–23 coordinates the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb as temple and light. D&C 93:12–21 combines Christ's premortal relation to the Father with His receipt of the Father's fulness.
- **The Holy Ghost is divine witness and sanctifier.** Acts 5:3–4 closely associates lying to the Holy Ghost with lying to God. Moses 1:24 says the Spirit bears record of the Father and Son.
- **Embodiment differs.** D&C 130:22–23 teaches that the Father and Son have tangible bodies of flesh and bones, while the Holy Ghost is a personage of Spirit.

The official Latter-day Saint synthesis—three distinct personages or beings, perfectly one in purpose, doctrine, love, and saving work—is taught in [[Godhead (Gospel Topics)]] and [[The Doctrines and Principles Contained in the Articles of Faith (Elder L. Tom Perry, October 2013)]]. That synthesis interprets the passages together; no single cited verse states every element in one technical formula.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
Two histories must be distinguished: the chronology of events narrated in scripture and the historical dates when the extant texts and doctrines were articulated.

Restoration scripture portrays early dispensations as knowing Father, Son, and Holy Ghost: Moses 1:24 and Moses 7:11 are clear examples. Because the Book of Moses is a nineteenth-century revelatory text about ancient events, it cannot without circularity serve as independent historical evidence for what surviving ancient Israelite sources taught. The Hebrew Bible generally emphasizes Israel's one God; references to God's spirit should not automatically be read as fully developed later pneumatology.

The New Testament gives abundant relational and triadic material without presenting the later creedal formula in one place. Matthew 3:16–17 distinguishes Father, Son, and Spirit; John 1, John 3, and John 17 describe Father–Son relations; Acts 5:3–4 associates the Spirit with God; and Colossians 2:9 gives Christ the fulness of deity. Early Christians then disputed how such claims cohered with monotheism. The controversies involving [[Arius]] and [[Athanasius]], and the deliberations associated with [[Nicaea]] under [[Constantine]], produced increasingly technical language. The history of that development is not seriously disputed; its theological meaning is. Traditional Christians see faithful clarification, while the Restoration account sees consequential loss and later recovery. See [[How did the doctrine of the Trinity develop]] and [[Are “Mormons” Christian (Essay)]].

The Book of Mormon, published in 1830, uses both strong unity formulas and Father-titles for Christ (Alma 11:28–44; Mormon 7:7; Ether 4:12). D&C 20:28, also from the Church's founding era, speaks of the three as one God. Joseph Smith's later teachings made personal and corporeal distinctions increasingly explicit: the 1838 history canonized in Joseph Smith—History distinguishes the Father and Son, and the 1843 material in D&C 130:22–23 distinguishes their bodies from the Spirit's personage. This is real development in explicitness. Believers may understand it as continuing revelation; historians may describe it as theological development. The data alone do not adjudicate revelation.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence is textual but supports different propositions with different force.

**Very strong for personal or narrative distinction:** Matthew 3:16–17, Acts 7:55–56, John 17, and Joseph Smith—History 1:17 present Father and Son as mutually related rather than interchangeable characters. Matthew 3 additionally depicts the Spirit. This defeats a simple claim that scripture never distinguishes them. It does not defeat classical Trinitarianism, which also insists on personal distinction.

**Very strong for canonical divine unity:** D&C 20:28, 3 Nephi 11:27, and Mormon 7:7 explicitly call or present the three as one. These texts prevent a responsible Latter-day Saint reading from treating the members as merely three independent gods. Their brevity leaves the mode of unity underdetermined.

**Very strong for the distinctively Latter-day Saint embodiment teaching:** D&C 130:22–23 directly states the differing corporeal condition of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost. It establishes what this canon teaches, although acceptance of its revelatory authority remains a prior faith commitment.

**Strong for Christ's divine status:** Colossians 2:9; Philippians 2:9–11; Revelation 21:22–23; and 2 Nephi 11:7 place Christ within divine identity, worship, creation, or fulness language. They do not independently settle every question about essence and person.

**Important but interpretively unstable:** John 17:20–23 supports a relational and participatory account of oneness because disciples are invited into analogous unity. Classical interpreters reasonably answer that participation in divine communion need not erase God's unique ontological unity. Ether 4:12 and Alma 11:38–44 genuinely complicate terminology by calling Christ Father while also using Father–Son language elsewhere.

**Historical evidence:** The emergence of technical creedal vocabulary is independently well documented. It establishes doctrinal development, not corruption. Likewise, later Restoration texts display increased specificity, but chronology alone cannot tell whether that is revelation, clarification, innovation, or some combination.

The isolated wording indexed from [[Conference Report, October 1928 (General Conference (full report), October 1928)]] resembles a traditional “one substance” formula. Its complete context must be examined before treating it as an endorsement or a contradiction.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. What does “one God” require beyond unity of purpose? D&C 20:28 and Mormon 7:7 demand a serious account of unity, while Matthew 3:16–17 and Acts 7:55–56 demand a serious account of distinction.
2. Is the disciples' unity in John 17:20–23 the controlling analogy for the Godhead, or only a creaturely participation in a deeper divine unity?
3. When the Book of Mormon calls Christ “Father,” does it mean Creator, covenant father, source of rebirth, bearer of divine authority, or personal identity with God the Father? Ether 4:12 and Alma 11:38–44 cannot responsibly be skipped.
4. How should John 4:24, which calls God Spirit, be related to D&C 130:22, which describes the Father as embodied? Proposed answers depend on the meaning of “spirit,” genre, embodiment, and acceptance of continuing revelation.
5. Does the Father's sending and presiding indicate only role-order, or some deeper asymmetry? John 3:34–35 and [[The Godhead and the Plan of Salvation (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2017)]] are starting points.
6. How much metaphysical precision should be demanded from scripture whose primary aims are worship, covenant, redemption, and discipleship rather than systematic theology?
7. Does historical development from D&C 20 to D&C 130 represent unfolding revelation or conceptual change? A fair answer must first acknowledge the development rather than deny it.
8. What can the [[First Vision]] establish doctrinally, and what historical questions remain when its several accounts are compared? See [[Why are there multiple First Vision accounts]].
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objections are not answered by merely repeating that the Father and Son are separate.

**Classical Trinitarian objection:** Baptismal and visionary scenes distinguish persons, something Nicene Christianity already affirms. They therefore do not prove three separate divine beings. The traditional model also argues that monotheistic formulas and Christ's inclusion in divine worship are better preserved by one divine essence than by three beings united principally in purpose.

**Charge of tritheism:** Calling the three “separate beings” can sound like three gods, especially when D&C 20:28 and Mormon 7:7 say “one God.” A sufficient Latter-day Saint account must explain why their unity is properly called divine oneness rather than a federation of deities. “One in purpose” is a useful beginning but may not exhaust the canonical claims.

**Internal-development objection:** Early Restoration texts use traditional-sounding unity language and strong Father-titles for Christ, while later teachings make embodied distinctions more explicit. Critics read this as Joseph Smith's theology changing substantially. Continuing revelation is a coherent believing framework, but invoking it does not erase the historical sequence or by itself prove divine origin.

**Book of Mormon Christology objection:** Mosiah 15:1–4, Alma 11:38–44, and Ether 4:12 can sound modalistic or otherwise closer to Father–Son identification than current Church explanations. Functional titles and divine investiture are plausible harmonizations, but they must arise from the broader text rather than serve as labels that make the difficult wording disappear.

**Biblical embodiment objection:** John 4:24 and biblical language about divine invisibility are often read against an embodied Father. Latter-day Saint responses distinguish glorified embodiment from mortal limitation and appeal to Restoration revelation, but the biblical texts alone do not state D&C 130's formulation.

**Historical objection to restoration narratives:** The claim in [[Are “Mormons” Christian (Essay)]] that Greek philosophy contributed to loss of true doctrine is a theological interpretation, not a neutral summary of scholarship. Greek conceptual tools affected Christian formulation, but early Christian evidence is diverse, and development does not logically equal apostasy.

**First Vision objection:** Its doctrinal force depends on the historical and revelatory credibility of Joseph Smith's report. Differences among the accounts deserve examination; neither dismissing all differences nor treating every difference as contradiction is adequate.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Scholarship does not divide neatly into “believing” and “non-believing.” Historians can agree on textual and chronological observations while disagreeing because of theological commitments or differing models of development.

New Testament scholars such as Larry Hurtado and Richard Bauckham emphasize how early Christian devotion and divine-identity language give Jesus an exceptionally high status within Jewish monotheism. James D. G. Dunn and Bart Ehrman place greater emphasis on diversity and development among early Christologies. These approaches differ over timing and interpretation, but neither makes the New Testament a ready-made statement of the later Nicene formula.

Patristic historians such as Lewis Ayres and J. N. D. Kelly document that fourth-century terminology emerged through extended controversy. Serious traditional scholarship does not usually claim that every conciliar technical term appears verbatim in scripture; it argues that the terms protect scripture's combined witness to monotheism, the divinity of Christ and Spirit, and personal distinction. Restorationist criticism instead regards important conciliar assumptions as departures; see [[How did the doctrine of the Trinity develop]], [[Arius]], [[Athanasius]], and [[Nicaea]].

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation reads Matthew 3, Acts 7, the [[First Vision]], and D&C 130 cumulatively as revelation of distinct personages, while reading John 17 and the Book of Mormon's unity formulas as perfect unity rather than numerical identity. Father-titles for Christ are commonly explained through creation, covenant parenthood, spiritual rebirth, and divine investiture of authority. Those explanations have real canonical support, but no single explanation obviously controls every passage.

Official sources such as [[Godhead (Gospel Topics)]], [[God the Father (Gospel Topics)]], [[Chapter 2 God the Eternal Father]], and [[The Godhead and the Plan of Salvation (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2017)]] establish present Church teaching. They should not be confused with independent historical demonstrations of what first-century Christians believed.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
A productive study proceeds from texts to synthesis rather than beginning with a preferred label.

1. **Clarify vocabulary:** read [[Romans 1]] at Romans 1:19–20 and [[Colossians 2]] at Colossians 2:8–10. Ask what “Godhead” means in each immediate argument.
2. **Map distinction and relationship:** read [[Matthew 3]], [[John 3]], [[John 14]], [[John 16]], [[John 17]], [[Acts 5]], and [[Acts 7]]. Record separately what each passage says about Father, Son, and Spirit; do not yet force a complete system.
3. **Map unity:** read D&C 20:28; 3 Nephi 11:25–36; 3 Nephi 19:20–29; and Mormon 7:7. Compare the unity of the Godhead with the prayed-for unity of disciples.
4. **Study Christ's divine status:** read [[John 1]], Philippians 2:5–11, [[Colossians 2]], and [[Revelation 21]]. Distinguish observation—what honors and functions the texts assign—from conclusions about divine substance.
5. **Face the Father-title passages:** study Mosiah 3:8; Mosiah 15:1–7; Alma 11:28–44; Ether 3:14; and Ether 4:12. Test Creator, covenant-parent, investiture, and modalistic readings against the full discourse.
6. **Study Restoration specificity:** read [[Joseph Smith—History 1]], [[D&C 76]], [[D&C 93]], and [[D&C 130]] in approximate chronological order. Note what becomes explicit and what remains unexplained.
7. **Compare institutional syntheses:** use [[Godhead (Gospel Topics)]], [[Chapter 4 Knowing and Honoring the Godhead]], and [[The Godhead and the Plan of Salvation (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2017)]]. Mark where these sources quote scripture and where they synthesize it.
8. **Add doctrinal history:** study [[How did the doctrine of the Trinity develop]], [[Nicaea]], [[Arius]], and [[Athanasius]], then compare the official restorationist argument in [[Are “Mormons” Christian (Essay)]]. Ask separately what history establishes and what each theology concludes from it.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The canon presents a stable cluster of claims but not one exhaustive metaphysical treatise. The Father, Son, and Holy Ghost are distinguishable: they speak of, send, witness of, pray to, glorify, and appear in relation to one another. They are also one God, not three unrelated deities. The Father presides; Jesus Christ is the divine, premortal Son through whom redemption and divine fulness come; the Holy Ghost bears witness and sanctifies. Restoration scripture adds the explicit teaching that the Father and Son possess tangible glorified bodies while the Holy Ghost is a personage of Spirit.

The strongest responsible Latter-day Saint formulation is therefore three distinct personages in perfect divine unity. Yet intellectual honesty requires two cautions. First, “one in purpose” is an interpretation and may not exhaust the force of “one God.” Second, scenes that distinguish Father and Son do not by themselves refute classical Trinitarianism, because that tradition also distinguishes the persons. The real disagreement concerns the kind of unity, embodiment, divine substance, and the authority of later councils versus modern revelation.

Book of Mormon Father-language for Christ is not noise to be edited out. It demonstrates that titles are flexible and that later technical categories cannot simply be imposed on every verse. Functional, creative, covenantal, and investiture readings are plausible; modalistic-sounding readings remain part of the textual challenge.

Historically, both Nicene theology and explicit Latter-day Saint Godhead teaching developed in precision over time. Traditional Christians call the former faithful clarification; Latter-day Saints call Joseph Smith's teaching restoration and continuing revelation; critics may call both human doctrinal development. History establishes the sequence and context, not which revelatory claim is true. Faith may affirm the Restoration, but the dossier should identify that affirmation as faith informed by scripture and testimony—not as a conclusion mechanically compelled by the historical evidence.
