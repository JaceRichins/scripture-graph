# Apostasy

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, apostasy is fundamentally a **turning away after some prior relation to revealed truth or covenant loyalty**. It can be individual or communal. Its forms include forsaking the Lord for other gods (Judges 2:11–13), abandoning a living source for religious substitutes (Jeremiah 2:13), repudiating Christ after receiving knowledge (Hebrews 10:26–29; 2 Peter 2:20–22), corrupting worship and practice (Alma 31:8–12), and teaching unbelief to a later generation (4 Nephi 1:38–39).

The phrase **Great Apostasy** has a narrower meaning in Latter-day Saint tradition. [[Apostasy (Gospel Topics)]] teaches that after Jesus established his Church and the Apostles died, widespread corruption and unauthorized changes resulted in priesthood authority being withdrawn. [[Restoration of the Gospel (True to the Faith)]] and [[Chapter 14 Dispensations of the Gospel]] make the claimed loss of the fulness and authority the necessary background to the [[Restoration of the Gospel]] and [[Priesthood Restoration]]. This definition does not require the stronger and historically less defensible claim that every Christian became insincere, every truth disappeared, or God ceased working among people.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon securely establishes that individuals and covenant communities can rebel after receiving genuine privileges (Judges 2:11–19; 1 Corinthians 10:1–12), that institutional promises may be conditional (1 Kings 9:4–9), and that corruption can arise through both outside pressure and internal teachers (Acts 20:29–30). Prior knowledge does not make falling away impossible (2 Peter 3:17). Widespread rebellion can also coexist with faithful individuals: [[1 Kings 19]] and Romans 11:2–5 describe a preserved remnant, although that observation alone does not settle whether authorized ministry or ordinances continued.

The New Testament anticipates a falling away ([[2 Thessalonians 2]]), departures from the faith (1 Timothy 4:1–3), false teachers (2 Peter 2:1–3), and corruption already present ([[Jude 1]]). These are strong textual observations. The further conclusion that post-apostolic developments produced a universal loss of priesthood authority is the official Latter-day Saint synthesis articulated in [[Apostasy (Gospel Topics)]], not the explicit wording of any single biblical passage.

The Book of Mormon gives that synthesis distinctive support within the Restoration canon. [[1 Nephi 13]] portrays plain and precious material being removed as the record passes to the Gentiles. [[4 Nephi 1]] associates social and religious decline with pride, class division, costly display, and teaching children not to believe; [[Alma 31]] joins doctrinal error to altered worship and abandoned religious practice.

Official contemporary teaching distinguishes institutional destiny from individual risk. [[Apostasy (True to the Faith)]] and [[In the Space of Not Many Years (Elder David A. Bednar, October 2024)]] teach that the restored Church will not be lost through another general apostasy, while individuals must still guard against falling away. That assurance is authoritative Latter-day Saint teaching, not a conclusion independently demonstrated by the historical patterns of prior dispensations.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The Hebrew Bible presents apostasy as a recurring covenantal problem rather than a single future event. [[Judges 2]] summarizes rebellion, oppression, deliverance, and relapse. Deuteronomy 32:13–18 links abundance with complacency and forgetfulness without making prosperity an inevitable cause. [[2 Kings 17]] interprets Israel's Assyrian defeat through accumulated idolatry, while [[Nehemiah 9]] retells Israel's history as repeated rebellion answered by mercy and judgment.

The New Testament places the danger inside early Christian communities. Acts 20:29–30 anticipates threats from outside and within the Ephesian leadership; Jude 1:3–4 describes corrupt teachers already present; and [[2 Thessalonians 2]] anticipates a major falling away associated with the man of sin. These texts establish serious present and anticipated crises, but they do not narrate the post-apostolic centuries or specify when authority would disappear.

The Book of Mormon depicts additional communal apostasies. [[Alma 31]] presents Zoramite departure in doctrinal, ritual, behavioral, and political terms. [[4 Nephi 1]] moves from unity to class division, competing churches, persecution, and the deliberate teaching of unbelief to children. [[Mormon 8]] portrays near-total loss of true knowledge among the surviving population while retaining an exception for disciples of Jesus. [[2 Nephi 26]] concerns the distinct dwindling of Lehi's descendants and should not be reassigned to post-apostolic Europe.

Latter-day Saint teaching places the Great Apostasy within a dispensational pattern of rejection and renewed revelation. [[The Gathering of Scattered Israel (Elder Russell M. Nelson, October 2006)]] states that earlier dispensations ended in apostasy. [[Apostasy (Gospel Topics)]], [[Chapter 1 The Restoration of the Gospel]], and [[Chapter 14 Dispensations of the Gospel]] apply the model to post-apostolic changes in doctrine, organization, ordinances, and authority. In this tradition, the answer is divine restoration through [[Joseph Smith Jr]], the [[First Vision]], and [[Priesthood Restoration]]. Other Christian traditions instead interpret substantial institutional and doctrinal continuity as preservation through development and reform.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The evidence is strongest when kept in layers.

**Strong textual evidence:** Scripture repeatedly portrays individuals and covenant communities as capable of serious rebellion after receiving genuine privileges (Judges 2:11–19; 1 Kings 9:4–9; Ezekiel 33:12–19; 1 Corinthians 10:1–12). Acts 20:29–30, 1 Timothy 4:1–3, Jude 1:3–4, and 2 Peter 3:17 make corruption and departure an explicit early Christian concern. These observations require no theory of later church history.

**Strong evidence within the Restoration canon:** [[1 Nephi 13]] portrays plain and precious material being removed during transmission to the Gentiles. For readers who accept the Book of Mormon as ancient revelation, this supplies substantial prophetic support for corruption and loss. It does not identify every historical mechanism or date the withdrawal of priesthood authority.

**Moderate cumulative support:** Second Thessalonians 2:3 predicts a falling away; Acts anticipates internal distortion; and post-apostolic history includes institutional conflict, doctrinal controversy, political entanglement, and significant development. These facts are compatible with the Great Apostasy model but do not exclusively demonstrate it. Catholic, Orthodox, and other Christians interpret much of the same history as contested development within a continuing church.

**Typological evidence:** Amos 8:11–12 is applied to the Great Apostasy in [[November 23–29. “Seek the Lord, and Ye Shall Live” Amos; Obadiah; Jonah]], but its immediate setting is judgment upon Israel. Isaiah 24:5 supplies a broad pattern of violated law, ordinance, and covenant. Neither passage independently identifies post-apostolic Christianity.

**Strong Book of Mormon literary evidence:** [[4 Nephi 1]] repeatedly places inequality, class formation, costly religious display, pride, and taught unbelief at stages of communal decline. This supports a social as well as doctrinal portrait of apostasy; it does not establish that wealth invariably causes apostasy.

**Historical limit:** Documents can establish change, continuity, conflict, and discontinuity more readily than a divine withdrawal of authority. Establishing universal priesthood loss historically would require an agreed criterion for valid authority and evidence that every authorized line ceased—points on which Latter-day Saints, Catholics, Orthodox Christians, and Protestants disagree.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Several questions remain central:

- Does the falling away of 2 Thessalonians 2:3 designate a first-century crisis, a long post-apostolic era, an end-time rebellion, or a pattern with more than one fulfillment?
- What would count as evidence that priesthood authority had been lost, as distinct from evidence that doctrine, liturgy, or administration changed?
- How much development can occur before development becomes corruption? This question connects directly with [[How did the doctrine of the Trinity develop]] and [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]].
- Does Christ's preservation of a faithful remnant preserve individual believers only, or also an authorized institution and valid ordinances?
- How should promises of divine preservation be read alongside warnings of apostasy, including Matthew 16:18, Matthew 28:20, John 10:27–29, Acts 20:29–30, and 2 Peter 3:17?
- Did different regions and Christian communities depart at different rates, making a single date for the Great Apostasy misleading?
- Which post-apostolic beliefs represent unauthorized alteration, and which preserve or clarify apostolic teaching?
- Can the restored Church be institutionally secure while its members and local cultures remain susceptible to the same pride, inequality, and inherited hostility diagnosed in [[4 Nephi 1]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objection is historical continuity. Catholic and Orthodox Christians can point to continuous worshiping communities, bishops, baptism, Eucharistic practice, scripture, martyrdom, creeds, and claims of succession from the early centuries onward. A model asserting simple disappearance can understate that continuity and treat every development as corruption without an independent criterion. The careful Latter-day Saint claim is narrower—loss of divine authority and fulness despite survival of much truth—but that narrower claim is correspondingly harder to demonstrate using ordinary historical evidence.

A second objection is exegetical. Second Thessalonians 2 links the falling away closely to the man of sin and the day of Christ, making an end-time rebellion a serious reading. Acts 20 and Jude address concrete local threats. Amos 8 concerns Israel. Isaiah 24 is a universal judgment oracle. Reading all of these as direct predictions of one post-apostolic era risks combining distinct texts because they fit a later theological framework.

A third objection comes from preservation promises. Matthew 16:18 says the gates of hell will not prevail against Christ's church; Matthew 28:20 promises Christ's presence to the end; John 10:27–29 strongly expresses protection of Christ's sheep. Catholic, Orthodox, and many Protestant interpreters understand such texts as incompatible with total ecclesial extinction. A Latter-day Saint response can distinguish Christ's ultimate victory, preservation of believers, and future restoration from uninterrupted institutional authority, but the passages themselves do not explicitly make that distinction.

A fourth objection concerns doctrinal development. Historical theologians argue that later formulations, including Nicene and Chalcedonian language, were attempts to preserve apostolic convictions under new conceptual pressures, not pagan replacements for Christianity. Mere lateness or use of philosophical vocabulary does not prove apostasy. Any charge of corruption must compare a development with well-established earlier teaching and explain why clarification, translation, or legitimate adaptation is inadequate.

Finally, the dispensational model can become selectively applied. If change, institutional conflict, prosperity, or imperfect leaders prove ancient apostasy, the same indicators must be assessed honestly within modern Restoration history. [[Why did so many early leaders leave the Church]] and [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]] prevent the concept from being used only against other traditions.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing Latter-day Saint treatments read the biblical warnings cumulatively and join them to [[1 Nephi 13]] and Restoration authority claims. Hugh Nibley's “The Passing of the Primitive Church,” published in *Church History* 30, no. 2 (1961), argued that early Christian sources preserve a consciousness of decline and loss. B. H. Roberts's *Outlines of Ecclesiastical History* represents an older, more sweeping Latter-day Saint presentation. Current official summaries such as [[Apostasy (Gospel Topics)]] more carefully allow that sincere believers and many truths remained while maintaining that priesthood authority and the fulness were lost.

Latter-day Saint historical consciousness is itself an object of scholarship. Miranda Wilcox and John D. Young's edited volume *Standing Apart: Mormon Historical Consciousness and the Concept of Apostasy* examines how apostasy narratives have shaped Restoration identity and how those narratives relate to more recent study of early and medieval Christianity. Such work does not merely ask whether corruption occurred; it also examines how the category apostasy is constructed and used.

General historians such as Henry Chadwick, James D. G. Dunn, and Diarmaid MacCulloch describe a complex movement from diverse early communities toward developing institutions, canons, creeds, and liturgies without adopting the Restorationist conclusion that divine authority vanished. Historical method can identify transformation and competing traditions, but it cannot directly observe an invisible withdrawal of priesthood.

Catholic and Orthodox scholarship interprets apostolic succession, episcopal continuity, councils, and sacramental life as evidence of preservation through development. Many Protestant histories acknowledge serious corruption while locating continuity in scripture, the gospel, or a faithful church rather than requiring new priesthood restoration. Restorationist traditions diagnose more systemic loss but disagree about authority and the form restoration should take.

The principal disagreement is not whether Christianity changed or whether grave corruption occurred. It concerns the theological meaning of change: legitimate development, reformable corruption within a continuing church, loss requiring divine restoration, or ordinary religious evolution. Historical evidence constrains these models, but views of revelation, church, ordinance, and authority remain decisive.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with the covenant pattern in [[Judges 2]], especially Judges 2:11–19, and then read Nehemiah 9:6–37. Note rebellion, consequence, mercy, deliverance, and relapse.

2. Study apostasy's motives and forms: prosperity and forgetfulness in Deuteronomy 32:13–18; false worship in 2 Kings 17:7–18; self-defeating substitutes in [[Jeremiah 2]], especially Jeremiah 2:13, 19; and conditional institutional promises in 1 Kings 9:4–9.

3. Read the New Testament warnings in sequence: Acts 20:29–30; 1 Timothy 4:1–3; [[2 Thessalonians 2]], especially 2 Thessalonians 2:1–12; Hebrews 10:26–29; 2 Peter 2:1–22; 2 Peter 3:17; and Jude 1:3–13. For each, distinguish its immediate audience from later application.

4. Test the remnant question through 1 Kings 19:10–18 and Romans 11:2–5. Ask whether preservation of believers entails uninterrupted institutional authority.

5. Study Book of Mormon models: [[Alma 31]] for doctrinal, ritual, and political dimensions; [[4 Nephi 1]] for social and intergenerational decline; [[Mormon 8]] for near-total communal loss; and [[2 Nephi 26]] for the distinct dwindling of Lehi's descendants.

6. Examine the principal Restorationist texts and applications: [[1 Nephi 13]] at 1 Nephi 13:24–29; Amos 8:11–12 alongside [[November 23–29. “Seek the Lord, and Ye Shall Live” Amos; Obadiah; Jonah]]; and the definitions in [[Apostasy (Gospel Topics)]], [[Chapter 1 The Restoration of the Gospel]], and [[Chapter 14 Dispensations of the Gospel]].

7. Compare models rather than proof texts. Read a Latter-day Saint account such as Hugh Nibley's “The Passing of the Primitive Church,” a general early-Christian history such as Henry Chadwick's *The Early Church*, and Catholic or Orthodox treatments of apostolic succession. Track separately what changed, when it changed, and what theological judgment each author places upon the change.

8. Conclude with personal application in [[Apostasy (True to the Faith)]] and [[In the Space of Not Many Years (Elder David A. Bednar, October 2024)]]. Compare their safeguards with the canonical causes already observed: pride, forgetfulness, false teaching, broken covenants, and estrangement from God.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scripture makes apostasy unmistakably possible. Israel repeatedly forsakes covenant loyalty; divinely founded institutions receive conditional warnings; early Christian writings anticipate or encounter false teaching, internal division, departure from faith, and loss of steadfastness; and the Book of Mormon portrays societies declining through pride, altered worship, inequality, violence, and taught unbelief. Apostasy is therefore more than ordinary doubt or every instance of sin. It is a consequential turning from God that can reshape teaching, worship, authority claims, social relations, and collective memory.

The Great Apostasy is the Latter-day Saint historical-theological synthesis of these patterns. [[Apostasy (Gospel Topics)]] teaches that widespread post-apostolic corruption and unauthorized change culminated in loss of priesthood authority, making the [[Restoration of the Gospel]] and [[Priesthood Restoration]] necessary. [[1 Nephi 13]] gives the synthesis distinctive support within the Restoration canon through its vision of plain and precious material being removed.

The evidence does not independently prove every element of that synthesis. Second Thessalonians 2:3 predicts a falling away but does not specify a centuries-long worldwide loss. Acts and Jude describe or anticipate concrete corruption but do not narrate the fate of every Christian community. Amos 8 and Isaiah 24 are illuminating applications whose immediate settings differ. Post-apostolic continuity in Christian communities, scripture, sacraments, worship, and ministry is historically real, even if Latter-day Saint doctrine judges that continuity insufficient to preserve priesthood authority.

The strongest defensible conclusion is layered: substantial apostasy is a pervasive canonical reality; serious corruption and doctrinal development occurred in Christian history; and universal withdrawal of priesthood authority is official Restoration doctrine supported distinctively by the Restoration canon, but contested by other Christian traditions and not demonstrable from institutional change alone. A preserved remnant establishes that sincere believers may survive mass apostasy; it does not by itself settle institutional or sacramental continuity.

The doctrine also has a reflexive purpose. [[4 Nephi 1]] and [[Apostasy (True to the Faith)]] turn attention from diagnosing other eras to resisting present pride, estrangement, covenant neglect, inequality, and deliberately transmitted unbelief. Faithful study can therefore deepen gratitude for the Restoration while retaining humility about the limits of historical proof and vigilance about analogous patterns in oneself and one's community.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
