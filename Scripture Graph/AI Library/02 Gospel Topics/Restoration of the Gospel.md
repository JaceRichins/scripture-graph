# Restoration of the Gospel

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In the Bible, “restoration” is not one uniform technical label. Acts 3:19–21 anticipates a future “restitution of all things” associated with Christ’s return and the prophets’ promises. Revelation 14:6–7 portrays an angel carrying the everlasting gospel worldwide before judgment, but does not itself say that the gospel had vanished or identify a nineteenth-century fulfillment. In [[Alma 41]], by contrast, restoration means resurrection and just recompense; it is an unrelated keyword match, not a reference to [[Restoration of the Gospel]].

In Latter-day Saint tradition, the capitalized Restoration denotes God’s work through [[Joseph Smith Jr]] and later revelation to renew access to the gospel of [[Jesus Christ]], prophetic revelation, scripture, priesthood authority, ordinances, covenants, the Church, gathering, and temple keys. These components unfolded over time, so the term names a process as well as a cluster of founding events. Official summaries such as [[Joseph Smith (Gospel Topics)]], [[First Vision (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Restoration of the Priesthood (Gospel Topics)]] state this faith claim. Historical demonstration that the movement and its claims arose is distinct from theological judgment that God caused them.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon’s Restoration account begins from estrangement: D&C 1:15–16 says people had departed from divine ordinances and broken the everlasting covenant. D&C 1:17–23 then presents revelation through Joseph Smith as God’s response, intended to increase faith, establish the covenant, and restore authorized divine speech. Joseph Smith—History 1:10–20 supplies the canonized account of the [[First Vision]] and the instruction not to join the existing denominations. D&C 1:30 makes the canon’s strongest institutional claim by identifying the restored church as the Lord’s only true and living church, while qualifying that divine approval does not extend unreservedly to every member.

The Restoration is broader than one vision or one ordination. The Book of Mormon is presented as an external record translated by divine means; see [[Book of Mormon (Gospel Topics)]], [[Translation of the Book of Mormon]], and [[Witnesses of the Book of Mormon]]. D&C 27:12–13 names Peter, James, and John in the restoration of apostolic authority. D&C 110:11–16 presents further keys committed by Moses, Elias, and Elijah; see [[Priesthood keys restored in the Kirtland Temple]]. D&C 20 connects restored authority with [[Baptism]], the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], sacramental worship, and [[Organization of the Church]].

Official Latter-day Saint doctrine therefore treats the Restoration as divinely initiated and necessary preparation for the [[Gathering of Israel]] and [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]]. Acts 3:19–21 and Revelation 14:6–7 are canonical precedents, but neither passage independently supplies the complete modern system. The detailed identification of those passages with this movement comes from later Restoration revelation and official interpretation.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The Restoration narrative belongs to a longer canonical pattern rather than beginning with the English word “restoration.” Israel’s scriptures repeatedly join covenant rebellion, judgment, remnant survival, renewed Zion, and gathering; [[Isaiah (prophet)]] is especially important to that pattern. Acts 3:19–21 turns prophetic restoration toward Christ’s future return, while Revelation 14:6–7 places worldwide gospel proclamation before judgment.

The Latter-day Saint historical sequence is cumulative. Joseph Smith’s canonized history dates the [[First Vision]] to 1820 and Moroni’s first visits to 1823; Joseph Smith—History 1:34–42 connects the plates with Israel’s prophetic promises and gives a Restoration form of Malachi’s Elijah prophecy. The Book of Mormon appeared and the [[Restoration of the Aaronic Priesthood]] and [[Restoration of the Melchizedek Priesthood]] were claimed before the 1830 [[Organization of the Church]]. Subsequent revelations developed church offices, ordinances, gathering, Zion, salvation for the dead, differentiated glories, and temple sealing. D&C 110 presents the 1836 Kirtland Temple visions as another restoration of keys, showing that the canonical process did not end at the Church’s organization.

Modern institutional memory often tells these events as one unified divine work. That synthesis is doctrinally coherent, but historians must still evaluate the sources for each event separately. [[Why are there multiple First Vision accounts]] and the histories behind [[Priesthood Restoration]] are therefore central rather than peripheral study. Recent discourse also uses “Restoration” prospectively: Dieter F. Uchtdorf’s 2014 address “Are You Sleeping through the Restoration?” calls it an ongoing process, a view highlighted in [[December 30–January 5 “The Promised Restoration Goes Forward” The Restoration of the Fulness of the Gospel of Jesus Christ]]. His later “Daily Restoration,” cited in [[April 13–19. “Stand Still, and See the Salvation of the Lord” Exodus 14–18]], applies restoration language to continuing discipleship. This ongoing usage should not erase the distinction between founding historical claims and later development.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
Evidence should be graded by the proposition it can actually support.

**Strong for historical existence and self-understanding:** Surviving revelations, publications, organizations, and participant records strongly establish that Joseph Smith founded a movement making concrete claims about visions, plates, translation, angels, authority, ordinances, and a restored church. The canon’s cumulative narrative in [[Joseph Smith—History 1]], [[D&C 1]], D&C 27, and D&C 110 is internally intelligible. That establishes what early Saints claimed and how the tradition understands itself; it does not independently establish divine causation.

**Substantial but disputed testimonial evidence:** The plates and witnesses matter because the Book of Mormon was not presented merely as an admitted allegory. [[Witnesses of the Book of Mormon]] and [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]] should be studied together. Continued witness affirmations can support sincerity and resistance to a simple recantation theory. They do not eliminate questions about visionary language, perception, memory, social influence, or the record’s antiquity.

**Strong intertextual observation, limited apologetic reach:** Later Restoration scripture frequently develops biblical language. The sun, moon, and stars of 1 Corinthians 15:40–42 visibly anticipate the imagery of [[D&C 76]], but Paul does not lay out the full Latter-day Saint system of [[Kingdoms of Glory]]. Joseph Smith—History 1:38–39 recasts Malachi 4:5–6 with explicit priesthood language. Moses 4 expands Genesis 3 by identifying Satan and supplying additional theological narrative. These relationships demonstrate deliberate continuity and reinterpretation; whether they are revealed restoration, inspired expansion, or modern creative reuse depends on judgments the verbal parallels alone cannot settle.

**Moderate cumulative religious evidence:** The movement’s scriptures, witness claims, reported spiritual experiences, institutional endurance, and moral or spiritual fruits can form a rational cumulative case for believers. Each category also admits non-supernatural explanations. No single item mechanically proves the whole Restoration, and cumulative reasoning is sound only if counterevidence and dependencies among the items are not hidden.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does Acts 3:21 itself require, and what comes only from later Latter-day Saint identification of its fulfillment?
- What exactly was lost in the apostasy: authority, ordinances, doctrines, an institution, spiritual gifts, or some combination? When and by what historical evidence can each loss be traced? See [[Apostasy]] and [[Apostasy (True to the Faith)]].
- Which Restoration teachings are recoveries of demonstrably ancient beliefs, and which are new revelations or fuller developments not recoverable from extant early Christian sources?
- How should variant and retrospective sources be weighed? [[Why are there multiple First Vision accounts]] is a necessary case study rather than an issue to bypass.
- How strong is the documentation for the several stages of [[Priesthood Restoration]], and should later canonical accounts be treated as independent evidence or as retrospective testimony?
- What can the witnesses establish about plates, and what remains unresolved about their contents and translation? Compare [[Witnesses of the Book of Mormon]], [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]], and [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]].
- How should D&C 1:30’s exclusive church claim relate to evident truth, goodness, spiritual experience, and continuity in other Christian communities? See [[How can there be one true church among so many]] and [[Are Latter-day Saints Christians]].
- When teaching changes, what criteria distinguish continuing revelation, correction, contextual policy, doctrinal development, and contradiction? See [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]].
- Is “ongoing Restoration” a bounded doctrinal claim with identifiable criteria, or can it become so flexible that any later change is redescribed as restoration?

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest biblical objection is exegetical: Acts 3:21 most naturally concerns the eschatological renewal associated with Christ’s return, and Revelation 14:6–7 concerns an angelic worldwide proclamation before judgment. Neither names Joseph Smith, predicts an American church, or enumerates priesthood offices. Using them as direct proofs of the nineteenth-century Restoration reads later claims back into texts that are less specific.

The strongest historical-Christian objection challenges the premise of a total apostasy. Christianity changed, diversified, acquired political power, and experienced institutional and doctrinal conflict, but churches, sacraments, scripture, devotion, and claims to apostolic succession continued. A sweeping assertion that Christ’s church simply disappeared can flatten complex history and overlook ancient Christian continuity. A Latter-day Saint response may distinguish preservation of truth and sincere discipleship from loss of divinely recognized keys or institutional authority, but that narrower claim still requires theological revelation; ordinary historical evidence cannot directly detect heavenly authority.

A further objection is that important Restoration distinctives often look like development rather than recovery. [[Kingdoms of Glory]], baptism for the dead, modern temple sealings, and some teachings about premortal and postmortal life are more detailed than their biblical precursors. Lost-teaching hypotheses can reconcile the gap, but without independent ancient evidence they risk becoming ad hoc. The honest believing position is that new revelation may restore, expand, or newly disclose truth—not that every modern detail is plainly recoverable from the Bible.

The founding evidence is also contestable. The canonized First Vision history was composed retrospectively, multiple accounts differ in emphasis, and the historical narration of priesthood restoration became more explicit over time. Those facts do not prove fabrication: memory, audience, genre, and later clarification can produce variation. They do prevent treating every later synthesis as though it were a contemporaneous transcript. See [[Why are there multiple First Vision accounts]], [[Restoration of the Aaronic Priesthood]], and [[Restoration of the Melchizedek Priesthood]].

Finally, spiritual experience and institutional fruit are not unique to one religion. Converts in incompatible traditions report guidance, healing, transformation, and certainty. Such experiences may rationally support personal faith, but without further criteria they do not uniquely identify one church as divinely restored; see [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]].

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing and critical historians often agree on a large documentary core: Joseph Smith’s movement arose within the religious world of the early United States, produced new scripture, organized a church, developed priesthood offices and temple practices, and rapidly acquired a distinctive identity. They divide chiefly over causal interpretation and the weight assigned to testimony, visionary experience, retrospective narration, and theological claims.

Richard Lyman Bushman’s *Joseph Smith: Rough Stone Rolling* (2005) places Joseph Smith sympathetically within his cultural setting while taking the prophetic self-understanding seriously; it does not pretend that professional history can prove revelation. Terryl L. Givens’s *By the Hand of Mormon* (2002) analyzes how the Book of Mormon operates simultaneously as sacred narrative, material-sign claim, and contested evidence. Grant Underwood’s *The Millenarian World of Early Mormonism* (1993) situates early Restoration language within biblical primitivism and millennial expectation.

Jan Shipps’s *Mormonism: The Story of a New Religious Tradition* (1985) interprets Mormonism as a genuinely new religious tradition formed through a reworking of Christian categories. Dan Vogel’s *Joseph Smith: The Making of a Prophet* (2004) offers a naturalistic critical account of Joseph Smith’s prophetic career. These approaches need not dispute that participants were religiously serious; they explain the same developments without accepting angelic or priesthood causation.

The main fault line is therefore not whether documents and a movement exist, but what best explains them. Believing scholarship permits divine action and often sees cumulative coherence; naturalistic scholarship restricts explanation to historically accessible causes such as revivalism, biblical interpretation, visionary culture, memory, charisma, and institution-building. Neither historical method alone nor devotional conviction should be mislabeled as a neutral demonstration of the other’s conclusion.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with [[Acts 3]]—especially Acts 3:19–21—and ask what Peter’s words require before applying them to later history. Read [[Revelation 14]] next, noting both its worldwide gospel proclamation and its lack of an explicit restoration verb.

2. Read [[D&C 1]] as the Restoration’s programmatic self-description. Track the stated problems in D&C 1:15–16, the divine response in verses 17–23, and the institutional claim in verse 30.

3. Read [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] in units: religious search and vision (verses 5–20), Moroni and biblical interpretation (verses 27–54), and the plates (verses 59–65). Then study [[First Vision (Gospel Topics)]] alongside [[Why are there multiple First Vision accounts]].

4. Study scripture and witnesses through [[Book of Mormon (Gospel Topics)]], [[Translation of the Book of Mormon]], [[Witnesses of the Book of Mormon]], and [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]]. Keep the existence of testimony distinct from conclusions about antiquity and translation.

5. Trace authority in sequence through [[Restoration of the Aaronic Priesthood]], [[Restoration of the Melchizedek Priesthood]], D&C 27:12–13, and D&C 110:11–16. Finish with [[Priesthood keys restored in the Kirtland Temple]] so that “priesthood restoration” is not reduced to a single moment.

6. Compare claimed recovery with canonical development: 1 Corinthians 15 with [[D&C 76]] on [[Kingdoms of Glory]]; Malachi 4 with Joseph Smith—History 1:38–39 on [[Elijah]]; Genesis 3 with Moses 4 on [[Fall of Adam and Eve]]; and New Testament initiation with D&C 20 on [[Ordinances]].

7. Test the framework historically through [[Apostasy]], [[How can there be one true church among so many]], and [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]]. End with [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ (True to the Faith)]] and [[December 30–January 5 “The Promised Restoration Goes Forward” The Restoration of the Fulness of the Gospel of Jesus Christ]] to compare completed-event and ongoing-process uses of “Restoration.”

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The Restoration of the Gospel is the Latter-day Saint claim that God renewed covenant, revelation, scripture, priesthood authority, ordinances, church organization, gathering, and temple keys through [[Joseph Smith Jr]] and subsequent revelation. [[D&C 1]] is its clearest programmatic canonical statement; [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] supplies its founding narrative; D&C 27 and D&C 110 present successive restorations of authority. Acts 3:19–21 and Revelation 14:6–7 provide important biblical frames of restitution and worldwide proclamation, but their identification with the modern movement comes from Restoration interpretation rather than from explicit names or dates in those passages.

The term covers both recovery and development. Some modern teachings reuse recognizable biblical language, while others are substantially more detailed than their biblical precursors. That may be understood faithfully as restored or continuing revelation; critically, it may be understood as creative religious innovation. Verbal parallels demonstrate relationship, not which explanation is true.

Historically, abundant evidence establishes the movement, its texts, institutions, witnesses, and participants’ claims. Witness testimony and the external-record framing of the Book of Mormon give the Restoration more evidentiary content than a purely private allegory, yet they do not force agreement about ancient plates, translation, angels, or divine authority. The First Vision and priesthood narratives likewise deserve both serious historical study and restraint about what retrospective sacred accounts can independently prove.

A trustworthy conclusion is layered. It is established that early Latter-day Saints proclaimed a restoration and built a durable religious tradition around concrete revelatory claims. It is strongly established that the canon coherently interprets those events as God’s latter-day work. Whether the claimed heavenly events occurred and whether the Church uniquely possesses restored authority remain theological and historical judgments informed—but not mechanically settled—by documents, testimony, spiritual experience, and faith. The faithful case is strongest when it accepts those limits rather than converting meaningful evidence into proof.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
