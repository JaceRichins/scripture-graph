# Covenants

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, a covenant is a solemn relationship involving promise, loyalty, obligation, or some combination of these. Covenant passages may identify parties, stipulations, oaths, signs, sacrifices, witnesses, written records, blessings, and consequences, but no single form contains every element. The Hebrew Bible applies covenant language to God's commitments to Noah and [[Abraham (patriarch)]], Israel at [[Mount Sinai]], the royal promise to [[David (king)]], marriage, ritual arrangements, and agreements among human parties. Genesis 9, Genesis 17, Exodus 24, Joshua 24, and Malachi 2 should therefore be compared without being collapsed into one rigid form.

Divine covenants are not contracts negotiated by equals. They characteristically begin with divine initiative, but many also demand a real human response. Exodus 19 places deliverance before Israel's pledge of obedience; Genesis 17 joins divine promise with circumcision and personal liability; Deuteronomy strongly emphasizes conditional blessing and judgment. Promissory and conditional dimensions should both remain visible.

In Latter-day Saint tradition, a covenant is a sacred promise or agreement between God and a person or people, commonly associated with authorized [[Ordinances]]. [[Covenant (Gospel Topics)]] relates covenant making to [[Baptism]], the [[Sacrament]], priesthood reception, temple endowment, and sealing. “Covenant path” is a modern organizing expression for receiving these ordinances and faithfully keeping their commitments, not a common scriptural phrase. See [[Do You Want to Be Happy (Elder Yoon Hwan Choi, October 2023)]] and [[Appendix A For Parents—Preparing Your Children for a Lifetime on God’s Covenant Path]].
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon's basic pattern is divine initiative, covenant response, lived loyalty, and promised communion. God remembers his covenant and delivers Israel (Exodus 6:2-8); the delivered people are then called to be a holy nation (Exodus 19:3-6). Covenant life reaches beyond ritual into worship, economic justice, family fidelity, care for vulnerable people, and communal holiness. [[Malachi 2]] is especially sharp: offerings do not compensate for treachery against “the wife of thy covenant” (Malachi 2:10-16).

Major canonical covenants contribute distinct dimensions. The Noahic covenant embraces humanity and living creatures ([[Genesis 9]]). The [[Abrahamic Covenant]] promises posterity, land, divine belonging, and blessing extending to nations ([[Genesis 17]]). The [[Sinai covenant and Ten Commandments]] constitutes Israel as a delivered, obligated people ([[Exodus 19]]; [[Exodus 24]]). The Davidic promise shapes royal and messianic hope ([[2 Samuel 7]]). The prophets announce both covenant judgment and restoration, including inward transformation and forgiveness ([[Jeremiah 31]]; [[Ezekiel 36]]; [[Ezekiel 37]]).

The New Testament identifies [[Jesus Christ]] as mediator of the new covenant. At [[The Last Supper]], the cup is the new covenant in his blood (Luke 22:19-20); Hebrews 8-10 relates that covenant to forgiveness, inward law, Christ's sacrifice, and access to God. Galatians 3:26-29 includes those who belong to Christ among Abraham's seed and heirs, while Romans 9-11 resists a simple conclusion that God has discarded Israel.

The Book of Mormon makes covenant both personal and communal. King Benjamin's people covenant lifelong obedience and receive the name and familial identity of Christ ([[Mosiah 5]]). At the [[Waters of Mormon]], baptism witnesses a commitment to serve God expressed by bearing burdens, mourning, comforting, and witnessing of Christ ([[Mosiah 18]]). Covenant identity is thus inseparable from discipleship and mutual care.

Official Latter-day Saint teaching further systematizes saving ordinances as covenantal. The sacrament renews baptismal commitments; Doctrine and Covenants 84:33-48 describes the oath and covenant belonging to the [[Priesthood]]; temple ordinances include further covenants; and Doctrine and Covenants 132:15-20 distinguishes mortal marriage from an authorized covenant sealed for eternity. [[Covenant (Gospel Topics)]], [[Covenants and Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], [[Marriage (Gospel Topics)]], and [[The Sealing Power (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2023)]] are official expositions. Their systematic formulations should be identified as Restoration doctrine rather than silently projected into every biblical use of “covenant.”
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The canonical history begins after the Flood, where the bow signifies God's covenant with Noah, his descendants, and living creatures ([[Genesis 9]]). The ancestral narratives then center covenant promise on [[Abraham (patriarch)]] and his seed: land, posterity, divine belonging, and blessing to nations ([[Genesis 12]]; [[Genesis 15]]; [[Genesis 17]]). [[Isaac]] and [[Jacob (Israel)]] inherit this promise.

The Exodus transforms a family promise into the identity of a delivered people. God remembers the covenant, brings Israel from bondage, and at [[Mount Sinai]] calls the nation to holiness and obedience ([[Exodus 6]]; [[Exodus 19]]; [[Exodus 24]]). Deuteronomy renews this covenant for a generation entering the land and makes remembrance, written instruction, and teaching children central ([[Deuteronomy 6]]; [[Deuteronomy 28]]; [[Deuteronomy 29]]). Joshua 24 stages another public renewal.

Israel's monarchy adds covenantal kingship. Elders covenant with David at Hebron (2 Samuel 5:1-3), while God's promise concerning David's house gives later messianic expectation a dynastic form ([[2 Samuel 7]]). The divided kingdoms, Assyrian conquest, and [[Babylonian captivity of Judah]] are repeatedly interpreted by biblical narrators and prophets as consequences of covenant rebellion. Yet judgment is not the final word: Jeremiah promises a new covenant, and Ezekiel joins gathering with cleansing, inner renewal, obedience, and restored belonging.

Jesus' Last Supper and apostolic interpretation place his death at the center of new-covenant forgiveness and access to God. Early Christian disagreement later concerned how this covenant relates to Mosaic law, Gentile inclusion, and continuing promises to Israel; Acts 15, Galatians 3, Hebrews 8, and Romans 9-11 preserve important parts of that discussion.

The Book of Mormon applies covenant categories to Nephite preaching, baptism, ecclesial identity, the gathering of Israel, and the latter-day reception of scripture. [[Mosiah 5]] and [[Mosiah 18]] give unusually concentrated accounts of covenant identity and practice; [[3 Nephi 20]] and [[3 Nephi 29]] connect latter-day gathering with fulfillment of promises to Israel.

Restoration scripture expands covenant theology around restored ordinances, priesthood, gathering, consecration, temple worship, and eternal sealing. Doctrine and Covenants 84 treats priesthood through an oath and covenant; Doctrine and Covenants 132 gives eternal marriage a covenantal and sealing framework. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century discourse sometimes used “new and everlasting covenant” especially for eternal or plural marriage, as illustrated by [[Conference Report, April 1914 (General Conference (full report), April 1914)]]. Current official explanations more often use the term broadly for the fulness of the gospel while retaining eternal marriage within it; compare [[Everlasting Covenant (Gospel Topics)]] and [[The Sealing Power (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2023)]].
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence is textual rather than archaeological: covenant is demonstrably an organizing theme across the canon. Promise to ancestors, Exodus deliverance, law, land, kingship, prophetic indictment, exile, restoration, Christ's blood, baptismal community, priesthood, and sealing repeatedly converge around covenant language. This establishes the canon's theological architecture with high confidence. It does not independently verify every narrated event or establish divine authorship.

Comparative ancient Near Eastern evidence is substantial but narrower. Exodus, Deuteronomy, and Joshua contain features familiar from treaties and grants: historical prologue, stipulations, witnesses, blessings and curses, written deposition, and public recital. George E. Mendenhall's “Covenant Forms in Israelite Tradition” (1954), Meredith G. Kline's *Treaty of the Great King* (1963), and Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* (1972) represent influential but differing uses of this comparison. The formal parallels illuminate the texts; they do not settle exact dates, direct dependence, or revelation.

The chapter-by-chapter reading also found several strong internal lines. First, covenant memory is transmitted through records, language, and teaching: Deuteronomy 6 and 31, Mosiah 1:2-7, Omni 1:17-18, and Moses 6:5-8 form a coherent pattern linked to [[Education and Learning]] and [[Records and Sacred Writings]]. This is strong canonical evidence but not a controlled historical test of literacy's social effects. Second, the prophets' reuse of Exodus language shows that election increases accountability as well as assurance: remembered deliverance can ground indictment and judgment ([[Ezekiel 20]]; [[Hosea 8]]). Third, Ezekiel 11:17-20, 36:24-28, and 37:21-28 closely link gathering, renewed heart and spirit, obedience, and the divine-human covenant formula.

Restoration passages furnish especially explicit covenant descriptions. Mosiah 18:8-13 gives the ethical substance and baptismal witness of covenant; Mosiah 5:5-12 connects covenant to Christ-centered rebirth and naming; Doctrine and Covenants 84:33-48 names an oath and covenant belonging to priesthood; Doctrine and Covenants 132:15-20 states the conditions claimed for an eternal marriage covenant. These passages securely establish what the texts and tradition claim. They do not provide publicly repeatable verification that the promised divine ratification or eternal effects occur.

Narrative case studies prevent abstraction. The Anti-Nephi-Lehies accept death rather than violate their covenant against bloodshed (Alma 24:15-19), while their sons' military service is expressly distinguished because the sons had not made that covenant (Alma 53:10-17). [[Malachi 2]] treats marital betrayal as covenant treachery that corrupts worship. Both cases show covenants functioning as costly obligations, not merely reassuring labels.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Several questions remain genuinely open or contested:

- How should unconditional-sounding promises to Abraham or David be related to passages that condition covenant enjoyment upon obedience? Genesis 17:7-14, 2 Samuel 7:14-16, Deuteronomy 28, and Jeremiah 31 should be held together rather than harmonized too quickly.
- Does the “new covenant” replace Israel, renew Israel, expand Israel's covenant family through Christ, or operate in more than one of these senses? Jeremiah 31 explicitly names Israel and Judah; Galatians 3 includes Gentile believers; Romans 9-11 warns against Gentile boasting. Christian traditions answer differently.
- What precisely is renewed during the sacrament? Official Latter-day Saint teaching says the baptismal covenant is renewed, while the sacramental prayers themselves repeat covenant commitments without using the word “renew.” See [[Sacrament]] and [[Covenant (Gospel Topics)]].
- Who is grammatically and ritually making the “oath” in the oath and covenant of priesthood, and how should Doctrine and Covenants 84:41's severe warning be related to repentance and forgiveness elsewhere? The text warrants seriousness but not casual declarations about particular individuals' final status.
- How broadly should “new and everlasting covenant” be read in Doctrine and Covenants 132? The section includes eternal marriage, but verses 4-7 speak in a wider covenantal register. [[Everlasting Covenant (Gospel Topics)]] adopts the broader fulness-of-the-gospel usage.
- How do authorized ordinances, personal faithfulness, grace, and the Holy Spirit of promise relate in divine ratification? Doctrine and Covenants 132:19 makes ceremony alone insufficient, but later pastoral summaries sometimes compress these distinctions.
- How should covenant inheritance language be taught without turning ancestry into spiritual superiority? Genesis 12:3 directs Abrahamic blessing outward to all families, and Galatians 3:26-29 makes belonging to Christ decisive.
- Which biblical covenant practices are enduring commands and which belong to a particular people, dispensation, or historical law? The Anti-Nephi-Lehite story itself demonstrates that a serious covenant made by one community need not be imposed automatically on another.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
A strong objection is that covenant can legitimate hierarchy and coercion. Biblical divine-human covenants are not agreements among equals; some include severe curses, collective punishments, conquest, exclusion, or inherited obligations. Deuteronomy 28 and portions of Joshua cannot be reduced to the gentle modern metaphor of a mutually chosen relationship. Believers may understand divine sovereignty and communal accountability as morally relevant, but that theological premise does not dissolve ethical questions about violence, children, outsiders, or unequal power.

Historical-critical scholarship also questions whether the canon preserves one continuous covenant system. The texts come from different periods, communities, genres, and editorial settings. Noahic promise, patriarchal grant, Deuteronomic treaty, Davidic dynasty, Jeremiah's new covenant, Pauline promise, and Hebrews' cultic argument may represent related but distinguishable theologies later gathered into a canonical whole. A unified “covenant path” is therefore a theological synthesis, not a neutral reconstruction of every author's original conceptual scheme.

Replacement theology presents another danger. Hebrews 8:13 can be read as making the first covenant obsolete, but Jeremiah's promise addresses Israel and Judah, and Romans 11 denies that God has simply cast away his people. Christian history shows that claims of supersession can feed contempt for Jews. Any Christian synthesis should acknowledge both the New Testament's claims concerning Christ and its unresolved, internally varied discussion of Israel.

Covenant blessing language can be distorted into prosperity theology or victim blaming. Deuteronomy correlates national fidelity and communal outcomes, but Job, many Psalms, Ecclesiastes, Jesus' suffering, and the deaths of faithful disciples refute a simple rule that present prosperity proves faithfulness or suffering proves covenant breach.

A Restoration-specific objection is that covenant authority claims ultimately depend on accepting Joseph Smith's revelations and priesthood claims. Doctrine and Covenants 84 and 132 clearly state those claims, but the texts cannot independently authenticate their own divine source. Temple and sealing practice has also developed historically, and past teachings about plural marriage, gender roles, lineage, and access create legitimate questions about what is essential and what has been administratively or culturally contingent. Official sources such as [[Marriage (Gospel Topics)]] describe current doctrine; they should not be treated as complete histories of every earlier formulation.

Finally, covenant language can encourage institutional loyalty to displace conscience, truth, or accountability. The canon itself supplies a correction: prophets accuse covenant institutions and rulers; Malachi rejects worship joined to marital treachery; and Mosiah 18 defines covenant belonging through burden bearing, mourning, comforting, and witness. Invoking “covenant” does not make an interpretation, leader's application, or institutional policy infallible.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship broadly agrees that covenant is important while disputing how comprehensive the category is and how its forms developed. George E. Mendenhall's “Covenant Forms in Israelite Tradition” (1954), Meredith G. Kline's *Treaty of the Great King* (1963), and Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School* (1972) compare biblical covenants with ancient Near Eastern treaties and grants. Jon D. Levenson's *Sinai and Zion* (1985) emphasizes distinct but interacting Sinai and Davidic-Zion traditions. Formal parallels are widely useful, but attempts to derive exact dates or direct literary dependence from a single treaty pattern remain disputed.

Historical-critical approaches analyze covenant texts by literary layer, political setting, and editorial history; canonical and theological approaches emphasize the received collection's interconnections. These approaches answer different questions and need not be treated as simple opposites. Jeremiah 31 and Ezekiel 36-37, for example, can be studied both as responses to national catastrophe and as parts of a larger canonical theology of restoration.

Jewish interpretation ordinarily locates *berit* within Israel's continuing relationship with God, Torah, peoplehood, and responsibility. Christian interpretation reads Israel's scriptures in light of Jesus and the new covenant but divides over Torah, Gentile inclusion, supersession, ecclesial identity, and Israel's continuing election. E. P. Sanders's *Paul and Palestinian Judaism* (1977) described much Second Temple Jewish religion through “covenantal nomism,” and the ensuing New Perspective on Paul, associated especially with James D. G. Dunn and N. T. Wright, challenged portrayals of Judaism as simple works-righteousness. The adequacy of that model and its application to Galatians and Romans remain debated; it should inform, not predetermine, interpretation of Paul's covenant arguments.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation extends biblical covenant history through the Book of Mormon, restored priesthood, gathering, temple ordinances, and sealing. [[Covenant (Gospel Topics)]], [[Everlasting Covenant (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Covenants and Responsibilities (President Dallin H. Oaks, April 2024)]] articulate current official teaching, while [[The Sealing Power (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2023)]] relates the Abrahamic covenant, the new and everlasting covenant, and sealing power. These sources are authoritative teaching within the Church, not a consensus shared by non-Latter-day Saint historians or biblical scholars.

The principal disputes concern historical origin, canonical unity, Israel's continuing status, ordinance efficacy, ecclesial authority, and whether Restoration developments are revelation or nineteenth-century religious innovation. The textual evidence establishes that covenant language exists and matters; it cannot by itself settle those larger historical and theological judgments.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with divine initiative and covenant variety: read [[Genesis 9]], then Genesis 12:1-3, [[Genesis 15]], and [[Genesis 17]]. Track parties, promises, obligations, signs, and beneficiaries. Use [[Abrahamic Covenant]] as the thematic guide.

2. Study deliverance before law: read [[Exodus 6]], [[Exodus 19]], [[Exodus 20]], and [[Exodus 24]]. Note how [[The Exodus from Egypt]], identity, worship, ethics, and covenant ratification fit together. Then compare [[Deuteronomy 6]], [[Deuteronomy 28]], and [[Deuteronomy 29]] for memory, teaching, blessings, curses, and renewal.

3. Trace land and kingship: read Joshua 24 and [[2 Samuel 7]]. Ask how human obedience relates to God's enduring purpose. Follow the consequences through [[1 Kings 11]], [[2 Kings 18]], and the [[Assyrian captivity of Israel]].

4. Read the prophets as covenant critics and restoration witnesses: [[Jeremiah 11]], [[Jeremiah 31]], [[Ezekiel 20]], [[Ezekiel 36]], [[Ezekiel 37]], [[Hosea 8]], and [[Malachi 2]]. Distinguish indictment, judgment, promise, and inward renewal.

5. Study the covenant through Christ: Luke 22:14-20, Galatians 3, Romans 9-11, and [[Hebrews 8]] through Hebrews 10. Compare how each author relates Christ, Abraham, Israel, law, sacrifice, Gentiles, and forgiveness.

6. Study Book of Mormon covenant community: [[Mosiah 5]], [[Mosiah 18]], Alma 24, Alma 53:10-17, [[3 Nephi 20]], and [[3 Nephi 29]]. Link these to [[Baptism]], [[Conversion]], [[War and Peace]], and [[Gathering of Israel]].

7. Study Restoration covenant structure: Doctrine and Covenants 20:37, 77-79; [[D&C 84]]; Doctrine and Covenants 97:8; and Doctrine and Covenants 132:4-20. Then compare [[Covenant (Gospel Topics)]], [[Covenants and Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], [[Marriage (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Everlasting Covenant (Gospel Topics)]].

8. Finish with lived responsibility: reread Mosiah 18:8-10, Malachi 2:13-16, and Deuteronomy 15:7-11. Use [[Cleave unto the Covenants (Barbara Thompson, October 2011)]] and [[Covenants and Responsibilities (President Dallin H. Oaks, April 2024)]] to examine modern application, while continuing to distinguish scriptural wording from later pastoral synthesis.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Covenant is one of the canon's principal ways of describing how divine grace creates an obligated people. God initiates, remembers, delivers, promises, and restores; human beings are called to trust, remember, obey, worship, teach, repent, and care for one another. The relationship is neither a bargain among equals nor permission for passive entitlement. Election brings vocation and accountability.

The canonical story develops through several related covenants rather than one undifferentiated formula. Noah receives a promise embracing living creation. Abraham receives posterity, land, belonging, and a mission of blessing. Israel is delivered from Pharaoh and constituted at Sinai. David receives a dynastic promise. Prophets expose covenant treachery while promising forgiveness, gathering, and transformed hearts. Jesus identifies his blood with the new covenant, and apostolic writings interpret him as its mediator. The Book of Mormon presents baptismal covenant as Christ-centered identity and burden-bearing community. Restoration scripture adds priesthood, temple covenants, and eternal sealing to this system.

The ethical center must remain visible. Covenant fidelity concerns truth, justice, mercy, marriage, care for the poor, communal peace, worship, and endurance—not ritual status alone. [[Malachi 2]] rejects worship joined to marital betrayal; [[Mosiah 18]] defines covenant community through mourning and comforting; the Anti-Nephi-Lehies demonstrate that covenant can impose costly restraint.

The evidence establishes with high confidence that covenant is a pervasive canonical structure and that Latter-day Saint doctrine consciously extends it through restored ordinances. Comparative treaty evidence illuminates biblical form. Neither internal coherence nor ancient parallels independently prove every historical narrative, supernatural promise, or Restoration authority claim. Those larger conclusions remain matters of historical argument, spiritual experience, and faith.

The most responsible reading therefore holds promise and responsibility together. It resists prosperity theology, inherited superiority, supersessionist contempt for Jews, and institutional uses of covenant that evade moral accountability. At its strongest, covenant theology teaches that grace does not abolish fidelity: it makes a people capable of belonging to God and responsible for embodying that belonging in enduring, truthful, and merciful relationships.
