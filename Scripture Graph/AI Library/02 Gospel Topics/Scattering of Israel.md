# Scattering of Israel

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
**Scattering of Israel** is the scriptural pattern in which the covenant people of Israel — descendants of Jacob, heirs of the [[Abrahamic Covenant]] — are removed from their land, dispersed among other nations, and, in many cases, lose knowledge of their own tribal identity. It is not one event: the canon's two great historical instances are the Assyrian conquest and deportation of the northern kingdom ([[2 Kings 17]]) and the Babylonian deportations of Judah ([[2 Kings 24]]; [[Jeremiah 52]]).

The word **Israel** is not univocal — it can mean Jacob's descendants generally, the northern kingdom in contrast to Judah, a covenant remnant, or a latter-day covenant people — so a careful reader tracks which sense a given passage uses rather than assuming one referent throughout.

The Book of Mormon explicitly calls scattering "both temporal and spiritual" (1 Nephi 22:3): physical displacement and estrangement from covenant knowledge both belong to the idea, and 3 Nephi 20:13 makes recovered knowledge of the Redeemer part of the counterpart doctrine, [[Gathering of Israel]]. [[Safely Gathered Home (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2023)]] teaches that rebellion occasioned the scattering and that God also used it to bless the nations — the latter is a theological interpretation of providence, not a proposition demonstrated by imperial records.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canonical pattern begins with [[Covenants]]. Deuteronomy warns that entrenched idolatry and disobedience will bring expulsion and dispersion, while repentance leaves open a return to God (Deuteronomy 4:25-31; 28:63-68; 29:24-28). The historical books interpret the fall of Samaria and the deportations of Judah through that covenant framework (2 Kings 17:7-23; 2 Chronicles 36:14-21). This is what the scriptural narrators teach; establishing that Assyria and Babylon conquered Israelite kingdoms is historically different from establishing God's causal judgment.

Judgment is not presented as annihilation or cancellation of the [[Abrahamic Covenant]]. Amos pictures Israel sifted among nations but not wholly destroyed (Amos 9:8-15). Jeremiah promises that the one who scattered Israel will gather and keep it (Jeremiah 31:7-10). Ezekiel joins the symbols of Judah and Joseph and associates restoration with one people, covenant faithfulness, land, kingship, and sanctuary (Ezekiel 37:15-28). These texts underwrite the inseparable relationship between scattering and [[Gathering of Israel]].

The Book of Mormon places [[Lehi (father of Nephi)]] and his descendants inside this pattern. [[1 Nephi 22]] describes Israel's dispersion as temporal and spiritual, acknowledges that the locations of many tribes were unknown, and gives Gentiles both a scattering role and a role within subsequent covenant fulfillment (1 Nephi 22:3-12). [[3 Nephi 20]] defines a central result of gathering as coming to knowledge of the redeeming Lord (3 Nephi 20:13). [[Thoughts to Keep in Mind “Jesus Will Say to All Israel, ‘Come Home’”]] therefore describes the Book of Mormon as a record of a branch of scattered Israel. That is the Church curriculum's confessional reading; the historical reality and geographic scale of Lehi's migration remain tied to the larger disputed question of Book of Mormon historicity.

Restoration scripture applies the pattern to modern Zion (D&C 101:1-18) and reports that Moses committed keys for Israel's gathering (D&C 110:11; see [[Priesthood keys restored in the Kirtland Temple]]). Modern conference teaching, including [[Lamentations of Jeremiah Beware of Bondage (Elder Quentin L. Cook, October 2013)]] and [[Safely Gathered Home (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2023)]], states that unrighteousness led to scattering while divine providence used the dispersion to bless nations. Students should keep three claims distinct: the occurrence of forced migration, the canon's interpretation of it as covenant judgment, and the tradition's further interpretation of providential blessing.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
1. **Covenant foundations.** The promises to [[Abraham (patriarch)]] concern posterity, land, and blessing to all nations; Deuteronomy frames continued possession of the land as covenant-accountable and warns of dispersion for disobedience (Deuteronomy 4:25-31; 28:63-68).

2. **The divided monarchies.** After the [[Division of the kingdom]], Assyria captured Samaria and deported inhabitants of the northern kingdom in the late 8th century BC ([[2 Kings 17]]), explained in the text as the result of generations of apostasy.

3. **Judah and Babylon.** Babylon removed Jehoiachin, elites, soldiers, and craftsmen in 597 BC (2 Kings 24:10-16), followed by Jerusalem's destruction and further deportations (Jeremiah 52:27-30). The [[Return from exile]] under Persian rule was historically real but did not exhaust prophetic hopes for a more comprehensive worldwide recovery.

4. **Prophetic expansion.** [[Amos 9]], [[Jeremiah 31]], and [[Ezekiel 37]] transform imperial displacement into a theology of remnant, purification, and renewed covenant relationship.

5. **The Book of Mormon.** [[Lehi's departure from Jerusalem]] occurs on the eve of the Babylonian crisis; [[1 Nephi 22]] and [[3 Nephi 20]] connect Israel, Gentile nations, and restored knowledge of Christ into the same arc. The Zenos prophecy in 1 Nephi 19:12-17 is an internal Book of Mormon witness with no independently surviving external text for comparison.

6. **The Restoration and later teaching.** D&C 101 applies the scattering-and-restoration pattern to the dispossessed Missouri Saints, while D&C 110:11 commits keys for both "the gathering of Israel from the four parts of the earth" and "the leading of the ten tribes from the land of the north" — a geographically specific claim that sits in real tension with 1 Nephi 22:4's confession that no one knew where the tribes had gone. Early conference sources ([[A Compendium of the Doctrines of the Gospel]]; [[Conference Report, April 1910 (General Conference (full report), April 1910)]]; [[Conference Report, October 1921 (General Conference (full report), October 1921)]]; [[Conference Report, October 1928 (General Conference (full report), October 1928)]]) show a period emphasis on literal, genealogical gathering; recent teaching ([[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]]; [[Safely Gathered Home (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2023)]]) foregrounds covenant allegiance more than geography. This is a real shift in emphasis, not a clean reversal — the 1910 source already teaches providential purpose.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
Strongest evidence is historical and event-level: Assyrian sources independently attest conquest and deportation connected with Samaria, and the Babylonian Chronicle (BM 21946; Grayson 1975) records Nebuchadnezzar's 597 BC capture of Jerusalem — corroborating the conquest-and-deportation core of 2 Kings 17:6 and 2 Kings 24:10-17, though royal inscriptions are propagandistic and their totals need critical handling (Lipschits 2005).

Textually strong: the canon repeatedly links covenant infidelity, displacement, a preserved remnant, and hoped-for recovery (Deuteronomy 4:25-31; 2 Kings 17:7-23; Amos 9:8-15; Jeremiah 31:7-10; Ezekiel 37:21-28), and the Book of Mormon clearly understands itself through this same pattern (1 Nephi 22:3-12; 3 Nephi 20:13-29).

Weak as geographic proof: universal phrases like "all nations" and the four cardinal directions (1 Nephi 22:3-4; 3 Nephi 20:13) convey scope, not a census — and 1 Nephi 22:4 itself states an epistemic limit on where the tribes went.

A keyword index overgenerates: Jeremiah 52:8 (an army scattered) and Jeremiah 10:21 (scattered flocks) are lexical matches unrelated to Israel's covenantal dispersion. Pages flagged only as "keyword match" — including [[Ezekiel 34]], [[Jeremiah 40]], [[1 Nephi 10]], [[Jeremiah 13]], [[3 Nephi 21]], [[Alma 16]], [[Alma 17]], [[Alma 18]], and [[Mormon 5]] — need individual passage review before being cited as evidence for this topic; several (Mormon 5's closing Zenos-gathering material is one candidate) may deserve promotion after that review, rather than blanket dismissal.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- When a passage says "Israel," does it mean the northern kingdom, Judah-inclusive covenant Israel, genealogical descendants, or an eschatological people? Compare [[2 Kings 17]], [[Jeremiah 52]], and [[Ezekiel 37]].
- Is gathering primarily return to land, political reunification, covenant conversion, or acceptance of Christ — or some combination? Ezekiel 37:21-28 and 3 Nephi 20:13 emphasize overlapping but nonidentical dimensions.
- What became of the deported northern Israelites? D&C 110:11 names "the land of the north" as their location, while 1 Nephi 22:4 states plainly that Nephi's own community did not know where they had gone — the canon itself holds both a confident geographic claim and a confessed uncertainty.
- How should "isles of the sea" and "all nations" be read — ancient geographic idiom, prophetic universalism, or literal demographic prediction?
- Does calling dispersion providentially useful risk minimizing the conquest, death, and dispossession recorded in [[2 Kings 17]], [[2 Kings 24]], and [[Jeremiah 52]]?
- Is the Zenos material in 1 Nephi 19 an ancient prophetic tradition or a 19th-century theological synthesis? No independently attested Zenos text exists for comparison.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest historical objection is that conquest and deportation were ordinary instruments of Assyrian and Babylonian empire. Political weakness, rebellion, tribute disputes, military strategy, and imperial population management can explain the observable events without invoking covenant judgment. A careful response concedes this methodological point: the historical mechanisms and the scriptural claim about divine meaning operate at different explanatory levels. External evidence supports the events, not God's intention.

A related critical view holds that biblical writers or editors interpreted national catastrophe after the fact, shaping older warnings and histories into a Deuteronomic scheme of sin, punishment, repentance, and restoration. On this view, theological coherence may reflect retrospective literary construction rather than predictive revelation. Believing readers may receive that interpretation as inspired, but the text's theological assertion cannot settle the compositional question by itself.

Popular discourse about “the lost ten tribes” can overstate both totality and separateness. Imperial deportations were selective; people remained in the land, moved south, intermarried, assimilated, or retained identities in varied ways. 2 Chronicles 30 provides an internal complication to a total-disappearance model. Claims identifying an isolated modern people as a complete lost tribe require evidence beyond resemblance, oral tradition, patriarchal language, or universal prophecy.

Worldwide language can be overread. “All nations” and the four directions may be conventional universal rhetoric. Even if interpreted literally as eventual worldwide diffusion, those phrases do not specify timing, percentages, biological descent, or identifiable tribal continuity.

The providential claim that scattering blessed nations can unintentionally sanitize forced migration. The narratives include siege, execution, dispossession, loss of sovereignty, and captivity. A morally responsible theology may discern later good without calling imperial violence good or treating victims chiefly as instruments for outsiders.

The Book of Mormon's account supplies a powerful internal scattering framework, but a critic can explain that framework as creative reuse of biblical exile-and-restoration traditions. The motif alone neither proves ancient authorship nor identifies Indigenous peoples generally as descendants of Lehi. [[What is the Book of Mormon's view of Native American origins]] and [[Does DNA evidence disprove the Book of Mormon]] require separate evidence and must not be settled by this topic page.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Historical-critical scholarship broadly accepts the reality of Assyrian and Babylonian conquest, deportation, demographic disruption, and later return or resettlement, while disputing totals, chronology at some points, and the extent of continuity. Mordechai Cogan and Hayim Tadmor's *II Kings* situates the biblical account beside ancient Near Eastern evidence. Lester L. Grabbe's *Ancient Israel: What Do We Know and How Do We Know It?* emphasizes calibrated use of texts, inscriptions, and archaeology. Oded Lipschits's *The Fall and Rise of Jerusalem: Judah under Babylonian Rule* stresses that Babylonian-period Judah was devastated but not simply emptied of population.

Scholars also commonly read 2 Kings 17 as theological historiography: it reports genuine imperial history while explaining that history through covenant infidelity. The principal divide is therefore not whether conquest occurred, but whether the narrator's theological explanation is accepted as revelation, analyzed as a retrospective ideology, or understood in some combination of those ways.

On the northern tribes, careful scholarship generally replaces the image of ten intact tribes disappearing together with a more complex picture of deportation, local survival, migration into Judah, assimilation, and persistent identity claims. This does not prove that every later tradition is false; it raises the evidentiary threshold for specific identifications.

The supplied Latter-day Saint library represents the believing position chiefly through institutional curriculum and general conference rather than peer-reviewed historical monographs. [[Thoughts to Keep in Mind “Jesus Will Say to All Israel, ‘Come Home’”]] reads Lehi's family as a branch of scattered Israel. [[Safely Gathered Home (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2023)]] receives rebellion and providential blessing as doctrine. [[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]] situates Israel within a contemporary covenant emphasis. Believing and critical readers can agree on these texts' meanings within Latter-day Saint discourse while differing sharply over revelation, Book of Mormon historicity, prophetic prediction, and divine providence.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Read Deuteronomy 4:25-31 and 28:63-68 for the covenant warning, linked to [[Abrahamic Covenant]].
2. Study the [[Division of the kingdom]], then [[2 Kings 17]] — distinguishing the reported Assyrian conquest from the narrator's theological explanation.
3. Follow Judah through [[2 Kings 24]], [[2 Chronicles 36]], and [[Jeremiah 52]]; compare the [[Babylonian captivity of Judah]] with the [[Return from exile]].
4. Read the restoration prophets together: [[Amos 9]]; [[Jeremiah 29]] and [[Jeremiah 31]]; [[Ezekiel 20]], [[Ezekiel 37]], [[Ezekiel 39]]; [[Isaiah 27]]; [[Zechariah 10]].
5. Turn to the Book of Mormon: 1 Nephi 10:12-14 (treat as unverified for this topic pending direct review); the Zenos material in [[1 Nephi 19]]; [[1 Nephi 22]]; [[3 Nephi 20]].
6. Examine the Restoration application in D&C 101 and D&C 110:11, noting the 'land of the north' claim.
7. Compare historical formulations across [[A Compendium of the Doctrines of the Gospel]], the 1910/1921/1928 conference reports, and modern statements from [[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]] and [[Safely Gathered Home (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2023)]], watching how emphasis shifts among land, genealogy, and covenant identity.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The scattering of Israel is best understood as a family of related events and interpretations, not one event. Historically firmest: the Assyrian conquest/deportation of the northern kingdom and the Babylonian conquest/deportations of Judah, both independently attested outside scripture. The biblical record interprets these through covenant warning; external evidence supports the events but cannot itself establish divine judgment as their cause.

The canon refuses to make scattering the last word — Amos preserves a remnant, Jeremiah promises the scatterer will gather, Ezekiel imagines reunion and covenant renewal — placing this topic beside [[Gathering of Israel]], [[Abrahamic Covenant]], and [[Return from exile]]. The Book of Mormon extends the pattern: 1 Nephi 22 calls it both temporal and spiritual and admits ignorance of where the tribes went; 3 Nephi 20 makes restored knowledge of Christ central to gathering.

Several restraints preserve intellectual integrity here: "Israel" does not always designate the same group; the ten tribes were not necessarily removed without remainder; universal phrases do not map tribal destinations; and D&C 110:11's confident "land of the north" sits unresolved beside Nephi's own confessed uncertainty. What is established is that Israelite communities experienced conquest, deportation, and partial return, and that the canon interprets this through judgment and promised restoration. What remains a matter of faith is God's precise purpose and the extent of fulfillment beyond recoverable history — this page keeps those levels distinct on purpose.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
