# Faith

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, **faith** is trust directed toward God and, in its explicitly Christian form, toward Jesus Christ. [[Hebrews 11]] describes it in relation to hoped-for and unseen realities (Hebrews 11:1). [[Alma 32]] adds that it is not perfect knowledge and that what is hoped for must be true (Alma 32:18–21). [[Ether 12]] connects it with action before witness (Ether 12:6), and [[James 2]] insists that a profession producing no action is dead (James 2:14–17).

These passages make faith more than intellectual assent, but they do not make thought irrelevant. Faith includes confidence, reliance, fidelity, willingness to act, and perseverance under incomplete sight. Alma's seed discourse portrays it as provisional but testable trust: a person gives place to the word, observes its effects, and continues nourishing it (Alma 32:27–43).

Official Latter-day Saint usage deliberately says **faith in Jesus Christ**, not merely faith in general. [[Faith in Jesus Christ (Gospel Topics)]] presents imperfect but real trust in Christ as something that can grow. [[Let Your Faith Show (Elder Russell M. Nelson, April 2014)]] similarly distinguishes faith in Christ from undirected religiosity, while [[Faith A Bond of Trust and Loyalty (Elder Sandino Roman, April 2025)]] emphasizes confidence, trust, and loyalty. Those are modern doctrinal formulations; the underlying scriptural data remain the controlling evidence.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon presents faith as trust directed toward God and, in Christian proclamation, toward [[Jesus Christ]]—his grace, promises, atoning work, resurrection, and name (Romans 3:22–25; Galatians 2:16; Moroni 7:25–26). Some Pauline phrases translated “faith of Jesus Christ” remain grammatically disputed, but the wider canon plainly calls people to believe in Christ.

Faith is not identical with perfect knowledge. [[Alma 32]] describes hope for unseen things that are true and a process in which provisional willingness, practice, observation, patience, and nourishment may yield limited knowledge (Alma 32:18–43). [[Ether 12]] places a promised witness after trial (Ether 12:6), without saying that faith begins in the absence of every prior reason or testimony.

Faith is expected to have practical and communal expression. [[James 2]] calls faith without works dead, while Ether 12:4 joins faith-generated hope with steadfastness and good works. Paul excludes boasting and justification by deeds of the law while still affirming that deeds matter in judgment (Romans 2:6; Romans 3:20–31; Romans 4:9–12). Christian traditions differ over how these claims fit together; describing works as fruit or embodiment of faith is a major harmonization, not a conclusion supplied without interpretation by one passage.

Faith is joined with [[Repentance]], [[Hope]], [[Charity]], [[Obedience]], ordinances, prayer, learning, and endurance. It can grow, be strengthened, endure persecution, or be overthrown. It does not guarantee immediate physical rescue: [[Hebrews 11]] honors both deliverance and faithful suffering unto death.

Official Restoration teaching identifies faith in Jesus Christ as the first principle of the gospel and assigns parents responsibility to teach faith, repentance, baptism, the gift of the Holy Ghost, prayer, and upright conduct (Articles of Faith 1:4; D&C 68:25–28). That official synthesis should remain distinct from disputed explanations of justification, covenant participation, ordinances, sanctification, perseverance, and final judgment.

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The earliest scriptural narratives portray trust through covenant response more often than through an abstract definition. Abraham acts on divine promise; Israel must continue trusting beyond initial deliverance; David locates deliverance in the Lord rather than conventional weapons. [[Hebrews 11]] later rereads figures from Abel onward as exemplars of faith. That is a canonical Christian interpretation of earlier history, not proof that every ancient figure articulated faith in later Christian terminology.

The New Testament centers faith on the advent, death, and resurrection of Christ. Paul develops its relationship to [[Grace]], the [[Law of Moses]], the [[Abrahamic Covenant]], Gentile inclusion, and justification, especially in [[Romans 3]], [[Romans 4]], [[Galatians 2]], and [[Galatians 3]]. [[James 2]] opposes inactive profession. The Thessalonian and pastoral letters show faith becoming a sustained communal identity that can grow, endure, or be abandoned.

Book of Mormon discourse makes the concept unusually explicit. [[Alma 32]] distinguishes faith from perfect knowledge and develops the seed experiment. [[Ether 12]] links trial, witness, hope, weakness, grace, and miracles. [[Moroni 7]] integrates faith in Christ with hope, charity, moral discernment, and laying hold on good things.

Restoration revelations apply faith institutionally and practically: witnesses act before seeing (D&C 17:2–5), seekers ask with honest hearts and morally disciplined desires (D&C 8:1, 10–11), parents teach faith with repentance and ordinances (D&C 68:25–28), and disciples seek learning by both study and faith (D&C 88:118–119). Modern teaching continues the Christ-centered emphasis. [[The Blessing of Scripture (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, April 2010)]] describes scripture's central purpose as filling souls with faith in the Father and Son; [[Spiritually Whole in Him (President Camille N. Johnson, April 2025)]] applies faith and hope to healing without making mortal wholeness automatic.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
**Strong textual findings.** Multiple canonical corpora converge on a recognizable pattern: faith concerns unseen but hoped-for truth; trusts God or Christ; acts; endures; and may receive later witness. The convergence is especially clear across [[Hebrews 11]], [[Alma 32]], [[Ether 12]], and [[James 2]]. It is also textually strong that the canon does not promise immediate physical rescue to every faithful person: Hebrews 11:33–40 honors both deliverance and suffering unto death.

**Strong contextual finding.** Paul and James cannot responsibly be reduced to competing slogans. Paul attacks boasting and justification by deeds of the law (Romans 3:20, 27–31; Galatians 2:16); James attacks a verbal profession that refuses embodied mercy (James 2:14–26). Context makes substantial reconciliation possible, though it does not erase every historical disagreement about justification.

**Moderate interpretive evidence.** Alma's experiment is a serious religious epistemology: it invites provisional participation and attention to experienced effects (Alma 32:27–43). It can support warranted trust and practical knowledge. It is not a controlled experiment, however, and it cannot by itself distinguish revelation from expectation, confirmation bias, community reinforcement, or ordinary psychological change. Its strongest direct result is knowledge that the word or seed is producing the described effects—not automatic proof of every associated historical claim.

**Limited apologetic reach.** D&C 17 describes faith preceding sight and testimony by the Three Witnesses. That is important for understanding the revelation's own model of faith and witness, but the passage alone does not independently authenticate the objects seen or adjudicate competing historical explanations. Likewise, cross-canonical coherence supports a unified theological reading but does not prove divine inspiration merely because several texts share a theme.

The chapter index's numerous “ambiguous name match” flags should not be treated as uncertainty about a person named Faith. Inspection of Galatians 3, Romans 4, 1 Thessalonians 3, D&C 17, and similar passages shows ordinary theological uses of *faith*, *believe*, or *faithful*. Mention counts locate concentrated material; frequency by itself does not determine doctrinal importance or prove an interpretation.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. **What may faith legitimately claim to know?** [[Alma 32]] distinguishes faith from perfect knowledge but also describes growth into knowledge. Does the experiment confirm a proposition, a way of life, the word's spiritual fruit, or all three?

2. **How do grace, faith, works, ordinances, and judgment fit together?** Romans 2:6, Romans 3:20–31, [[Romans 4]], [[James 2]], and Ether 12:4 must all remain in view. Different Christian traditions organize these texts differently; see [[Grace]] and [[Justification and Sanctification]].

3. **Why does faithful action sometimes lead to a witness and sometimes to silence or suffering?** Ether 12:6 and D&C 17:2–5 emphasize witness after trial, while Hebrews 11:35–40 honors those who received no mortal deliverance. This leads directly to [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]] and [[Why does God allow suffering]].

4. **How should spiritual experience be evaluated amid religious diversity?** Alma's language of growth and illumination is significant, but comparable confidence appears in incompatible traditions. See [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]].

5. **When does doubt become unbelief, and when is it honest inquiry?** Scripture condemns some forms of unbelief but also commands learning, testing, and seeking (Alma 32:27; D&C 88:118). The canon gives no simple psychological test for every case.

6. **Does Paul's phrase mean faith in Christ or Christ's faithfulness?** Romans 3:22 and Galatians 2:16, 20 permit a real grammatical debate. Either reading can preserve a human response of trust, but each places emphasis differently.

7. **Can faith and disciplined inquiry correct one another?** D&C 88:118–119 joins study and faith. The practical boundaries of that partnership remain a central issue for [[How do faith and science fit together]].
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
**“Faith is belief without adequate evidence.”** Hebrews 11 and Ether 12 openly locate faith before sight, so the evidentialist objection cannot simply be dismissed. Alma answers partly by making faith provisional, truth-directed, and responsive to experienced fruit. The reply is limited: inner enlargement is evidence of some kind, but it is not public proof of every metaphysical or historical claim.

**“The seed experiment is circular and vulnerable to confirmation bias.”** A person first desires belief, gives the claim space, and then interprets positive feelings as growth. Expectation, selective attention, community pressure, and sunk-cost effects can imitate the described process. A believing response is that Alma requires patience, discernible fruit, continued testing, and truth rather than mere intensity. Even so, the passage does not provide a neutral control condition; alternative explanations must be assessed rather than defined away.

**“Paul and James contradict one another.”** Paul says a person is justified by faith without deeds of the law (Romans 3:28), while James says a person is justified by works and not by faith only (James 2:24). The strongest reconciliation notes their different opponents and uses of terms: Paul excludes boasting and law-based status; James exposes empty profession. The strongest counterreply is that contextual difference does not automatically make their wording identical, and later harmonization can conceal genuine diversity of emphasis.

**“Faith claims survive by explaining away failed outcomes.”** Successful deliverance can be credited to faith, while failure can be called a trial or deferred blessing. That structure risks unfalsifiability and survivor bias. [[Hebrews 11]] at least refuses the cruel inference that sufferers necessarily lacked faith, but it does not explain why outcomes differ or supply a public rule for predicting miracles. See [[Miracles]] and [[Does intercessory prayer work]].

**“Religious experience cannot identify one true tradition.”** People in incompatible religions report peace, transformation, and perceived answers. This seriously limits any argument from experience to exclusive doctrinal truth. A Latter-day Saint may treat experience as one strand within a cumulative case involving scripture, Christ, moral fruit, covenant practice, and historical claims, but the pluralism objection remains substantial.

**“Faith can reward loyalty over truth.”** Communities may label criticism, disconfirming evidence, or moral protest as faithlessness. The canon itself offers correctives—truth as faith's proper object (Alma 32:21), exclusion of favoritism (James 2:1), moral fruit (James 2:14–17), humility rather than boasting (Romans 3:27), and study as well as faith (D&C 88:118). These safeguards require actual use; invoking faith does not make a claim true or a leader trustworthy.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship widely recognizes that Greek *pistis* can carry relational senses including trust, faithfulness, reliability, and fidelity, not merely assent to propositions. Teresa Morgan's *Roman Faith and Christian Faith* places early Christian usage within the trust relationships of the Greco-Roman world. Some recent writers use “allegiance” to recover faith's enacted and loyal dimensions, though critics warn that no single English word captures every Pauline use.

Romans 3:22 and Galatians 2:16, 20 stand at the center of the *pistis Christou* debate. The traditional reading is “faith in Christ”; Richard B. Hays and others have argued for “the faithfulness of Christ.” Many scholars adopt mediating accounts in which Christ's fidelity grounds and evokes human trust. This debate affects emphasis, but neither side can responsibly erase Paul's explicit calls for people to believe.

The so-called New Perspective on Paul, associated especially with E. P. Sanders and James D. G. Dunn, challenged portrayals of ancient Judaism as simple works-righteousness. It emphasizes covenant membership and, in Dunn's formulation, the role of works of the law in Jewish-Gentile boundary questions. Traditional Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox, and newer Pauline readings dispute how far that account should reshape justification. The strongest reading of the dossier therefore avoids claiming that “works” always means one thing.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation characteristically reads [[Alma 32]] as an invitation to experiment on the word and grow from desire toward spiritual knowledge, while [[Ether 12]] supplies the pattern of witness after trial. Critical approaches point out that literary sophistication and psychological fruit do not independently prove supernatural origin or Book of Mormon historicity. Both observations can stand: the discourses offer a substantial theology of faith, and their theological quality alone does not settle questions of provenance.

Modern official teaching is comparatively stable on the principal object of faith. [[Faith in Jesus Christ (Gospel Topics)]], [[Let Your Faith Show (Elder Russell M. Nelson, April 2014)]], and [[Faith A Bond of Trust and Loyalty (Elder Sandino Roman, April 2025)]] define the relevant principle through trust in Christ. These ecclesial sources state the tradition's doctrine; they are not substitutes for independent historical or philosophical argument.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with [[Alma 32]]. Track the distinctions among desire, belief, faith, knowledge, experimentation, neglect, patience, and fruit (Alma 32:18–43). Ask exactly what becomes known at each stage.

2. Read [[Hebrews 11]] as a whole, not only verse 1. Make two columns: deliverance received (Hebrews 11:29–35) and suffering endured without mortal fulfillment (Hebrews 11:35–40). Connect this with [[Endurance]] and [[Exodus and Deliverance]].

3. Read [[Ether 12]]. Trace the sequence faith → hope → steadfast good works (Ether 12:4), trial → witness (Ether 12:6), and weakness → humility → grace (Ether 12:27). Do not turn one sequence into a guarantee of immediate miracles.

4. Read [[Moroni 7]] for the integration of faith, moral discernment, hope, prayer, good works, and [[Charity]] (Moroni 7:21–48).

5. Study Paul in sequence: [[Romans 3]], [[Romans 4]], Romans 5:1–2, Romans 9:30–33, and Romans 10:1–17; then [[Galatians 2]] and [[Galatians 3]]. Mark every reference to grace, boasting, law, Abraham, Jews and Gentiles, Christ, and believing.

6. Set [[James 2]] beside Paul. Identify the question each author asks, the error each opposes, and how each uses Abraham. Preserve both the contextual harmonies and the remaining differences in formulation.

7. Examine faith as a lived communal virtue in 1 Thessalonians 3, 2 Thessalonians 1, 1 Timothy 4 and 6, and 2 Timothy 2–3. Note how faith grows, is taught, is threatened, and is joined with love and patience.

8. Read D&C 8:1–11, D&C 17:2–7, D&C 68:25–28, and D&C 88:118–119. Compare faithful asking, witness, family instruction, and learning by study and faith. Continue through [[Prayer]], [[Family]], [[Testimony]], and [[Education and Learning]].

9. Finish with modern doctrinal application: [[Faith in Jesus Christ (Gospel Topics)]], [[The Blessing of Scripture (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, April 2010)]], [[Let Your Faith Show (Elder Russell M. Nelson, April 2014)]], [[Invite Christ to Author Your Story (Camille N. Johnson, October 2021)]], and [[Spiritually Whole in Him (President Camille N. Johnson, April 2025)]]. Keep pastoral promises distinct from claims that can be historically or empirically demonstrated.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scriptural faith is neither certainty mislabeled as knowledge nor arbitrary belief praised for lacking evidence. It is truth-directed trust under incomplete sight, centered in God and, in Christian and Restoration teaching, in [[Jesus Christ]]. It hopes, acts, repents, prays, learns, loves, and endures.

[[Alma 32]] gives the canon's most sustained account of growth from desire through experiment toward limited experiential knowledge. [[Hebrews 11]] portrays both deliverance and faithful suffering without mortal fulfillment. [[Ether 12]] joins faith to hope, trial, witness, weakness, and grace. [[James 2]] rejects inactive profession. Paul excludes boasting and places faith at the center of grace, justification, and Gentile inclusion. Paul and James address distinguishable errors, but their relationship still requires interpretation; Protestant, Catholic, Orthodox, Wesleyan, New Perspective, and Latter-day Saint accounts do not organize every element identically.

The canon therefore supports several firm boundaries. Faith's object is claimed to be true. Faith does not make study unnecessary. It does not guarantee immediate miracles or rescue. Sincerity, intensity, positive feeling, and communal loyalty do not by themselves establish historical or metaphysical truth. Alma's experiment may warrant personal trust and knowledge of experienced fruit, but it does not independently exclude expectation, confirmation bias, social reinforcement, or ordinary psychological change.

The strongest practical conclusion is that faith is reliance on Christ expressed through sustained covenantal action and hope. The corresponding epistemic caution is essential: literary coherence, spiritual experience, and faithful endurance may support belief without proving divine inspiration, historicity, or every unseen proposition associated with that belief. Responsible faith therefore remains open to truth, humility, moral fruit, correction, disciplined study, and honest inquiry.
