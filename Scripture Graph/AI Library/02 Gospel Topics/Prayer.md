# Prayer

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, prayer is deliberate address and responsive openness to God. Its forms include praise, thanksgiving, confession, lament, petition, intercession, submission, and silent inward desire. It can be private (Matthew 6:6), communal ([[Moroni 6]]; Moroni 6:5, 9), liturgical ([[1 Kings 8]]), urgent ([[Enos 1]]), or continually present as a disposition of the heart (Alma 34:27).

In Latter-day Saint teaching, ordinary prayer is directed to the Father in the name of [[Jesus Christ]], with the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]] assisting sincere communication and discernment. [[3 Nephi 18]] states this pattern directly in verses 19–21. [[3 Nephi 19]] also preserves an exceptional scene in which disciples pray to Jesus while he is physically among them; verse 22 gives that circumstance as the narrative's explanation. [[Prayer (Gospel Topics)]] provides the Church's concise instructional treatment. Conference addresses such as [[Be Your Best Self (President Thomas S. Monson, April 2009)]] are authoritative pastoral teaching but are not additions to the scriptural canon.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon teaches prayer as relationship and covenantal practice, not a technique for controlling God. Jesus centers prayer on the Father's holiness, kingdom, daily provision, forgiveness, moral deliverance, and submission ([[Matthew 6]]; Matthew 6:9–13). At Bountiful he commands prayer to the Father in his name, including prayer within households, and joins watchfulness with resistance to temptation ([[3 Nephi 18]]; 3 Nephi 18:15–21).

Prayer requires sincerity rather than display. Jesus condemns prayer performed for an audience and the supposition that sheer verbal quantity compels a hearing (Matthew 6:5–7). This does not prohibit all repeated language: Gethsemane includes repeated petition together with “thy will be done” (Matthew 26:39–44). Likewise, “pray always” need not mean uninterrupted speech. Alma describes the heart remaining drawn out when vocal prayer ceases (Alma 34:27), and the Lord knows the silent prayers of Alma's oppressed people ([[Mosiah 24]]; Mosiah 24:11–12).

Faithful prayer is morally situated. [[Alma 34]] declares devotion vain when it coexists with refusal to assist the needy (Alma 34:28); [[Mosiah 4]] connects daily prayer and retained forgiveness with humility and imparting to others. Prayer also accompanies diligent action: Enos “prayed and labored” (Enos 1:12), Nehemiah prays before acting, and D&C 88 joins prayer with study and ordered learning. Thus [[Faith]], [[Charity]], [[Service]], and [[Education and Learning]] are not competitors to prayer but parts of its canonical ecology.

The canon promises real divine involvement but does not provide a simple outcome formula. Sometimes rescue is narrated ([[Mosiah 9]]); sometimes strength precedes release ([[Mosiah 24]]); sometimes peace rather than changed circumstances is promised ([[Philippians 4]]); and Jesus's Gethsemane request submits to a path that still includes suffering. James 5:14–16 warrants prayer and anointing for the sick, but the whole canon does not justify blaming continuing illness on inadequate faith. [[Ponder the Path of Thy Feet (President Thomas S. Monson, October 2014)]] appropriately holds the Lord's Prayer beside Gethsemane's submission.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
Hebrew Bible prayer develops within family, prophetic, royal, temple, and exilic settings. The Psalms supply a durable vocabulary of praise, lament, confession, petition, and trust; [[Psalm 86]] grounds requests in God's merciful character while also asking to be taught God's way. Solomon's dedication places prayer within covenant, temple, sin, exile, forgiveness, and restoration ([[Dedication of Solomon's Temple]]; 1 Kings 8:22–53; 2 Chronicles 6:12–42). Exilic and postexilic prayers such as Daniel 9 and [[Nehemiah 1]] emphasize confession, remembered covenant, fasting, and action.

Jesus inherits Jewish prayer while sharpening its inward and filial dimensions. [[Matthew 6]] opposes public performance and verbal manipulation; the Lord's Prayer makes God's kingdom, dependence, reconciliation, and deliverance central. In Gethsemane, petition and submission coexist. The earliest Jerusalem disciples continue temple prayer after Pentecost ([[Acts 3]]; Acts 3:1), while Christian communities also pray in homes and assemblies. Paul depicts prayer as thanksgiving, intercession, solidarity in mission, and openness to the Spirit's help (Romans 8:26–27; Romans 15:30–32; Philippians 4:6–7; 1 Thessalonians 5:17, 25).

The Book of Mormon intensifies several motifs: Enos's prayer expands from personal forgiveness to enemies ([[Enos 1]]); King Benjamin joins prayer to retaining forgiveness and serving the poor ([[Mosiah 4]]); oppressed believers pray silently and receive strength before escape ([[Mosiah 24]]); Amulek makes every sphere of life an object of petition while declaring prayer vain without mercy ([[Alma 34]]); and the risen Christ establishes a prayerful church at Bountiful ([[3 Nephi 18]], [[3 Nephi 19]]). [[Moroni 6]] depicts frequent fasting, prayer, mutual care, and Spirit-led meetings.

The Restoration's founding account presents prayer for wisdom as the immediate setting of [[The First Vision]]. Later revelations connect prayer with family formation, temple worship, schooling, fasting, and church order. Historical Latter-day Saint practice includes dedicatory prayers, congregational prayers ratified by “amen,” family and personal prayer, fasting, healing administration, and prayers seeking revelation. [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[Conference Report, April 1904 (General Conference (full report), April 1904)]], and [[Chapter 10 Prayer and Personal Revelation]] document aspects of that developing instructional tradition. These later teachings interpret and apply the canon; they should not all be treated as equally binding or infallible.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence here is textual. Across distinct canonical collections, prayer is repeatedly joined to humility, confession, gratitude, moral change, concern for others, and action. That convergence strongly establishes what the canon teaches prayer ought to be. It does not independently prove God's existence, the historicity of every narrated answer, or supernatural causation.

Some narrative sequences are especially informative. [[Mosiah 24]] moves from silent prayer to an assurance of eventual deliverance, strengthened endurance, and finally escape (Mosiah 24:12–21). Within the story's theology, divine help includes both bearing suffering and release from it. [[Enos 1]] moves from personal reconciliation to concern for Nephites and Lamanites. [[Alma 34]] tests prayer ethically by the worshipper's treatment of the poor. These are strong literary-theological findings; their status as independently verified historical events is a separate question.

The index itself needs quality control. Its “ambiguous name match” label is a retrieval warning, not a judgment that the chapters are irrelevant. [[3 Nephi 19]], [[Nehemiah 1]], [[James 5]], and [[2 Nephi 32]] contain unmistakable devotional prayer. Conversely, in Micah 3:1 and 3:9 the English expression “I pray you” means approximately “please,” so raw occurrence counts overstate theological references. Mention frequency must never substitute for verse-level interpretation.

Empirical research bears most directly on the narrower question [[Does intercessory prayer work]]. Herbert Benson and colleagues' 2006 STEP cardiac-bypass trial did not find improved outcomes from assigned distant intercession among patients uncertain of their assignment; a 2009 Cochrane review by Leanne Roberts and colleagues judged the clinical evidence insufficient for confident benefit claims. Such studies are relevant and prevent easy assertions that distant prayer reliably changes measurable medical outcomes. Their limits are also real: they cannot test every meaning of prayer, prevent unassigned prayer, or force divine action into an experimental protocol. The honest conclusion is neither “science proved prayer” nor “science disproved all prayer.”

Experiential reports of peace, guidance, repentance, and enlarged compassion are genuine data about human experience, but their cause remains contestable. Believers may interpret them as interaction with God; naturalistic accounts invoke reflection, expectation, emotional regulation, coincidence, and community reinforcement. [[Watch Ye Therefore, and Pray Always (President M. Russell Ballard, October 2020)]] emphasizes transformation of the worshipper, a claim more modest and often more observable than guaranteed alteration of external events.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Significant questions remain:

- What does it mean for an omniscient and unchanging God to respond to requests? Is prayer intended to alter God's action, the worshipper, a relationship, or some combination?
- How should promises about receiving be read beside Gethsemane, Paul's unremoved affliction, delayed deliverance, illness, and death?
- When is an impression an answer from God rather than desire, fear, coincidence, or ordinary cognition? [[Revelation]] and [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]] are essential companion studies.
- Why do some urgent and morally worthy petitions appear unanswered? [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]] and [[Why does God allow suffering]] carry the pastoral and philosophical weight that simplified testimony can evade.
- How should James 5's healing language be taught without blaming sick people or discouraging competent medical care?
- What does praying “in the name of Christ” require beyond closing language—alignment with Christ's character, authorized representation, submission, or all three?
- How are commands to “pray always” embodied without turning prayer into anxious compulsivity or empty routine?
- How should the exceptional direct prayer to Jesus in 3 Nephi 19:18–22 relate to the ordinary Father-directed pattern of 3 Nephi 18:19–21?
- How can communal prayer express shared faith without becoming performance, coercion, political signaling, or presumed access to God's endorsement?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest empirical objection is that controlled studies of assigned distant intercession have not shown a robust, reproducible medical benefit. Retrospective testimony is also vulnerable to selection, confirmation, and attribution effects: memorable apparent answers may receive more attention than unanswered petitions, while coincidence, natural recovery, changed behavior, and social support remain live explanations. These considerations do not prove that God never answers prayer, but they rule out presenting testimony as controlled demonstration.

A philosophical objection asks why an omniscient and perfectly good God would need to be informed or persuaded. Several live models answer differently. Classical providence treats prayer as a foreknown means through which God forms participants and brings about intended ends. Open or dynamic models hold that some future contingencies are genuinely unsettled, allowing petitions to affect a responsive divine relationship. Finitist or process-oriented models revise divine power or immutability more substantially. Each may make petition coherent within its premises; none is experimentally established, and the canon does not provide one systematic metaphysics of prayer.

The moral objection is severe: rescue narratives stand beside equally sincere prayers from people who remain abused, displaced, ill, or bereaved. Simplified claims about miracles can trivialize divine silence and encourage blame toward sufferers. Scripture resists some triumphalism through lament, Gethsemane, Paul's unremoved affliction, delayed deliverance, and prayer for endurance, but it does not dissolve the problem.

Naturalistic accounts can explain many reported benefits: structured reflection may clarify goals, confession may reduce conflict, gratitude may redirect attention, and communal prayer may strengthen social support. Such mechanisms may operate whether or not God exists; psychological benefit alone therefore cannot authenticate revelation or supernatural causation.

Prayer can also be abused through public performance, coercion, claims of divine endorsement, spiritualization of injustice, or substitution for medical and practical action. Matthew 6:5-7 and Alma 34:28 condemn close analogues. A faithful response may appeal cumulatively to scripture, conscience, long-term moral fruit, communal discernment, and lived experience. That can make prayer reasonable within belief in a personal God, but it does not compel those who reject that larger framework.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship broadly agrees that prayer is not a single literary form. Hebrew Bible studies examine lament, hymn, confession, petition, thanksgiving, royal prayer, and communal liturgy. Samuel E. Balentine's *Prayer in the Hebrew Bible: The Drama of Divine-Human Dialogue* and Patrick D. Miller's *They Cried to the Lord: The Form and Theology of Biblical Prayer* emphasize prayer as dialogical covenant speech shaped by Israel's worship and crises. Historical-critical work can describe these forms and their development without deciding whether God actually answered.

New Testament scholarship studies Jesus's Jewish prayer context, the Lord's Prayer, Gethsemane, prayer in Luke–Acts, Pauline thanksgiving and intercession, and early Christian worship. Oscar Cullmann's *Prayer in the New Testament* offers a theological reading centered on trust, petition, and submission. Confessional and nonconfessional scholars often agree on textual form and historical setting; they part when moving from description to claims about revelation, providence, and divine agency.

Latter-day Saint interpretation gives special weight to prayer as a means of personal revelation and covenant formation. [[Prayer (Gospel Topics)]], [[Gospel Doctrine]], and [[Chapter 10 Prayer and Personal Revelation]] represent instructional and historical strands. Dallin H. Oaks's “The Language of Prayer,” cited by the Gospel Topics resource, addresses reverent usage as a devotional norm. These sources illuminate the tradition's self-understanding; they are not neutral empirical demonstrations of its truth claims.

Psychology and medical research address narrower questions. Herbert Benson and colleagues' STEP trial and the Roberts-led Cochrane review do not support confident claims of predictable clinical benefit from assigned distant intercession. Research on personal prayer, meditation, coping, and religious participation sometimes reports associations with well-being, but causation is difficult to isolate because belief, community, behavior, health, and selection effects interact. Scholarship is strongest when it distinguishes literary meaning, reported experience, measurable consequence, and metaphysical explanation.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
A disciplined canonical pathway is:

1. Begin with Jesus's instruction in [[Matthew 6]] and [[3 Nephi 13]]: identify the rejected motives, then outline the priorities of the model prayer.
2. Read Gethsemane in Matthew 26:36–46 and Luke 22:39–46 beside [[Ponder the Path of Thy Feet (President Thomas S. Monson, October 2014)]]. Ask how honest petition and submission coexist.
3. Study the risen Christ's community in [[3 Nephi 18]] and [[3 Nephi 19]]. Track recipient, Christ's name, household prayer, temptation, unity, desire for the Holy Ghost, and the exceptional prayer addressed to Jesus.
4. Follow prayer's moral expansion in [[Enos 1]], then compare [[Mosiah 4]] and [[Alma 34]]. Mark every place where prayer is joined to labor, enemies, the poor, and the welfare of others.
5. Compare forms of help in [[Mosiah 23]], [[Mosiah 24]], Philippians 4:6–9, and 2 Corinthians 12:7–10. Distinguish preservation, endurance, peace, delayed release, and an unchanged circumstance.
6. Read temple and communal prayer through [[1 Kings 8]], [[2 Chronicles 6]], [[Nehemiah 1]], [[Acts 3]], and [[Moroni 6]]. Note confession, corporate assent, sacred place, action, fasting, and Spirit-led worship.
7. Study Romans 8:26–34 and James 5:13–18 for the Spirit's and Christ's intercession, mutual prayer, anointing, healing, and forgiveness. Then test interpretations against mortality and unanswered prayer.
8. Read D&C 68:25–28 and D&C 88:76–80, 118–119 alongside [[Family]] and [[Education and Learning]]. Observe how Restoration scripture integrates prayer with parenting, study, order, and worship.
9. Only then address [[Does intercessory prayer work]], [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]], and [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]]. Keep textual theology, personal testimony, historical claims, and experimental evidence in separate columns before attempting synthesis.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scripture presents prayer as truthful relationship with God: adoration without flattery, confession without despair, petition without entitlement, gratitude without denial of pain, and listening without surrendering discernment. Its normative Latter-day Saint form addresses the Father in the name of [[Jesus Christ]], assisted by the Spirit; its canonical practice ranges from private speech and communal worship to silent inward longing.

The strongest cross-canonical finding is ethical. Prayer that does not reshape conduct is repeatedly called into question. Enos prays and labors; Nehemiah confesses and acts; Amulek declares prayer vain without mercy; Paul joins prayer to thanksgiving, fellowship, and mission. [[Faith]], [[Charity]], [[Service]], [[Fasting]], and diligent learning belong with prayer rather than replacing it.

The texts also resist a prosperity formula. Some prayers precede dramatic rescue, but others yield strength, peace, moral clarity, or submission while suffering remains. Gethsemane prevents “ask and receive” from meaning that faithful desire controls outcomes. [[Mosiah 24]] shows aid within bondage before escape; Philippians promises guarded hearts rather than a trouble-free life. Healing prayer belongs in the canon, but using it to blame the ill contradicts the canon's wider witness and outruns the evidence.

Prayer's metaphysical efficacy remains a matter of faith rather than laboratory demonstration. Controlled studies do not establish reliable medical benefit from assigned distant intercession, while testimony and transformative experience admit both religious and naturalistic explanations. That uncertainty should discipline claims, not erase the practice's meaning. A careful believer can affirm answered prayer while acknowledging selection bias, silence, competing interpretations, and the suffering of equally sincere petitioners.

On the canon's own terms, mature prayer seeks more than changed circumstances: it seeks God's kingdom, forgiveness, deliverance from evil, the welfare of enemies and neighbors, courage to act, and a will increasingly aligned with the good. Its credibility is strongest where humility, truthfulness, mercy, discernment, and responsible action become visible.
