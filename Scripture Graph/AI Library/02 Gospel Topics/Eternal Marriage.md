# Eternal Marriage

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In Latter-day Saint scripture, **eternal marriage** or **celestial marriage** is a marriage covenant intended to remain valid after death and resurrection. Its most explicit canonical descriptions are D&C 131:1–4 and D&C 132:7, 15–20. The latter requires the covenant to be made according to God's word, administered through appointed priesthood authority, sealed by the Holy Spirit of promise, and abidingly kept. Official summaries such as [[Marriage (Gospel Topics)]], [[Marriage (True to the Faith)]], and [[Temples (Gospel Topics)]] call the administering ordinance a temple sealing.

The terminology needs discipline. The **new and everlasting covenant** can denote the restored covenant order more broadly; in D&C 131:2 it is specifically glossed as the new and everlasting covenant of marriage. Likewise, **sealing** has several scriptural meanings. Revelation 7:3–4 marks God's servants, 2 Nephi 27:7–11 closes a record, Jeremiah 32:10–14 authenticates legal documents, and D&C 76:53 describes divine ratification. These are not references to weddings. [[Ye Must Be Born Again (Elder David A. Bednar, April 2007)]] explicitly warns against reducing all sealing language to eternal marriage.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canonical center is [[D&C 131]] and [[D&C 132]]. D&C 131:1–4 places the marriage covenant within the highest degree of celestial glory. D&C 132:15–20 distinguishes mortal-only marriage, an unauthorized claim to eternal marriage, and a covenant said to endure because it is made by divine law, sealed through appointed authority, and ratified by the Holy Spirit of promise. The promised result includes an enduring union, exaltation, and continuation of the seeds; the text does not fully explain the metaphysics of those promises.

[[Matthew 19]] supplies an important biblical foundation but not the complete Restoration doctrine. Jesus appeals to creation, describes spouses as one flesh, and says that what God has joined should not be separated by humans (Matthew 19:4–6). [[Matthew 16]] gives Peter keys and authority to bind and loose (Matthew 16:19). Reading that authority as the power to seal marriages is a Restoration synthesis dependent on later revelation, not an explicit definition in Matthew.

The doctrine distinguishes administration from final ratification. Receiving an ordinance is not presented as an unconditional guarantee: D&C 132:19 requires abiding in the covenant, while official teaching repeatedly stresses continued faithfulness; see [[Marriage (Gospel Topics)]], [[Temples (True to the Faith)]], and [[Eternal Marriage Is an Eternal Journey (Elder Neil L. Andersen, April 2026)]]. D&C 76:50–70 also places sealing by the Holy Spirit of promise within a larger pattern of faith in Christ, authorized ordinances, overcoming, and grace.

Official contemporary teaching situates marriage sealing among the ordinances of the temple; see [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], [[Family (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Eternal Life (True to the Faith)]]. That official synthesis should be distinguished from conclusions supplied by any single verse.

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The biblical creation narratives portray companionship, one-flesh union, sexual differentiation, and fruitfulness (Genesis 1:27–28; 2:18, 24). Israelite scripture also preserves monogamy, polygyny, levirate marriage, divorce, and corporate husband-wife covenant metaphors. It does not clearly describe a priesthood ordinance making an individual marriage survive resurrection.

In the New Testament, Jesus grounds marriage in creation and opposes easy divorce in [[Matthew 19]]. The Synoptic resurrection controversy—Matthew 22:23–30, Mark 12:18–25, and Luke 20:27–36—says resurrected persons neither marry nor are given in marriage. Historic Christianity generally did not derive the distinctive Latter-day Saint marriage-sealing system from [[Matthew 16]] or [[Matthew 19]].

Restoration development occurred in stages. [[D&C 110]] records Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery's report that Elijah appeared in the Kirtland Temple and committed keys associated with turning fathers' and children's hearts. The chapter does not itself name marriage sealing or ordinances for the dead; those applications developed through later revelation and institutional interpretation. In May 1843, [[D&C 131]] explicitly connected the highest celestial degree with the new and everlasting covenant of marriage. In July 1843, Joseph Smith dictated the surviving revelation now published as [[D&C 132]], although relevant teachings and practices predated that dictation.

D&C 132 joins eternal-duration marriage teaching with plural-marriage defense and regulation. Early history therefore cannot responsibly separate celestial sealing from Joseph Smith's plural marriages, secrecy, disputed consent, dynastic or eternity-only sealings, and plural households. See [[40 United in an Everlasting Covenant]], [[Life of Heber C. Kimball]], [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]], and [[Did Joseph Smith marry other men's wives]]. Believing interpreters may distinguish an enduring sealing principle from a time-limited plural-marriage command through continuing revelation; critics question whether that later distinction adequately resolves the canonical and historical entanglement.

The 1890 Manifesto began the institutional discontinuation of new plural marriages, but cessation was not immediate: post-Manifesto plural marriages occurred, and enforcement hardened especially around the 1904 Second Manifesto. Monogamous temple sealing became the normal public form of celestial marriage while D&C 132 remained canonical. See [[Did plural marriage continue after the Manifesto]].

Access was also unequal. Until 1978, the priesthood and temple restriction affecting members of Black African descent excluded affected Black couples from temple sealing. Official Declaration 2 expressly opened priesthood ordination to worthy men without regard to race or color, with temple access opening as an institutional consequence. See [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]].

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong textual evidence

[[D&C 131]] and [[D&C 132]] unmistakably teach a distinctive Restoration doctrine in which an authorized, divinely ratified marriage may endure after death. This establishes what these texts claim. It does not independently establish that Joseph Smith possessed divine authority or that the promised postmortal state exists.

D&C 132:7, 18–19 strongly supports the distinction between an ordinance's administration and God's ratification. D&C 76:51–53 independently uses the Holy Spirit of promise in its description of celestial heirs, making this more than an isolated phrase about marriage.

### Significant historical evidence

[[D&C 110]] is strong documentary evidence that Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery reported a temple vision linking restored keys with gathering and Elijah's promise. Its force as evidence of supernatural appearances depends upon one's assessment of revelation, visionary experience, transmission, and the witnesses. The document establishes the claim much more directly than it establishes its supernatural cause.

[[40 United in an Everlasting Covenant]] and [[Life of Heber C. Kimball]] connect early celestial-marriage practice with plural marriage. This association is historically strong, not incidental. It neither proves divine command nor proves conscious fraud.

### Genuine counterevidence or tension

[[Matthew 22]], [[Mark 12]], and [[Luke 20]] say that resurrected persons do not marry or enter marriage. The common Latter-day Saint reconciliation—no new marriage is contracted after resurrection, while a premortal sealing can continue—is textually possible. It is not stated by the Synoptic writers, so it should be presented as a harmonizing interpretation rather than as the explicit answer of those passages.

### Excluded keyword evidence

[[Revelation 7]], [[2 Nephi 27]], [[D&C 77]], and [[Jeremiah 32]] contain many forms of *seal*, but their immediate subjects are apocalyptic marking, closed revelation, epochs of history, gospel ministers, or legal documents. Counting them as evidence for eternal marriage would be a lexical false positive.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should Matthew 22:30, Mark 12:25, and Luke 20:34–36 be reconciled with D&C 132:19? Does the proposed distinction between forming and continuing a marriage adequately answer the Synoptic texts?

- What exactly does it mean for the Holy Spirit of promise to seal a covenant, and how can participants know whether divine ratification has occurred? Compare D&C 76:51–53 with D&C 132:7, 19.

- How should D&C 132:19's requirement to abide in the covenant be related to D&C 132:26's difficult language about later sin? Neither simplistic unconditional security nor salvation by flawless marital performance captures the whole passage.

- What do increase and continuation of the seeds mean after resurrection (D&C 131:4; 132:19)? Scripture supplies evocative promises but few explanatory mechanics.

- How are promises applied to faithful people who remain single, experience divorce, escape abuse, have a spouse reject the covenant, or cannot marry in mortality? Official pastoral assurance is broader than the canon's detailed explanation of timing and implementation.

- How should the doctrine address gender asymmetries in D&C 132 and in historical sealing practice? The revelation speaks predominantly through male authority and embeds eternal marriage in a plural-wife framework.

- What theological status should be assigned to early eternity-only, dynastic, plural, or polyandrous sealings? See [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]] and [[Did Joseph Smith marry other men's wives]].

- How can the Church articulate the doctrine faithfully while accounting for the lived experience of LGBTQ members whose marriages are not eligible for temple sealing? See [[How does the Church regard LGBTQ members and same-sex marriage]].

- What does intergenerational turning in Malachi 4:5–6 and D&C 110:13–16 require on its own terms, and which connections to genealogy and ordinances depend upon later revelation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest biblical objection is direct: Jesus says resurrected people neither marry nor are given in marriage (Matthew 22:30; Mark 12:25; Luke 20:34–36). The Latter-day Saint distinction between post-resurrection contracting and continuation of a prior sealing can reconcile the texts, but it is not supplied by the Synoptic passages themselves. A critic may therefore judge D&C 132 to be a doctrinal innovation contrary to the New Testament's ordinary sense.

The strongest historical-ethical objection concerns authority and conflict of interest. [[D&C 132]] declares Joseph Smith the unique contemporary holder of the relevant keys, validates relationships concerning him, promises his exaltation, and pressures named family members to comply (D&C 132:7, 48–55). Its treatment of plural wives in verses 61–65 is difficult to reconcile with modern expectations of reciprocal consent and gender equality. A divine-command response is logically possible but cannot simply erase concerns about secrecy, unequal authority, emotional pressure, or the young ages and existing marriages involved in some historical cases. [[40 United in an Everlasting Covenant]], [[Life of Heber C. Kimball]], [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]], and [[Did Joseph Smith marry other men's wives]] belong in any full assessment.

A broader theological objection is that eternal well-being can appear dependent on access to an institutionally controlled ordinance. Historical racial exclusion makes that concern concrete rather than hypothetical; see [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]]. Questions about unmarried, divorced, widowed, disabled, or LGBTQ persons likewise test whether the doctrine's administration matches claims about divine justice and mercy.

A sociological alternative reads eternal marriage as a powerful community-forming theology: it sacralizes family commitment, binds generations to an institution, and gives grief a hopeful horizon. Those effects can be real without proving its metaphysical claims. Conversely, social function does not disprove revelation.

A pastoral objection concerns misuse. Treating sealing as a guarantee can minimize agency, repentance, abuse, or broken trust. Treating it as perpetually precarious can foster anxiety and perfectionism. The texts' combination of covenant fidelity, divine ratification, Christ's grace, and moral accountability resists both extremes.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship generally reads Matthew 19 within debates over divorce and creation, and Matthew 16:19 within traditions of keys, communal authority, and binding and loosing. Major critical commentaries such as W. D. Davies and Dale Allison's *Matthew*, Ulrich Luz's *Matthew*, and R. T. France's *The Gospel of Matthew* do not derive a Latter-day Saint temple-sealing system from these passages. The Synoptic resurrection controversy is commonly read as rejecting the Sadducees' assumption that resurrected existence merely continues mortal marriage structures.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation reads these biblical motifs cumulatively through modern revelation: creation establishes the union, Christ affirms divine joining, Peter receives heavenly binding authority, Elijah restores keys, and D&C 131–132 explains eternal application. [[Marriage (Gospel Topics)]], [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Temples (True to the Faith)]] represent the official synthesis. Its distinctive force comes primarily from Restoration revelation, not from an uncontested historical-critical reading of the Bible.

Historians across belief positions broadly agree that eternal sealing, priesthood authority, plural marriage, and Joseph Smith's developing temple theology were historically intertwined. They differ sharply over explanation and moral assessment. Richard Lyman Bushman's *Joseph Smith: Rough Stone Rolling* treats Joseph's revelatory claims seriously while acknowledging secrecy and cost. Brian C. Hales's *Joseph Smith's Polygamy* offers a detailed believing defense. Todd Compton's *In Sacred Loneliness* centers the plural wives' biographies and suffering. Laurel Thatcher Ulrich's *A House Full of Females* analyzes how plural marriage shaped women's lives and early Utah society. D. Michael Quinn's *The Mormon Hierarchy: Origins of Power* emphasizes institutional authority and development.

The main divide is not over whether the documents and marriages existed. It is over whether the development is best understood as genuine revelation through an imperfect prophet, sincere but culturally conditioned religious innovation, misuse of charismatic authority, or some mixture. Historical methods can illuminate chronology, language, relationships, and consequences; they cannot by themselves verify a postmortal sealing or rule out revelation.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with creation and Jesus's teaching on marriage: Genesis 1:26–28; 2:18–24 and [[Matthew 19]], especially Matthew 19:3–9. Record what these passages affirm and what they do not say about eternity.

2. Read the strongest New Testament challenge before attempting harmonization: [[Matthew 22]] 22:23–33, Mark 12:18–27, and Luke 20:27–40. Compare the precise wording and ask whether continuation of an existing marriage is addressed.

3. Study keys and binding in [[Matthew 16]] 16:13–20. Consult mainstream biblical commentary before moving to Restoration applications.

4. Trace Elijah's promise through Malachi 4:5–6, D&C 2:1–3, and [[D&C 110]] 110:13–16. Keep the chapter's explicit content distinct from later temple interpretation; then follow [[Elijah]], [[Priesthood keys restored in the Kirtland Temple]], and [[Gathering of Israel]].

5. Read [[D&C 131]] 131:1–4, then [[D&C 132]] in full rather than extracting verses 19–20. Mark every condition, promise, authority claim, reference to Abraham, plural-marriage rule, and named command.

6. Compare the Holy Spirit of promise in D&C 76:50–70 with D&C 132:7, 19, 26. Then study [[Covenants]], [[Ordinances]], [[Eternal Life]], and [[Kingdoms of Glory]].

7. Separate unrelated uses of sealing by sampling [[Revelation 7]], [[2 Nephi 27]], [[D&C 77]], Jeremiah 32, 2 Corinthians 1:21–22, and Ephesians 1:13–14.

8. Place the doctrine in history through [[40 United in an Everlasting Covenant]], [[Life of Heber C. Kimball]], [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]], [[Did Joseph Smith marry other men's wives]], and [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]]. Read both believing and critical historians.

9. Finish with present official teaching in [[Marriage (Gospel Topics)]], [[Marriage (True to the Faith)]], [[Endowment and Sealing Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], [[Family (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Eternal Marriage Is an Eternal Journey (Elder Neil L. Andersen, April 2026)]]. Note carefully where pastoral synthesis goes beyond the detail supplied by one canonical passage.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Eternal marriage is a distinctive Restoration teaching with an unmistakable canonical center. [[D&C 131]] associates the highest celestial degree with the new and everlasting covenant of marriage. [[D&C 132]] claims that a marriage can survive death when made according to divine law, administered through appointed sealing authority, ratified by the Holy Spirit of promise, and faithfully kept. Official teaching locates that ordinance in the temple and treats it as part of the covenantal path toward [[Eternal Life]].

The doctrine draws meaningful but incomplete connections to the wider canon. Genesis describes one-flesh union; [[Matthew 19]] calls marriage a divine joining; [[Matthew 16]] speaks of keys and heavenly binding; Malachi 4 and [[D&C 110]] connect Elijah with turning generations toward one another. None of these texts alone states the full modern doctrine. Its distinctiveness depends upon accepting Restoration revelation and its interpretation of earlier scripture.

The most important textual difficulty must remain visible. The Synoptic Gospels say that resurrected people neither marry nor are given in marriage. Distinguishing new post-resurrection marriage from continuation of an earlier sealing offers a coherent Latter-day Saint model, but the Gospels do not state that distinction. The result is a possible reconciliation, not a demonstrated one.

The historical difficulty is equally substantial. The principal revelation combines eternal marriage with plural marriage and gives Joseph Smith exceptional authority in matters directly affecting him. That structure creates a real conflict-of-interest problem and demands serious attention to consent, secrecy, gender, and the experiences of women—not dismissal through an appeal to sacredness. Historical racial exclusion and present questions involving singleness, divorce, abuse, and LGBTQ lives also reveal gaps between an exalted ideal and unequal mortal access.

What is established is that the Restoration canon teaches eternal marriage, that early Saints organized consequential practices around it, and that contemporary Latter-day Saint teaching makes covenant fidelity rather than ceremony alone central. Whether sealing authority is divine and whether such relationships actually persist after resurrection are theological claims not independently demonstrable by textual or historical study. A believing conclusion can reasonably rest on revelation, spiritual experience, and trust in Christ, but intellectual integrity requires naming where evidence ends and faith begins.
