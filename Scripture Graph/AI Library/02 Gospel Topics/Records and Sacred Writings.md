# Records and Sacred Writings

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, a **record** is a deliberately preserved account: covenant words, law, genealogy, prophecy, history, testimony, or administrative memory. A **sacred writing** is not simply any written object. Its sacredness arises from its claimed revelatory content, covenantal use, authorized stewardship, and purpose before God. Mosiah 24:4–7 is a useful control: people can be taught literacy and record keeping while religious knowledge is withheld.

The media vary. Moses writes a covenant book (Exodus 24:4–7); Israel deposits and publicly reads a written law (Deuteronomy 31:9–26); Restoration scripture describes Adam's book of remembrance (Moses 6:5–6); and the Book of Mormon describes brass, gold, and other metal plates. “Canon” is a related but narrower category: a community-recognized collection of authoritative sacred writings, discussed in [[Canon (Bible Dictionary)]]. Within Latter-day Saint tradition, [[Gold Plates (Gospel Topics)]] refers specifically to the plates said to contain Mormon's abridgment and translated by [[Joseph Smith Jr]] as the Book of Mormon.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon teaches several recurring principles.

1. **God's words are to be remembered and transmitted.** Moses writes covenant words, the king must possess and read a copy of the law, and later custodians preserve teaching for future generations (Exodus 24:4–7; Deuteronomy 17:18–20; Mosiah 1:2–7).
2. **Records enlarge communal memory.** Alma says preserved words helped bring thousands to repentance, while Omni contrasts literate Nephite preservation with Zarahemla's loss of language and genealogy (Omni 1:14–18; Alma 37:8–9). This connects [[Records and Sacred Writings]] with [[Scripture Study]], [[Education and Learning]], and [[Conversion]].
3. **Recordkeepers are stewards, editors, and fallible writers.** Nephi selects according to purpose; Jacob cites the labor of engraving; Mormon abridges sources; Moroni acknowledges weaknesses in writing (1 Nephi 6:1–6; Jacob 4:1–3; Mormon 9:32–34; Ether 12:23–25).
4. **Preservation does not require unrestricted disclosure.** Alma withholds destructive secret oaths, and Jesus permits some things to remain unwritten (Alma 37:27–32; 3 Nephi 27:23).
5. **Records remain subject to correction.** Jesus' audit of an omitted prophecy in [[3 Nephi 23]] depicts written memory as accountable rather than automatically complete.
6. **Written testimony has eschatological weight.** Words of Mormon 1:11 relates the records to [[Final Judgment]], though it does not make documents the sole ground of judgment.

Official Latter-day Saint teaching, represented by [[Canon (Bible Dictionary)]], [[Gold Plates (Gospel Topics)]], and [[The Life and Ministry of Joseph Smith]], treats ancient prophetic record keeping and Joseph Smith's receipt and translation of plates as real sacred history. The further conclusion that every editorial event described by the Book of Mormon is historically recoverable is an inference of faith and historical argument, not something established merely by the text's internal claims.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
Restoration scripture places record keeping at humanity's beginnings: Adam's descendants maintain a book of remembrance and learn to read and write (Moses 6:5–6). In the Pentateuch, writing becomes part of covenant administration—Moses records divine words, gives the law to priestly custodians, and provides for recurring public reading (Exodus 24:4–7; Deuteronomy 31:9–26). Joshua records covenant renewal (Joshua 24:25–27). In Josiah's reign, discovery and public reading of a law book catalyze reform, illustrating both preservation and the possibility that authoritative texts can be neglected or lost from ordinary awareness (2 Kings 22:8–13; 23:1–3).

The Book of Mormon presents a second long recordkeeping history. [[Nephi (son of Lehi)]] obtains the brass plates, creates large and small plates for different emphases, and transfers the small plates to [[Jacob (son of Lehi)]] (1 Nephi 3–5; 9; Jacob 1:1–4). Successive custodians sometimes write extensively and sometimes scarcely at all; moral worth and custodianship are not equated, as Omni's self-assessment shows (Omni 1:1–3). Amaleki gives the plates to [[King Benjamin]], after which they join a royal archive (Omni 1:25; Words of Mormon 1:10–11). [[Mosiah II]] receives records and interpreters, translates the twenty-four plates, and transfers the collection to [[Alma the Younger]] (Mosiah 28:11–20). Alma then charges [[Helaman (son of Alma)]] to preserve and transmit them (Alma 37:1–32).

[[Mormon (prophet-historian)]] describes himself as abridger and curator rather than original author of the whole collection. At [[Cumorah]] he hides the larger archive and entrusts a limited record to [[Moroni (son of Mormon)]] (Mormon 6:6). Moroni completes and conceals it for a future audience (Mormon 8:1–5).

The Restoration adds a new historical claim: Joseph Smith said [[Moroni (angel)]] directed him to buried plates and commissioned their translation (Joseph Smith—History 1:34–35, 51–54, 59–60). That confessional history is summarized in [[The Life and Ministry of Joseph Smith]], [[Chapter 4 The Book of Mormon Keystone of Our Religion]], and [[07 Fellow Servants]]. Later Church practice extended the theological value of memory into institutional record keeping; [[Conference Report, October 1901 (General Conference (full report), October 1901)]] illustrates concern for accurate local Church records. Administrative records, however, should not automatically be treated as canon or revelation.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest finding is literary and internal: the Book of Mormon maintains a sustained recordkeeping architecture. The large/small plates distinction in [[1 Nephi 9]], Jacob's receipt of the small plates, the chain of custodians through [[Omni 1]], and Mormon's discovery and incorporation of that record in [[Words of Mormon 1]] fit together closely. Likewise, the unidentified twenty-four plates in Mosiah 8 and 21 are translated in Mosiah 28 and become the source named in Ether 1. These are strong examples of narrative integration and editorial self-consciousness.

The text also preserves unusually candid notices of limitation: omissions, source selection, physical difficulty, linguistic weakness, uncertainty about divine purpose, and belated correction (1 Nephi 9:1–6; Jacob 4:1–3; Words of Mormon 1:5–7; 3 Nephi 23:6–14; Mormon 9:32–34). This strengthens the conclusion that the implied narrators understand records as mediated artifacts rather than exhaustive transcripts.

The evidentiary limit is crucial. Internal consistency strongly supports the proposition that the received text has deliberate architecture. It only weakly bears on whether the named plates and recordkeepers existed historically, because ancient compilation and sophisticated modern composition can both produce integration. Similarly, the relics in Mosiah 8 function as evidence inside the narrative but are not independently recovered artifacts.

The Restoration's historical evidence is different in kind: Joseph Smith and other participants reported plates, and formal witnesses affirmed encounters with them. Those reports deserve direct study through [[Witnesses of the Book of Mormon]], [[Witnesses shown the plates]], and [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]]. They establish that a concrete plate claim was central to the movement and that multiple people gave supporting testimony. They do not by themselves date the object, establish its contents, or demonstrate supernatural translation. [[Were metal plates used for records in antiquity]] addresses general material plausibility; [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]] addresses the substantially larger historical conclusion.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What turns a record into scripture: divine origin, prophetic authorship, community recognition, liturgical use, or some combination? Compare [[Canon (Bible Dictionary)]] and [[Who decided which books are in the Bible]].
- How should readers distinguish revelation from the recordkeeper's selection, language, memory, and interpretation? Jacob 4:1–3, Mormon 9:32–34, and Ether 12:23–25 make mediation explicit.
- Why did God command the small plates? Nephi and Mormon both invoke a “wise purpose,” but their chapters do not identify it. The traditional connection should be evaluated through [[What happened to the lost 116 pages]].
- What exactly were the languages and scripts of the Nephite records? Begin with 1 Nephi 1:2, Mosiah 1:4, and Mormon 9:32–34, then examine [[What is reformed Egyptian]].
- How much of the Book of Mormon's continuity reflects source history, and how much could arise from a single compositional intelligence? [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]] frames the larger dispute.
- What were the physical character and provenance of the plates reported by Joseph and the witnesses? Study [[Gold Plates (Gospel Topics)]], [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]], and [[Were metal plates used for records in antiquity]] without treating general metal-writing parallels as proof of this specific artifact.
- How should textual correction affect concepts of scriptural authority? Compare Jesus' correction in [[3 Nephi 23]] with [[How were the Book of Mormon text changes after 1830 made]] and [[How reliable is the biblical text]].
- When is restricted access justified? Alma 37:27–32 and 3 Nephi 27:23 endorse some restraint, but secrecy can also obstruct accountability.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest critical objection is that a text's claims about its own sources cannot authenticate those sources. A modern author can invent archives, editorial transitions, physical relics, and chains of custody. The integration between [[1 Nephi 9]], [[Words of Mormon 1]], Mosiah 8–28, and Ether therefore demonstrates literary design, not antiquity.

A second objection concerns absent provenance. The gold plates are no longer available for ordinary examination, so their material, inscription, and date cannot be independently tested. Witness testimony is important historical evidence, but critics dispute whether all encounters were ordinary visual and tactile examinations, and sincere religious testimony can be mistaken. [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]] must therefore be considered alongside, not replaced by, devotional retellings.

A third objection is ethical. Calling a record sacred can authorize violence or protect questionable claims from scrutiny. The killing of [[Laban (keeper of the plates)]] in 1 Nephi 4 is justified within the narrative by the need to preserve law and identity, but readers may reject the claimed revelation, question proportionality, or view the rationale as literary legitimation. The record's value does not make those objections disappear.

A fourth objection concerns textual mediation. If authors admit omissions, linguistic weakness, abridgment, and correction, then appeals to scriptural inerrancy become difficult. A believing response may distinguish divine purpose from human verbal perfection, but that is a theological model, not a conclusion forced on every reader.

Finally, parallels with ancient metal writing establish only possibility. They cannot show that Joseph Smith possessed an ancient American record. Comparative evidence must avoid the fallacy “this kind of object existed, therefore this particular object was authentic.” Cautionary cases such as [[What were the Kinderhook plates]] and [[What were the Mark Hofmann forgeries]] show why provenance and independent testing matter.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing scholarship commonly reads the Book of Mormon's editorial notices as traces of genuine source history: multiple plates, inherited archives, abridgment, and author-specific purposes. Terryl L. Givens's *By the Hand of Mormon* (2002) situates the material record and translation claim within the book's religious and cultural significance. Grant Hardy's *Understanding the Book of Mormon* (2010) closely analyzes Nephi, Mormon, and Moroni as distinct narrators; its literary observations can be assessed even by readers who suspend judgment about historicity. Royal Skousen's *Book of Mormon Critical Text Project* studies the English textual transmission from surviving manuscripts and editions, a question distinct from the unavailable source plates.

Historical scholarship on Joseph Smith divides over explanation. Richard Lyman Bushman's *Joseph Smith: Rough Stone Rolling* (2005) presents the plates and translation within a sympathetic but historically contextualized biography. Dan Vogel's *Joseph Smith: The Making of a Prophet* (2004) advances a naturalistic, critical reconstruction. Both take early testimony and setting seriously; they differ sharply on Joseph's credibility, explanatory assumptions, and the adequacy of supernatural claims.

Biblical textual and canon scholarship supplies an important comparison. Scholars broadly agree that biblical books underwent composition, collection, copying, variation, and communal canonization over long periods. That does not by itself settle Latter-day Saint claims, but it discourages treating “scripture” as a single untouched manuscript. [[How reliable is the biblical text]], [[Was the Bible changed by councils and copyists]], and [[Who decided which books are in the Bible]] separate textual transmission from later myths about arbitrary council decisions.

The principal divide is not whether the Book of Mormon displays editorial complexity—it plainly does—but what explains it. Believing models attribute the architecture principally to ancient writers and divine preservation; critical models attribute it to Joseph Smith and his nineteenth-century environment. Literary analysis can clarify the phenomenon, but historical adjudication requires evidence outside the text as well.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with biblical foundations: Exodus 24:4–7; Deuteronomy 17:18–20; 31:9–26; Joshua 24:25–27; 2 Kings 22–23. Ask how writing serves covenant, custody, public reading, and reform.
2. Read Moses 6:5–6 with [[Canon (Bible Dictionary)]] to understand the Restoration's expanded history of literacy and sacred records.
3. Follow the brass plates through [[1 Nephi 3]], [[1 Nephi 4]], [[1 Nephi 5]], and Mosiah 1:2–7. Keep the historical, theological, and ethical questions separate; use [[Obtaining the brass plates]] and [[Laban (keeper of the plates)]].
4. Study Nephi's editorial program in [[1 Nephi 6]], [[1 Nephi 9]], and [[1 Nephi 19]]. List what he includes, omits, and assigns to each record.
5. Trace custody through [[Jacob 1]], [[Jarom 1]], [[Omni 1]], and [[Words of Mormon 1]]. Notice both faithful stewardship and sparse or self-critical entries.
6. Trace the twenty-four plates through [[Mosiah 8]], Mosiah 21:25–28, [[Mosiah 28]], and Ether 1:1–5. Distinguish what each stage actually knows from later identification.
7. Read Alma 37:1–32 for preservation, conversion, interpreters, and responsible withholding; pair it with [[Becoming More Powerful Priesthood Holders (Elder Walter F. González, October 2009)]].
8. Examine correction and authorized limits in [[3 Nephi 23]] and [[3 Nephi 27]].
9. Finish the internal history with Mormon 6:6; Mormon 8:1–5; Mormon 9:30–34; and Ether 12:23–25.
10. Study the Restoration claim through Joseph Smith—History 1:27–65, [[The Life and Ministry of Joseph Smith]], [[Gold Plates (Gospel Topics)]], [[07 Fellow Servants]], and [[Chapter 4 The Book of Mormon Keystone of Our Religion]]. Then test competing assessments through [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]], [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]], [[Were metal plates used for records in antiquity]], and [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]].

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scripture portrays records as instruments of covenant memory. They preserve law, language, genealogy, prophecy, testimony, and history beyond the lives of their authors. They can support conversion and communal identity, but writing alone is not sacred: Mosiah 24 shows literacy separated from knowledge of God, and Alma 37 shows that some recorded knowledge can be destructive when transmitted irresponsibly.

The Book of Mormon makes record production part of its story. [[Nephi (son of Lehi)]] distinguishes two plate sets and discloses selection; Jacob describes material constraints; generations transmit the small plates; [[Mormon (prophet-historian)]] abridges sources and incorporates a discovered record; [[Moroni (son of Mormon)]] writes under linguistic and historical extremity. Jesus' correction in [[3 Nephi 23]] is especially important: sacred records matter enough to inspect and amend. Authority is therefore compatible with mediation, incompleteness, and correction.

This architecture is strong evidence of deliberate literary organization. It is not, standing alone, proof of ancient authorship. The Restoration adds participant testimony about physical plates and translation, giving the claim historical specificity but also exposing it to historical testing. The unavailable artifact, disagreements about witness experience, and alternative naturalistic explanations keep the plates' antiquity disputed. General evidence that ancient peoples wrote on metal establishes feasibility, not identification.

A defensible believing conclusion is that God works through remembered, selected, translated, and sometimes corrected human words, and that faithful custody can serve readers centuries later. A defensible critical conclusion is that the same features can be explained as sophisticated modern composition and religious memory. What the canon unambiguously asks of its readers is neither credulity nor contempt: give heed, preserve truth, test testimony, remember human limitation, and accept responsibility for what is written, withheld, transmitted, and lived.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
