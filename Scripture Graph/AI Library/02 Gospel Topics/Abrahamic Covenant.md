# Abrahamic Covenant

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
The **Abrahamic covenant** is the collective name for God's promises and covenant requirements given to [[Abraham (patriarch)]] and transmitted through Isaac and [[Jacob (Israel)]]. Its foundational texts are [[Genesis 12]], [[Genesis 15]], [[Genesis 17]], [[Genesis 22]], and [[Abraham 2]]. Together they concern relationship with God, land or eternal inheritance, numerous posterity, a great name or nation, and blessing reaching the families or nations of the earth. Circumcision is the covenant sign in Genesis 17:9-14.

The expression is a theological summary: Genesis narrates several covenant encounters rather than presenting one compact list entitled “the Abrahamic covenant.” Different passages emphasize different elements. [[Abraham 2]] 2:9-11 makes gospel ministry and priesthood service to the nations more explicit than Genesis does.

In official Latter-day Saint usage, the covenant is not merely an ancestral entitlement. [[Abrahamic Covenant (True to the Faith)]] and [[Abrahamic Covenant (Gospel Topics)]] teach that people may be counted as Abraham's seed through obedience to the gospel even without literal descent, drawing especially on Galatians 3:26-29 and Abraham 2:10. [[Patriarchal Blessings (True to the Faith)]] applies this bloodline-or-adoption framework to Church members. That is an official Restoration interpretation of the scriptural complex, not simply a lexical definition supplied by Genesis.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon begins with divine initiative. God calls Abraham, promises land and posterity, and declares that blessing will extend through him to all families of the earth ([[Genesis 12]] 12:1-7). [[Genesis 15]] addresses the apparent impossibility of an heir, associates Abraham's belief with righteousness, and ratifies the land promise in a covenant ceremony (15:1-18). [[Genesis 17]] calls the covenant everlasting, promises a God-people relationship, and establishes circumcision as its sign (17:4-14). The post-Akedah oath restates posterity and worldwide blessing while explicitly recognizing Abraham's obedience ([[Genesis 22]] 22:15-18).

The promises pass to [[Isaac]] and [[Jacob (Israel)]] in Genesis 26:3-5 and 28:3-15. They then become a framework for Israel's national story: God remembers the patriarchal covenant in the exodus ([[Exodus 3]] 3:6-8; [[Exodus 6]] 6:2-8), and Joshua describes settlement in the land as fulfillment of the oath to the fathers ([[Joshua 21]] 21:43-45). Deuteronomy 30:1-5, 20 nevertheless anticipates renewed return after scattering. Canonically, an earlier declaration of fulfillment and later expectations of restoration coexist.

The New Testament places Jesus Christ at the center of the promised blessing. Zacharias relates salvation to the oath sworn to Abraham ([[Luke 1]] 1:67-79). Peter applies the blessing to repentance and turning from iniquity ([[Acts 3]] 3:25-26). Paul teaches that the blessing comes to the Gentiles through Christ and that those who belong to Christ are Abraham's seed and heirs according to promise ([[Galatians 3]] 3:7-9, 14-18, 26-29). These passages resist reducing covenant membership either to ancestry alone or to possession of land alone.

Restoration scripture expands this pattern. [[Abraham 2]] 2:9-11 connects Abraham's seed with priesthood, ministry, gospel reception, and blessing the nations. [[1 Nephi 15]], [[1 Nephi 22]], [[2 Nephi 29]], [[3 Nephi 20]], [[Mormon 5]], and [[Ether 13]] place scattering and [[Gathering of Israel]] within God's remembrance of Abraham's seed. Doctrine and Covenants 110:12 identifies Elias's Kirtland commission as the dispensation of the gospel of Abraham.

Official Latter-day Saint teaching further identifies covenant participation with gospel laws and [[Ordinances]], including baptism and temple sealing; see [[Covenant (True to the Faith)]], [[Abrahamic Covenant (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Everlasting Covenant (Gospel Topics)]]. In [[The Sealing Power (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2023)]], President Russell M. Nelson is quoted as calling the Abrahamic covenant and the new and everlasting covenant essentially the same covenant under different phrasing. That is an authoritative apostolic synthesis within the tradition; Genesis 17 and Doctrine and Covenants 132:6 do not themselves state the equivalence in those words.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The traditional timeline places Abraham and the Covenant of the Pieces around 2000–1900 BC, but that dating depends on traditional biblical chronology and is not independently secure. In the narrative sequence, the covenant begins with Abraham's call in [[Genesis 12]], is ceremonially ratified in [[Genesis 15]], receives an everlasting formulation and sign in [[Genesis 17]], and is reaffirmed after the [[Binding of Isaac]] in [[Genesis 22]]. It is transmitted to [[Isaac]] and [[Jacob (Israel)]] in [[Genesis 26]] and [[Genesis 28]].

Israel's later scriptures transform family promises into a national memory. [[Exodus 3]] and [[Exodus 6]] connect deliverance from [[Egypt]] with covenant remembrance. [[Joshua 21]] presents possession of [[Canaan]] as fulfillment, while Deuteronomy 30 and later restoration traditions keep the land oath active after displacement. Psalm 105:8-11, 42-45 retells exodus and settlement around God's remembered word to Abraham.

Early Christian witnesses reread the covenant around [[Jesus Christ]]. [[Luke 1]] joins Abraham's oath to salvation and remission of sins. [[Acts 3]] joins it to repentance. [[Galatians 3]] treats faith in Christ, rather than ethnic descent alone, as decisive for heirship. This Christian interpretation opened the Abrahamic family to Gentiles, although subsequent Christian claims that the Church simply displaced Israel have been deeply contested.

The Book of Mormon makes the covenant a governing explanation of scattering, Gentile responsibility, recovery of Israel, restoration of records, and recognition of the Redeemer. Trace that development through [[1 Nephi 15]], [[1 Nephi 22]], [[2 Nephi 29]], [[3 Nephi 20]], [[Mormon 5]], and [[Ether 13]].

In the latter-day Restoration, Doctrine and Covenants 110:12 reports restoration of the gospel of Abraham in the [[Kirtland Temple dedication]] setting; [[The Times of Restitution of All Things (Elder David A. Bednar, April 2025)]] interprets that event as restoration of the Abrahamic covenant. Modern Church teaching applies covenant heirship to members by lineage or adoption through Christ; see [[Patriarchal Blessings (Gospel Topics)]], [[Patriarchal Blessings (True to the Faith)]], and [[When to Receive Your Patriarchal Blessing (Elder Kazuhiko Yamashita, April 2023)]]. [[Walking in Covenant Relationship with Christ (President Emily Belle Freeman, October 2023)]] summarizes Genesis 28 in terms of inheritance, posterity, and responsibility to bless the nations.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong textual findings

The strongest evidence is intertextual, not archaeological. The land-seed-worldwide-blessing pattern recurs with explicit references to Abraham in Genesis 12:1-3; 26:3-5; Acts 3:25-26; 1 Nephi 15:18; Abraham 2:9-11; and Doctrine and Covenants 110:12. This firmly establishes that later canonical writers consciously interpret their own communities and missions through Abraham. It does **not** independently prove the historical date of the patriarchal narratives or every later theological application.

The exodus and gathering connections are likewise explicit. Exodus 2:24 and 6:2-8 make covenant remembrance a reason for deliverance. Second Nephi 29:1-2, 12-14 frames the recovery of people and records by covenant remembrance. Mormon 5:19-21 moves from Gentile scattering to remembrance of Abraham's covenant. Ether 13:11 directly describes gathering to the old Jerusalem as covenant fulfillment. These are stronger than merely thematic parallels.

### Strong canonical interpretation, broader conclusion open

Joshua 21:43-45 says that the land oath was fulfilled, yet Deuteronomy 30:1-5 and Restoration texts expect return after scattering. This establishes multiple canonical horizons of fulfillment. Whether these should be understood as recurring, typological, conditional, or still awaiting a final geopolitical completion is interpretive rather than textually settled.

Genesis 12, 15, and 17 differ in form and emphasis. Historical-critical scholars often attribute these differences to distinct literary traditions or compositional stages; final-form readers emphasize their integrated theological progression. Both approaches account for real textual features. Compositional diversity neither proves nor disproves revelation or an underlying historical Abraham.

### Limited or disputed indicators

First Nephi 15:18 says “kindreds of the earth,” closely resembling Acts 3:25 in the KJV rather than Genesis 22:18. The wording correspondence is objective; its explanation is not. It can be read as modern biblical influence, as familiar biblical diction used in translation, or as common Christian covenant vocabulary. It is weak evidence for any larger historical conclusion.

The traditional 2000–1900 BC date for the Covenant of the Pieces is useful for a devotional timeline but should not be presented as archaeologically demonstrated. The texts provide theological memory more securely than independently datable biographical evidence.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How do the land promise, the New Testament's christological reading, and later gathering prophecies relate? Does fulfillment recur at national, spiritual, and eschatological levels, or should one level control the others? Compare Joshua 21:43-45, Deuteronomy 30:1-5, [[Acts 3]], and [[Ether 13]].

- What is unconditional and what is conditional? Genesis 15 dramatizes divine commitment, while Genesis 17:1-2, Genesis 22:16-18, and Genesis 26:5 emphasize faithful walking and obedience. The canon preserves both poles.

- What does “seed” mean in a given passage: biological descendants, covenant Israel, a royal or messianic representative, people joined to Christ, or more than one of these? Study Genesis 17:7, Galatians 3:16, 26-29, and Abraham 2:10-11.

- What precisely is restored by the “gospel of Abraham” in Doctrine and Covenants 110:12? The verse names the commission and its multigenerational blessing but does not identify Elias or provide a complete administrative description. See [[Priesthood keys restored in the Kirtland Temple]] and [[The Times of Restitution of All Things (Elder David A. Bednar, April 2025)]].

- How should modern believers speak of lineage without turning covenant election into racial hierarchy? [[Patriarchal Blessings (True to the Faith)]] stresses bloodline or adoption, while Acts 3:25-26 and Abraham 2:9-11 define election through responsibility and blessing.

- To what extent do Genesis 12, 15, and 17 preserve separate literary traditions? Even if they do, how should their final canonical arrangement guide theology?

- Does apostolic identification of the Abrahamic and new and everlasting covenants mean exact identity in every historical detail, or unity within a larger divine covenant? [[The Sealing Power (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2023)]] offers the official synthesis, but further conceptual work is required.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
### “The covenant narratives project later Israelite concerns into the patriarchal past.”

Historical-critical readings explain the differing presentations in [[Genesis 12]], [[Genesis 15]], and [[Genesis 17]] as products of distinct literary traditions or compositional stages shaped by later questions about land, ancestry, exile, and identity. A particularly important version assigns much of Genesis 17 to Priestly composition, often dated to the exilic or post-exilic period, and reads its everlasting covenant and circumcision emphasis as reaffirming identity and permanence amid national loss. Source boundaries and dates remain debated. Final-form coherence shows that the received text integrates the passages; it does not independently prove that every narrated encounter occurred exactly as described.

### “The land promise sacralizes conquest and dispossession.”

The land language is real and should not be spiritualized away. [[Joshua 21]] describes Israel's settlement as fulfillment of the ancestral oath, raising the ethical problem treated under [[Why did God command the conquest of Canaan]]. The canon also gives election an outward vocation: all families are to be blessed, covenant heirs are called to repentance, and Abraham's seed bears ministry to nations. Those themes resist ethnic supremacy but do not automatically settle ancient conquest ethics or modern territorial claims.

### “Christian interpretation erases Jewish covenant identity.”

[[Galatians 3]] opens Abrahamic heirship to those who belong to Christ, and Christian supersessionism has sometimes turned Gentile inclusion into a claim that Jews were rejected or replaced. Romans 9–11 resists the conclusion that God simply cast away Israel. Jewish traditions also recognize conversion while retaining the covenantal significance of Abraham's physical descendants and Torah-shaped communal life. Paul's reading is authoritative for Christians, but it is not a neutral summary of Jewish interpretation.

### “Latter-day Saint doctrine reads a later system into Genesis.”

Genesis explicitly teaches land, posterity, divine relationship, worldwide blessing, obligation, and circumcision; it does not independently state the full Restoration system of priesthood ministry, temple sealing, or adoptive heirship. Those elements depend on [[Abraham 2]], Doctrine and Covenants 110, other Restoration texts, and apostolic synthesis such as [[The Sealing Power (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2023)]]. Latter-day Saints may accept that expanded-canon development as revelation, but it should not be represented as Genesis's lexical content.

### “Book of Mormon covenant language derives from the King James Bible.”

The correspondence between 1 Nephi 15:18 and Acts 3:25 is genuine and supports a plausible modern-dependence explanation. A believing model may propose that inspired translation used familiar biblical diction. The wording alone cannot distinguish direct dependence, KJV-mediated translation, or broader Christian scriptural vocabulary, and it is not independent proof of antiquity.

### “Covenant lineage encourages racial or spiritual elitism.”

Claims about chosen lineage can be misused as claims of superiority. The canon's strongest internal correctives are that ancestry without repentance is inadequate and that election entails service and blessing others. Official Latter-day Saint sources also allow covenant heirship through adoption. Patriarchal lineage declarations should not be treated as genetic tests or warrants for racial hierarchy.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Academic study broadly recognizes the ancestral promises—land, descendants, relationship with God, and blessing—as major organizing features of Genesis and the Pentateuch. David J. A. Clines, *The Theme of the Pentateuch* (1978), influentially treats the patriarchal promises and their partial fulfillment as a narrative engine. R. W. L. Moberly, *The Theology of the Book of Genesis* (2009), offers a theological reading attentive to historical criticism and to Abraham's significance for the nations.

Historical-critical scholarship commonly understands [[Genesis 12]], [[Genesis 15]], and [[Genesis 17]] as preserving distinct sources or compositional stages. Joel S. Baden, *The Promise to the Patriarchs* (2013), presents a sustained source-critical account. In a prominent reconstruction, Genesis 17 belongs substantially to Priestly composition, frequently dated to the exilic or post-exilic period; its everlasting covenant, divine-name usage, land assurance, and circumcision requirement are consequently read against the crisis of lost land and national identity. Particular source assignments and dates remain disputed, and compositional development does not prevent a coherent final-form reading.

Jon D. Levenson, *Inheriting Abraham* (2012), emphasizes that Judaism, Christianity, and Islam inherit Abraham differently. Paul's christological interpretation in [[Galatians 3]] is authoritative within Christianity but is not a neutral account of Jewish covenant theology. Jewish readings characteristically preserve the significance of Abraham's physical descendants and covenantal practice while also providing established means for converts to join the covenant community.

Latter-day Saint interpretation depends on an expanded canon. [[Abraham 2]] explicitly connects the covenant with priesthood ministry and adoptive seed; Book of Mormon passages connect it with scattering, records, repentance, and gathering. Official syntheses appear in [[Abrahamic Covenant (Gospel Topics)]], [[Abrahamic Covenant (True to the Faith)]], and [[Thoughts to Keep in Mind The Covenant]]. Apostolic teaching in [[Covenants]], [[The Sealing Power (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2023)]], and [[The Times of Restitution of All Things (Elder David A. Bednar, April 2025)]] connects the covenant with Restoration ordinances, sealing, and the Kirtland commission.

The principal divide is methodological rather than simply believing versus unbelieving. Historical-critical work asks how covenant traditions developed and what ancient social settings they addressed; canonical and confessional readings ask how the received texts jointly witness to God's purposes. Literary development is not itself disproof of revelation, while canonical harmony is not independent historical verification.
<!-- SG:END scholarship -->

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. **Begin with the promise cycle.** Read [[Genesis 12]] 12:1-7, [[Genesis 15]] 15:1-18, [[Genesis 17]] 17:1-14, and [[Genesis 22]] 22:15-18. Make four columns: divine relationship, land, seed, and blessing to others. Add covenant obligations and signs separately.

2. **Trace transmission through the patriarchs.** Read Genesis 26:3-5; [[Genesis 28]] 28:3-15; and [[Genesis 48]] 48:3-4. Ask what remains stable and what changes as the promise passes through [[Isaac]], [[Jacob (Israel)]], and [[Joseph (of Egypt)]].

3. **Follow covenant remembrance into national history.** Read Exodus 2:23-25, [[Exodus 3]] 3:6-8, [[Exodus 6]] 6:2-8, Deuteronomy 30:1-5, 20, and [[Joshua 21]] 21:43-45. Compare deliverance, land fulfillment, scattering, and return through [[Exodus and Deliverance]], [[Entry into the Promised Land]], and [[Gathering of Israel]].

4. **Study Israel's retrospective theology.** Read Psalm 105:8-11, 42-45. Observe how the psalm selects and orders history around a remembered covenant.

5. **Study the New Testament reinterpretation.** Read [[Luke 1]] 1:67-79, [[Acts 3]] 3:25-26, Romans 4, Romans 9–11, and [[Galatians 3]]. Distinguish what each author says about ancestry, faith, Christ, repentance, and Israel's continuing place.

6. **Read the Restoration expansion.** Start with [[Abraham 2]] 2:6-11, then follow [[1 Nephi 15]] 15:12-18; [[1 Nephi 22]] 22:3-12; [[2 Nephi 29]] 29:1-2, 12-14; [[3 Nephi 20]] 20:25-27; [[Mormon 5]] 5:19-21; and [[Ether 13]] 13:11. Track the linked themes of gospel, records, Gentiles, scattering, repentance, and gathering.

7. **Examine latter-day application.** Read Doctrine and Covenants 110:12 and 124:58, then compare [[Abrahamic Covenant (Gospel Topics)]], [[Patriarchal Blessings (True to the Faith)]], [[Walking in Covenant Relationship with Christ (President Emily Belle Freeman, October 2023)]], and [[The Sealing Power (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2023)]]. Mark where scripture ends and official interpretive synthesis begins.

8. **Test the difficult questions.** Compare Joshua's fulfilled-land language with later restoration expectations; compare literal descent with Galatians 3:26-29; and compare Genesis alone with the expanded claims of Abraham 2 and modern revelation. This prevents a later doctrinal system from being silently read back into every earlier verse.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The Abrahamic covenant is best understood as a scriptural architecture rather than a single proof text. [[Genesis 12]], [[Genesis 15]], [[Genesis 17]], and [[Genesis 22]] establish its recurring elements: God forms a relationship with Abraham, promises land and posterity, requires covenant fidelity, and assigns to Abraham's line a role in blessing the world. [[Genesis 26]] and [[Genesis 28]] transmit those promises to [[Isaac]] and [[Jacob (Israel)]]. [[Exodus 3]], [[Exodus 6]], and [[Joshua 21]] interpret Israel's deliverance and land through that patriarchal oath.

The covenant is both particular and universal. It elects a family, yet its stated horizon is all families or nations. Later texts increasingly explain how that outward blessing works. [[Acts 3]] identifies it with turning people from iniquity. [[Galatians 3]] centers it in [[Jesus Christ]] and opens heirship to those who belong to him. [[Abraham 2]] presents Abraham's seed as bearing gospel ministry and priesthood to the nations. Election is therefore gift, identity, obligation, and service—not a warrant for ethnic superiority.

The Book of Mormon makes covenant remembrance the logic of the [[Scattering of Israel]] and [[Gathering of Israel]]. [[1 Nephi 15]], [[1 Nephi 22]], [[2 Nephi 29]], [[3 Nephi 20]], [[Mormon 5]], and [[Ether 13]] connect recovered peoples, recovered records, repentance, land, and recognition of the Redeemer. Doctrine and Covenants 110:12 places the gospel of Abraham within the Kirtland restoration of priesthood commissions.

Official Latter-day Saint teaching applies these promises through Christ, gospel obedience, [[Ordinances]], and literal or adoptive membership in covenant Israel; see [[Abrahamic Covenant (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Abrahamic Covenant (True to the Faith)]]. Apostolic teaching further relates this covenant to the new and everlasting covenant and sealing, especially in [[The Sealing Power (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2023)]]. Those are legitimate statements of Restoration doctrine, while their full formulation depends on Restoration scripture and prophetic interpretation rather than Genesis alone.

What is firmly established is the covenant's pervasive canonical role and the repeated joining of Abraham, seed, land, divine relationship, worldwide blessing, repentance, and gathering. What remains open includes the historical formation and dating of the Genesis traditions, the precise relationship between past and future land fulfillment, the meaning of lineage in individual cases, and how best to relate Jewish, Christian, and Latter-day Saint interpretations. Faith may affirm divine continuity across the whole canon; historical and literary evidence can illuminate that affirmation but cannot compel every larger theological conclusion.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
