# Book of Mormon (Gospel Topic)

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
The term **Book of Mormon** has several related but nonidentical referents. Within the narrative, Mormon is a named prophet-historian who calls his own compiled record the Book of Mormon ([[Mormon 1]] 1:1); [[Words of Mormon 1]] describes him abridging earlier records and incorporating the small plates. In modern usage, the term ordinarily means the English volume first published in 1830, including its translated narrative, editorial apparatus, title page, and witness statements. These layers should not be conflated.

The record presents itself as an abridgment drawn largely from the plates of Nephi, supplemented by other records, completed by [[Mormon (prophet-historian)]] and [[Moroni (son of Mormon)]], deposited on plates, and later translated through [[Joseph Smith Jr]]. [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] 1:34-35 describes gold plates and two interpreter stones fastened to a breastplate, called the Urim and Thummim. This should be kept distinct from the later, eschatological use of the same term in [[D&C 130]] 8-10, where a sanctified earth and a personal white stone are called a Urim and Thummim — a different referent that should not be read back into the 1829 translation account. The modern subtitle, "Another Testament of Jesus Christ," summarizes the Church's understanding of the book's religious function; it is modern paratext, not a sentence from the ancient narrative.

In Latter-day Saint tradition, the Book of Mormon is scripture, a witness of [[Jesus Christ]], and foundational to the [[Restoration of the Gospel]]. "Translation" is used here for a revelatory process claimed to produce an English text from the plates; it should not be silently equated with ordinary learned-language translation, since Joseph Smith did not claim conventional competence in the source language and surviving historical accounts describe revelatory instruments rather than scholarly method. See [[Book of Mormon Translation (Gospel Topics)]], [[Book of Mormon Translation (Essay)]], and [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]].
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
Canonically, the book is presented as scripture with covenantal force, not merely a historical curiosity. [[D&C 20]] states Joseph Smith received "power from on high, by the means which were before prepared, to translate the Book of Mormon" (D&C 20:8) — asserting a divine instrument without describing its mechanics. [[D&C 84]] pronounces the whole Church under a "condemnation" until it repents and remembers "the new covenant, even the Book of Mormon" (D&C 84:57), treating right reception of the text as an ongoing covenant obligation. [[D&C 135]], written to seal Joseph and Hyrum Smith's testimony after their martyrdom, states Joseph "translated by the gift and power of God" and that the book "cost the best blood of the nineteenth century" to bring forth (D&C 135:3, 6). [[Moroni 10]] supplies the book's own proposed epistemology: a promise that sincere prayer with real intent and faith in Christ, after reading and remembering God's mercy, will bring personal confirmation by the Holy Ghost (Moroni 10:3-5) — a claim of spiritual, not documentary, verification, and one that should be held distinct from historical-evidentiary claims about the plates. Official statements (D&C 20, D&C 84, D&C 135) are declarations of covenantal status and instrumental means; they do not specify translation method, and later doctrine (e.g., [[D&C 130]] 8-10) reuses "Urim and Thummim" in an unrelated eschatological sense that should not be read back into the 1829 account.

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The book's own history begins with recordkeeping. Nephi distinguishes between sets of plates in [[1 Nephi 9]]. [[Words of Mormon 1]] says Mormon found the small plates after abridging other Nephite records and included them for a purpose he did not fully know. [[Mormon 1]] depicts him receiving responsibility for the records; [[Mormon 7]] addresses a future remnant; Moroni completes and seals the record in [[Mormon 8]], [[Mormon 9]], and [[Moroni 10]]. These are internal narrative claims and are not independent archaeological verification.

In the Restoration account, Moroni announced the deposited record in 1823 (Joseph Smith—History 1:27-54), and Joseph obtained the plates in 1827 (Joseph Smith—History 1:59). The first major dictation phase with Martin Harris ended in the loss of the manuscript known as the 116 pages; [[05 All Is Lost]], [[D&C 3]], [[D&C 5]], and [[D&C 10]] preserve different dimensions of that crisis. Oliver Cowdery arrived in Harmony in April 1829 and became the principal scribe as translation resumed; see Joseph Smith—History 1:66-67 and [[Historical Summary]]. [[06 The Gift and Power of God]] and [[Book of Mormon Translation (Gospel Topics)]] assemble accounts of the translation instruments and working procedure.

Translation was substantially completed in 1829, after which witnesses supplied published attestations; [[07 Fellow Servants]], [[Witnesses shown the plates]], and [[Witnesses of the Book of Mormon]] are essential here. Printing and publication followed, as documented in [[08 The Rise of the Church of Christ]]; D&C 19:26 connects Martin Harris's property directly to financing the printing. The book then became foundational to the organization and missionary activity of the Church; see [[Restoration of the Church (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Organization of the Church]]. D&C 135:1-6 later placed the Book of Mormon beside Joseph and Hyrum Smith's martyrdom testimony.

Later understanding has become more historically specific. Popular portrayals often emphasized Joseph looking at the plates through the interpreters, whereas published historical work now openly discusses a seer stone used in a hat as well as the Nephite interpreters. This development changes the level of public description, not the canonical claim of divine power. Textual study has likewise shifted from treating the current edition as untouched dictation to comparing manuscripts and editions; [[How were the Book of Mormon text changes after 1830 made]] should therefore accompany any claim about exact wording.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
Evidence should be graded by the proposition it can actually support.

**Strong observations:** A substantial English text was dictated in a compressed 1829 production period with scribal assistance; a book was printed in 1830; named witnesses publicly affirmed experiences involving plates; and the English text displays extensive, sometimes near-verbatim King James Bible language. These points are historically or textually accessible even when their explanation is disputed. [[Book of Mormon Translation (Essay)]], [[07 Fellow Servants]], and [[08 The Rise of the Church of Christ]] provide entry points.

**Significant but interpretation-dependent evidence:** The book is long, narratively layered, doctrinally integrated, and capable of sustaining serious literary reading. Believing scholars point to features such as chiasmus, names, covenant patterns, ancient recordkeeping analogues, and proposed Old World correspondences. Each proposal must be tested separately for specificity, dating, independence, and selection effects. [[Do chiasmus and Hebraisms show the Book of Mormon is ancient]], [[Is Nahom evidence for Lehi's journey]], and [[Were metal plates used for records in antiquity]] prevent a bundle of suggestive observations from being mistaken for one conclusive proof.

**Strong English-text evidence with disputed historical significance:** Moroni 7:45 closely follows 1 Corinthians 13:4-7; 3 Nephi 28:8 uses the KJV expression from 1 Corinthians 15:52; Enos 1:8 uses a Gospel healing formula; Mosiah 13 follows the biblical Decalogue; and [[2 Nephi 27]] expands [[Isaiah 29]]. These relationships strongly support biblical mediation of the English text. They do not alone distinguish a divinely guided translation using familiar biblical language from modern composition dependent on the KJV. The more discriminating questions are addressed in [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Isaiah and the King James Bible]], [[Why does the Book of Mormon reproduce King James italics]], and [[Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew]].

**Witness evidence:** The published testimonies are evidence that identifiable people committed themselves publicly to claims about the plates. The Three and Eight Witnesses did not describe precisely the same kind of encounter. Their persistence is relevant, but sincerity is not equivalent to correct interpretation, and testimony to plates does not by itself establish their age or contents. See [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]].

**Spiritual evidence:** Moroni 10:3-5 proposes a prayerful, morally engaged mode of knowing. Such experience can be powerful evidence for the person who has it. Because comparable religious experiences occur across conflicting traditions and are not publicly repeatable on demand, their evidentiary reach is principally personal and theological; see [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]].
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
A careful student should keep several questions distinct:

- What exactly did Joseph Smith and his associates mean by “translation,” and how do the interpreters, seer stone, plates, and reported dictation procedure relate? See [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]], [[Why did Joseph Smith use a seer stone]], and [[Book of Mormon Translation (Gospel Topics)]].
- Which words are claimed to derive closely from a source text, and which may reflect Joseph's language, biblical memory, scribes, or later editors? [[How were the Book of Mormon text changes after 1830 made]] is necessary before reasoning from one edition's exact wording.
- How should extensive KJV dependence be explained, especially passages from New Testament writings or later biblical forms? See [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Isaiah and the King James Bible]], [[Why does the Book of Mormon reproduce King James italics]], and [[Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew]].
- What historical proposition did each witness claim, and how much does that claim establish? See [[Witnesses of the Book of Mormon]] and [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]].
- Does the evidence favor an ancient record, a modern composition, or a revelatory model not reducible to either ordinary translation or ordinary authorship? See [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]] and [[Did Joseph Smith copy the Book of Mormon from other books]].
- Which geographic, demographic, and ancestry claims are required by the text, and which entered through later assumptions? See [[Where did the Book of Mormon take place]], [[How large were Book of Mormon populations]], [[Does DNA evidence disprove the Book of Mormon]], and [[What is the Book of Mormon's view of Native American origins]].
- How should anachronism claims be graded individually rather than defended or rejected as a single package? See [[What about horses, steel and other anachronisms in the Book of Mormon]].
- What can a spiritual witness establish for an individual, and what historical claims remain open after that experience? Moroni 10:3-5 and [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]] frame that epistemic question.

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest critical case is cumulative. The English text repeatedly tracks the King James Bible, including distinctive translation language, New Testament expressions placed in pre-Christian settings, and passages whose form appears related to the Bible available to Joseph Smith. Proposed anachronisms, uncertain geography, population questions, and lack of generally accepted archaeological or linguistic confirmation add pressure. Joseph's prior use of seer stones and the limited direct accessibility of the plates make a naturalistic model possible: a gifted nineteenth-century religious author dictated a text shaped by the Bible, contemporary debates, oral composition, and collaborators or sources. [[Did Joseph Smith copy the Book of Mormon from other books]], [[Was Joseph Smith a treasure digger]], [[Was Joseph Smith tried in 1826 for glass looking]], and [[What about horses, steel and other anachronisms in the Book of Mormon]] present components of that case.

A serious believing response cannot rest on denying well-attested translation accounts or treating every parallel as proof of antiquity. It argues instead that revelatory translation could legitimately use Joseph's biblical register; that rapid, extended dictation and complex internal structure call for a fuller explanation than casual copying; that the witness record resists simple dismissal; and that some proposed ancient correspondences deserve consideration. It may also adopt limited-geography and limited-ancestry models where the text permits them. Those models are only persuasive when derived from textual constraints and external evidence, not introduced solely to escape a difficulty. See [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]], [[Do chiasmus and Hebraisms show the Book of Mormon is ancient]], [[Is Nahom evidence for Lehi's journey]], and [[Does DNA evidence disprove the Book of Mormon]].

The best counter to the witness argument is not that every witness recanted; that is inaccurate. It is that sincere testimony can be conditioned by visionary expectations, close relationships, ambiguous experience, or a real but misidentified object. Conversely, continued witness affirmation does not prove every associated claim, but it remains evidence requiring explanation. [[How did the Three Witnesses live out their lives]] and [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]] should be read together.

Ezekiel 37:16-22 is also frequently overread. Its explicit interpretation concerns reunited Israel, not two future books. The Bible-and-Book-of-Mormon application is a Latter-day Saint typology and should be defended as such, not presented as the uncontested grammatical meaning of Ezekiel.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing scholarship generally begins with the book's antiquity or revelatory origin and examines how textual structure, covenant patterns, geography, names, witnesses, and ancient analogues might fit that premise. John W. Welch's work on chiasmus is influential, but the observation that a passage can be arranged chiastically must be distinguished from the stronger claim of intentional ancient Hebrew composition. Royal Skousen's Critical Text Project uses manuscripts and editions to reconstruct the earliest recoverable English text; that work is valuable regardless of one's conclusion about origins. Michael Hubbard MacKay and Gerrit J. Dirkmaat's *From Darkness unto Light* places the translation accounts, interpreters, seer stones, and scribes in documentary context. [[Faith Is Not by Chance, but by Choice (Elder Neil L. Andersen, October 2015)]] points readers to John W. Welch and Tim Rathbone's compilation of basic translation history.

Critical scholarship emphasizes the text's nineteenth-century production environment, KJV dependence, revival language, contemporary theological controversies, and proposed anachronisms. B. H. Roberts, a believing Church leader writing candidly about difficulties, remains important because his *Studies of the Book of Mormon* explored possible environmental explanations without reducing the problem to caricature. Collections such as Brent Lee Metcalfe's *New Approaches to the Book of Mormon* argue that close biblical and historical analysis weighs against a straightforward ancient-record model. Naturalistic scholars disagree among themselves about specific sources and mechanisms; no proposed source should be accepted merely because it resembles part of the Book of Mormon.

Literary scholars can bracket historicity and still find sophisticated narration, embedded documents, editorial voices, recurring covenant language, and theological argument. Grant Hardy's *Understanding the Book of Mormon* is a major example of reading Nephi, Mormon, and Moroni as distinct narratorial personalities without claiming that literary sophistication proves ancient authorship.

The central division is explanatory rather than observational. Most researchers can recognize the manuscripts, rapid dictation, multiple scribes, biblical language, published witness statements, and later editing. They part over whether those data are best explained by ancient records mediated through revelation, a modern revelatory production not corresponding closely to plate language, or naturalistic nineteenth-century composition. [[Book of Mormon Translation (Essay)]] states the Church's historical and confessional synthesis; it should not be mistaken for a neutral scholarly consensus.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with the book's own claims about records and authorship: [[1 Nephi 9]], [[Words of Mormon 1]], [[Mormon 1]], [[Mormon 7]], [[Mormon 8]], [[Mormon 9]], and [[Moroni 10]]. Mark who claims to write, abridge, translate, seal, or address future readers.

2. Read the Restoration narrative and revelations chronologically: [[Joseph Smith—History 1]], [[D&C 3]], [[D&C 5]], [[D&C 10]], [[D&C 11]], [[D&C 19]], and [[D&C 20]]. Then use [[05 All Is Lost]], [[06 The Gift and Power of God]], and [[Historical Summary]] to distinguish canonical claims from later historical reconstruction.

3. Study publication and witnesses separately from translation: [[07 Fellow Servants]], [[08 The Rise of the Church of Christ]], [[Witnesses shown the plates]], [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]], and [[How did the Three Witnesses live out their lives]]. Ask exactly what each source is early enough and competent enough to establish.

4. Trace the book's scriptural self-understanding through [[2 Nephi 29]], [[Moroni 10]], [[D&C 20]], and [[D&C 84]]. Compare [[Ezekiel 37]] only after reading through verse 22, so its Israel-reunification meaning is not displaced by the later two-books typology.

5. Examine biblical dependence directly: compare [[Isaiah 29]] with [[2 Nephi 27]], 1 Corinthians 13:4-7 with Moroni 7:45, 1 Corinthians 15:52 with 3 Nephi 28:8, and Exodus 20 and Deuteronomy 5 with Mosiah 13. Continue through [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Isaiah and the King James Bible]], [[Why does the Book of Mormon reproduce King James italics]], and [[Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew]].

6. Test external claims one category at a time: [[Were metal plates used for records in antiquity]], [[What is reformed Egyptian]], [[Is Nahom evidence for Lehi's journey]], [[Book of Mormon Geography]], [[Does DNA evidence disprove the Book of Mormon]], and [[What about horses, steel and other anachronisms in the Book of Mormon]]. Record whether each item is established, plausible, disputed, or speculative.

7. Only then compare integrated models in [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]], [[Did Joseph Smith copy the Book of Mormon from other books]], and [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]]. End by returning to Moroni 10:3-5 while keeping spiritual, historical, and textual judgments explicit rather than forcing one method to answer every question.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The Book of Mormon is simultaneously a text, a claimed ancient record, an 1829 dictated translation, an 1830 publication, and Latter-day Saint scripture. Sound study keeps those categories connected without treating them as interchangeable. Internally, Nephi, Mormon, and Moroni describe a layered record preserved for future readers. Restoration scripture says Joseph Smith translated it through divinely prepared means and calls readers to live its teachings, not merely endorse the book verbally. Official Church teaching therefore receives it as an ancient witness of [[Jesus Christ]] and a foundation of the [[Restoration of the Gospel]].

Several historical facts are comparatively firm: the English text emerged through dictation to scribes; the lost opening manuscript affected the production sequence; Oliver Cowdery became the principal scribe in April 1829; witnesses published claims about plates; and the book was printed in 1830. Historical sources also describe both interpreters and a seer stone. [[Book of Mormon Translation (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Book of Mormon Translation (Essay)]] provide a more accurate account than simplified artwork or inherited assumptions.

The evidence does not point in only one effortless direction. Rapid production, sustained narrative voices, witness persistence, and some proposed ancient correspondences give believing readers material for a reasoned case. Extensive King James dependence, possible anachronisms, uncertain external geography, and the availability of nineteenth-century religious language give critics a substantial cumulative case. Neither a single chiasm nor a single borrowed phrase responsibly settles the whole question. [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]] must weigh the line of evidence rather than award victory point by point.

The book itself finally asks for covenantal and spiritual engagement in Moroni 10:3-5 and D&C 84:57. That invitation can be honored without pretending that prayer functions as archaeological measurement or that historical uncertainty nullifies religious experience. The most defensible conclusion is layered: the existence, production, publication, biblical texture, and religious influence of the English book are historically observable; its divine translation and ancient historical identity remain confessional claims supported by arguments and testimony but not established to universal scholarly standards. A believer can affirm those claims faithfully while remaining exact about what the available evidence does and does not prove.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
