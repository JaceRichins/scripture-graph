# Truth

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
Scripture uses **truth** in several related senses rather than as one flat technical term. D&C 93:24 gives the canon's clearest explicit definition: knowledge of things as they are, were, and will be. [[Jacob 4]] describes the Spirit as disclosing things as they really are and will be (Jacob 4:13). These passages emphasize correspondence with reality.

Other texts emphasize reliability and fidelity. In [[Zechariah 8]], truth belongs with righteous judgment and peace; in [[Isaiah 59]], its collapse is social and judicial. The Johannine writings add a strongly personal and revelatory dimension: truth is in Jesus, Jesus bears witness to it, and Jesus identifies himself as the truth (John 14:6; 18:37). Truth is also something disciples know, obey, love, speak, and "walk in" (John 8:32; Ephesians 4:15, 25; 2 John 1:4; 3 John 1:4).

The tradition represented by [[Gospel Doctrine]] and several early [[Conference Report, April 1920 (General Conference (full report), April 1920)]] discussions frequently joins truth to light, intelligence, revelation, and the gospel. That reception history is important, but later sermons and conference discourse should not be silently treated as additional definitions carrying the same authority as canonized text.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon supports five central propositions.

1. **Truth corresponds to reality.** D&C 93:24 and Jacob 4:13 reject the idea that revelation properly understood is divine falsehood or useful fiction.
2. **Truth is disclosed supremely in Christ.** In John, Jesus does not merely teach true propositions; he calls himself "the truth" and makes access to the Father dependent on him (John 14:6). D&C 93:9-11 likewise applies "Spirit of truth" language to the incarnate Only Begotten.
3. **The Spirit mediates truth.** John 16:13, [[D&C 50]], Jacob 4:13, and Moroni 10:4-5 connect divine knowledge with the Spirit. D&C 50 requires both preacher and hearer to participate by that Spirit.
4. **Truth demands a truthful life.** The canon connects it with obedience, love, justice, peace, repentance, and freedom from sin (John 8:31-36; Romans 2:8; Ephesians 4:15, 25; Zechariah 8:16-19; 1 John 2:4).
5. **Human handling of truth remains accountable and corrigible.** Deuteronomy 19:15-18 requires corroboration and investigation; 2 Timothy 2:15-25 warns that religious teachers can mishandle the word and spread error.

For Latter-day Saints, D&C 93 is canonized scripture and therefore doctrinally authoritative. The further claim that a particular philosophical interpretation of "intelligence" is official doctrine does not follow automatically. Conference addresses and works such as [[Gospel Doctrine]] document influential teaching, but publication or repetition alone does not canonize every exposition.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
This is best read as a history of canonical presentation, not a claim that every passage was composed in the narrative order in which it appears.

In the Old Testament, truth commonly appears as reliability, covenant fidelity, honest judgment, and speech worthy of trust. Exodus 34:6 describes God as 'merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth'; Psalm 86:15 and other later texts echo this description with the variant phrase 'plenteous in mercy and truth.' [[Zechariah 8]] imagines restored Jerusalem as a city of truth, while [[Isaiah 59]] depicts truth fallen in the street when justice fails.

The New Testament preserves those ethical senses but gives truth an intensified Christological focus. John contrasts the law given by Moses with grace and truth through Jesus Christ (John 1:17), identifies Jesus as the way, truth, and life (John 14:6), and presents the Spirit as guiding disciples into truth (John 16:13). Pauline and Johannine letters describe truth as believed, obeyed, correctly handled, spoken in love, and embodied in conduct.

The Book of Mormon continues the revelatory and ethical strands. Jacob 4:13 says the Spirit reveals reality plainly for salvation; Alma 32 describes a disciplined, experiential growth from faith toward limited knowledge; Moroni 10:3-5 invites a spiritually mediated inquiry under stated conditions.

The 1833 revelation now recorded as [[D&C 93]] gives the Restoration's most explicit formulation: truth concerns what is, was, and will be and is joined to light, intelligence, agency, and premortal existence. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Latter-day Saint discourse repeatedly returned to this language. [[Nauvoo Neighbor 1843-05-03 (Vol. 1 No. 1)]] emphasized the limits and duties of a witness; [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[Conference Report, April 1920 (General Conference (full report), April 1920)]], and [[Conference Report, October 1926 (General Conference (full report), October 1926)]] show truth becoming a recurring vocabulary for revelation, education, testimony, and institutional mission.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence here is **textual**, not an external proof of the canon's supernatural claims.

- **Strong:** D&C 93:24 explicitly defines truth, while Jacob 4:13 independently supplies close reality-language. The wording is direct and the conceptual parallel is clear.
- **Strong:** Texts from several canonical collections consistently oppose truth to lying, deception, injustice, and doctrinal error, while joining it to faithful conduct (Zechariah 8:16; Isaiah 59:4, 14-15; John 8:44-45; Ephesians 4:25; 2 Timothy 2:18).
- **Strong as an internal literary finding:** Johannine texts organize truth around Jesus and the Spirit (John 14:6; 16:13; 17:17; 18:37). This establishes what those texts claim, not that their claims are historically or metaphysically proven.
- **Moderate:** The recurrence of truth as reality, fidelity, truthful speech, and obedient life supports a coherent canonical theme. Hebrew, Greek, and English Restoration usage nevertheless have different semantic histories, so one verse should not be forced into every occurrence.
- **Strong for reception history, weak for independent verification:** [[Conference Report, April 1899 (General Conference (full report), April 1899)]], [[Conference Report, April 1902 (General Conference (full report), April 1902)]], [[Conference Report, April 1928 (General Conference (full report), April 1928)]], and [[Conference Report, October 1928 (General Conference (full report), October 1928)]] repeatedly reproduce D&C 93 language. Repetition demonstrates influence, not metaphysical truth.
- **Strong for sincerity, insufficient for truth:** Mosiah 17:9-20 portrays [[Abinadi]] as refusing to recant at the cost of his life. Martyrdom supports sincerity but cannot establish correctness.

The automated index's "ambiguous name match" labels should be handled cautiously. Truth is not a same-name person or place in these passages. Chapters such as [[3 John 1]], [[2 John 1]], and [[D&C 50]] are genuinely relevant, but their raw mention counts are not measures of doctrinal weight.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does D&C 93:24 define every scriptural use of truth, or provide one Restoration formulation among several overlapping senses?
- When John calls Jesus "the truth," is the primary claim ontological, revelatory, covenantal, soteriological, or some combination?
- In D&C 93, does "Spirit of truth" always designate Jesus Christ, sometimes the Holy Ghost, or also a divine quality? The immediate referent and pronouns must be examined passage by passage.
- What precisely is uncreated in D&C 93:29: individual intelligence, a capacity for truth, an underlying principle, or something else? The verse does not resolve later metaphysical models by itself.
- How should a seeker distinguish revelation from emotion, expectation, group formation, and sincere but contradictory religious experience? See [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]].
- How broad is Moroni 10:5's "truth of all things," and what limits are supplied by its literary and covenant setting?
- What is the proper relationship among spiritual witness, historical investigation, textual criticism, and multiple human witnesses? See [[Testimony]], [[Revelation]], and [[How reliable is the biblical text]].
- Does "truth shall make you free" describe spiritual freedom from sin specifically, or may it responsibly be extended to intellectual and political liberation? John 8:31-36 directly supports the former; broader applications require further argument.
- How should institutions respond when loyalty, reputation, or inherited interpretation appears to conflict with fuller disclosure? Isaiah 59 and Ephesians 4 make communal truthfulness a theological concern.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objections concern both meaning and method.

**Spiritual confirmation is epistemically underdetermined.** People in mutually incompatible religions report peace, certainty, visions, and transformed lives. Therefore an experience interpreted as the Spirit cannot, without further criteria, publicly establish which competing propositions are true. Moroni 10:3-5 states a canonical promise; it does not eliminate the comparative problem explored by [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]]. A believing response may appeal to fruits, repeated testing, coherence, revelation, and divine accommodation, but those criteria still require disciplined application and do not make every strong feeling infallible.

**The canon contains claims, not automatic proof of its own authority.** That D&C 93 defines truth establishes what the revelation says. It does not independently prove that the revelation came from God. Likewise, John's Christological assertions establish Johannine theology but do not by themselves settle every historical question about Jesus. Circular reasoning results if scripture is declared true solely because the same scripture claims divine authority.

**Different passages need not share one philosophical theory.** Hebrew terms often translated "truth" can emphasize firmness or faithfulness, while Greek usage can emphasize truth, reality, or disclosure. D&C 93's definition should illuminate but not erase these differences. A harmonization that treats every occurrence as a technical reference to propositional correspondence overreads the evidence.

**Truth claims can become instruments of power.** Communities may label dissent "error" while protecting mistaken leaders or inherited assumptions. The canon itself supplies the answer in principle but not an effortless institutional mechanism: witnesses must be examined (Deuteronomy 19:15-18), teachers are accountable (2 Timothy 2:15), and truth must be spoken in love within a mutually responsible body (Ephesians 4:15, 25).

**Truth does not visibly free everyone from ordinary suffering.** John 8's promise can sound empirically false if interpreted as guaranteed political, psychological, or material freedom. Its immediate explanation is narrower: Jesus speaks of bondage to sin and freedom through the Son (John 8:34-36). Wider applications may be valuable, but they are interpretations rather than the passage's explicit guarantee.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Lexical scholarship generally cautions against importing a single English abstraction into every biblical occurrence. Standard Hebrew and Greek lexicons describe semantic ranges that include reliability, faithfulness, truthfulness, actuality, and truth. Lexicons clarify usage; they cannot decide whether a revelation is divinely true.

Johannine scholarship broadly agrees that truth in John is bound to God's revelation in Jesus rather than functioning only as an abstract philosophical definition. Raymond E. Brown's *The Gospel According to John* and Marianne Meye Thompson's *John: A Commentary* treat truth within John's interconnected themes of revelation, witness, the Father, the Son, and the Spirit. Scholars differ over the Gospel's historical sources, community setting, and the extent to which its speeches reproduce Jesus's exact words. Rudolf Bultmann's historically critical reading emphasizes revelation and existential encounter while remaining much more skeptical than traditional believers about using John as straightforward historical transcript.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation usually reads [[D&C 93]] as revealed expansion: truth, light, intelligence, agency, Christ's premortal identity, and human premortality belong to one theological vision. Terryl Givens's *Wrestling the Angel* places these ideas within the distinctive architecture of Mormon thought, while Steven C. Harper's *Making Sense of the Doctrine & Covenants* reads revelations in their historical and devotional settings. Interpretations still diverge over what "intelligence" denotes and how much metaphysical detail the revelation settles.

Historical-critical scholarship can describe the 1833 setting, vocabulary, literary relationships, and later reception of D&C 93 without accepting its divine origin. Believing scholarship may use those same historical tools while also treating revelation and spiritual witness as legitimate sources of knowledge. The principal divide is therefore not whether the words exist or have influenced Latter-day Saint thought, but whether supernatural revelation is admitted as an explanatory and epistemic category.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with public and covenantal truth in [[Zechariah 8]] and [[Isaiah 59]]. Note the links among truthful speech, justice, peace, and social integrity.
2. Read Exodus 34:6, Psalm 86:11-15, and Psalm 89:14 to examine truth or faithfulness as part of God's character. Compare [[Justice and Mercy]].
3. Study the Johannine sequence: [[John 8]], John 14:6, [[John 16]], [[John 17]], and [[John 18]]. Track truth as discipleship, person, revelation, sanctification, witness, and contested claim.
4. Read 1 John 2:4, 21, 27; [[2 John 1]]; and [[3 John 1]]. Ask what it means to have truth "in" oneself and to walk in it.
5. Read Romans 2:2-8, Ephesians 4:15-25, 2 Thessalonians 2:10-13, and [[2 Timothy 2]]. Observe how truth becomes obedience, love, responsible teaching, and resistance to error.
6. Turn to [[Jacob 4]], [[Alma 32]], and Moroni 10:3-5. Distinguish plain disclosure, experimental faith, and promised spiritual manifestation.
7. Read [[D&C 50]] before [[D&C 93]]. The first examines the spiritual conditions of teaching and receiving; the second develops truth's relationship to Christ, reality, light, intelligence, agency, and premortal existence.
8. Compare the canonical findings with the reception history in [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[Conference Report, April 1920 (General Conference (full report), April 1920)]], and [[Conference Report, October 1926 (General Conference (full report), October 1926)]]. Keep scripture, later exposition, and personal synthesis in separate columns.
9. Finish with methodological checks through [[Testimony]], [[Records and Sacred Writings]], [[How reliable is the biblical text]], and [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]]. Ask of every conclusion: what was observed, what was inferred, and what remains a matter of faith?

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The canon does not reduce truth to either correct facts or sincere religious feeling. Truth corresponds to reality (D&C 93:24; Jacob 4:13), reflects God's reliability, is disclosed personally in [[Jesus Christ]], is mediated by the Spirit, and becomes visible in truthful speech, justice, love, obedience, and freedom from sin. A person may therefore possess accurate information yet fail to "walk in truth," while sincerity alone cannot turn an error into truth.

The strongest textual center is the conjunction of [[D&C 93]] and the Johannine writings. D&C 93 defines truth and joins it to light and intelligence; John identifies truth with God's self-disclosure in Christ. [[D&C 50]], [[Jacob 4]], and Moroni 10 connect truth-seeking with the Spirit. [[Zechariah 8]], [[Isaiah 59]], and Ephesians 4 prevent this theology from becoming purely private: communities either embody truth through justice and honest relationship or betray it through lies and corruption.

Epistemic humility remains necessary. Canonical texts can establish what their authors and narrators claim, but they do not automatically prove their own divine authority to every reader. Spiritual experience can be profound without being publicly decisive, martyrs can be sincere without being infallible, and repeated institutional teaching documents influence rather than independent confirmation. The canon itself resists careless certainty by requiring investigation of witnesses, warning against doctrinal error, and commanding responsible handling of the word.

A faithful but careful approach therefore seeks truth through converging practices: attention to the actual text, honest historical inquiry, spiritual discernment, moral fruits, correction by witnesses and community, and willingness to revise interpretations. What is established is the canon's sustained commitment to reality, divine disclosure, and truthful living. Whether its supernatural claims are ultimately true remains a question involving historical judgment, philosophical assumptions, lived discipleship, and faith.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
