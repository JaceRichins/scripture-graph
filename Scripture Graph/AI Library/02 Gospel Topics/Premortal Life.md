# Premortal Life

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In current Latter-day Saint usage, **premortal life** or **pre-earth life** is the existence human beings had before mortal birth. [[Premortal Life (Gospel Topics)]] says that all people lived with God as His spirit children; [[Premortality (Gospel Topics)]] adds that they did not yet have physical bodies. The term therefore means more than God's foreknowledge of future persons: it denotes personal existence in God's presence.

The scriptures use several overlapping expressions rather than one technical vocabulary. Abraham 3:22 speaks of “intelligences” organized before the world; D&C 93:29 says humanity was “in the beginning with God” and that intelligence was not created; D&C 138:55-56 speaks of “spirits” who learned before birth. [[Abraham 3]] calls premortal fidelity the “first estate” and mortality the “second estate” (Abraham 3:25-26). Official teaching synthesizes these passages, but the canon does not fully define the relation among intelligence, spirit, organization, and divine parentage.

The **Council in Heaven** is the premortal setting in which the Father presented the work associated with mortal life, a Redeemer was chosen, and rebellion occurred. See [[Council in Heaven (Gospel Topics)]], [[Plan of Salvation]], [[Agency]], [[Jesus Christ]], and [[Satan]].
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The strongest canonical outline comes from [[Abraham 3]], [[Moses 4]], [[D&C 93]], [[D&C 76]], and [[D&C 138]]. Taken together, these texts teach that persons existed with God before earth life; that intelligence is not simply described as creation from nothing; that premortal spirits could learn, choose, receive assignments, and rebel; that the earth was prepared as a place of testing; and that mortal life is a “second estate” (Abraham 3:22-28; D&C 93:29; D&C 138:55-56).

At the council, the Father asked whom He should send. The chosen Son submitted to the Father's will, while Satan sought God's honor and proposed to destroy human agency (Abraham 3:27-28; Moses 4:1-4). Lucifer and his followers were cast down (D&C 29:36-38; D&C 76:25-28). Official summaries in [[Council in Heaven (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Plan of Salvation (Gospel Topics)]] place this choice within humanity's progression toward embodied, resurrected life.

Foreordination is real but should not be confused with irresistible fate. Abraham 3:23 and D&C 138:55-56 describe selection and preparation before birth; Abraham 3:25-26 simultaneously emphasizes testing and fidelity. [[Foreordained to Serve (Elder Steven R. Bangerter, April 2024)]] applies that teaching to service. Neither these texts nor official summaries justify inferring a person's premortal standing from mortal race, nationality, wealth, disability, office, or adversity.

Several familiar details belong to interpretation rather than an explicit, comprehensive scriptural account. The canon does not explain how spirits were organized, how spirit parentage relates to uncreated intelligence, how long premortality lasted, how premortal memory worked, or whether “a third part” in D&C 29:36 is intended as an exact statistic.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
In the biblical texts, [[Job 38]] depicts heavenly beings rejoicing at creation, while [[Jeremiah 1]] depicts God knowing and appointing Jeremiah before birth. Neither passage independently teaches universal conscious human premortality. The New Testament explicitly teaches Christ's preexistence and speaks of believers being chosen before the world's foundation, but it does not offer an equally explicit account of all humans living as spirit children before birth. Revelation 12 contributes disputed war-in-heaven imagery.

Ideas of soul preexistence also appeared outside Latter-day Saint thought. Origen taught a form of rational-soul preexistence, but his larger cosmology should not be equated with modern LDS doctrine. Later Christian tradition rejected universal human preexistence; anti-Origenist condemnations are associated with the sixth century, although historians dispute the precise status and conciliar relationship of particular anathemas.

The Restoration canon makes premortal anthropology increasingly explicit. [[Moses 4]] emerged from Joseph Smith's Bible revision beginning in 1830; [[D&C 93]] dates to 1833; the Book of Abraham was published in 1842 after work beginning in 1835; and Joseph F. Smith's 1918 vision, now [[D&C 138]], describes instruction before birth. These texts supply the vocabulary of intelligence, spirits, estates, agency, foreordination, and premortal preparation.

Later Church teaching organized these passages into the familiar plan-of-salvation narrative. [[Premortal Life (Gospel Topics)]], [[Premortality (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Council in Heaven (Gospel Topics)]] present the current official synthesis. This historical development describes increasing explicitness within the LDS corpus; it does not by itself resolve whether the Restoration texts recover ancient revelation or reflect nineteenth- and twentieth-century doctrinal development.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong and direct

The internal canonical evidence is strong. Abraham 3:22 directly locates organized intelligences before the world; Abraham 3:24-28 places planning, testing, two estates, and rebellion in that setting. D&C 93:29 says humanity was with God in the beginning. D&C 138:55-56 explicitly describes spirits learning and preparing before birth. Moses 4:1-4 and D&C 76:25-28 identify Christ and Satan within the premortal conflict. Together these passages establish the doctrine within the Latter-day Saint canon more securely than any one proof text.

Ether 3:9, 16 independently portrays the premortal Christ's spirit body. This is strong evidence for Christ's personal preexistence, but it does not by itself define every human spirit's condition.

### Substantial but qualified

Jeremiah 1:5 strongly establishes divine knowledge and prophetic appointment before Jeremiah's birth. It is compatible with conscious premortality, but divine foreknowledge alone is an adequate alternative explanation. [[October 19–25. “Before I Formed Thee in the Belly I Knew Thee” Jeremiah 1–3; 7; 16–18; 20]] appropriately uses the passage as an entry into study rather than a complete exposition.

Alma 13:3-5 is genuine but inconclusive evidence for premortal foreordination. “From the foundation of the world,” God's foreknowledge, and the passage's ordering language permit a premortal reading. Its syntax does not unmistakably place the described faith and works in pre-earth life, so claims of premortal merit should remain qualified.

### Compatible antecedents, not independent proofs

Job 38:4-7 shows a populated heavenly realm rejoicing at creation. Identifying its “sons of God” with all future humans is possible within Latter-day Saint synthesis but is not required by Job itself. Ephesians 1:4-5 establishes pretemporal election “in Christ,” not necessarily the conscious existence of those elected. Revelation 12:4-9 parallels the Restoration rebellion narrative, but its apocalyptic symbolism admits other chronological readings.

The evidentiary conclusion must therefore be scoped: the doctrine is explicit and cumulative **within Restoration scripture**. That internal strength does not, by itself, prove the antiquity or revelatory origin of the Book of Abraham, Moses, or modern revelations to a reader who disputes their authority.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Several important questions remain open because the canon supplies affirmations rather than a complete metaphysics:

- What is the precise relationship among uncreated “intelligence” in D&C 93:29, the “intelligences” of Abraham 3:22, and the “spirits” of D&C 138:55? The available texts permit more than one model.
- What does it mean for intelligences to have been “organized”? Does this concern personal identity, spirit embodiment, relationship to heavenly parents, ordering into a community, or something else?
- How should divine parentage be described without contradicting the claim that intelligence was not created or made? Official teaching affirms both, but the mechanism is unrevealed.
- Were all premortal assignments based on prior choices, inherent capacities, grace, divine purpose, or some combination? Abraham 3 and D&C 138 do not give a formula.
- Does Alma 13:3-5 locate faith and works in premortality, mortality, or a rhetorically compressed combination of both?
- Are Job's “sons of God” future human spirits, heavenly beings of another order, or poetic members of a divine court?
- Is the “third part” of D&C 29:36 an exact fraction or an apocalyptic description of a division?
- How do premortal agency and divine foreknowledge coexist? The canon asserts both but does not resolve every philosophical model.
- Why is premortal life ordinarily forgotten? Mortal testing supplies a theological rationale, but no canonical passage offers a neurological or metaphysical account of the veil.
- How much detail in popular retellings comes from scripture, and how much from later sermons, curriculum, artistic convention, or inference?

These questions should be studied alongside [[Agency]], [[Creation]], [[Plan of Salvation]], [[Foreordained to Serve (Elder Steven R. Bangerter, April 2024)]], and [[God the Father (Gospel Topics)]].
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest textual objection is that the Hebrew Bible and New Testament do not plainly teach universal conscious human premortality. Job 38:7 can naturally refer to members of a heavenly court; Jeremiah 1:5 can express divine foreknowledge and vocation; Ephesians 1:4 can describe God's prior purpose; and Revelation 12 uses symbolically dense apocalyptic imagery. Christ's preexistence is explicit, but His unique identity means it cannot automatically be generalized to everyone.

A historical-critical account argues that the complete system becomes explicit in nineteenth-century Restoration texts, especially [[Abraham 3]], [[Moses 4]], and [[D&C 93]], and develops further in later revelation and teaching. Readers who do not accept these texts as revelation may understand the doctrine as Joseph Smith's theological development rather than recovered antiquity. Because Abraham 3 is the fullest single account, questions addressed by [[Is the Book of Abraham a translation of the papyri]] materially affect its external evidentiary weight. Believing translation, missing-papyrus, and revelatory or catalyst models should be represented alongside the critical conclusion that the surviving Egyptian material does not translate into the published Book of Abraham.

A philosophical objection concerns personal identity. D&C 93 calls intelligence or the light of truth uncreated, Abraham 3 describes intelligences as organized, and D&C 138 speaks of spirits. The canon does not fully explain whether these are identical categories, successive conditions, or related terms used differently. Consequently, no detailed mechanism of spirit birth or organization should be asserted as established fact.

A moral objection concerns claims about premortal merit. Abraham 3:22-23 and D&C 138 describe selected and prepared figures, but they do not authorize assigning present people premortal rank from race, nationality, wealth, disability, adversity, or institutional office. Historically, theories of lesser premortal valiance were among explanations offered for restrictions on Black members; those theories should be studied directly under [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]], not hidden behind vague references to inequality. Such explanations are textually underdetermined, unfalsifiable, and capable of blaming sufferers.

The strongest faithful response is limited but substantial: if the authority of Restoration scripture is granted, the canon clearly teaches premortal existence and coherently connects it with agency, Creation, Christ's mission, and mortal purpose. That establishes internal canonical clarity and coherence, not independent historical proof that the revelations are ancient or divine.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Terryl L. Givens, in *When Souls Had Wings: Pre-Mortal Existence in Western Thought* (Oxford University Press, 2010), places premortal-existence ideas in a long history extending beyond Mormonism. That history prevents two opposite errors: the concept is not uniquely imaginable only within the Restoration, but neither was universal human premortality the settled position of later orthodox Christianity.

Biblical scholarship generally distinguishes several ideas that Latter-day Saint interpretation brings together: a heavenly council in texts such as Job 1 and Job 38; prophetic foreknowledge in Jeremiah 1; election in Ephesians 1; Christ's preexistence in John; and apocalyptic conflict in Revelation 12. Many scholars accept those textual observations while rejecting the inference that they collectively teach every human's conscious premortal life. Michael S. Heiser's *The Unseen Realm* (Lexham Press, 2015), although written from a confessional Christian standpoint, illustrates the divine-council reading of “sons of God” without identifying them generally as unborn humans.

Scholarship on Mormon doctrinal development emphasizes that Restoration texts make premortality more explicit over time. Charles R. Harrell's *“This Is My Doctrine”: The Development of Mormon Theology* (Greg Kofford Books, 2011) treats the doctrine developmentally. Samuel Morris Brown's *In Heaven as It Is on Earth: Joseph Smith and the Early Mormon Conquest of Death* (Oxford University Press, 2012) situates Joseph Smith's teachings about human identity, kinship, and eternity in their historical and theological setting.

The principal divide is not over whether Abraham 3 and D&C 93 contain premortal concepts—they plainly do—but over how those texts originated and what authority they possess. Believing scholars read them as revelation and often as recovery of ancient truths. Critical scholars read them within Joseph Smith's nineteenth-century environment. Debate over the Book of Abraham is represented by works such as Robert K. Ritner's *The Joseph Smith Egyptian Papyri: A Complete Edition* (Signature Books, 2013) and John Gee's *An Introduction to the Book of Abraham* (Religious Studies Center and Deseret Book, 2017). Their disagreement affects the external evidentiary weight assigned to Abraham 3, though not the observation that the received text teaches premortality.

Responsible study should therefore keep three judgments separate: what a passage says, how Latter-day Saints synthesize it, and whether the source is accepted as ancient revelation.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with [[Abraham 3]], especially Abraham 3:18-28. Mark every statement about intelligence, organization, rulers, Creation, testing, estates, the two candidates, and rebellion. Separate what is explicit from what later summaries add.

2. Read [[Moses 4]] 4:1-4 and [[D&C 76]] 76:25-28. These passages identify the motives and identities only partly specified in Abraham 3. Then read D&C 29:36-38 for the role of agency and the “third part.”

3. Read [[D&C 93]] 93:23-30. Ask exactly what is said to be in the beginning, what is uncreated, and where agency enters the argument. Resist prematurely forcing “intelligence” into a complete metaphysical system.

4. Read [[D&C 138]] 138:53-56. Compare its explicit premortal instruction and preparation with Abraham's “noble and great ones.” Use [[Foreordained to Serve (Elder Steven R. Bangerter, April 2024)]] to distinguish foreordination from guaranteed outcomes.

5. Study Christ's premortal identity in John 1:1-3; John 17:5; 1 Peter 1:20; Ether 3:9, 16; and Moses 4:2. [[To Be a Follower of Christ (Elder Alfred Kyungu, October 2021)]] develops Christ's submission as a model of [[Humility]].

6. Only then evaluate the qualified biblical and Book of Mormon witnesses: [[Job 38]] 38:4-7, [[Jeremiah 1]] 1:5, Ephesians 1:4-5, Revelation 12:4-9, and [[Alma 13]] 13:3-5. For each, write down both the premortal reading and the strongest alternative reading.

7. Compare the resulting textual inventory with [[Premortal Life (Gospel Topics)]], [[Premortality (Gospel Topics)]], [[Council in Heaven (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Plan of Salvation (Gospel Topics)]]. This reveals where official synthesis is directly quoted from scripture and where it responsibly combines several passages.

8. Finish by studying implications rather than speculative rankings: [[Agency]], [[Creation]], [[Jesus Christ]], [[Satan]], [[Resurrection]], and [[Eternal Life]]. [[Be Peaceable Followers of Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, October 2023)]] offers a concise modern synthesis.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Premortal life is firmly taught by the Latter-day Saint canon. [[Abraham 3]] places organized intelligences before the world and describes planning, testing, two estates, the choice of a messenger, and rebellion. [[D&C 93]] says humanity was with God in the beginning and that intelligence was not created or made. [[D&C 138]] explicitly describes spirits learning and preparing before birth. [[Moses 4]], D&C 29, and [[D&C 76]] identify Christ's submission, Satan's assault on agency, and the casting down of the rebels.

The doctrine belongs inside the larger [[Plan of Salvation]]. Premortal agency makes the council morally meaningful; [[Creation]] supplies the arena for embodied testing; [[Jesus Christ]] makes redemption and resurrection possible; and mortal life remains consequential rather than predetermined. Foreordination can describe preparation and calling, but it does not erase agency or authorize claims that visible mortal advantage proves superior premortal worth.

The evidence must nevertheless be graded. The cumulative Restoration-scripture case is direct and strong. Jeremiah 1:5 and Alma 13:3-5 offer substantial but qualified support for foreordination. Job 38:4-7, Ephesians 1:4-5, and Revelation 12:4-9 are compatible parallels whose identity, ontology, or chronology remains disputed. The Bible explicitly establishes Christ's preexistence more readily than universal human premortality.

Consequently, two conclusions should not be confused. **Textual conclusion:** the accepted Latter-day Saint canon clearly teaches conscious pre-earth existence. **Historical or apologetic conclusion:** those texts do not authenticate their own antiquity or revelatory origin merely by forming a coherent doctrine. Believers receive the full portrait through Restoration revelation; critical scholars commonly understand it as doctrinal development. Honest study can acknowledge that divide while also recognizing the doctrine's scriptural depth, its emphasis on agency and Christ, and the substantial questions its brief metaphysical language leaves unanswered.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
