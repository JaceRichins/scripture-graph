# Death and the Spirit World

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In Latter-day Saint teaching, **physical death** is the separation of the spirit from the mortal body; the body returns to the earth while the spirit continues (Alma 40:11; compare Ecclesiastes 12:7; [[Death, Physical (Gospel Topics)]]). **Resurrection** is the permanent reunion of spirit and body, not merely survival after death (Alma 11:42–45; [[Resurrection]]).

The **spirit world** is the intermediate state between death and resurrection. [[Alma 40]] explicitly identifies that interval and describes paradise—a condition of happiness, rest, and peace—for the righteous, and darkness, fear, and anticipated judgment for the wicked (Alma 40:6–14). Both conditions precede resurrection and therefore should not be confused with final glory or final exclusion.

In common Latter-day Saint usage, **spirit prison** is a harmonizing label for the condition of spirits who have not received the gospel. It is not Alma 40's own term: Alma speaks of darkness, while the phrase “spirits in prison” comes from 1 Peter 3:19 and is incorporated into [[D&C 138]]. That revelation adds organized gospel instruction among the dead (D&C 138:29–37). The canon does not settle whether paradise and prison are geographical compartments, relational conditions, communities, or some combination.

Bodily death, temporary postmortem conditions, and the final **second death** are distinct concepts (D&C 76:37; Revelation 20:6, 14). Consequently, high keyword counts in chapters such as [[Romans 6]], [[Numbers 35]], and [[Leviticus 20]] do not by themselves establish relevance to the spirit world.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The clearest canonical sequence is:

1. Mortality ends when spirit and body separate (Ecclesiastes 12:7; Alma 40:11).
2. Every spirit continues through an interval before resurrection (Alma 40:6–11).
3. That interval includes morally significant but provisional conditions: rest and peace for the righteous, and darkness and fear for the wicked (Alma 40:12–14).
4. Christ entered the world of spirits after his death. [[D&C 138]] depicts him appearing among the faithful, proclaiming liberty, and commissioning messengers to teach those in darkness (D&C 138:16–37). This is the Restoration's authoritative reading of 1 Peter 3:18–20 and 4:6.
5. The dead can receive gospel instruction. Doctrine and Covenants 138:31–34 names faith, repentance, and vicarious ordinances, joining postmortal response to [[Baptism for the Dead]]. Doctrine and Covenants 137:7–9 promises a just judgment for those who died without receiving the gospel; see [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]].
6. Christ's resurrection guarantees humanity's deliverance from physical death (1 Corinthians 15:21–22; 2 Nephi 9:10–13; Helaman 14:15–17). At resurrection, body and spirit are permanently reunited (Alma 11:42–45).
7. Resurrection and [[Final Judgment]] lead to final outcomes; the temporary spirit world is therefore not identical to the [[Kingdoms of Glory]] or the second death (Revelation 20:12–15; D&C 76:37).

These propositions belong to the canon and current official teaching represented by [[Death, Physical (Gospel Topics)]], [[Spirit (True to the Faith)]], and [[Chapter 38 The Spirit World]]. Details about occupations, social organization, time, appearance, or routine generally do not. [[Trust in the Lord (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2019)]] explicitly warns that personal visions and inspired impressions are not thereby official Church doctrine.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The Hebrew Bible does not present one fully systematized doctrine of the intermediate state. It speaks of the grave or Sheol, portrays the dead as gathered below, affirms that the spirit returns to God, and occasionally voices hope of redemption from death (Psalm 49:14–15; Ezekiel 32:21–32; Ecclesiastes 12:7). These strands matter, but reading the later Restoration scheme wholesale into each passage would exceed the texts.

The New Testament gives a more resurrection-centered horizon. Jesus promises paradise in Luke 23:43; the parable of the rich man and Lazarus imagines conscious comfort and anguish after death in [[Luke 16]]; 1 Peter 3:18–20 and 4:6 associates Christ and gospel proclamation with the dead or imprisoned spirits; and [[Revelation 20]] distinguishes Hades, resurrection, judgment, and the second death. Early Christians debated these passages, and they did not generate a single uncontested account of Christ's descent or postmortem conversion.

The Book of Mormon supplies a more explicit intermediate-state discourse. [[Alma 40]] responds to [[Corianton]] by identifying a space between death and resurrection, describing paradise and the condition of the wicked, and keeping both prior to embodied judgment. [[2 Nephi 9]] places deliverance from death and hell within the justice and mercy of Christ's atonement.

Restoration revelations develop the picture further. [[D&C 76]] distinguishes final outcomes and the second death. [[D&C 137]], prompted by Joseph Smith's vision of [[Alvin Smith]], addresses those who die without gospel knowledge. In 1918 [[Joseph F. Smith]] meditated on 1 Peter and received the [[Vision of the redemption of the dead]], now [[D&C 138]]. It depicts Christ organizing a mission among the dead rather than personally preaching among the wicked. [[Gospel Doctrine]] and later instructional works incorporated this teaching; [[The Vision of the Redemption of the Dead (President M. Russell Ballard, October 2018)]] recounts its significance.

This history can be understood confessionally as continuing revelation and historically as doctrinal development within an expanding canon. Those descriptions answer different questions and should not be confused.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence here establishes **what the Latter-day Saint canon teaches**. [[Alma 40]] explicitly describes an interval between death and resurrection and contrasting conditions within it (Alma 40:6–14). [[D&C 138]] explicitly portrays Christ among the faithful dead and commissioned messengers teaching other spirits (D&C 138:11–37). These are strong textual observations; disagreement about their divine authority is a different question.

The texts also exhibit **internal canonical coherence**. Luke 23:43, 1 Peter 3:18–20 and 4:6, Alma 40, [[D&C 137]], and D&C 138 can be read together as an account of continuing personal existence, differentiated conditions, postmortal proclamation, just opportunity, resurrection, and judgment. This coherence is significant for interpreting the Restoration canon but is not independent empirical corroboration. D&C 138:7–8 quotes 1 Peter rather than furnishing a separate witness, and later Restoration texts may consciously interpret and expand earlier scripture.

There is **strong historical evidence of reception and development**. The documentary record can establish that Joseph F. Smith reported the vision, interpreted 1 Peter through it, and that the Church later accepted the vision as canon. Historical method alone cannot verify the unseen events or divine origin claimed by the revelation.

Important alternative readings remain. In historical-critical interpretation, 1 Peter's “spirits in prison” may be rebellious angels, Noah's deceased contemporaries, or defeated spiritual powers; 1 Peter 4:6 may concern people evangelized before death. [[Luke 16]] is commonly read as a parable rather than literal geography, and [[Revelation 20]] is apocalyptic imagery whose precise chronology is interpreted differently. [[D&C 138]] supplies the controlling Latter-day Saint synthesis without creating a historical-critical consensus about those earlier texts.

External corroboration for the metaphysical model is limited in the supplied corpus. The warranted conclusion is therefore asymmetrical: the doctrine's canonical content and historical reception are well established; its internal coherence is meaningful but not decisive evidence of external reality; and the reality described remains a religious claim received through scripture and revelation. Raw keyword counts are weaker still, because many occurrences of *death* concern execution, mortality, sin, or metaphorical death rather than the intermediate state.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Are paradise and spirit prison places, conditions of consciousness, relationships to God, or some combination? Alma 40:12–14 and Doctrine and Covenants 138:18–24 describe states and communities but do not supply physical geography.

- How should Alma's statement that all spirits are 'taken home to that God who gave them life' be related to his immediate distinction between paradise and darkness (Alma 40:11–14)? Direct divine presence, return to God's jurisdiction, and return to the divinely governed spirit realm are possible readings.

- How much change remains possible after death? Doctrine and Covenants 138:31–34 clearly permits instruction and response, while Alma 34:32–35 warns against deliberately postponing repentance. The canon establishes both accountability and postmortal opportunity but does not quantify their limits.

- Does 1 Peter itself teach missionary work among deceased humans? [[D&C 138]] authoritatively answers yes within the Restoration canon, but historical-critical interpretation of 1 Peter 3:19 remains divided among deceased humans, Noah's contemporaries, and rebellious angelic beings.

- What does 'today' modify in Luke 23:43? Standard punctuation supports an immediate promise of paradise, but the earliest Greek manuscripts did not carry modern punctuation, allowing a minority alternative reading.

- How is time experienced by spirits? Alma 40:8 says all is as one day with God but does not describe the subjective duration experienced by the dead.

- How do proxy ordinances and postmortal consent interact? Doctrine and Covenants 138:33 joins them, but the canon supplies little phenomenological detail. See [[Baptism for the Dead]] and [[Agency]].

- What continuity survives death besides personal identity? Doctrine and Covenants 130:18–19 supports continuing intelligence, but questions about complete memory, trauma, disability, and social status remain open; see [[Education and Learning]].

- How should temporary spirit prison be distinguished from final hell? That distinction is crucial to [[Is hell eternal]], [[Final Judgment]], and [[Kingdoms of Glory]].
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
A strong objection is that the doctrine looks like cumulative development rather than recovery of one plainly biblical system. The Hebrew Bible's Sheol language, Jesus's parables, 1 Peter's difficult sentences, Alma's intermediate state, and Joseph F. Smith's organized missionary world are not equally detailed. A critic can reasonably argue that later texts resolve ambiguities by adding new concepts. The believing response calls this continuing revelation, but that theological designation does not erase the observable development.

A second objection concerns 1 Peter. 'Spirits in prison' may refer to rebellious angels associated with Noah's day, and 'the dead' in 1 Peter 4:6 may mean people evangelized while alive who subsequently died. These are serious grammatical and historical options, not evasions. [[D&C 138]] chooses and expands the deceased-human reading for Latter-day Saints, but it does not create a historical-critical consensus about Peter's original referent.

A third objection is genre. [[Luke 16]] is a parable and may reuse familiar Jewish afterlife imagery to condemn wealth without compassion and refusal to heed Moses and the prophets. Treating Abraham's bosom, flames, and the gulf as literal cartography may press the story beyond its purpose. Similarly, Luke 23:43 is a single saying whose punctuation and precise chronology have been debated.

A fourth objection is moral: postmortal opportunity may seem to weaken mortal urgency, while irreversible postmortal consequences may seem unfair to people shaped by ignorance, trauma, or unequal access to truth. The Restoration synthesis answers the access problem through Doctrine and Covenants 137:7–9, 138:30–34, proxy ordinances, and divine judgment of hearts. It does not disclose enough about postmortal psychology to dissolve every concern.

A fifth objection is evidentiary. Descriptions of an unseen realm are not ordinarily testable, and cross-scriptural agreement may result from literary dependence or later harmonization. That objection succeeds against claims of empirical proof. It does not show that the doctrine is incoherent or that the texts fail to teach it; it limits the kind of conclusion the evidence warrants.

Finally, popular accounts often go beyond the canon. Detailed claims derived from dreams, near-death experiences, folklore, or reported sermons can conflict with one another. [[Trust in the Lord (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2019)]] gives the appropriate institutional caution: personal experiences may comfort individuals but should not be taught as official doctrine.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Biblical scholarship generally treats Israelite and early Christian afterlife language as historically diverse. Philip S. Johnston's *Shades of Sheol* emphasizes the variety and ambiguity of Old Testament descriptions of the dead. Alan F. Segal's *Life After Death* traces changing Jewish and Christian models rather than a single static doctrine. N. T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* emphasizes that early Christian hope centered on future bodily resurrection, not merely disembodied survival. These approaches support the important distinction between the intermediate state and resurrection while resisting the projection of a complete later system into every early text.

Interpretation of 1 Peter 3:18–20 is notably divided. Commentators such as J. Ramsey Michaels and Karen Jobes survey readings involving fallen angels, Noah's contemporaries, Christ's proclamation of victory, or evangelization of the human dead. Historical-critical scholarship therefore does not regard postmortal missionary work as the passage's uncontested meaning. Latter-day Saint interpretation differs because [[D&C 138]] functions as additional canonical commentary on 1 Peter 3 and 4.

Scholarship on Luke commonly treats the rich man and Lazarus as a parable using recognizable afterlife imagery to teach moral reversal and the sufficiency of Moses and the prophets. That does not make its postmortem setting meaningless, but it cautions against deriving a detailed geography from [[Luke 16]]. Luke 23:43 is stronger evidence for the evangelist's paradise language, although a minority punctuation dispute remains.

Historians of Mormonism, including Samuel Morris Brown in *In Heaven as It Is on Earth*, situate Restoration teachings about death within Joseph Smith's larger transformation of salvation, kinship, embodiment, and work for the dead. A believing scholar may accept that historical setting while also affirming revelation; a non-believing historian normally explains change through cultural inheritance, grief, institutional development, and creative scriptural interpretation. The historical data alone do not adjudicate between those ultimate explanations.

Official Church sources answer a different question from academic scholarship. [[Chapter 37 Understanding Death and Resurrection]], [[Chapter 38 The Spirit World]], [[Spirit World (Gospel Topics)]], and [[The Vision of the Redemption of the Dead (President M. Russell Ballard, October 2018)]] state and teach the received doctrine. [[Trust in the Lord (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2019)]] also marks its epistemic boundary: basic canonical teachings are official; abundant personal speculation is not.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. **Begin with death and resurrection as distinct events.** Read Ecclesiastes 12:7, Alma 11:42–45, and 1 Corinthians 15:20–26. Compare the definitions in [[Death, Physical (Gospel Topics)]], [[Spirit (True to the Faith)]], and [[Resurrection]].

2. **Study the interval directly.** Read all of [[Alma 40]], tracking Corianton's question, the phrase 'space betwixt,' paradise, the condition of the wicked, resurrection, and judgment. Mark what Alma says he knows and what he leaves with God, especially Alma 40:8 and 40:20.

3. **Read the New Testament witnesses in their own genres.** Study Luke 23:39–43 as crucifixion narrative, [[Luke 16]] as parable, 1 Peter 3:18–22 and 4:6 as difficult apostolic argument, and [[Revelation 20]] as apocalypse. Do not flatten narrative, parable, epistle, and vision into one kind of statement.

4. **Then read the Restoration interpretation.** Read [[D&C 137]] before [[D&C 138]]. Note how the first addresses [[Alvin Smith]] and the unevangelized, while the second explains organized preaching and vicarious ordinances. Follow with [[Baptism for the Dead]] and [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]].

5. **Place the subject within the whole plan.** Read Moses 6:48, [[2 Nephi 9]], Romans 5:12–21, and 1 Corinthians 15:20–58. Trace [[Fall of Adam and Eve]], [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], death, spirit world, resurrection, [[Final Judgment]], and [[Kingdoms of Glory]] as related but nonidentical stages or doctrines.

6. **Separate canon from extrapolation.** Read [[Chapter 37 Understanding Death and Resurrection]], [[Chapter 38 The Spirit World]], and [[Trust in the Lord (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2019)]]. Make two columns: 'text explicitly says' and 'I infer or have heard.' This prevents consoling folklore from silently becoming doctrine.

7. **Test alternative readings.** Consult responsible commentaries on 1 Peter 3:18–20, Luke 16, and Old Testament Sheol. Ask whether each interpretation comes from the biblical passage itself, from later Christian tradition, or from the additional authority of Restoration scripture.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The Latter-day Saint canon presents death as real separation, not extinction and not resurrection. The mortal body returns to the earth while the spirit continues; an interval follows before spirit and body are permanently reunited (Ecclesiastes 12:7; Alma 40:6–14; Alma 11:42–45). [[Alma 40]] calls this interval a 'space' and describes sharply contrasting conditions of peace and fear. Because both conditions precede resurrection and judgment, neither should be confused without qualification with final celestial glory or the second death.

The doctrine's center is Christ, not postmortal geography. Through the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] and [[Resurrection of Jesus]], physical death is universally overcome (1 Corinthians 15:21–22; 2 Nephi 9:10–13). Luke 23:43 associates Christ with paradise after death. First Peter speaks of spirits in prison and preaching to the dead. [[D&C 138]] gives the Restoration's authoritative interpretation: Christ appeared among the faithful dead, proclaimed liberation, and commissioned righteous messengers to teach those in darkness (D&C 138:11–37).

This ministry supplies part of the canonical answer to unequal mortal opportunity. [[D&C 137]] says those who died without gospel knowledge are judged according to their hearts and what they would have received; Doctrine and Covenants 138:31–34 describes instruction, repentance, and vicarious ordinances. Thus [[Agency]], accountability, grace, and [[Baptism for the Dead]] remain connected. The teaching is not simple universalism: it preserves response and judgment. Nor is it a doctrine that mortal choices are irrelevant: scripture still treats mortal probation with urgency.

Historically, the picture becomes progressively more explicit. The Hebrew Bible contains diverse language about Sheol, return to God, and redemption from death. The New Testament centers hope on Christ's resurrection while offering several compressed postmortem images. The Book of Mormon defines the interval more clearly, and Restoration revelations extend it into an organized work of salvation. A believer can call this line-upon-line revelation; a historian can describe it as doctrinal development. The documentary evidence establishes development and canonical acceptance, not by itself divine origin.

Several questions remain open: whether paradise and prison are spatial or relational, how time is experienced, how much postmortal change occurs, and how consciousness and memory operate without the body. Competing readings of 1 Peter and the parabolic character of [[Luke 16]] must also be acknowledged. The strongest honest conclusion is therefore bounded: the Latter-day Saint canon clearly teaches conscious continued existence, differentiated intermediate conditions, Christ's victory and ministry among the dead, postmortal proclamation, universal resurrection, and final judgment. It does not authorize confident detail about everything that happens there. [[Trust in the Lord (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2019)]] makes that restraint part of responsible faith rather than a defect in it.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
