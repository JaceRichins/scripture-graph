# Atonement of Jesus Christ

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, **atonement** is not exhausted by one metaphor. In the Levitical material it denotes rites by which priests deal with sin, impurity, threatened judgment, and the contamination of sacred space; persons, the congregation, and even the sanctuary or altar can be objects of atonement (Leviticus 16:6, 16-20, 33). In Romans 5:10-11, the KJV's “atonement” occurs in a context that repeatedly says “reconciled,” emphasizing restored relationship with God.

In the Book of Mormon and Doctrine and Covenants, the expression becomes an explicit name for the saving work of [[Jesus Christ]]. That work overcomes death and hell ([[2 Nephi 9]]), makes repentance and mercy possible ([[Alma 34]]; [[Alma 42]]), covers little children and persons without moral knowledge ([[Mosiah 3]]), enables inward transformation (Mosiah 3:19), and includes Christ taking pains, sicknesses, and infirmities so that he can succor his people ([[Alma 7]]). [[D&C 19]] places Christ's own suffering at the center.

In Latter-day Saint usage, therefore, the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] ordinarily means Christ's entire reconciling and redemptive achievement, centrally expressed in his suffering, death, and resurrection. [[Gethsemane]], the [[Crucifixion of Jesus Christ]], and the [[Resurrection of Jesus]] are distinguishable events but belong to one saving work. The official study entry [[Atonement of Jesus Christ (Gospel Topics)]] should be kept distinct from any single scholarly model used to explain how that work operates.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon's central affirmations are these:

- Humanity is fallen, subject to sin, death, and judgment, and cannot produce its own complete rescue (2 Nephi 9:6-10; Alma 34:9-12).
- Jesus Christ bears sins and suffering, dies, and rises; the texts associate his work with reconciliation, resurrection, forgiveness, healing, and eternal life (Isaiah 53:4-12; Romans 5:10-11; 2 Nephi 9:10-13; Alma 7:11-13; Moroni 7:41).
- The atonement is called infinite, but saving relationship is not portrayed as morally automatic. Faith, repentance, covenant fidelity, and endurance remain meaningful responses (Alma 34:15-17, 31-35; Alma 42:22-24; D&C 19:15-17).
- Those lacking moral capacity or revealed law are not treated as knowing rebels. Mosiah 3:11, 16, 18 and 2 Nephi 9:25-26 explicitly connect their deliverance with Christ.
- Mercy does not declare evil harmless. In [[Alma 42]], the atonement permits mercy to claim the penitent without destroying justice; in Leviticus 6:4-7, restitution precedes ritual atonement and forgiveness.
- Salvation includes transformation, not only acquittal. Mosiah 3:19 describes yielding to the Holy Spirit, putting off the natural man, and becoming a saint through Christ.
- Christ's work is presented as reaching beyond guilt alone. [[Alma 7]] names pains, sicknesses, infirmities, death, and sins, and gives embodied succor as one stated purpose; [[Moroni 7]] joins atonement and resurrection to [[Hope]].

Explanatory theories such as satisfaction, penal substitution, moral influence, ransom, healing, participation, and Christus Victor are interpretations of this material. Each may illuminate part of the canon, but none should be confused with the whole scriptural portrait or treated as established merely by attaching a theological label to the verses.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The canonical history begins with Israel's sacrificial and priestly world. [[Leviticus 4]], [[Leviticus 5]], [[Leviticus 6]], and [[Leviticus 16]] connect atonement with offerings, priestly mediation, forgiveness, purification, restitution, and sanctuary cleansing. These chapters are genuine background, but their keyword matches do not by themselves name the later doctrine “Atonement of Jesus Christ.” The index's “ambiguous name match” warning is therefore important: the same English word can describe a local ritual function without directly identifying Christ's universal work.

[[Isaiah 53]] then gives the influential portrait of a servant who bears griefs and iniquities, brings healing, justifies many, and intercedes for transgressors. The servant's referent in Isaiah's original setting is debated; Christian scripture applies the passage to Jesus, and Matthew 8:16-17 specifically connects it with his healings.

The New Testament interprets Jesus's death and resurrection through several inherited images: sacrifice, Passover, covenant blood, ransom, reconciliation, victory, justification, and priestly entry. Romans 5:10-11 emphasizes reconciliation; Revelation 1:5-6 joins cleansing by Christ's blood to a redeemed priestly people.

The Book of Mormon speaks with exceptional explicitness before Christ's birth. Jacob calls the atonement infinite and ties it to resurrection ([[2 Nephi 9]]); Benjamin's angel relates it to the Fall, the law of Moses, children, ignorance, judgment, and conversion ([[Mosiah 3]]); Abinadi subordinates the law to God's saving action; Amulek argues for an infinite and eternal sacrifice ([[Alma 34]]); Alma connects it with embodied succor ([[Alma 7]]) and with justice and mercy ([[Alma 42]]). Believers receive these as ancient prophetic disclosure. Critical readers regard their Christian precision as compatible with, or evidence for, nineteenth-century composition. The textual observation is secure; the historical inference is not settled by frequency alone. [[Sacred Scriptures—the Foundations of Faith (Elder Quentin L. Cook, October 2024)]] highlights this Book of Mormon emphasis.

[[D&C 19]] adds a first-person description of Christ's suffering and gives [[Suffering in Gethsemane]] particular prominence in Latter-day Saint reflection. Later Christian history generated competing explanatory models—ransom or victory, satisfaction, penal substitution, moral influence, and participatory or healing accounts. Latter-day Saint teaching draws from several of them while usually presenting the atonement as broader than any one theory.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
### Strong textual findings

The strongest evidence is internal and textual. [[Leviticus 16]] demonstrably applies atonement to persons, the community, and sacred space; [[Isaiah 53]] demonstrably describes representative bearing and healing; Romans 5:10-11 demonstrably places the KJV word beside reconciliation; [[2 Nephi 9]], [[Mosiah 3]], [[Alma 7]], [[Alma 34]], [[Alma 42]], and [[D&C 19]] demonstrably develop resurrection, mercy, succor, sacrifice, justice, repentance, and suffering. Together these passages strongly establish a multidimensional canonical portrait.

Matthew 8:17 is especially valuable because the text itself makes the intertextual move: Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to Jesus's healing ministry. This establishes Matthew's reading, though not the servant's sole original referent or a complete mechanism of atonement.

Leviticus 6:4-7 strongly establishes that, for the offenses discussed there, sacrificial reconciliation does not replace restitution. That finding guards against using [[Grace]] or atonement to evade accountability.

### Qualified findings

The Levitical system is strong conceptual background for later Christian interpretation, but detailed one-to-one predictions are an interpretive construction. Likewise, the Book of Mormon's unusually explicit pre-Christian atonement teaching is textually certain and doctrinally significant. Whether that explicitness is ancient prophecy, modern composition, or partly a feature of English translation depends on wider evidence about the book's origin; it cannot be settled by these passages alone.

The canon strongly supports the claim that Christ's work includes several dimensions. It does not supply a technical, exhaustive account of how suffering transfers consequences, how divine foreknowledge relates to Christ's experience, or how justice is metaphysically “satisfied.” Those are theological questions prompted by the texts, not observations stated in full by them.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. What does “infinite” mean in 2 Nephi 9:7 and Alma 34:12—unlimited persons, unlimited duration, the divine identity of the agent, inexhaustible efficacy, or some combination?
2. How are [[Gethsemane]], the [[Crucifixion of Jesus Christ]], and the [[Resurrection of Jesus]] related within one atonement without allowing one event to eclipse the others?
3. Does Christ bear punishment itself, the destructive consequences of sin, humanity's estrangement and mortality, or all of these in different senses?
4. In [[Alma 42]], are the “demands of justice” an external moral order, an expression of God's own perfectly consistent character, a pedagogical metaphor, or some combination?
5. How does universal resurrection relate to conditional reconciliation and [[Eternal Life]]?
6. How should Alma 7:11-13 be related to Isaiah 53 and Matthew 8:17? Does “take upon him” describe substitution, complete participation in mortal suffering, healing identification, or more than one of these?
7. How are people who never possessed law or adequate knowledge included? Mosiah 3:11 and 2 Nephi 9:25-26 give principles, while [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]] pursues the wider question.
8. How should modern readers understand sacrificial blood imagery ethically and historically, especially if they find it alienating?
9. Can any single atonement theory account adequately for forgiveness, resurrection, victory, healing, covenant, justice, and transformation, or is a disciplined plurality of models preferable?
10. What historical weight, if any, should be placed on the Book of Mormon's explicit pre-Christian Christology apart from the larger evidence concerning its origin?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objections should be heard without dilution.

**Moral objection to substitution.** Punishing an innocent person instead of the guilty appears unjust, especially if God could forgive without violence. A response that merely says “justice demanded it” restates rather than resolves the problem. Scripturally, Alma 34:11 itself denies that an ordinary person's blood can answer for another's crime, and the canon also uses reconciliation, healing, victory, covenant, and resurrection rather than only transferred punishment. Defenders answer that Christ acts voluntarily and representatively, not as an unrelated third party, and that God in Christ bears the cost of reconciliation. Whether that fully answers the objection depends on the model being defended.

**Objection to sacrificial religion.** Animal and blood imagery may look like the projection of ancient ritual violence onto God. Historical scholarship confirms that these texts belong to an ancient sacrificial world. Yet Leviticus also depicts atonement as purification, restoration of sacred order, access to God's presence, and forgiveness—not merely divine appetite for death. Historical explanation can clarify the imagery without proving its revelation or moral authority.

**Objection from canonical diversity.** Scripture offers sacrifice, ransom, victory, reconciliation, justification, healing, priesthood, covenant, and transformation, but never one fully systematized mechanism. This genuinely challenges claims that one later theory is simply “the biblical doctrine.” A multidimensional reading fits the evidence better, although it may leave readers wanting greater metaphysical precision.

**Objection from conditions.** If the atonement is infinite and Christ suffers for all, requirements such as repentance, obedience, and endurance can seem to make grace only partially effective. The texts distinguish provision from reception and universal resurrection from conditional eternal life, but traditions disagree over how much human response contributes and whether calling it a “condition” risks turning gift into wage.

**Historical-critical objection.** Isaiah 53 may not originally have referred exclusively to Jesus, and the Book of Mormon's detailed pre-Christian Christian language may reflect nineteenth-century theological concerns. Christian and Latter-day Saint readings answer through typology, prophetic foreknowledge, and continuing revelation. Those are coherent faith claims, but textual parallels alone do not compel the historical conclusions.

**Problem of suffering.** A doctrine that Christ understands every pain can console, but it does not itself explain why a loving God permits devastating suffering or why healing is uneven. [[Alma 7]] asserts Christ's capacity to succor; it does not provide a complete theodicy. The separate question [[Why does God allow suffering]] must not be treated as solved merely by repeating that Christ suffered too.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Scholarship agrees more readily on texts and historical development than on theological truth. Jacob Milgrom's *Leviticus 1–16* (Anchor Bible, 1991) influentially emphasizes purification of the sanctuary from Israel's accumulated impurities; Mary Douglas's *Leviticus as Literature* (1999) stresses the symbolic and social coherence of Levitical ritual. Such work cautions against reducing every occurrence of “atonement” to punishment transferred to an animal.

On Isaiah 53, historical-critical interpreters debate whether the servant is corporate Israel, a faithful remnant, a prophet-like individual, or a deliberately open figure. Christian interpretation reads the passage christologically because New Testament authors apply its language to Jesus. Matthew 8:17 is an explicit example, but its application to healing also complicates accounts concerned only with penal suffering.

Classic Christian models differ. Gustaf Aulén's *Christus Victor* (1931) foregrounds liberation from enslaving powers. Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo* develops satisfaction in a medieval framework of divine honor; Reformation traditions sharpened penal and forensic themes. Simon Gathercole's *Defending Substitution* (2015) argues that representative substitution is deeply rooted in early Christian texts. J. Denny Weaver's *The Nonviolent Atonement* (2001) objects that satisfaction and penal accounts can sacralize violence. Joel B. Green and Mark D. Baker's *Recovering the Scandal of the Cross* (2000) argues for attending to the New Testament's plurality of images. David M. Moffitt's *Atonement and the Logic of Resurrection in the Epistle to the Hebrews* (2011) argues that resurrection and heavenly presentation are integral to Hebrews' account, not a mere appendix to death.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation accepts the Book of Mormon and modern revelation as genuine disclosures, giving special weight to “infinite atonement,” premortal preparation, universal resurrection, accountability according to knowledge, Christ's succoring experience, and [[Suffering in Gethsemane]]. Critical scholarship treats those same Restoration texts as nineteenth-century sources whose language must be explained within Joseph Smith's environment. The deepest division is therefore not over whether the passages contain these ideas—they plainly do—but over revelation, authorship, historicity, and whether theological claims describe realities beyond the texts.

The most responsible synthesis does not sort all scholars simply into “believing” and “non-believing.” Scholars of varied commitments can agree on philology, ritual context, literary dependence, and doctrinal diversity while differing sharply on prophecy, miracle, resurrection, and canonical authority.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
A productive canonical sequence is:

1. Begin with [[Leviticus 16]], then Leviticus 4:20-35, 5:5-16, and 6:1-7. Track who or what receives atonement, what the priest does, and how cleansing, restitution, sacrifice, and forgiveness relate. Use [[Law of Moses]] and [[Sacrifice]] as guides, while respecting the index warning that these are keyword matches rather than direct names for Christ's saving work.
2. Read [[Isaiah 53]] closely. List what the servant bears, what others receive, and what the chapter leaves unidentified. Then compare Matthew 8:16-17 rather than assuming every later application was explicit in Isaiah's earliest setting.
3. Read Romans 5:6-21, noting reconciliation, grace, Adam, Christ, death, and life. Follow with Revelation 1:5-6 and Jacob 4:11.
4. Study [[2 Nephi 9]] for the Fall, bodily resurrection, judgment, death and hell, law, knowledge, justice, and the meaning of “infinite.” Connect it to [[Fall of Adam and Eve]], [[Resurrection]], and [[Final Judgment]].
5. Read [[Mosiah 3]] together with Mosiah 13:27-32 and 15:1-9. Ask why a divinely given law still cannot save independently of Christ, and compare Mosiah 3:16-18 with Moroni 8:8-20 and [[Infant Baptism (rejected doctrine)]].
6. Read [[Alma 7]] for the atonement's relation to suffering and succor, then [[Alma 36]] as a lived conversion narrative rather than an abstract theory.
7. Read [[Alma 34]] for necessity, infinity, sacrifice, mercy, and urgent repentance. Then read [[Alma 42]] in the full Corianton sequence of Alma 39-42 so that justice and mercy remain pastoral rather than merely mechanical. Link the result with [[Chastity]], [[Repentance]], and [[Justice and Mercy]].
8. Read [[D&C 19]] beside the Gospel passion narratives, keeping [[Gethsemane]], [[Crucifixion of Jesus Christ]], and [[Resurrection of Jesus]] together.
9. Finish with Moroni 7:40-48. Test whether the resulting doctrine produces the passage's named fruits: [[Faith]], [[Hope]], and [[Charity]].
10. Only after this textual work compare later models—Christus Victor, satisfaction, penal substitution, moral influence, healing, participation, and covenant renewal. Ask what each explains well, what it neglects, and whether it claims more exclusivity than the canon warrants.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The canon portrays the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] as God's saving action through Jesus Christ that answers sin, estrangement, death, judgment, suffering, and humanity's inability to restore itself. Its scriptural roots lie in Israel's rites of purification, forgiveness, restitution, mediation, and restored sacred order. [[Isaiah 53]] contributes the servant who bears what is not his own so that others may be healed and justified. The New Testament applies that pattern to Jesus and speaks additionally of reconciliation, ransom, covenant, victory, priesthood, cleansing, death, and resurrection.

Restoration scripture makes the doctrine unusually explicit. [[2 Nephi 9]] calls the atonement infinite and connects it with escape from death and hell. [[Mosiah 3]] places it behind the law of Moses, protects children and the unknowingly accountable, and describes Spirit-enabled transformation. [[Alma 7]] includes pains, sicknesses, infirmities, death, sin, and Christ's capacity to succor. [[Alma 34]] joins infinite sacrifice to faith and repentance. [[Alma 42]] insists that mercy does not abolish justice. [[D&C 19]] gives a first-person account of Christ's suffering. [[Moroni 7]] makes atonement and resurrection the ground of concrete hope for eternal life.

What is firmly established by the texts is a multidimensional portrait: forgiveness, reconciliation, resurrection, cleansing, mercy, healing, covenant access, moral repair, and transformation all belong. What is not established is that one later explanatory theory exhausts the doctrine. Penal, satisfaction, victory, healing, participatory, and moral-influence models each illuminate part of the record and can distort it when made exclusive.

The doctrine does not trivialize wrongdoing. Leviticus requires restitution; Alma requires repentance; mercy claims the penitent rather than declaring justice unreal. Nor does it promise that mortal suffering becomes intelligible merely because Christ shares it. Its central faith claim is instead that God in Christ enters the human condition, bears and defeats its estranging powers, opens resurrection and reconciliation, and can transform those who receive him. The texts establish that this is what their authors proclaim. Whether the proclamation corresponds to transcendent reality finally depends on judgments about Christ, resurrection, revelation, and the authority of the canon—judgments for which textual study supplies essential evidence but not coercive proof.
