# Gospel of Jesus Christ

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, *gospel* means good news, but its exact scope depends on context. Mark announces the good news that God's kingdom is at hand and calls hearers to repent and believe (Mark 1:14-15). Paul uses the term for God's saving message concerning Jesus Christ—his promised Davidic identity, death for sins, resurrection, and saving power received through faith (Romans 1:1-17; 1 Corinthians 15:1-8). The Book of Mormon's “doctrine of Christ” describes how people enter and remain in that salvation through faith, repentance, baptism, reception of the Holy Ghost, and endurance (2 Nephi 31:2-21; 3 Nephi 11:31-40). In 3 Nephi 27:13-21, “my gospel” embraces both Christ's saving acts and the hearer's covenantal response.

Contemporary Latter-day Saint teaching often uses *gospel of Jesus Christ* and *doctrine of Christ* synonymously for this Christ-centered covenantal way; see [[“This Is My Gospel”—“This Is My Church” (Elder Dale G. Renlund, October 2024)]] and [[The Powerful, Virtuous Cycle of the Doctrine of Christ (Elder Dale G. Renlund, April 2024)]]. Official reference works also use *gospel* more broadly for the Father's plan of happiness centered in the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]; see [[Gospel (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Gospel (True to the Faith)]]. These uses overlap but should not be flattened into one lexical formula. The gospel is at once news about what God has done in [[Jesus Christ]], an invitation to covenant discipleship, and the divine saving order within which that discipleship occurs.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon's center is Jesus Christ, not an abstract program. Receiving his gospel is receiving him (D&C 39:5). Its indicative comes before its imperative: Christ came to do the Father's will, was lifted up, died for sins, was buried, rose again, and will draw humanity to judgment (1 Corinthians 15:3-8; 3 Nephi 27:13-15). Its invitation is to exercise [[Faith]], turn through [[Repentance]], receive [[Baptism]], receive the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], and continue in [[Endurance]] (2 Nephi 31:5-21; 3 Nephi 11:31-40; D&C 39:6).

This pattern is neither salvation earned by a checklist nor assent without transformation. Romans calls the gospel God's saving power received through faith (Romans 1:16-17); Third Nephi joins repentance and baptism to sanctification by the Holy Ghost (3 Nephi 27:19-20); Second Nephi describes a lifelong course nourished by Christ's word and love (2 Nephi 31:18-20). [[Grace]], [[Covenants]], and [[Ordinances]] belong together because every commanded response depends on Christ's prior saving work and divine power.

The gospel creates a people. Christ's church must bear his name and be built upon his gospel (3 Nephi 27:8-11). Galatians shows that “the truth of the gospel” governs communal conduct: Peter's withdrawal from Gentile fellowship contradicted that truth (Galatians 2:11-16). Mission therefore crosses ethnic boundaries without erasing Israel's covenantal place (Romans 1:16; Romans 11:28-29). In Latter-day Saint scripture its reach also crosses death: those without an adequate mortal opportunity may be taught in the spirit world (D&C 137:7-9; D&C 138:30-37), connecting the gospel with [[Death and the Spirit World]] and [[Baptism for the Dead]].

Official Latter-day Saint teaching identifies the central doctrine as Christ's Atonement and commonly lists faith in Christ, repentance, baptism, the Holy Ghost, and enduring to the end as the doctrine's principal elements; see [[Gospel (Gospel Topics)]], [[Gospel (True to the Faith)]], and [[Trusting the Doctrine of Christ (Elder Evan A. Schmutz, April 2023)]]. That official synthesis is consistent with the Restoration canon, while remaining broader than every individual biblical use of the word.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
Within the Latter-day Saint canon, the gospel is transdispensational. Moses 8:19 says Noah received the gospel already given to [[Enoch]]. This is the Pearl of Great Price's theological history; the surviving ancient Genesis text does not independently use developed Christian gospel language for Noah.

Israel's scriptures supply the vocabulary of good tidings: Isaiah's herald announces peace, salvation, and God's reign (Isaiah 52:7). Paul later applies this herald imagery to commissioned Christian preaching (Romans 10:14-17). In the New Testament era, Jesus proclaims the arrival of the [[Kingdom of God]] (Mark 1:14-15), and after his death the apostles announce his crucifixion and [[Resurrection of Jesus]] as saving events (1 Corinthians 15:1-8). Paul argues that Gentiles need not become Jews through circumcision to share the gospel, while continuing to affirm Israel's covenantal priority (Galatians 2:1-16; Romans 1:16; Romans 11:28-29).

The Book of Mormon presents the doctrine with unusual procedural clarity. [[2 Nephi 31]] places Christ's baptismal example, the Father's and Son's commands, the Holy Ghost, and endurance within one saving way. [[3 Nephi 11]] gives the risen Christ's concise declaration, and [[3 Nephi 27]] joins his saving acts to the church's identity and the disciple's response. Mormon says the Nephite record and the record from the Jews will function as complementary witnesses that Jesus is the Christ (Mormon 3:21; Mormon 7:8-9).

The Latter-day Saint historical claim is that apostolic authority and gospel fulness required restoration through [[Joseph Smith Jr]]; see [[Joseph Smith (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Restoration of the Gospel]]. Doctrine and Covenants 39:11 calls the latter-day gospel a covenant sent forth to recover Israel, while Joseph Smith—History 1:69 associates restored [[Aaronic Priesthood]] authority with the gospel of repentance and baptism. Doctrine and Covenants 138 later expanded the articulated reach of missionary work by describing an organized proclamation among the dead. These are authoritative claims inside the tradition; whether the Restoration represents divine recovery, theological development, or nineteenth-century innovation is a larger historical and religious judgment not settled merely by noting canonical continuity.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence here is textual and comparative, not archaeological. Several corpora with different settings converge on a recognizable center: God's saving action in Christ and a summons to believing, repentant, Spirit-filled allegiance (Mark 1:14-15; Romans 1:1-17; 1 Corinthians 15:1-8; 2 Nephi 31:2-21; 3 Nephi 27:13-21; D&C 39:5-6). This strongly establishes what these texts teach. It does not by itself establish their supernatural claims or make their formulations identical.

Within the New Testament, 1 Corinthians 15:3-8 is especially important. Paul's language of receiving and delivering indicates inherited tradition, and a wide range of scholars regard its basic death-and-resurrection formulation as earlier than the letter. That is strong evidence that resurrection belief was early rather than a much later Christian invention. It remains evidence for early testimony, not a laboratory demonstration of resurrection; [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]] requires a larger historical assessment.

The Restoration canon exhibits substantial internal coherence. The gospel is projected backward to Enoch and Noah (Moses 8:19), explained by the risen Christ in the Book of Mormon (3 Nephi 11:31-40; 3 Nephi 27:13-21), associated with restored covenant and authority (D&C 39:11), and extended to the dead (D&C 138:30-37). This is strong evidence for a deliberate, integrated Latter-day Saint theology and weak-to-moderate evidence, standing alone, for the claimed ancient history. A critical model can explain the same coherence through composition, harmonization, and nineteenth-century restorationist concerns.

The reading also surfaced consequential links. Romans 10 reuses Isaiah 52:7, Isaiah 53:1, Psalm 19:4, and Deuteronomy 32:21 in an argument about proclamation, rejection, and Gentile reception; these links show Paul reading Israel's scriptures christologically and missionally. Galatians 2 demonstrates that gospel claims affected ethnic boundaries and shared meals. Philippians 4:2-3 supplies strong textual evidence that women labored materially in Paul's gospel mission. Doctrine and Covenants 137–138 supplies the Latter-day Saint answer to unequal access to the gospel. None of these observations independently proves that every institutional or doctrinal application made from them is correct.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Several questions remain productive rather than merely troublesome:

- How should proclamation and process be related? Paul can summarize the gospel through Christ's death and resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:1-8), while Second Nephi emphasizes the covenantal path (2 Nephi 31:2-21). Are these competing definitions, or the saving event and the response it creates?

- What does “fulness” add? D&C 39:11 speaks of the fulness of the latter-day gospel, and D&C 76:14 associates a vision of postmortal glory with the fulness. Does *fulness* denote complete saving authority and ordinances, a broader body of revealed truth, or both?

- How does the gospel relate to the church? Third Nephi makes being built on Christ's gospel a condition of Christ's church (3 Nephi 27:8-11). This supports more than nominal Christianity, but it does not by itself answer [[How can there be one true church among so many]].

- What is the relation of faith, works, grace, covenant, and ordinances? Romans stresses faith and divine power; Restoration texts command baptism and endurance. Careful study must avoid both earning salvation and reducing covenant obedience to optional symbolism.

- How much continuity exists across dispensations? Moses 8:19 explicitly claims continuity with Enoch and Noah, but Genesis does not preserve the same terminology. Students must distinguish the canon's claim from independent historical corroboration.

- What opportunity constitutes a fair reception or rejection of the gospel? D&C 137:7-9 and [[Vision of the redemption of the dead]] address those without knowledge, but questions remain about agency, postmortal conditions, and proxy ordinances; see [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]].

- How should culturally conditioned applications be identified? Galatians 2 shows Paul treating Gentile inclusion as gospel truth, while Christian communities still disagree about which current social and institutional questions are similarly intrinsic to the gospel.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest semantic objection is that Latter-day Saint teaching sometimes turns the gospel from news about Christ into a sequence of human actions. In the New Testament, *euangelion* often denotes proclamation—God's kingdom and the death and resurrection of Christ—not a five-step formula. This objection correctly warns against making Christ's saving act secondary. The best canonical response is not to deny the difference in emphasis: 3 Nephi 27:13-21 itself begins with Christ's work and then gives the covenantal response, while 2 Nephi 31 repeatedly grounds obedience in Christ's example, promise, and Spirit. Event and response can be distinguished without being separated.

A historical objection is that Jesus preached the kingdom, whereas Paul preached the crucified and risen Jesus; on this account Paul transformed Jesus' message into a religion about Jesus. The difference in emphasis is real. Yet the canonical narratives connect them through Jesus' predictions and interpretation of his death, the Last Supper, resurrection testimony, and the claim that God's reign arrives through the Messiah. Whether this continuity reflects Jesus' own teaching or later Christian interpretation is a historical question considered more fully by [[Did Paul invent Christianity]] and [[Why do the Gospels differ from one another]].

A critical objection to the Book of Mormon and Pearl of Great Price is that their unusually explicit Christian gospel language before and outside the New Testament is anachronistic. From this perspective, Moses 8 and 2 Nephi 31 reflect nineteenth-century Christian debates about faith, baptism, the Holy Ghost, authority, and restoration. Believing interpretation attributes the explicitness to revelation and lost or renewed dispensational knowledge. Internal consistency cannot adjudicate between those origins; relevant larger questions include [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]] and [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]].

The restoration claim can also appear circular: Restoration scripture declares that a fulness was restored, and the restored institution cites that scripture as authority. Believers add historical witness claims, revelatory experience, priesthood narratives, and the fruits of practice; critics argue that these remain insider evidence susceptible to natural explanations. The dossier should therefore state restoration as official doctrine rather than as a result established by lexical comparison alone.

Finally, a gospel centered on exclusive salvation through Christ faces the problem of unequal access and religious diversity. Doctrine and Covenants 137–138 gives a morally serious internal answer through divine judgment of intent and postmortal teaching, but the model depends on accepting additional revelation. It does not neutralize broader objections about divine hiddenness or competing religious experiences; see [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]] and [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]].
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
New Testament scholarship broadly agrees that *euangelion* means good news or proclamation, but debates how its Jewish scriptural, Greco-Roman, political, and theological resonances should be weighted. Isaiah's announcements of God's return and reign provide an important Jewish background, visibly used in Romans 10:15. Some scholars also note that proclamations of imperial victory or accession formed part of the wider Roman environment. The presence of such resonance does not prove that every gospel occurrence is a direct anti-imperial slogan.

Scholars commonly recognize 1 Corinthians 15:3-8 as containing tradition Paul received, though they debate the formula's precise extent, date, and implications. Believing scholars may regard its early witness list as significant historical support for resurrection; skeptical scholars grant early belief while explaining the experiences through visionary, psychological, legendary, or social processes. Early testimony narrows some explanations but does not eliminate disagreement over miracle claims.

Pauline scholarship also disputes the organization of salvation language. Reformation readings have often foregrounded individual justification by faith. The “new perspective on Paul,” represented in different ways by E. P. Sanders and James D. G. Dunn, emphasizes covenant, Jewish identity markers, and Gentile inclusion; N. T. Wright places the gospel within Israel's story and the announcement of Jesus' lordship. These approaches are not interchangeable, and no scholarly consensus reduces Romans 1:17's “righteousness of God” to a single uncontested meaning.

Latter-day Saint scholarship, including work by Noel B. Reynolds on 2 Nephi 31, often argues that the doctrine of Christ is the Book of Mormon's recurring theological core. Contemporary official expositions such as [[The Powerful, Virtuous Cycle of the Doctrine of Christ (Elder Dale G. Renlund, April 2024)]] emphasize repeated lifelong transformation rather than a one-time checklist. Critical scholarship can acknowledge that literary centrality while disputing ancient provenance and reading the formulation within early American restorationism. Thus believing and critical readers can agree closely about what the text emphasizes while disagreeing sharply about why the text exists and what authority it has.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with Jesus' proclamation in [[Mark 1]] and ask what is being announced before listing required responses (Mark 1:1, 14-15).

2. Read [[1 Corinthians 15]] for Paul's compact account of the message he received and transmitted (1 Corinthians 15:1-8, 12-28). Then read [[Romans 1]] for prophetic promise, Christ's identity, saving power, faith, and God's righteousness (Romans 1:1-17).

3. Follow the gospel into community and mission through [[Galatians 2]] and [[Romans 10]]. Note how Gentile inclusion, table fellowship, scriptural interpretation, hearing, and authorized sending become gospel questions (Galatians 2:1-16; Romans 10:8-21).

4. Study the Book of Mormon's extended definitions in [[2 Nephi 31]], [[3 Nephi 11]], and [[3 Nephi 27]]. Make two columns: what Christ has done, and how disciples respond. This prevents either grace or covenant practice from swallowing the other.

5. Compare the concise Restoration formulation in [[D&C 39]] with those Book of Mormon passages (D&C 39:5-6, 11, 18). Then examine how priesthood, church, ordinances, and the [[Gathering of Israel]] enter the restored-gospel claim.

6. Extend the study beyond mortality through D&C 137:7-9 and [[D&C 138]], alongside 1 Peter 3:18-20 and 1 Peter 4:6. Continue into [[Death and the Spirit World]], [[Baptism for the Dead]], and [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]].

7. Compare the resulting textual map with the official syntheses in [[Gospel (Gospel Topics)]], [[Gospel (True to the Faith)]], [[Trusting the Doctrine of Christ (Elder Evan A. Schmutz, April 2023)]], and [[“This Is My Gospel”—“This Is My Church” (Elder Dale G. Renlund, October 2024)]]. Mark which statements are direct scriptural wording, which are institutional synthesis, and which are your own interpretation.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The gospel of Jesus Christ is the good news that God acts to save and reign through [[Jesus Christ]]. Its irreducible center is Christ himself—his identity, atoning death, resurrection, authority, and promised judgment and life. The canonical response is trusting allegiance expressed through [[Faith]], [[Repentance]], [[Baptism]], reception of the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], and faithful endurance. In the Restoration canon, this is commonly called the doctrine of Christ.

No single verse supplies the whole synthesis. Mark foregrounds the approaching [[Kingdom of God]]; Paul foregrounds Christ's death, resurrection, faith, and Jew-Gentile inclusion; [[2 Nephi 31]] foregrounds the covenantal path; [[3 Nephi 27]] most clearly holds saving event and required response together; [[D&C 39]] joins the gospel to Christ, ordinances, the Spirit, covenant, and Israel's recovery. Doctrine and Covenants 137–138 extends its offer to those who lacked a fair mortal opportunity.

The strongest conclusion is textual: these writings consistently place salvation in Christ and call for transformed, covenantal discipleship empowered by the Holy Ghost. Their differences are real but need not be contradictions; “gospel” has a stable Christ-centered core and a flexible contextual scope. The evidence strongly establishes the texts' teaching and the early existence of resurrection proclamation. It does not, without further historical and spiritual argument, prove miraculous events, Book of Mormon antiquity, priesthood restoration, or exclusive institutional authority.

A faithful reading can therefore be confident without pretending every question is closed. The gospel is neither bare information nor a human achievement system. It is news that creates a way of life, a covenant people, a mission to all nations, and—within Latter-day Saint revelation—hope for the living and the dead. Its test in 3 Nephi is not Christ's name alone but whether a church and its works are actually built upon his gospel (3 Nephi 27:8-11).
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
