# Agency

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In the canon, agency is the God-given capacity and permission to act and choose under morally significant conditions. Its clearest vocabulary appears in [[Moses 4]], [[D&C 58]], and D&C 101:78; [[2 Nephi 2]] and [[Helaman 14]] supply the fuller conceptual setting. That setting includes opposed alternatives, enticement, law, knowledge of good and evil, power to act, and consequences (2 Nephi 2:11-16, 26-27; Helaman 14:30-31).

Agency is therefore not mere preference, political liberty, or freedom from all constraint. Nor does the canon define it as freedom from consequences. Lehi instead locates freedom within embodiment, the Fall, law, temptation, and redemption through [[Jesus Christ]] (2 Nephi 2:26-27).

Modern Latter-day Saint teaching generally prefers **moral agency** to the once-common phrase **free agency**. [[These Things I Know (President Boyd K. Packer, April 2013)]], [[Moral Discipline (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2009)]], and [[Be One with Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2024)]] describe it as both the power to choose and the obligation to answer for choices. This is an official modern synthesis; it should not be mistaken for the exact conceptual vocabulary of every biblical writer.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The Latter-day Saint canonical synthesis teaches that agency belongs to God's purpose for humanity and existed in [[Premortal Life]]. Satan rebelled, sought God's honor, and attempted to destroy human agency (Moses 4:1-4); other spirits turned away through their agency (D&C 29:36-38). The texts do not reveal the psychology of that choice or precisely how Satan proposed to eliminate agency.

Mortal agency is situated rather than absolute. [[2 Nephi 2]] joins it to [[Creation]], opposition, the [[Fall of Adam and Eve]], law, enticement, knowledge, embodiment, and Christ's redemption. Humanity becomes free to act because the Messiah redeems people from the Fall (2 Nephi 2:26). The permitted choices have consequences: liberty and eternal life through the Mediator stand against captivity and death (2 Nephi 2:27), while Helaman 14:30-31 emphasizes personal accountability.

Agency includes constructive initiative. [[D&C 58]] teaches that disciples should not wait to be commanded in everything but should voluntarily bring about righteousness (D&C 58:26-28). It is thus more than selecting among options; it includes becoming an effective moral actor.

Official teaching links agency to accountability, repentance, and judgment, not to unaided self-salvation. Philippians 2:12-13 places human action beside God's work in willing and doing. [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Grace]], [[Repentance]], [[Justice and Mercy]], and [[Final Judgment]] therefore belong inside the doctrine, not outside it. The canon does not reduce this relationship to a settled philosophical formula.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The Hebrew Bible frequently commands obedience and portrays accountable human decisions, yet it also attributes decisive historical action to God. Pharaoh's hardening (Exodus 4:21; 14:4, 8, 17), Joseph's account of divine and human intentions (Genesis 50:20), and the lament of Isaiah 63:17 preserve tensions that later theories of free will attempt to resolve.

The New Testament likewise places response and divine initiative together. Philippians 2:12-13 commands action while crediting God with work in human willing; Romans 8:29-30 and 9:15-18 strongly emphasize divine purpose and mercy. These writings predate the later technical disputes associated with [[Augustine of Hippo]], [[Martin Luther]], libertarianism, and compatibilism.

Restoration scripture makes agency unusually explicit and integrates it across eras. [[2 Nephi 2]] gives a sustained Fall-and-redemption account; [[Moses 4]] and D&C 29 extend agency into [[Premortal Life]]; [[D&C 58]] emphasizes voluntary righteous initiative; D&C 101:78 uses the expression "moral agency." Alma 13:3-5 places foreknowledge and premortal preparation beside agency, supporting the later distinction between foreordination and guaranteed outcomes.

Later Latter-day Saint discourse often used **free agency**, as represented in [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]] and [[Gospel Doctrine]]. Recent teaching more often stresses **moral agency**, accountability, and disciplined action: see [[Moral Discipline (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2009)]], [[These Things I Know (President Boyd K. Packer, April 2013)]], [[“The Principles of My Gospel” (Elder David A. Bednar, April 2021)]], and [[Be One with Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2024)]]. This is development in emphasis and terminology, not evidence that one exhaustive metaphysical definition has been officially revealed.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence is textual, not experimental.

- **Strong:** [[2 Nephi 2]] is a sustained argument joining opposition, law, choice, Fall, posterity, redemption, and joy (2 Nephi 2:11-27). This establishes literary and theological integration within the chapter.

- **Strong:** [[Moses 4]], D&C 29, [[D&C 58]], D&C 101, and [[Helaman 14]] independently use explicit agency, freedom, action, and accountability language. Together they give the Latter-day Saint synthesis substantial canonical coherence.

- **Strong observation, disputed interpretation:** Biblical hardening, predestination, divine enablement, and command passages place divine initiative beside human responsibility (Exodus 4:21; 14:4, 8, 17; Ezekiel 36:26-27; Romans 8:29-30; 9:15-18; Philippians 2:12-13). The tension is demonstrably present; its philosophical resolution is not.

- **Moderate:** The recurrence of agency before and during mortality supports a unified Latter-day Saint account of [[Plan of Salvation]]. However, not every component is equally explicit or equally early in every scriptural collection, and 2 Nephi 2 itself does not use the phrase "plan of salvation."

- **Weak for larger conclusions:** Literary coherence does not independently prove revelation, ancient authorship, or a particular account of creation. Nor can scriptural exhortations alone demonstrate libertarian free will as a psychological or metaphysical fact.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What minimum degree of knowledge and practical control is required for accountability? Alma 29:5 supports differentiated accountability, but it does not supply a clinical or legal test.

- How do divine foreknowledge, foreordination, prophecy, and intervention relate to alternatives genuinely available to a person? Alma 13:3-5 resists simple fatalism without explaining the metaphysics.

- How should divine hardening in Exodus and Romans be reconciled with judgment? The texts preserve the tension more clearly than they resolve it.

- Can agency be severely impaired without being wholly lost? [[Addiction (Gospel Topics)]], trauma, coercion, mental illness, ignorance, and entrenched habit make binary language inadequate for many individual cases.

- What exactly did Satan propose in [[Premortal Life]]? Moses 4:1-4 states the promised outcome, demand for honor, rebellion, and attempted destruction of agency, but not the mechanism.

- Does God's command structure present genuinely open alternatives, or can responsibility coexist with divine determination? Scripture contains material used by both libertarian and compatibilist readers.

- How do unequal mortal opportunities fit claims of just accountability? The doctrine points toward differentiated judgment and Christ's redemption, but the indexed passages do not explain every unequal circumstance.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest philosophical objection is that divine foreknowledge, foreordination, prophecy, and causal governance appear incompatible with genuinely open alternatives. Exodus intensifies the problem by saying that God hardens Pharaoh before and during the confrontation (Exodus 4:21; 14:4, 8, 17). Romans 9:15-18 similarly foregrounds divine mercy and hardening. Merely repeating that people are accountable does not solve the causal problem.

A second objection is empirical: choices are conditioned by genetics, development, trauma, addiction, social power, ignorance, and unconscious processes. A simplistic picture of an isolated chooser can become pastorally cruel. Canonical references to knowledge, enticement, captivity, and differentiated accountability partly accommodate this concern, but they do not provide a modern psychology of volition.

A historical-critical objection is that the doctrine called [[Agency]] is a harmonized Restoration synthesis rather than one uniform doctrine taught identically throughout the Bible. That criticism has force: biblical texts use varied idioms and sometimes emphasize divine sovereignty more than autonomous choice. Canonical coherence is real at the level of Latter-day Saint theology, but historical identity among all authors has not been established.

Another objection challenges the premortal narrative: Moses 4 does not explain how Satan would destroy agency. Familiar assertions that his plan relied on coercion or eliminated all consequences may be reasonable inferences, but presenting them as quoted revelation overstates the text.

A further internal-canon tension, distinct from the sovereignty objection above: Doctrine and Covenants 93:29-30 teaches that "intelligence, or the light of truth, was not created or made, neither indeed can be," and that it "act[s] for itself... otherwise there is no existence." Some Latter-day Saint theologians read this as implying that agency, at some level, is co-eternal with God rather than a capacity God simply bestowed — in tension with language such as Moses 4:3, which describes agency as something God "had given" man. The canon does not explain how these two ways of speaking about agency's origin relate to each other.

Finally, describing agency as a "gift" can obscure coercive circumstances and unequal options. The strongest Latter-day Saint response appeals to proportionate accountability, redemption, and divine judgment informed by complete knowledge. That is a theological answer grounded in trust in God; the indexed texts do not make it an independently testable resolution.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Discussion divides along more than a believing/non-believing line. Philosophers distinguish libertarian freedom, compatibilism, hard determinism, and revisionist accounts of responsibility; *Four Views on Free Will* by John Martin Fischer, Robert Kane, Derk Pereboom, and Manuel Vargas (2007) is a useful map. Those are later analytic categories, and no single proof text settles among them.

Biblical scholarship generally reads choice, hardening, election, and divine action first within each document's literary and historical setting. Brevard S. Childs, *The Book of Exodus* (1974), treats Pharaoh's hardening as a central theological feature rather than explaining it away. Pauline interpreters disagree sharply over individual or corporate election and the scope of Romans 9; the existence of that disagreement cautions against claiming that Romans alone resolves agency.

Believing Latter-day Saint scholarship usually gives agency a robust premortal and mortal role. Sterling M. McMurrin's mid-twentieth-century philosophical studies of Mormon theology, Blake T. Ostler's "The Mormon Concept of God" in *Dialogue* 17, no. 2 (1984), and Terryl Givens's *Wrestling the Angel* (2015) discuss a theology in which real creaturely action is important to eternal development. They differ in method and should not be treated as official declarations.

Critical or historical scholarship can accept that Restoration texts form a coherent agency theology while explaining that coherence through nineteenth-century composition and theological development rather than ancient transmission or revelation. Believing scholarship may accept development while adding revelatory continuity. The textual pattern alone does not adjudicate those larger origin claims.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with [[2 Nephi 2]]. Trace the argument from law and opposition through enticement, Fall, redemption, freedom, and consequence (2 Nephi 2:5, 11-16, 22-27). Do not isolate verse 27 from verse 26's claim that freedom depends on the Messiah.

2. Read [[Moses 3]] and [[Moses 4]] together. Distinguish the genuine command and preserved choice in Moses 3:16-17 from the later account of Satan's proposal and rebellion in Moses 4:1-4.

3. Add D&C 29:36-44 and [[Premortal Life]]. Mark what the texts explicitly disclose and what later explanations infer.

4. Read [[Helaman 14]] alongside Alma 29:5. Compare freedom, knowledge, desire, consequence, and blameless ignorance.

5. Study [[D&C 58]] and D&C 101:78. Ask how initiative, law, accountability, and being an agent differ from simply having options.

6. Test the synthesis against difficult texts rather than only supportive ones: Exodus 4:21 and 14:4-17; Genesis 50:20; Isaiah 63:17; Ezekiel 36:26-27; Romans 8:29-30 and 9:15-18; Philippians 2:12-13.

7. Read modern interpretations in chronological conversation: [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]], [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[Moral Discipline (Elder D. Todd Christofferson, October 2009)]], [[These Things I Know (President Boyd K. Packer, April 2013)]], [[“The Principles of My Gospel” (Elder David A. Bednar, April 2021)]], and [[Be One with Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2024)]]. Note shifts from "free agency" toward "moral agency," disciplined action, and accountability.

8. Finish with limiting cases through [[Addiction (Gospel Topics)]], [[Satan and Temptation]], [[Grace]], [[Justice and Mercy]], and [[Final Judgment]]. Ask what is established by the canon and what remains philosophical or pastoral interpretation.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
[[Agency]] in Latter-day Saint scripture is real, morally significant, and inseparable from relationship with God. It is not depicted as unlimited autonomy. [[2 Nephi 2]] locates freedom amid opposition, law, embodiment, enticement, the Fall, and redemption; people become free to act because of the Messiah (2 Nephi 2:26). [[Helaman 14]] joins that freedom to knowledge and accountability, while [[D&C 58]] adds voluntary initiative—the capacity to bring about righteousness without being directed in every detail.

The doctrine reaches backward into [[Premortal Life]]. [[Moses 4]] says Satan sought to destroy human agency, and D&C 29 says heavenly beings turned away through agency. The canon establishes rebellion and meaningful premortal choice but does not disclose the mechanism of Satan's proposal or the exact psychology of premortal decision.

Agency also belongs inside, not outside, [[Plan of Salvation]]. [[Creation]], the [[Fall of Adam and Eve]], [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Grace]], [[Repentance]], [[Justice and Mercy]], and [[Final Judgment]] form its theological environment. Choice has consequences, yet salvation is not represented as unaided willpower. Philippians 2:12-13 places human action and God's enabling work side by side.

The honest limit is important. Scripture strongly supports accountable action, but it does not deliver a complete theory of free will. Divine hardening, foreknowledge, election, satanic influence, addiction, ignorance, and unequal circumstances complicate any simple model. Libertarian and compatibilist readings can each appeal to real textual data; neither may dismiss the passages that resist it.

What is established within the canon is a coherent summons: persons are addressed as agents who can respond, initiate good, repent, and become accountable before God. What remains open is how that agency operates metaphysically and how responsibility is measured under every form of impairment. Trust that Christ's redemption and God's judgment are adequate to those complexities is a doctrinal commitment of faith, not a conclusion independently demonstrated by the agency passages alone.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
