# Resurrection

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In the scriptural usage most relevant here, resurrection is the raising of the dead into an immortal embodied condition. It is not merely continued conscious existence after death: [[Alma 11]] and [[Alma 40]] distinguish the separated condition of spirit and body from their subsequent reunion. It is also not simply resuscitation to ordinary mortal life. Paul describes what is raised as incorruptible and immortal in [[1 Corinthians 15]].

Latter-day Saint usage normally distinguishes **immortality**, the universal conquest of physical death through [[Resurrection of Jesus]], from **eternal life**, a saving relationship with God and a favorable final inheritance. That distinction prevents Alma 11:41 and 2 Nephi 9:22, which say all rise, from being misread as saying all receive an identical judgment or glory. See [[Eternal Life]], [[Final Judgment]], and [[Plan of Salvation]].

The term can also appear metaphorically, as when baptism signifies walking in newness of life in Romans 6:4. Such moral rebirth should not be conflated with the future raising of the dead that Paul defends in [[1 Corinthians 15]].
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon's central claims are these:

- Jesus Christ truly rose from the dead; Christian preaching and hope depend upon that claim ([[John 20]]; [[1 Corinthians 15]]).
- Because Christ broke the bands of death, every person will be raised, both just and unjust (2 Nephi 9:22; Alma 11:41; John 5:28–29; D&C 76:16–17).
- Resurrection reunites spirit and body in an immortal, incorruptible condition (Alma 11:42–45; Alma 40:21–23).
- Death does not immediately equal resurrection. [[Alma 40]] and [[D&C 138]] place an intermediate [[Death and the Spirit World]] between mortality and resurrection.
- Resurrection is followed by presentation before God and [[Final Judgment]]. Universality of resurrection does not erase moral agency or differentiated outcomes (Mosiah 16:10–11; Alma 11:41–44).
- Christ is the firstfruits and the ground of humanity's resurrection; Paul makes this part of his explicit [[Adam]]–Christ comparison (1 Corinthians 15:20–23, 45).

Restoration scripture adds teachings about differentiated resurrections and [[Kingdoms of Glory]] in [[D&C 76]], preaching among the dead in [[D&C 138]], continuity of acquired intelligence in [[D&C 130]], and the possible continuation of a divinely sealed covenant in [[D&C 132]]. These additions should be stated with their qualifications. In particular, D&C 132's teaching about [[Eternal Marriage]] stands in prima facie tension with Matthew 22:30, Mark 12:25, and Luke 20:35. The familiar distinction between no new marriage in the resurrection and continuation of an earlier sealing is a reconciliation, not an explanation supplied explicitly by the Synoptic passages.

Devotional works such as [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[Discourses of Brigham Young]], [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]], and [[A Compendium of the Doctrines of the Gospel]] witness to the doctrine's importance in Latter-day Saint teaching history. They are not interchangeable with canon, and statements within them must be weighed according to genre, authorship, and later authoritative teaching.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The Hebrew Bible contains several different kinds of language about restoration from death. Ezekiel's dry bones primarily symbolize national Israel's restoration in the chapter's own explanation, while [[D&C 138]] also applies the vision to bodily resurrection. Those readings need not be silently conflated. Daniel 12:2 is among the clearest biblical statements of a future resurrection with differentiated outcomes.

By the New Testament period, resurrection was a live Jewish dispute. The Sadducees denied it, while Jesus defended it from God's covenantal identity as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob ([[Matthew 22]], [[Mark 12]], [[Luke 20]]). The seven-brothers case uses the law of levirate [[Marriage]] as a reductio against resurrection.

The earliest surviving Christian writings make [[Resurrection of Jesus]] foundational. Paul reports received traditions of appearances and argues that if Christ did not rise, Christian preaching, forgiveness, and hope are empty ([[1 Corinthians 15]]). The Gospels later narrate the empty tomb and appearances with both substantial convergence and real differences. [[John 20]] emphasizes encounters involving [[Mary Magdalene]] and [[Thomas (apostle)]]. [[Acts 2]] portrays [[Peter (apostle)]] publicly grounding Jesus's lordship in resurrection.

Within the Book of Mormon's own claimed narrative chronology, prophets teach a remarkably explicit resurrection doctrine before Christ: Jacob connects it with the Fall and judgment in [[2 Nephi 9]], Abinadi with Christ's victory in [[Mosiah 15]] and [[Mosiah 16]], and Amulek and Alma with universal bodily restoration in [[Alma 11]], [[Alma 12]], and [[Alma 40]]. Calling these texts independent ancient evidence, however, depends on the separately disputed historicity of the [[Book of Mormon (Gospel Topic)]].

Restoration revelation further systematized the intermediate state and differentiated resurrection. [[D&C 76]] links resurrection categories with final inheritances; [[D&C 138]] records Joseph F. Smith's vision of redemption among the dead. Historical Latter-day Saint treatments include [[Conference Report, October 1899 (General Conference (full report), October 1899)]], [[Conference Report, April 1921 (General Conference (full report), April 1921)]], and [[Gospel Doctrine]]. Recent devotional witnesses include [[That They Do Always Remember Him (Elder Claudio R. M. Costa, October 2015)]], [[And There Shall Be No More Death (Elder Paul V. Johnson, April 2016)]], and [[And We Talk of Christ (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, April 2025)]]. These illustrate reception and emphasis; their devotional authority should remain distinct from textual observation.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest historical observation is not that resurrection has been experimentally demonstrated, but that belief in Jesus's resurrection appeared very early and was central rather than decorative. In 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 Paul passes on a received formulation naming death, burial, resurrection, and appearances; he also includes himself among recipients of an appearance. This strongly supports early testimony and conviction.

The canonical Gospels add empty-tomb and appearance narratives. They agree on an empty tomb discovered by women and on subsequent resurrection proclamation, but differ over personnel, sequence, angelic figures, geography, and appearances ([[Matthew 28]], [[Mark 16]], [[Luke 24]], [[John 20]]). Mark 16:1–8 also requires textual-critical care because the familiar longer ending is absent from the earliest major manuscripts. These facts neither dissolve the common core nor permit the accounts to be treated uncritically as four wholly independent depositions.

The prominence of women, including [[Mary Magdalene]], is sometimes used as an authenticity argument because women's testimony faced limits in some ancient settings. It has modest evidentiary value at most: women were plausible visitors to a tomb, and an author could use disregarded witnesses for theological reversal. Likewise, the disciples' doubt in Luke 24:11 and John 20:24–29 resists a portrait of uncomplicated credulity but does not rule out visionary, psychological, or legendary explanations.

Within the Latter-day Saint canon, [[2 Nephi 9]], [[Alma 11]], [[Alma 40]], [[1 Corinthians 15]], and [[D&C 138]] form a coherent arc from Fall and death through the spirit world to bodily resurrection and judgment. That is strong evidence of canonical coherence, not independent empirical proof. The question whether resurrection best explains the historical data belongs with [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]] and requires explicit judgments about testimony, natural explanations, and [[Do miracles violate the laws of nature]].
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Significant questions remain even after the canon's central affirmations are stated:

- What exactly is the continuity between a mortal body and Paul's transformed spiritual body in 1 Corinthians 15:35–54?
- How should first resurrection language in [[Mosiah 15]], [[Alma 40]], and Restoration revelations be harmonized, especially when Alma acknowledges uncertainty about timing?
- How much detail may responsibly be inferred from restoration to a proper and perfect frame? The text does not define resurrected age, appearance, or every implication for disability.
- How should Matthew 22:30, Mark 12:25, and Luke 20:35 be related to [[D&C 132]] and [[Eternal Marriage]]?
- What is the precise relation between universal immortality, salvation, exaltation, and the differentiated [[Kingdoms of Glory]]?
- How should apparently physical encounters in Luke 24 and [[John 20]] be related to Paul's language of transformation?
- What historical conclusion is justified by the empty-tomb and appearance traditions, and where does historical inference end and faith begin?
- How literally should Ezekiel's dry bones be read outside Ezekiel's own national-restoration interpretation?
- What can be known about everyday life in the spirit world? [[Alma 40]] and [[D&C 138]] establish an interval and redemptive activity but leave most social and experiential details unstated.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest historical objection is that texts establish early resurrection belief, not automatically the event that belief describes. Bereavement visions, religious experiences, cognitive dissonance after Jesus's execution, scriptural reinterpretation, legendary development, or some combination could explain at least part of the evidence. Paul's list supplies early testimony but little narrative detail, and historians disagree over whether their discipline can identify a divine act as the best explanation.

A second objection concerns the Gospel accounts. Their differences are sometimes harmonized by adding unstated events and movements. Such harmonizations can be possible without being probable. Literary dependence among the Synoptics also means agreement cannot simply be counted as independent corroboration. Conversely, calling every difference a contradiction can ignore selective narration and perspective. The evidence permits neither effortless harmonization nor effortless dismissal.

A third objection reads Paul's spiritual body as nonphysical survival rather than bodily resurrection. That reading takes the adjective spiritual seriously, but it must also explain why Paul repeatedly discusses what kind of body is raised, contrasts corruption with incorruption, and invokes Christ's resurrection as firstfruits. Spiritual need not mean immaterial; nevertheless, Paul's transformed embodiment should not be reduced to mere reanimation.

A fourth objection is internal to the Latter-day Saint canon. The Synoptic statement that resurrected persons do not marry is not an easy verbal fit with D&C 132's perpetuation of sealed marriage. The continuation-versus-contracting distinction is coherent within Latter-day Saint theology but remains textually underdetermined in the Gospels.

Finally, Book of Mormon and modern-revelation passages cannot serve as independent ancient or apostolic corroboration for a critic who does not already accept their provenance. Their doctrinal coherence may support faith internally, but historical independence must be argued rather than assumed.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing and non-believing scholars overlap more than polemics sometimes suggest. Many accept that Jesus was crucified, that some followers soon believed he had appeared to them alive, and that resurrection proclamation became central very early. They divide over the empty tomb's historical probability, the nature of the experiences, and whether historical reasoning may infer a miracle.

N. T. Wright's *The Resurrection of the Son of God* argues that the early Christian mutation of Jewish resurrection belief and the combined tomb-and-appearance traditions are best explained by bodily resurrection. Dale C. Allison Jr.'s *The Resurrection of Jesus: Apologetics, Polemics, History* weighs both apologetic and skeptical arguments, treating several historical claims as defensible while maintaining caution about the movement from history to metaphysics. Bart D. Ehrman's *How Jesus Became God* accepts that disciples had experiences they understood as appearances but argues that historical method cannot establish God's miraculous action. Their disagreement is therefore partly about evidence and partly about allowable explanatory frameworks.

Exegetically, substantial scholarship understands Paul's spiritual body in 1 Corinthians 15:44 as a body transformed and animated by God's Spirit, not as an immaterial ghost. Debate remains over how Paul's conception relates to the more tangible scenes in Luke 24 and [[John 20]]. Textual criticism also broadly recognizes that Mark 16:9–20 is a later ending; responsible resurrection arguments should not depend upon treating that passage as part of Mark's earliest recoverable conclusion.

Latter-day Saint scholarship reads [[2 Nephi 9]], [[Alma 11]], [[Alma 40]], [[D&C 76]], and [[D&C 138]] as mutually illuminating Restoration witnesses. Critical scholarship will not treat them as independent ancient evidence unless the historical claims of the [[Book of Mormon (Gospel Topic)]] and modern revelation are established on other grounds. This is a methodological divide, not a detail that can be settled by accumulating internal cross-references.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
A disciplined study can proceed in six passes:

1. Begin with the event claims: read [[Mark 16]] through verse 8, [[Matthew 28]], [[Luke 24]], and [[John 20]]. List agreements and differences before attempting harmonization.
2. Read [[1 Corinthians 15]] as a complete argument. Separate Paul's received testimony in verses 3–8, his logical case in verses 12–34, and his account of transformed embodiment in verses 35–58.
3. Trace the Adam–Christ structure through [[Romans 5]] and 1 Corinthians 15:21–22, 45–49; connect it carefully with [[Fall of Adam and Eve]] and [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]].
4. Study Book of Mormon exposition in sequence: [[2 Nephi 9]], [[Mosiah 15]], [[Mosiah 16]], [[Alma 11]], [[Alma 12]], and [[Alma 40]]. Mark separately what is stated, what Alma says remains mysterious, and what later readers infer.
5. Place resurrection within the intermediate state and judgment by reading [[Death and the Spirit World]], [[D&C 76]], and [[D&C 138]]. Keep temporary spirit-world conditions distinct from final judgment and the second death.
6. Examine difficult extensions rather than hiding them: compare [[Matthew 22]], [[Mark 12]], and [[Luke 20]] with [[D&C 132]] on [[Eternal Marriage]], and compare [[D&C 130]] with [[Education and Learning]].

After the textual work, consult both a robust defense such as N. T. Wright and a methodologically cautious or critical treatment such as Dale Allison or Bart Ehrman. The goal is to identify where disagreement concerns the data, where it concerns source dependence, and where it concerns prior judgments about miracle and divine action.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scripture presents resurrection as God's answer to embodied death: through [[Jesus Christ]], every person is restored to immortal embodied life and brought before God. It is therefore larger than the survival of a spirit and smaller than a guarantee of identical salvation. [[Alma 11]], [[Alma 40]], and [[1 Corinthians 15]] emphasize bodily continuity and transformation; [[2 Nephi 9]], [[Mosiah 16]], and [[D&C 76]] place resurrection within Fall, redemption, judgment, and differentiated destiny.

The historical foundation of Christian proclamation is substantial but bounded. Paul's early received tradition strongly establishes that resurrection claims and appearance testimony belonged to Christianity's earliest recoverable period. The Gospels provide converging but nonidentical tomb and appearance narratives. These sources make late invention of the entire belief difficult to sustain, but they do not mechanically prove divine action. Naturalistic explanations face questions about the total pattern; miraculous explanation requires philosophical and theological judgments history alone cannot compel. See [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]].

Restoration scripture gives a particularly integrated picture: spirits continue between death and resurrection; Christ's redemption reaches the dead; all rise; judgment follows; and intelligence and divinely ratified covenants may have postmortal continuity. Its clarity is valuable within Latter-day Saint faith. Its status as independent historical evidence remains tied to disputed claims about revelation and Book of Mormon antiquity.

The doctrine's honest center is therefore both confident and modest: the canon clearly teaches universal resurrection through Christ, embodied immortality, and judgment. It does not clearly answer every question about timing, transformed physiology, age, disability, relationships, or daily life after death. Faith is strengthened by keeping those revealed affirmations distinct from harmonization, devotional elaboration, and speculation.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
