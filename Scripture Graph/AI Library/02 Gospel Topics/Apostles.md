# Apostles

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In the New Testament, *apostle* denotes one who is sent or commissioned as an authorized emissary. It overlaps with, but is not identical to, *disciple*: Luke says Jesus chose twelve disciples and named them apostles (Luke 6:12–13). The expression **the Twelve** normally identifies the symbolic and historical group chosen by Jesus, including [[Peter (apostle)]], [[Andrew (apostle)]], [[James (son of Zebedee)]], [[John the Apostle]], [[Matthew (apostle)]], [[Thomas (apostle)]], and initially [[Judas Iscariot]]; *apostle* can be broader, since Acts 14:14 calls [[Barnabas]] and [[Paul (apostle)]] apostles.

The text consequently does not permit a simple equation of every apostle with the original Twelve. Galatians 1:19 apparently uses the designation for [[James (brother of the Lord)]], while Romans 16:7 may or may not include Andronicus and Junia within a wider apostolic circle. These are distinct people and disputed usages, not additional names to insert casually into the Gospel lists.

In the Book of Mormon, “the twelve apostles of the Lamb” ordinarily means Jesus's Old World Twelve (1 Nephi 11:34; 12:9; 13:24). The twelve ministers chosen at Bountiful are normally called *disciples* in the text (3 Nephi 12:1), even though Latter-day Saints often describe their function as apostolic. In Restoration scripture, *apostle* is first applied broadly to [[Joseph Smith Jr]] and [[Oliver Cowdery]] (D&C 21:1, 10), then becomes the formal name of the traveling Quorum of the Twelve, defined as special witnesses of Christ's name in all the world (D&C 107:23, 33).
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
**What the canon directly teaches.** Jesus chooses and sends apostles; their ministry centers on his kingdom and on witness concerning him ([[Luke 6]]; [[Matthew 10]]; [[Acts 1]]). Their authority remains under [[Jesus Christ]], the chief cornerstone, and is to be exercised through [[Service]] rather than Gentile-style domination (Ephesians 2:20; Luke 22:24–30). They teach, administer, deliberate, suffer, and help preserve communal unity ([[Acts 5]]; [[Acts 8]]; [[Acts 15]]; [[Ephesians 4]]). They remain fallible: Judas betrays Jesus, the Twelve dispute greatness and scatter, and they initially disbelieve the women's report of the empty tomb and angelic announcement (Matthew 26:14, 56; Luke 22:24; 24:9–11). Revelation 2:2 commends testing people who claim apostolic identity.

The Twelve also carry Israelite symbolism. Jesus promises them thrones judging the twelve tribes (Luke 22:29–30), and [[Revelation 21]] places the tribes' names on the city's gates and the Lamb's apostles' names on its foundations. This supports covenant-continuity readings without settling the exact relationship between Israel and the Church.

**Latter-day Saint canonical teaching.** D&C 27:12 attributes apostolic ordination, confirmation, and ministerial keys to Peter, James, and John; this is the text's Restoration claim, not independent verification of the event. [[D&C 107]] defines the modern Twelve as a traveling presiding high council and special witnesses charged to build up and regulate the Church worldwide under the Presidency (D&C 107:23, 33). The Seventy work under their direction (D&C 107:34). Verses 24 and 26 describe corporate quorums as equal in authority while other verses assign distinct direction and jurisdiction.

**Current practice in the supplied library.** Conference proceedings document the public sustaining of the Twelve and its President or Acting President. They establish institutional practice and common consent, not by themselves the divine authorization or correctness of every calling or decision.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
**Jesus and the first generation.** In the traditional AD 30–33 setting, Jesus selects the Twelve after prayer ([[Luke 6]]), commissions them ([[Matthew 10]]), and teaches leadership through service (Luke 22:24–30). After Judas's death, Matthias is numbered with the eleven ([[Acts 1]]). Acts portrays apostles testifying, enduring arrest, participating in the Samaritan mission, and deliberating with elders at the [[Jerusalem conference]] ([[Acts 5]]; [[Acts 8]]; [[Acts 15]]).

**A wider first-century usage.** The sources distinguish the defined Twelve from a broader apostolic category. Acts calls [[Paul (apostle)]] and [[Barnabas]] apostles; Galatians probably includes [[James (brother of the Lord)]], while Romans 16:7 leaves the status of Junia and Andronicus disputed. This diversity need not mean that every person called an apostle held an office identical to membership in the Twelve.

**Later Christian models.** Catholic and Orthodox traditions generally treat the foundational apostolate as unique while claiming continuing apostolic authority through bishops. Many Protestants regard apostles as a completed founding generation whose testimony continues in scripture; some continuationist and charismatic traditions retain apostolic functions or offices in qualified forms. These positions cannot be reduced honestly to one universal non-Latter-day Saint cessation model.

**Book of Mormon and Restoration development.** First Nephi distinguishes the Lamb's Twelve from twelve Nephite ministers in their judgment roles ([[1 Nephi 12]]) and connects the records of the Lamb's apostles with later scriptural witnesses ([[1 Nephi 13]]). At Bountiful, Jesus gives authority to a group the narrative commonly calls twelve disciples ([[3 Nephi 12]]). D&C 21 applies apostolic titles to Joseph Smith and Oliver Cowdery before a formal Twelve exists; D&C 27 presents the Restoration claim involving Peter, James, and John; and [[D&C 107]] defines the later quorum's witness and worldwide jurisdiction. Modern sustaining records document continuing institutional practice but do not independently verify its revelatory claims.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
**Strong direct evidence.** The canon clearly establishes that Jesus selected a body called the Twelve, named them apostles, commissioned them, and associated them with Israel's twelve tribes (Luke 6:12–16; Matthew 10:1–8; Luke 22:29–30). It also establishes that New Testament *apostle* can extend beyond that body: Acts 14:14 explicitly names Paul and Barnabas. Ephesians assigns apostles and prophets a foundational role but makes Christ the cornerstone (Ephesians 2:19–22). D&C 107 directly defines the Latter-day Saint Twelve and their assigned worldwide duties.

**Strong observations with disputed implications.** Matthias's selection demonstrates at least one replacement in the Twelve ([[Acts 1]]). It supports continuity but does not, alone, prescribe every later succession. Ephesians 4:11–14 places apostles among Christ's gifts for equipping and maturing the Church; continuationists read the stated goals as supporting an enduring office, while cessationist and historical readings can treat the apostles as the founding generation whose witness continues through teaching and scripture.

Acts 8:12–17 gives real narrative precedent for baptism followed by apostolic prayer and laying on of hands for the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]]. Its force as a universal ordinance template is moderate rather than decisive because the episode may highlight the incorporation of [[Samaria]], and Acts contains varied sequences of initiation.

**Restoration-specific evidence.** D&C 21, 27, and 107 form a coherent internal line: early apostolic designation, claimed restoration of keys by ancient apostles, and institutional definition of the Twelve. For readers who accept modern revelation, this is strong canonical evidence for restored apostleship and [[Priesthood Restoration]]. Historically, however, the texts attest what the Restoration movement claimed; they do not independently verify the heavenly visitation of D&C 27:12.

**Interesting but inconclusive evidence.** First Nephi's claim that latter records establish the truth of the apostolic records ([[1 Nephi 13]]) supplies a theological model of mutually reinforcing witnesses. It cannot serve as independent proof of either ancient Book of Mormon origins or a detailed history of biblical corruption without additional evidence. Likewise, the symmetry of twelve tribes and twelve apostles in [[Revelation 21]] strongly supports covenant symbolism but does not settle ecclesiology or supersessionism.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How precisely did first-century Christians distinguish the Twelve, other apostles, missionaries, and local leaders? Acts 14:14 and Galatians 1:19 show wider usage, but no surviving text supplies a comprehensive definition.

- Does Matthias's replacement establish perpetual apostolic succession, or was restoring the symbolic Twelve before Pentecost a unique event? [[Acts 1]] supports continuity in that case but does not narrate replacements for every later death.

- Does Ephesians 4 describe offices required in every age, or foundational ministries whose work persists through their testimony? The answer depends partly on broader assumptions about church continuity, gifts, and [[Apostasy]].

- Does Romans 16:7 identify Junia as an apostle? The feminine name is strongly supported; whether the phrase means “notable among” or “well known to” the apostles remains grammatically and historically disputed. Even an inclusive reading would not make Junia one of the Twelve.

- What exactly constitutes a “special witness” in D&C 107:23: office, worldwide commission, personal encounter, authoritative testimony, or some combination? The verse states the calling and scope but does not disclose each holder's private spiritual experience.

- How should “equal in authority” in D&C 107:24 and 26 be reconciled with the Presidency's direction of the Twelve and the Twelve's direction of the Seventy? The revelation invites a corporate and jurisdictional reading but leaves practical development to later governance.

- Why does the Book of Mormon usually call the Bountiful twelve *disciples* rather than apostles? [[1 Nephi 12]] distinguishes them from the Lamb's Twelve, even while assigning parallel covenant functions.

- What standards should believers use to test claimed apostles, given Revelation 2:2, without treating either institutional recognition or private suspicion as self-validating?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
**Founding-generation objection.** A strong Christian alternative holds that apostles were uniquely commissioned witnesses belonging to the Church's founding generation. Acts 1:21–22 and Ephesians 2:20 can support this view: Matthias's qualifications arise in the original eyewitness setting, and a foundation is laid once. Later churches may therefore preserve apostolic teaching without appointing new apostles. Catholic and Orthodox accounts add that apostolic authority continues through episcopal succession without recreating the original apostolate or a recurring quorum identical to the Twelve.

**Historical-discontinuity objection.** The surviving record does not demonstrate an institution identical to the modern Latter-day Saint Quorum continuing unchanged through the intervening centuries. Early Christian offices and authority structures developed in diverse ways. Modern apostleship should therefore be described as a restoration claim rather than uninterrupted institutional transmission.

**Circular-authority and documentary objections.** D&C 27 and 107 strongly support modern apostleship only for readers who grant revelatory authority to [[Joseph Smith Jr]]. Using modern apostles to authenticate the revelations that establish their authority risks circularity. Historical study must also examine the dating and textual development of D&C 27 and early Restoration authority narratives rather than treating the present canonical wording as a complete contemporaneous record of every claimed event.

**Fallibility objection.** Scripture depicts apostles competing for rank, fleeing, disagreeing, discounting the women's report of the empty tomb and angelic announcement, and—in Judas's case—betraying Jesus. Office cannot therefore be equated with moral perfection, omniscience, or automatic correctness. Revelation 2:2 additionally requires discernment when apostolic status is claimed.

**Representation objection.** The Gospel Twelve and the modern Latter-day Saint quorum are male. Critics see exclusion, especially given women's indispensable role as the first recipients and messengers of Luke's empty-tomb announcement and the likelihood that Junia was a woman. Romans 16:7 may include Junia among a wider apostolic circle, but it does not identify her as one of the Twelve or by itself resolve [[Why do women not hold the priesthood]].

**Historical-critical and corruption objections.** Critical scholarship disputes Pauline authorship of Ephesians and treats Acts as a theologically shaped narrative. These judgments do not erase the canonical claims but qualify historical reconstruction. Likewise, [[1 Nephi 13]] does not identify a complete list, date, agent, or manuscript history for lost material and should not be inflated into the claim that a single council rewrote the Bible; compare [[How reliable is the biblical text]] and [[Was the Bible changed by councils and copyists]].
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Across confessional lines, scholars commonly distinguish **the Twelve** from the broader word **apostle** and recognize the Twelve's symbolic relationship to restored Israel. E. P. Sanders and John P. Meier, despite different theological locations, treat the formation of the Twelve as important evidence for Jesus's Israel-centered mission. This historical judgment is stronger than the claim that every Gospel commission detail can be reconstructed with equal certainty.

Pauline scholarship treats Paul's apostleship as both a divine-call claim and a contested social status. [[Galatians 1]] and [[Galatians 2]] show Paul defending independence from Jerusalem while also seeking recognition and partnership from James, Cephas, and John. James D. G. Dunn emphasizes both the diversity and negotiated unity of earliest Christianity; readings of [[Acts 15]] differ over how neatly Luke's account harmonizes with Paul's own description.

On Romans 16:7, Eldon Jay Epp's *Junia: The First Woman Apostle* argues strongly that Junia is a woman and that she and Andronicus are notable apostles. Many contemporary scholars accept the feminine name, but debate persists over whether the Greek phrase includes the pair among the apostles or says they were esteemed by apostles. Responsible use separates those two questions.

Critical scholarship frequently regards Ephesians as written by a later Pauline disciple, while many conservative and believing scholars defend Pauline authorship. Andrew T. Lincoln's commentary represents a major critical treatment. The authorship dispute affects dating and models of ministerial development, but Ephesians 2:20 and 4:11–14 remain clear evidence for how the canonical letter portrays apostles.

Latter-day Saint interpretation reads [[D&C 27]] and [[D&C 107]] within an accepted revelatory history of [[Priesthood Restoration]] and sees Ephesians 4 as consistent with restored continuing offices. Non-Latter-day Saint historians can securely report when these revelations and institutions appeared within the movement but generally do not treat angelic ordination as historically verifiable by ordinary methods. The principal divide is consequently not over the wording of D&C 107:23, but over the revelatory status of the document and the legitimacy of restoration as an explanatory model.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Read [[Luke 6]] and [[Matthew 10]]. Distinguish disciple, apostle, and the Twelve; note the commission's message, authority, and limits.

2. Read [[Luke 22]], [[Matthew 26]], and [[Luke 24]]. Trace promised responsibility, ambition, desertion, the women's report of the empty tomb and angelic announcement, and the apostles' initial disbelief.

3. Read [[Acts 1]], especially Acts 1:15–26. Separate what Matthias's case explicitly establishes from later theories of universal succession.

4. Read [[Acts 4]], [[Acts 5]], [[Acts 8]], and [[Acts 15]]. Compare testimony, communal resources, persecution, Samaritan incorporation, laying on of hands, and collective discernment. Connect these with [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], [[Service]], and the [[Jerusalem conference]].

5. Distinguish the Twelve from wider apostleship using Acts 14:4, 14; [[Galatians 1]]; [[Galatians 2]]; 1 Corinthians 9:1–5; Romans 16:7; and 2 Corinthians 12:11–12. Keep [[James (brother of the Lord)]] distinct from [[James (son of Zebedee)]] and treat Junia's apostolic status as disputed.

6. Read [[Ephesians 2]] and [[Ephesians 4]]. Ask what foundation, cornerstone, gifts, and until establish—and what they leave unresolved about continuing offices.

7. Read [[1 Nephi 12]] and [[Revelation 21]]. Compare the tribes, the Lamb's Twelve, the Nephite twelve, judgment, gates, and foundations without collapsing distinct groups.

8. Read [[1 Nephi 13]], [[3 Nephi 12]], [[D&C 21]], [[D&C 27]], and [[D&C 107]]. Separate each text's wording from later historical and doctrinal conclusions.

9. Use [[Sustaining Church Leaders]] and the supplied conference pages to observe modern public practice, while treating their metadata cautiously and distinguishing institutional recognition from divine authentication.

10. Read Revelation 2:2 with Luke 22:24–27. A complete model must accommodate authority, service, testing, fallibility, accountability, and Christ's supremacy.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The canon's strongest portrait is neither “apostles are merely ordinary missionaries” nor “apostles are automatically infallible.” Jesus prayerfully chooses twelve disciples, names them apostles, and commissions them as representatives of his kingdom ([[Luke 6]]; [[Matthew 10]]). Their number invokes Israel, explicitly so in Luke 22:29–30 and [[Revelation 21]]. Their witness and teaching are foundational, but [[Jesus Christ]] alone is the chief cornerstone (Ephesians 2:20).

The original Twelve are a defined group, yet New Testament apostleship is wider: Acts calls [[Barnabas]] and [[Paul (apostle)]] apostles, Galatians probably includes [[James (brother of the Lord)]], and Romans 16:7 leaves an important dispute about Junia. The evidence therefore requires careful naming. “Apostle,” “one of the Twelve,” and “later member of a formal Quorum of the Twelve” are related descriptions, not interchangeable ones.

Apostolic ministry is shown through proclamation, healing, administration, ordination or laying on of hands, council, suffering, and worldwide mission. Its ethic is service rather than domination (Luke 22:24–27). Its bearers remain human: they misunderstand, fear, disagree, and sometimes fail gravely. Revelation 2:2 makes the complementary point that a claimed apostolic title can be false and must be tested.

The Book of Mormon connects apostolic records with latter witnesses ([[1 Nephi 13]]) and distinguishes the Lamb's Twelve from twelve Nephite disciples ([[1 Nephi 12]]; [[3 Nephi 12]]). Restoration scripture then claims that Peter, James, and John conveyed apostolic ordination and keys (D&C 27:12) and defines a modern traveling Quorum of the Twelve as special witnesses with worldwide responsibility ([[D&C 107]]). This is a coherent and strong Latter-day Saint canonical case for restored continuing apostleship. It is not neutral historical proof of angelic ordination or of the exclusive legitimacy of the modern Church.

The honest conclusion is layered. Textually, apostles are commissioned witnesses whose authority remains under Christ and whose work serves the covenant community. Historically, the New Testament reveals both the Twelve and broader, developing apostolic usage. Doctrinally, Latter-day Saints accept a restored continuing quorum; other Christians plausibly read apostles as the unique foundation of the first generation. Faith may affirm the Restoration claim, but neither the title nor institutional succession eliminates the scriptural obligations of humility, discernment, accountability, and fidelity to Christ.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
