# Plan of Salvation

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In Restoration scripture, the **plan of salvation**, **plan of redemption**, **great plan of happiness**, and **plan of mercy** are overlapping names for God’s work to rescue and transform fallen humanity through [[Jesus Christ]] (Alma 42:5, 8, 11, 15). Their immediate contexts emphasize different facets: salvation names deliverance, redemption release from sin and death, happiness the intended end, and mercy the gift made compatible with justice.

In modern Latter-day Saint teaching, the expression is broader: God’s children exist before birth, enter mortality through [[Creation]] and the [[Fall of Adam and Eve]], exercise [[Agency]], receive redemption through the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], pass through [[Death and the Spirit World]], rise in the [[Resurrection]], face [[Final Judgment]], and inherit an eternal condition. [[Plan of Salvation (True to the Faith)]] is an official concise synthesis. This outline is canonical in aggregate; it is not all stated in one passage.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon presents God’s purpose as human immortality and eternal life (Moses 1:39). [[Abraham 3]] depicts premortal spirits or intelligences, an earth where they would be proved, and a chosen messenger (Abraham 3:22-28). Restoration teaching combines this with Moses 4:1-4 as the premortal setting of the plan.

[[2 Nephi 2]] and [[Alma 42]] make mortality purposeful but morally serious. Opposition permits meaningful choice (2 Nephi 2:11-16); the Fall brings death and a fallen condition, yet also posterity and the possibility of joy (2 Nephi 2:22-25). Exclusion from the [[Tree of Life]] creates “space for repentance” rather than fixing humanity forever in its fallen state (Alma 12:24-26; 42:4-5). This is the Restoration’s “fortunate Fall” reading; Genesis 3 alone does not articulate the full interpretation.

The plan is Christ-centered. Humanity cannot repair sin and death unaided; reconciliation comes through the merits, mercy, death, and resurrection of [[Jesus Christ]] (2 Nephi 2:6-10; Alma 22:13-14; 34:8-16). In Alma 42, the Atonement does not erase moral order: it permits mercy to claim the repentant without destroying [[Justice and Mercy]] (Alma 42:13-15, 22-25).

The revealed response includes [[Faith]], [[Repentance]], [[Baptism]], the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], and enduring transformation. Moses 6:52-68 joins these with [[Justification and Sanctification]] and calls the whole teaching “the plan of salvation unto all men.” Resurrection is universal (1 Corinthians 15:20-22; Alma 11:41-45), but the canon distinguishes universal resurrection from fuller salvation and [[Eternal Life]]. Doctrine and Covenants 76:50-112 describes differentiated [[Kingdoms of Glory]]. Doctrine and Covenants 138:30-35, 57-59 and [[Baptism for the Dead]] extend meaningful opportunity beyond mortal access to the gospel.

Official modern teaching is summarized in [[The Godhead and the Plan of Salvation (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2017)]], [[Covenant Belonging (Elder Gerrit W. Gong, October 2019)]], and [[God’s Intent Is to Bring You Home (Elder Patrick Kearon, April 2024)]]. These talks authoritatively teach the Church’s present understanding; they should not be mistaken for independent historical proof of premortal or postmortal realities.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
Two histories should be distinguished.

**Within the scriptural narrative:** the plan is described as prepared “from the foundation of the world” (Mosiah 4:6–7; Alma 12:25, 30; 22:13). Alma's account places disclosure through angels before the post-Fall commandments under discussion (Alma 12:28–32). [[2 Nephi 2]] explains opposition, the Fall, agency, repentance, and the Messiah without using the later standardized plan terminology. Jacob warns against mocking the plan (Jacob 6:8), Jarom says earlier writers had already revealed it (Jarom 1:2), and the missionary summaries in Alma 18 and 22 use Creation–Fall–Redemption sequences. These are claims made within the record; they do not independently verify the antiquity of the narrated events.

**Across the production and interpretation of the Restoration canon:** the Book of Mormon repeatedly names and integrates the plan. Moses 1:39 supplies a statement of divine purpose, [[Abraham 3]] supplies premortal beings, testing, estates, and a chosen messenger, and later revelations add differentiated glories and ministry among the dead. Nineteenth-century and later Latter-day Saint teaching increasingly organized these materials into the familiar premortality–mortality–postmortality outline. Current manuals and conference teaching present that synthesis as revealed doctrine. Historical development alone does not decide whether the development represents restoration, continuing revelation, theological innovation, or some combination.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence here is textual rather than archaeological.

**Strong textual observations:** [[Alma 42]] interrelates the Fall, death, probation, repentance, atonement, justice, mercy, happiness, and several names for the plan. [[Alma 12]] supplies a closely parallel tree-of-life, preparatory-state, resurrection, and commandments argument. Moses 6:52–68 integrates faith in Christ, repentance, baptism, the Holy Ghost, justification, sanctification, rebirth, and divine sonship, and explicitly calls the teaching “the plan of salvation unto all men.”

**Useful literary connections:** Alma 12 and Alma 22 share a Creation–Fall–Redemption sequence; Alma 18 presents a related missionary narrative; Alma 39:15–19 argues that the plan was as necessary before Christ's advent as afterward. These parallels establish recurring literary and theological organization in the received text. They do not establish a fixed historical catechism, because common Nephite theology, Mormon's redaction, shared translation language, or unified modern composition can account for the pattern.

**Moderate cumulative significance:** [[2 Nephi 2]], [[Alma 12]], [[Alma 42]], [[Moses 6]], [[Abraham 3]], and later revelations form a coherent Latter-day Saint canonical synthesis. That strongly establishes what the canon teaches. It supports ancient provenance or metaphysical truth only weakly, because coherence can arise through dependence, later revelation, common authorship, or retrospective systematization.

**Not established:** these literary findings do not prove a premortal council, a particular historical model of [[Adam]] and [[Eve]], divine authorship, Book of Mormon antiquity, resurrection, or postmortal kingdoms.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the plan require [[Adam]] and [[Eve]] to be the sole biological first parents, or can its theological claims survive representative or archetypal readings? See [[Were Adam and Eve real people]].
- How can a loving and purposeful plan include devastating suffering, unequal opportunity, disability, abuse, and apparently random tragedy? Calling mortality a test does not by itself answer [[Why does God allow suffering]].
- What counts as a genuinely free and informed opportunity to receive Christ, whether in mortality or the spirit world? See [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]].
- How are [[Grace]], obedience, ordinances, covenant faithfulness, and transformation related without reducing salvation either to unaided merit or to consequence-free assent?
- Which outcomes are unconditional gifts, especially resurrection, and which depend on repentance, covenant, desire, or continuing growth?
- How much postmortal change remains possible? Restoration scripture teaches ministry among the dead but does not answer every question about postmortal repentance or progression after judgment.
- How can divine foreknowledge and morally accountable [[Agency]] both be affirmed?
- How should premortal-life teachings be used after earlier Latter-day Saint authorities invoked supposed premortal conduct to explain racial restrictions—explanations later disavowed by the Church? The canon does not authorize inferring that present race, disability, suffering, wealth, or status was deserved before birth. See [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]] and [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]].
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objections concern historical development, dependence, moral coherence, and verification.

**Historical-development objection:** the Bible contains Creation, sin, Christ, resurrection, and judgment but not the fully systematized Latter-day Saint outline. Distinctive premortal, postmortal, and eternal-outcome teachings become explicit chiefly in Restoration texts. A critical model can therefore interpret the doctrine as Joseph Smith's developing nineteenth-century theology rather than restoration of one ancient system. A believing model interprets the same expansion as continuing revelation. The textual development alone does not decide between them.

**Textual-dependence objection:** agreement among the Book of Mormon, Moses, Abraham, and later revelations is not automatically independent attestation. It securely establishes canonical coherence, but common redaction, translation language, dependence, or one developing authorial environment can explain the recurrence without proving antiquity.

**Problem-of-evil objection:** describing mortality as probation may be morally inadequate where suffering destroys agency rather than refining it. Premortal consent and eventual compensation are not empirically available explanations and can become unfalsifiable if applied to every tragedy.

**Fairness objection:** salvation tied to ordinances and authorized covenants can appear exclusionary. Postmortal teaching and proxy ordinances provide an internally substantial LDS response, but that response depends on accepting Restoration revelation and leaves open what counts as a meaningful opportunity.

**Agency objection:** foreknowledge, inherited conditions, trauma, psychological constraint, and unequal environments complicate simple claims about free choice. Scripture affirms accountable agency but does not supply a complete philosophical reconciliation.

**Historical misuse objection:** premortal-life speculation was used by some Latter-day Saint authorities to rationalize racial priesthood and temple restrictions, and those explanations were later disavowed. This demonstrates the danger of turning an underdefined premortal doctrine into claims about why particular people suffer or occupy unequal social positions. See [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]].

**Empirical objection:** premortal life, resurrection, the spirit world, and kingdoms of glory are not directly testable by ordinary historical methods. Internal coherence and existential value are compatible with truth but do not constitute public proof.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Believing scholarship usually treats the doctrine as a Restoration synthesis that illuminates scattered biblical teachings. It emphasizes the positive function of the Fall, embodied existence, expansive salvation for the dead, degrees of glory, and human participation in divine life. Terryl Givens, in *Wrestling the Angel*, situates these themes within a distinctive Latter-day Saint theology; Blake T. Ostler’s *Exploring Mormon Thought* examines philosophical questions surrounding divine embodiment, freedom, and human potential. Their theological constructions should not automatically be treated as official doctrine in every detail.

Historical scholarship asks when the synthesis became explicit. Jan Shipps’s *Mormonism: The Story of a New Religious Tradition* and Douglas J. Davies’s *An Introduction to Mormonism* analyze Mormonism as a developing religious tradition. Charles R. Harrell’s *This Is My Doctrine* traces changes and expansions in Latter-day Saint teachings, including doctrines incorporated into the modern plan. Such works can document development without deciding whether development represents revelation, restoration, innovation, or some combination.

Biblical historical-critical scholarship generally reads Genesis 2-3 within ancient Israelite literary and theological contexts rather than as an explicit account of a premortal plan or a fortunate Fall. Traditional Western Christian readings more often stress original sin or inherited fallenness, while Eastern Christian approaches commonly emphasize mortality, corruption, and deification in different proportions. Latter-day Saint scripture shares Christian themes of Fall, Christ, resurrection, and judgment but reorganizes them through additional revelations.

The principal divide is therefore not over the observation that the modern doctrine is a synthesis. It is over the status of that synthesis. Believing readers regard later Restoration texts as genuine revelations recovering and enlarging divine truth. Critical readers can account for the same sequence through Joseph Smith’s theological development and nineteenth-century religious questions. Textual study can define the alternatives and test historical claims, but it cannot by itself adjudicate revelation.
<!-- SG:END scholarship -->

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
A productive canonical sequence is:

1. Read [[Moses 1]], especially Moses 1:27-39, for God’s stated purpose and the scale of Creation.
2. Read [[Abraham 3]], especially Abraham 3:22-28, and distinguish what it explicitly says from the fuller premortal narrative constructed with Moses 4 and later teaching.
3. Compare Genesis 2-3, Moses 4-5, and [[2 Nephi 2]]. Mark what each says about the Fall, death, posterity, knowledge, agency, and joy.
4. Read [[Alma 12]] and [[Alma 42]] together. Trace tree of life → death → probation → repentance → Atonement → resurrection → judgment, and note the four names given to the plan.
5. Study the missionary summaries in Alma 18:24-39 and Alma 22:7-14. Compare their Creation–Fall–Redemption order with [[Gospel of Jesus Christ]].
6. Read Moses 6:48-68 for the plan as lived response: faith, repentance, baptism, Holy Ghost, justification, sanctification, rebirth, and sonship.
7. Compare Alma 11:41-45, 1 Corinthians 15:20-42, and Doctrine and Covenants 76:50-112 to separate universal resurrection from differentiated eternal outcomes.
8. Read Doctrine and Covenants 137:7-9 and 138:11-35, 57-59 alongside 1 Peter 4:6 and 1 Corinthians 15:29 for the unevangelized dead and [[Baptism for the Dead]].
9. Only then read modern syntheses: [[Plan of Salvation (True to the Faith)]], [[The Godhead and the Plan of Salvation (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2017)]], [[Covenant Belonging (Elder Gerrit W. Gong, October 2019)]], and [[God’s Intent Is to Bring You Home (Elder Patrick Kearon, April 2024)]]. Note where they summarize scripture and where they make pastoral applications.
10. Stress-test the synthesis through [[Why does God allow suffering]], [[What happens to people who never heard of Christ]], and [[Were Adam and Eve real people]].

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The Plan of Salvation is the Restoration canon’s integrated account of why embodied mortal life exists, why it includes genuine agency and grave risk, how Christ answers sin and death, and what God intends humanity to become. Its names emphasize deliverance, redemption, happiness, and mercy (Alma 42:5, 8, 11, 15).

Its strongest scriptural architecture appears in [[2 Nephi 2]], [[Alma 12]], [[Alma 42]], [[Moses 6]], and [[Abraham 3]]. Together they portray premortal purpose, Creation, Fall, opposition, mortal probation, Christ’s Atonement, repentance, ordinances, sanctification, resurrection, judgment, and eternal life. Doctrine and Covenants 76 and 138 enlarge the picture with kingdoms of glory and redemptive ministry among the dead. The result is coherent, expansive, and unusually optimistic about embodiment, postmortal opportunity, and God’s desire to save.

Honesty requires three qualifications. First, this familiar outline is a synthesis; no single passage contains the whole diagram. Second, biblical texts do not explicitly teach every distinctive Restoration element, and Genesis alone does not state the fortunate-Fall interpretation. Third, canonical coherence establishes the content of Latter-day Saint belief more strongly than it establishes antiquity or metaphysical truth. Believers interpret the expansions as revelation; critics can interpret them as theological development.

The doctrine’s moral power lies in its insistence that suffering, failure, and death are not ultimate, that agency matters, and that redemption depends on [[Jesus Christ]]. Its hardest questions concern apparently unequal suffering, the limits of agency, the fate of the unevangelized, and the empirical inaccessibility of premortal and postmortal claims. [[God’s Intent Is to Bring You Home (Elder Patrick Kearon, April 2024)]] captures the tradition’s governing pastoral conviction: the plan’s purpose is not to trap God’s children but to bring them home. That conviction is official religious teaching and an object of faith, not a conclusion established by textual structure alone.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
