# Marriage

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, marriage is not presented through one exhaustive definition. [[Genesis 2]] supplies the foundational image: a man leaves his parents, cleaves to his wife, and the two become one flesh (Genesis 2:24). Other texts treat marriage as a household and legal relationship, a setting for sexual fidelity and mutual duty, an instrument of kinship and inheritance, and a covenant witnessed by God ([[Malachi 2]], especially Malachi 2:14). Biblical laws and narratives nevertheless reflect different eras and social structures; description or regulation of a practice is not automatically divine endorsement of that practice for every age.

In Latter-day Saint usage, civil marriage is recognized as legal marriage, while temple or celestial marriage denotes a sealing covenant intended to continue beyond death. [[Marriage (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Eternal Marriage]] express this traditional distinction. Current Church teaching defines marriage doctrinally as the union of a man and a woman; [[Two Great Commandments (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2019)]] states that position while distinguishing it from the legal status of civil marriages. This is an official ecclesiastical definition, not a neutral description of all contemporary civil, scholarly, or religious understandings of marriage.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canon's clearest recurring ideal is faithful one-flesh union. [[Genesis 2]] introduces leaving, cleaving, and one flesh; Jesus makes that creation pattern normative in [[Matthew 19]] and [[Mark 10]]. D&C 42:22 adds wholehearted love and exclusive cleaving. [[Malachi 2]] calls a wife the wife of a covenant and treats betrayal within marriage as treachery that affects worship.

Marriage also entails reciprocal moral duties. First Corinthians 7:3-5 uses strikingly mutual language about intimacy and agreed abstinence. Ephesians 5:21-33 places marriage within both mutual submission and an asymmetrical ancient household vocabulary, while requiring husbands to imitate Christ's self-giving love. These passages have been interpreted both hierarchically and more reciprocally; the asymmetry should neither be hidden nor converted into permission for domination.

The canon does not make marriage the only faithful mortal vocation. [[1 Corinthians 7]] permits marriage but values singleness for some, especially under 'present distress'; Matthew 19:10-12 treats renunciation of marriage for the kingdom as something given to some rather than commanded of all. Conversely, 1 Timothy 4:1-5 and D&C 49:15-16 reject religious prohibition of marriage.

Jesus treats Mosaic divorce regulation as a concession to hard hearts rather than the creation ideal (Matthew 19:7-9; Mark 10:4-9). Matthew includes an exception connected with porneia; Mark gives a stricter formulation. Neither passage offers a complete modern safety pastoral protocol for danger, abuse, abandonment, legal protection, or remarriage.

Distinctively Latter-day Saint doctrine teaches that a marriage sealed by authorized priesthood and the Holy Spirit of promise can remain after resurrection (D&C 132:15-20). [[Marriage (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Eternal Marriage Is an Eternal Journey (Elder Neil L. Andersen, April 2026)]] present this as official teaching. Matthew 22:30, Mark 12:25, and Luke 20:34-36 remain important countertexts: Jesus says resurrected people neither marry nor are given in marriage. The common Latter-day Saint distinction between forming a new marriage then and continuing an earlier sealing is possible, but it is not explicit in those Synoptic verses.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The earliest canonical layer presents marriage as kinship formation in [[Genesis 2]], but Genesis narratives also depict polygyny, concubinage, infertility arrangements, contested inheritance, and vulnerable wives. These stories are indispensable historical and theological data; their inclusion does not prove approval of every action.

The [[Law of Moses]] regulated existing household institutions. [[Numbers 5]], [[Numbers 30]], [[Deuteronomy 24]], and [[Deuteronomy 25]] address suspected adultery, wives' vows, divorce documents, and levirate duty within a patriarchal legal order. Numbers 27 and 36 connect daughters' inheritance to in-tribe marriage. [[Ruth 4]] develops related concerns about widowhood, kinship, redemption, property, and preservation of a family name.

The prophets moralize marriage more explicitly as covenant conduct. [[Malachi 2]] condemns treachery against the wife of one's youth and connects such betrayal with unacceptable worship. Its criticism of marriage involving a 'daughter of a strange god' explicitly concerns religious allegiance; reducing it to a timeless racial rule would overread the text.

Jesus appeals behind divorce regulation to creation ([[Matthew 19]]; [[Mark 10]]). Paul addresses concrete congregational problems: intimacy, separation, mixed-faith households, remarriage, singleness, and eschatological urgency in [[1 Corinthians 7]]. Ephesians later uses marriage to image Christ and the Church ([[Ephesians 5]]).

Restoration scripture intensifies exclusive marital loyalty in [[D&C 42]], rejects forbidding marriage in [[D&C 49]], and introduces an eternal sealing theology in D&C 132:15-20. The same D&C 132 is also inseparable from plural marriage. The official [[Plural Marriage in The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] says Joseph Smith instituted the practice among Church members in the early 1840s and that some Saints practiced it for more than half a century. [[Plural Marriage in Kirtland and Nauvoo (Essay)]], [[37 We Will Prove Them]], and [[40 United in an Everlasting Covenant]] lead into the more difficult documentary history.

Current institutional teaching identifies one man and one woman as the standing law and treats nineteenth-century plural marriage as an exceptional commanded practice. The first statement is a verifiable description of current doctrine; the claimed divine origin of the exception remains a matter of revelation and historical-theological judgment rather than something historical method can demonstrate.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
Strong textual evidence supports a canonical line from creation to covenant fidelity: Genesis 2:24 is reused in Matthew 19:4-6, Mark 10:6-9, and Ephesians 5:31; D&C 42:22 closely echoes cleaving language; Malachi 2:13-16 makes marital treachery relevant to worship. This establishes the prominence of faithful union, though it does not erase the canon's other marital forms or settle every modern policy.

Strong textual evidence also establishes diversity and tension. Mosaic texts regulate institutions that modern readers often find patriarchal ([[Numbers 5]], [[Numbers 30]], [[Deuteronomy 25]]). Paul simultaneously permits marriage and praises singleness in [[1 Corinthians 7]]. Jacob 2:27 prohibits plural wives, while Jacob 2:30 has competing interpretations. D&C 132 joins eternal sealing to a revelation that also defends plural marriage. The Synoptic Gospels say resurrected persons do not marry, while D&C 132 says authorized sealings endure. A responsible synthesis must preserve these differences before attempting harmonization.

The resurrection tension is especially significant. Mark 12:25 is direct and occurs in parallels at Matthew 22:30 and Luke 20:34-36. The Latter-day Saint 'no new marriages after resurrection' reconciliation is coherent with D&C 132:15-20, but it is an inferred harmonization rather than an explicit qualification in Jesus's recorded answer.

The official plural-marriage essays are strong evidence that the Church acknowledges the basic chronology and practice of plural marriage. Because they are institutional and confessional sources, they do not by themselves establish divine authorization or resolve contested questions of consent, secrecy, age, sexual relationships, and power. [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]] and [[Did Joseph Smith marry other men's wives]] belong in that evidentiary inquiry.

Keyword counts are discovery aids, not doctrinal weights. The many matches in [[1 Corinthians 7]] genuinely mark sustained discussion, but counts in legal or narrative chapters may only reflect repeated labels such as husband and wife. Conversely, Genesis 2:24 has few words but exceptional influence because later texts quote it. The index's ambiguous-match warnings should therefore be retained.

Historical claims about superior temple-marriage stability also require caution. [[Conference Report, April 1918 (General Conference (full report), April 1918)]] preserves an early argument based on divorce counts, but the excerpt provides insufficient methodology for causal inference. It evidences what leaders argued, not a controlled finding that the ordinance itself reduces divorce.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Does Genesis 2:24 define an exclusive universal structure, or does it articulate an ideal of kinship union without addressing every form later found in scripture?

2. How should Jacob 2:30 be parsed in relation to the unequivocal prohibition in Jacob 2:27? Does it authorize an exceptional command, continue the warning, or remain genuinely ambiguous?

3. What precisely continues after resurrection? Do Matthew 22:30, Mark 12:25, and Luke 20:34-36 deny all postmortal marriage, only new marriage formation, or the Sadducees' assumption that resurrection reproduces mortal levirate claims?

4. How should Matthew's porneia exception be related to Mark's stricter form, Paul's abandonment discussion in 1 Corinthians 7:15, and modern circumstances of abuse or danger?

5. Which Mosaic marriage laws embody durable moral principles, and which regulate an ancient patriarchal economy without prescribing it for later societies?

6. How should Ephesians 5:21-33 be taught without suppressing either its hierarchical wording or its demands for mutual submission and self-sacrificing love?

7. What evidentiary standard can evaluate nineteenth-century claims of a divine plural-marriage command while also taking secrecy, unequal authority, and participants' religious experiences seriously? See [[Plural Marriage in Kirtland and Nauvoo (Essay)]], [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]], and [[Why did Emma Smith deny plural marriage]].

8. How can a tradition centered on [[Eternal Marriage]] avoid treating unmarried, divorced, widowed, infertile, or LGBTQ people as spiritually incomplete? [[How does the Church regard LGBTQ members and same-sex marriage]] is directly relevant.

9. When scripture uses wedding imagery for salvation—John 3:29 and Revelation 19:7-9—which conclusions concern Christ and the covenant community, and which, if any, may legitimately be transferred to individual marriages?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
A strong objection to any claim that scripture gives one simple and timeless marriage model is the canon's own variety. Genesis contains monogamy, polygyny, concubinage, surrogate-like infertility arrangements, and marriages shaped by lineage and property. Mosaic law regulates unequal household authority. The honest response is not to deny that variety but to distinguish narrative description, legal accommodation, prophetic criticism, and later theological idealization. That distinction is interpretive and must be argued passage by passage.

A second objection is that appeals to 'biblical marriage' can conceal patriarchy. Numbers 5 tests a wife under a husband's jealousy; Numbers 30 grants husbands power over wives' vows; Deuteronomy 24 speaks from the divorcing husband's legal position; Ephesians 5 calls the husband head. Reciprocal duty in 1 Corinthians 7 and sacrificial love in Ephesians do not make those asymmetries disappear. Claims that every ancient arrangement directly prescribes modern gender authority exceed the texts.

A third objection concerns divorce teaching. Reading Jesus's sayings as exceptionless modern policy can trap people in violent or coercive relationships. The passages strongly oppose casual repudiation and remarriage, but they do not discuss every safety or legal circumstance. Saying this is not weakening the text; it marks where the text stops speaking explicitly.

A fourth objection is the direct force of Mark 12:25 and its parallels against [[Eternal Marriage]]. The Latter-day Saint reconciliation may be possible, but simply inserting 'new' before 'marry' is not translation. D&C 132 supplies the distinction; the Synoptic saying does not. A believer may accept the later revelation as clarification, while a critic may see doctrinal contradiction or development.

A fifth objection is that plural marriage sits uneasily beside Genesis 2:24, D&C 42:22, and Jacob 2:27. The traditional exception model appeals to Jacob 2:30 and D&C 132; critics dispute Jacob's syntax and challenge the moral credibility of the Nauvoo implementation. Official essays confirm enough of the difficult history that it cannot responsibly be dismissed as hostile invention. Historical evidence can clarify events but cannot independently certify revelation.

A sixth objection is ethical exclusion. Current Church teaching limits doctrinal marriage to a man and a woman, leaving same-sex couples outside temple marriage and creating acute costs for gay and lesbian members. Quoting official doctrine accurately does not settle the moral or pastoral debate. [[How does the Church regard LGBTQ members and same-sex marriage]] should be studied alongside [[Charity]], [[Agency]], [[Family]], and the lived consequences of the teaching.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Historical-critical scholarship generally treats biblical marriage as a changing collection of kinship, property, sexual, reproductive, and covenantal practices rather than a single legal institution unchanged across the canon. That approach explains differences among Genesis narratives, Pentateuchal law, prophetic critique, Jesus's divorce sayings, Pauline counsel, and later household teaching. Confessional interpreters may accept the same historical diversity while also reading a theological trajectory toward faithful one-flesh covenant.

On divorce, David Instone-Brewer's Divorce and Remarriage in the Bible situates Jesus and Paul in Jewish legal debates and argues that their sayings should be read within that context. Other scholars reach stricter conclusions, particularly concerning remarriage. The meaning of Matthew's porneia exception, the relationship between Matthew and Mark, and the reach of 1 Corinthians 7:15 remain disputed; there is no honest scholarly shortcut from one English word to a complete pastoral rule.

Many New Testament scholars classify Ephesians 5:21-6:9 as engagement with Greco-Roman household ordering. Carolyn Osiek and David Balch's Families in the New Testament World provides relevant social context. Egalitarian readers emphasize Ephesians 5:21, Christlike self-giving, and the reciprocal language of 1 Corinthians 7; complementarian readers retain differentiated marital roles. Both readings must account for the entire passage, including its asymmetry.

Social historians such as Stephanie Coontz in Marriage, a History and Lawrence Stone in The Family, Sex and Marriage in England, 1500-1800 trace major changes in marriage's economic, familial, legal, and companionate functions. Their work cautions against projecting the modern romantic-companionate ideal unchanged into antiquity. It does not determine theological truth.

Latter-day Saint believing scholarship reads D&C 132 and temple sealing as revealed clarification of postmortal marriage and often reads Jacob 2:30 as an exception clause. Critical scholarship more often treats eternal and plural marriage as nineteenth-century doctrinal development and evaluates Joseph Smith's authority claims historically rather than accepting them devotionally. Historians across commitments substantially agree that Nauvoo plural marriage occurred; they differ sharply about revelation, motives, consent, secrecy, and moral legitimacy. [[Plural Marriage in Kirtland and Nauvoo (Essay)]] is an important official source, but it should be read with primary documents and independent historical work rather than treated as the final scholarly word.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
Begin with the textual foundation rather than a modern definition:

1. Read [[Genesis 2]] with Genesis 1:27, noting exactly what 'one flesh' says and what it does not say. Then trace its reuse in [[Matthew 19]], [[Mark 10]], and [[Ephesians 5]].

2. Study marriage as ancient law and household structure in [[Numbers 5]], [[Numbers 30]], [[Deuteronomy 24]], and [[Deuteronomy 25]]. Follow inheritance and marriage through Numbers 27 and 36, then [[Ruth 1]] and [[Ruth 4]]. Ask whether each passage describes, regulates, criticizes, or idealizes.

3. Read [[Malachi 2]] for marriage as covenant and for the relationship between family conduct and worship. Compare Ezra 9-10 and Nehemiah 13 without assuming that thematic proximity makes all three policies identical.

4. Read the divorce accounts side by side: Matthew 19:1-12 and Mark 10:1-12. Mark the differences, especially Matthew's exception clause. Add 1 Corinthians 7:10-16 before drawing pastoral conclusions.

5. Read all of [[1 Corinthians 7]], not merely verses 2-5. Track mutuality, concession, singleness, mixed-faith marriage, present distress, and Paul's distinction between command and judgment. Compare 1 Timothy 4:1-5 and D&C 49:15-19.

6. Read the resurrection controversy in Matthew 22:23-33, Mark 12:18-27, and Luke 20:27-38 before D&C 132:15-20. State the tension first; then test the Latter-day Saint distinction between new marriage and continuing sealing.

7. Read [[Jacob 2]] as a complete sermon, especially Jacob 2:23-35. Compare its condemnation of David and Solomon with D&C 132, identifying rather than smoothing their different formulations.

8. For modern Latter-day Saint history, proceed from [[Plural Marriage in The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] to [[Plural Marriage in Kirtland and Nauvoo (Essay)]], then to [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]], [[Did Joseph Smith marry other men's wives]], and [[Why did Emma Smith deny plural marriage]]. Separate agreed chronology, disputed reconstruction, ethical evaluation, and faith claims.

9. Finish with present teaching in [[Marriage (Gospel Topics)]], [[Eternal Marriage Is an Eternal Journey (Elder Neil L. Andersen, April 2026)]], and [[Two Great Commandments (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2019)]]. Ask how doctrine is applied to actual marriages and to people whose lives do not fit the marital ideal.

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Scripture presents marriage as consequential but not simple. [[Genesis 2]] gives its most influential image: leaving, cleaving, and one flesh. Jesus invokes that image against easy divorce; [[Malachi 2]] treats betrayal of a spouse as covenant treachery that corrupts worship; [[D&C 42]] commands exclusive, wholehearted marital love. These are strong foundations for fidelity, unity, and moral accountability.

The same canon records substantial diversity. Ancient marriage is entangled with lineage, property, fertility, widow protection, male household authority, polygyny, and political alliance. Some texts regulate damaged social conditions rather than declare an ideal. Paul values both marriage and singleness and gives notably reciprocal counsel about intimacy. Ephesians combines sacrificial love and mutual submission with hierarchical language that remains contested.

Latter-day Saint teaching adds a distinctive claim: authorized temple sealing may endure after death. That doctrine gives marriage eschatological importance and grounds [[Eternal Marriage]]. Yet Matthew 22:30, Mark 12:25, and Luke 20:34-36 genuinely sound resistant to post-resurrection marriage. The usual distinction between marriages contracted then and sealings continued from mortality is a possible synthesis supplied by Restoration theology, not an explicit phrase in the Synoptic texts.

Plural marriage is the subject's sharpest historical and ethical complication. Jacob 2:27 plainly prohibits plural wives in its setting; Jacob 2:30 is disputed; D&C 132 defends both eternal sealing and plural marriage. Official Church histories acknowledge Joseph Smith's introduction of plural marriage and its nineteenth-century practice. They provide important evidence but cannot, merely by recounting revelation claims, establish that God authorized the practice. Questions of secrecy, consent, authority, and harm remain legitimate.

Current Church doctrine teaches marriage between a man and a woman as the standing law and temple sealing as the fullest form of the covenant. That position should be stated accurately, but its difficult consequences for unmarried, divorced, widowed, infertile, and LGBTQ people should also be faced. The most defensible scriptural synthesis is therefore modest: marriage is a major covenantal arena for love, fidelity, holiness, kinship, and discipleship; the canon does not erase singleness, settle every family form, or remove the need for historical context and careful moral judgment.
