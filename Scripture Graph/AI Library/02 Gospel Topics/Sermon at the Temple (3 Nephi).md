# Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
“Sermon at the Temple” is a modern study title, not a phrase used by the canonical narrator. In its narrowest sense it names Jesus's discourse in [[3 Nephi 12]]-[[3 Nephi 14]], the close Nephite parallel to the [[Sermon on the Mount]]. In a broader and often more illuminating sense, it belongs to the risen Christ's ministry beginning in [[3 Nephi 11]]: his appearance at the temple in Bountiful, authorization of disciples, doctrine of baptism, ethical teaching, explanation of the fulfilled law, identification of the “other sheep,” healing, prayer, and sacrament.

The boundaries should therefore be stated rather than assumed. [[3 Nephi 15]] interprets the sermon, chapters 16-18 extend it into covenant history and worship, and [[3 Nephi 27]] supplies a later doctrinal culmination. Calling all of this one sermon is convenient, but it can blur separate occasions and literary units.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The canonical presentation is centered on the risen [[Jesus Christ]], not on an autonomous moral code. He identifies himself as the crucified and resurrected Lord, permits personal witness of his wounds, authorizes [[Baptism]], and defines his doctrine through faith, repentance, baptism, reception of the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], and enduring fidelity (3 Nephi 11:10-40).

The ethical core calls disciples beyond public performance and minimal compliance. Reconciliation, chastity of heart, fidelity, truthful speech, nonretaliation, enemy-love, secret devotion, forgiveness, trust in God, charitable judgment, discernment, and actually doing Christ's words characterize life in his kingdom (3 Nephi 12:20-48; 13:1-24; 14:1-27). The command to become as Christ is later made explicit in 3 Nephi 27:27.

Jesus says the [[Law of Moses]] is fulfilled in him but that not all prophetic promises are fulfilled (3 Nephi 15:1-10). He identifies the Nephites as a remnant of Joseph and as the “other sheep” of John 10:16, while also mentioning still other groups (3 Nephi 15:12-24; 16:1-3). Thus discipleship is joined to the [[Abrahamic Covenant]] and [[Gathering of Israel]].

In official Latter-day Saint teaching, the event is treated as a historical ministry of the resurrected Christ. [[Covenants and Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]] uses the invitation at Bountiful to teach that ordinances and covenants bring people to Christ. [[The Greatest Easter Story Ever Told (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, April 2023)]] likewise presents the account as an additional resurrection witness. Those are confessional claims of the tradition; literary comparison alone cannot verify them historically.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
Within the canonical narratives, [[John 10]] places the “other sheep” saying during Jesus's mortal ministry. [[3 Nephi 11]]-[[3 Nephi 15]] then depict the risen Christ appearing at the temple in Bountiful, authorizing disciples, delivering teaching closely parallel to Matthew 5-7, declaring the [[Law of Moses]] fulfilled, and identifying the Nephite hearers as the “other sheep.” Subsequent chapters extend the account into healing, worship, covenant teaching, and the [[Gathering of Israel]]. [[3 Nephi 27]] records a later appearance, after years had passed, and should not be treated as a continuation of the first temple discourse.

The supplied modern sources show an established Latter-day Saint devotional reception of the narrative. [[The Book of Mormon What Would Your Life Be Like without It (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2017)]], [[This Day (Elder Ronald A. Rasband, October 2022)]], [[The Greatest Easter Story Ever Told (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, April 2023)]], and [[He Is Risen (Elder Ronald A. Rasband, April 2026)]] present the Bountiful encounter as additional witness of the risen Christ. These sources document recent confessional use; they do not supply a complete history of interpretation since 1830.

Modern comparative study has also examined the discourse's extensive relationship to Matthew and proposed temple-centered covenant readings. Those developments belong to the history of interpretation, not to the canonical narrative itself.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
**Strong textual observations.** [[3 Nephi 12]]-[[3 Nephi 14]] and Matthew 5-7 share extensive wording, sequence, images, and argument. A literary relationship is beyond reasonable dispute. Equally observable are Nephite adaptations: baptism and the Holy Ghost appear in the opening blessings; instructions about food and clothing are explicitly directed to the twelve; and the discourse is followed by explanations about the fulfilled law and Israelite identity.

**Strong within the narrative, not independent historical corroboration.** [[3 Nephi 11]] depicts an embodied risen Christ examined one person at a time, while 3 Nephi 17:25 estimates about 2,500 hearers. This clearly establishes what the record claims. By itself it does not establish that the event occurred outside the text.

**Strong evidence of English textual dependence; disputed historical significance.** The close King James-style rendering makes an English relationship to Matthew highly probable. A critical model explains this as Joseph Smith's direct adaptation of the King James Bible. A believing translation model holds that revelation used familiar biblical wording where the teachings corresponded. Both address the English evidence; neither can be mechanically derived from verbal similarity alone. See [[Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew]].

**Moderate interpretive evidence.** The temple setting, authorized ministry, ritual actions, covenant teaching, and command to become like Christ support John W. Welch's covenant-oriented reading. The motifs are real, but identifying a complete ancient temple ritual is less secure and depends on choices about structure and comparison.

**Theological rather than externally testable evidence.** The identification of the Nephites as the “other sheep” is explicit in 3 Nephi 15:21. It coherently connects [[John 10]] with the Book of Mormon's Israel theology, but it is internal testimony, not independent evidence that ancient American disciples were intended by the historical Jesus's saying.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Where should the “Sermon at the Temple” begin and end: with the Matthew parallel in chapters 12-14, the first-day ministry in chapters 11-18, or the larger Bountiful ministry?
- Does the King James form of the discourse represent direct modern borrowing, a revealed translation strategy, or some combination? [[Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew]] is indispensable to this question.
- Which differences from Matthew plausibly reflect a distinct post-resurrection setting, and which may be literary adaptations made during composition or translation?
- Does the temple setting encode a specific covenant ceremony, or does it simply provide an appropriate sacred gathering place?
- How does fulfillment of the [[Law of Moses]] relate to Jesus's insistence that the prophets are not all fulfilled (3 Nephi 15:4-10)?
- How should the command to resist evil and love enemies inform discipleship, public justice, and [[War and Peace]] without turning difficult sayings into slogans?
- Who are the additional sheep of 3 Nephi 16:1-3? The text affirms their existence but leaves them unidentified.
- How should a reader distinguish the narrative's claim to eyewitness testimony from external historical verification of that claim?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest literary objection is cumulative: Third Nephi reproduces not merely isolated teachings attributable to Jesus but Matthew's extended arrangement, transitions, themes, and King James English. Critical readers argue that direct nineteenth-century use of the King James Bible explains this evidence more simply than an ancient American discourse translated into nearly the same form. The presence of context-specific additions demonstrates adaptation, but a modern author could make those additions just as readily as an ancient speaker or inspired translator.

A related objection is historical. The appearance, mass encounter with the risen Jesus, healings, angelic ministry, and identification of American Israelites receive no independent contemporaneous corroboration currently accepted in mainstream historical scholarship. The record's internal specificity may strengthen its narrative force, but internal detail is not independent confirmation.

The “other sheep” interpretation also faces a contextual challenge. Readers of [[John 10]] can understand the saying within the first-century Mediterranean mission—for example, other Israelites or future Gentile believers—without positing Nephites. Third Nephi's identification is unambiguous about its own meaning, but accepting it as the historical meaning intended in John requires accepting Third Nephi's revelatory authority.

Believing responses are substantial but limited. A translation intended to present equivalent teachings in familiar scriptural English could naturally resemble the King James Bible, while the Nephite changes integrate the discourse deeply into resurrection, baptismal, covenant, and Israelite themes. That model is possible within belief in revelation, but the textual parallels do not independently compel it. Likewise, a temple-covenant structure can enrich interpretation without proving antiquity.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Comparative scholarship begins from a secure observation: [[3 Nephi 12]]-[[3 Nephi 14]] shares Matthew 5-7's extended sequence and much of its received English wording. Disagreement concerns the mechanism, direction, historical setting, and significance of that relationship.

Krister Stendahl's “The Sermon on the Mount and Third Nephi” compares the texts while attending to Third Nephi's Christological, baptismal, ministerial, and post-resurrection framing. John W. Welch's *Illuminating the Sermon at the Temple and Sermon on the Mount* develops a believing temple-and-covenant interpretation from the setting, sequence, authorized ministry, and ritual motifs. The motifs are textually present, but reconstruction of a complete ancient ritual pattern is disputed and does not independently establish antiquity.

Stan Larson's critical essay is properly identified as “The Sermon on the Mount: What Its Textual Transformation Discloses Concerning the Historicity of the Book of Mormon,” first published in *Trinity Journal* 7 (1986) and reprinted in *New Approaches to the Book of Mormon* (1993). Larson argues that Matthean order and King James English are most economically explained by modern dependence. Revelatory translation models answer that familiar biblical English could mediate genuinely parallel teachings, but that mechanism is a theological proposal rather than something verbal similarity independently proves.

These studies share much of the textual data but assign it different evidentiary significance. They illustrate important believing, comparative, and critical approaches; they should not be presented as an exhaustive map or unanimous scholarly consensus. See [[Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew]].

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
1. Begin with [[3 Nephi 11]]. Mark the narrative sequence: divine voice, descent, identification, wounds, worship, authorized ministry, baptism, and the doctrine of Christ.
2. Read [[3 Nephi 12]]-[[3 Nephi 14]] beside Matthew 5-7. Record exact agreements separately from additions, omissions, audience markers, and changed settings. Do not infer a historical explanation until the comparison is complete.
3. Read [[3 Nephi 15]] and 3 Nephi 16:1-20 for fulfillment of the [[Law of Moses]], the remnant of Joseph, the “other sheep,” Gentiles, and gathering. Compare John 10:1-18 in its full literary context rather than isolating John 10:16.
4. Continue through 3 Nephi 17-18. Observe how healing, blessing children, [[Sacrament]], [[Prayer]], and ministry enact the sermon communally.
5. Read 3 Nephi 20-22 for the [[Abrahamic Covenant]] and [[Gathering of Israel]]. This prevents reducing the sermon to private ethics.
6. Read [[3 Nephi 27]], carefully distinguishing this later appearance from the first temple discourse. Trace the gospel summary and the command to become like Christ.
7. Study the competing analyses gathered under [[Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew]]. Compare Stendahl, Welch, and Larson by asking which observations they share and where interpretation begins.
8. Finish with modern devotional applications in [[Following Christ (President Dallin H. Oaks, October 2024)]], [[The Greatest Easter Story Ever Told (Elder Gary E. Stevenson, April 2023)]], and [[Covenants and Ordinances (Gospel Topics)]], keeping pastoral use distinct from historical demonstration.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
The Sermon at the Temple presents discipleship as a response to the embodied, risen [[Jesus Christ]]. Its moral demands are inseparable from his identity, authorized [[Baptism]], the Holy Ghost, covenant belonging, worship, and the charge to become like him. The temple audience is not merely told what to believe: its members approach Christ, witness his wounds, hear his words, receive healing and sacramental emblems, pray, and become responsible for carrying his light.

Textually, the heart of the sermon in [[3 Nephi 12]]-[[3 Nephi 14]] is unmistakably related to Matthew 5-7. That is strong evidence of literary dependence and should never be minimized. Third Nephi also adapts the material to its own post-resurrection narrative through baptismal language, audience distinctions, fulfillment of the Mosaic law, and Israelite covenant teaching. Those adaptations make the discourse theologically coherent, but they do not by themselves identify its date or author.

The honest historical assessment is therefore layered. The contents and differences are observable. Christ-centered, covenantal, and temple-oriented readings are interpretations with substantial textual support. The claim that the scene records an ancient American appearance of the risen Christ belongs to Latter-day Saint faith and the Book of Mormon's own testimony; the literary evidence neither independently proves nor simply makes impossible a revelatory account. A student can receive the sermon devotionally while still confronting the force of [[Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew]]. Its enduring invitation is practical and demanding: hear Christ, enter his covenant, embody his teachings, gather others to him, and measure discipleship by likeness to the Teacher.
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
