# Fall of Adam and Eve

## Definition
<!-- SG:BEGIN definition -->
In scripture, the **Fall of Adam and Eve** is the transition narrated through the forbidden-fruit episode and expulsion from the [[Garden of Eden]]. Genesis describes the event but does not call it “the Fall” as a technical term. Genesis 3:6-24 emphasizes disobedience, judgment, hard labor, return to dust, and exclusion from the [[Tree of Life]].

Later texts interpret that narrative. Paul uses [[Adam]] in an Adam–Christ contrast in Romans 5:12-21 and 1 Corinthians 15:21-22. Restoration scripture gives the term its fullest Latter-day Saint meaning: the Fall brought mortality, posterity, temporal death, spiritual separation, and a probationary setting for [[Agency]], while the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] makes redemption possible (2 Nephi 2:19-27; Alma 12:22-37; Alma 42:6-15).

The tradition therefore uses “Fall” for more than one act. It can designate Adam and [[Eve]] eating the fruit, their changed mortal condition, or humanity's resulting fallen condition. These senses should be distinguished. [[Fall of Adam and Eve (Gospel Topics)]], [[Fall of Adam and Eve (Bible Dictionary)]], and [[Fall (True to the Faith)]] present the official Latter-day Saint synthesis; that synthesis includes interpretive propositions not stated in Genesis by itself.
<!-- SG:END definition -->

## Doctrinal Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrinal-summary -->
The Latter-day Saint canon teaches the following connected doctrines:

- God created Adam and Eve, placed them in Eden, gave a genuine command concerning the tree, and preserved their capacity to choose (Genesis 2:16-17; Moses 3:16-17).
- They partook of the fruit and were expelled from Eden and barred from the tree of life (Genesis 3:6-24; Moses 4:12-31).
- Restoration texts identify the tempter with [[Satan]], whereas Genesis itself calls the tempter a serpent without making that identification explicit (2 Nephi 2:18; Mosiah 16:3).
- The Fall introduced the mortal setting in which posterity, opposition, moral choice, suffering, and death occur. Lehi presents it as instrumental to existence and joy, not merely as catastrophe (2 Nephi 2:15-25).
- Alma distinguishes temporal death from spiritual death or separation from God's presence (Alma 42:6-9). Mortality also becomes a probationary period in which repentance can operate (Alma 12:23-37).
- Humanity is not left without remedy. Jacob, Abinadi, Samuel, Paul, and Alma all connect deliverance from the fallen condition to Jesus Christ, resurrection, and atonement (2 Nephi 9:6-13; Mosiah 16:3-15; Helaman 14:15-18; Romans 5:12-21; Alma 42:11-15).

Official summaries such as [[Chapter 3 The Plan of Salvation]] and [[Fall of Adam and Eve (Gospel Topics)]] say the Fall formed part of God's plan and was not a divine surprise. That is an authoritative Latter-day Saint doctrinal synthesis. It should still be distinguished from the narrower observations available from Genesis alone.

Calling the Fall necessary does not require calling the prohibited act intrinsically virtuous. The canon preserves both sides: it was a transgression with painful consequences, yet God incorporated it into a redemptive movement toward mortality, posterity, joy, and return through Christ.
<!-- SG:END doctrinal-summary -->

## Historical Development
<!-- SG:BEGIN history -->
The earliest canonical layer for study is [[Genesis 2]]–[[Genesis 3]]. It supplies the garden, command, serpent, eating, judgments, mortality imagery, and guarded tree. The Hebrew narrative itself does not present the later fully systematized doctrine of “the Fall,” nor does it explicitly call the serpent Satan.

Jewish interpretation before and around the New Testament was diverse. Wisdom of Solomon 2:24 connected death with the devil's envy, while Sirach 25:24 placed unusual blame on Eve; neither statement simply reproduces Genesis. Paul then made Adam central to his account of sin, death, grace, and resurrection in [[Romans 5]] and [[1 Corinthians 15]].

Early Christians also differed. [[Irenaeus]] emphasized Christ's recapitulation of Adamic history and often treated humanity as immature and destined for growth. [[Augustine of Hippo]] gave inherited original sin a particularly influential Western formulation. Eastern Christian traditions generally emphasized inherited mortality and corruption more than inherited culpability. “The Christian doctrine” is therefore historically plural, even where traditions agree that Christ reverses Adamic ruin.

Within the Book of Mormon's narrated chronology, [[Lehi (father of Nephi)]] teaches a purposeful-Fall account in [[2 Nephi 2]], and Jacob, [[King Benjamin]], [[Abinadi]], Alma, and Samuel develop its relation to atonement and resurrection. As a matter of publication history, these formulations entered modern discussion with the Book of Mormon in 1830. Moses 3–5, produced during Joseph Smith's Bible revision beginning in 1830, further expanded the Genesis narrative with a premortal Satan scene, explicit agency language, sacrifice, and Adam and Eve's rejoicing.

Modern Church teaching consolidates these passages into the [[Plan of Salvation]]. [[The Book of Mormon What Would Your Life Be Like without It (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2017)]] highlights the necessity of the Fall and Eve's wisdom; [[The Great Plan of Happiness (Elder Earl C. Tingey, April 2006)]] emphasizes the two deaths; and [[January 19–25. The Fall of Adam and Eve Genesis 3–4; Moses 4–5]] provides the current curriculum setting. These are modern teachings and applications, not additional evidence about the ancient composition of Genesis.
<!-- SG:END history -->

## Evidence & Study Notes
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
The strongest evidence here is textual and theological, not archaeological.

**Strong:** [[2 Nephi 2]] is a sustained argument rather than an isolated slogan. Law, opposition, creation, enticement, Fall, Messiah, freedom, and final choice are mutually connected in 2 Nephi 2:5-29. [[Alma 12]] and [[Alma 42]] likewise give internally coherent explanations of the guarded tree, mortality, probation, temporal death, spiritual death, justice, mercy, and redemption.

**Strong within the canon:** Multiple Restoration texts converge on a recognizable doctrinal pattern: the Fall creates a mortal and spiritually separated condition; Christ answers it through atonement and resurrection (2 Nephi 9:6-13; Mosiah 3:11-19; Mosiah 16:3-15; Helaman 14:15-18). This strongly establishes what the Latter-day Saint canon teaches.

**Strong observation, disputed significance:** Moses 4 closely parallels Genesis 3 while adding explicit claims about Satan and premortal conflict. Believers may regard the additions as restored ancient revelation; critical readers may regard them as Joseph Smith's nineteenth-century expansion of the King James text. The comparison itself cannot choose between those explanations.

**Moderate intertextual connection:** Revelation 22:3's declaration that there will be no more curse plausibly reverses the curse imagery of Genesis 3, while Revelation 22:1-2 restores access to the tree of life. This is a strong literary-theological connection, but it does not reconstruct the historical circumstances of Eden.

None of this evidence by itself establishes a historical first biological couple, universal descent from a single pair, the absence of animal death before humans, or a particular scientific chronology. Those are additional propositions requiring additional evidence and careful genre judgments.
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Significant Questions
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Significant questions remain open even after the doctrinal pattern is clear:

- How historical and how archetypal is the Eden narrative? See [[Were Adam and Eve real people]].
- Does scriptural “death through the Fall” mean all biological death, human mortality, spiritual estrangement, or different things in different passages?
- How should 2 Nephi 2:22-23, which says the created order would have remained unchanged and Adam and Eve would have had no children, be related to the pre-Fall command to multiply in Genesis 1:28? Source-critical readers note that Genesis itself combines distinct creation traditions (commonly labeled P and J), so part of this tension may reflect the composite history of Genesis rather than requiring a single harmonizing explanation.
- If God prohibited the fruit while the plan required a transition to mortality, what exactly was expected of Adam and Eve? Foreknowledge, command, necessity, and moral responsibility are not identical concepts.
- Did the fruit itself alter human nature, did God impose the changed condition, or does the narrative use physical imagery to express theological realities?
- What kind of knowledge did the tree confer? Genesis 3:22 says the humans became as the divine beings in knowing good and evil, but interpreters disagree whether this means moral discernment, maturity, autonomy, sexual knowledge, or comprehensive wisdom.
- Which consequences are universal features of mortality, which are judgments on the first pair, and which reflect the ancient social world of Genesis?
- How should [[Agency]] and inherited conditions be reconciled? Latter-day Saint teaching rejects inherited personal guilt, yet everyone inherits mortality and a morally damaged environment.
- How should faith claims about Adam and Eve relate to genetics, paleoanthropology, and evolutionary history? See [[How do faith and science fit together]] and [[How old is the earth according to the Bible]].

The canon answers the soteriological question—redemption is through Christ—more directly than it answers these historical and scientific questions.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Objections & Alternative Views
<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objections should not be reduced to misunderstandings.

**The command-plan tension:** If mortality and posterity required the Fall, commanding Adam and Eve not to eat can appear to create a divine double bind. Saying God foreknew and incorporated a free transgression distinguishes planning from coercion, but it does not fully explain why this precise arrangement was morally necessary.

**The justice problem:** It can seem unjust that all people suffer death and estrangement because of an ancestor's act. Latter-day Saint teaching answers that descendants do not inherit Adam's personal guilt and that Christ universally overcomes temporal death. Even so, people plainly inherit unequal mortal conditions they did not choose; the broader problem of suffering remains.

**Genesis and later doctrine are not identical:** Genesis does not call the episode “the Fall,” identify the serpent as Satan, or explicitly teach a premortal plan in which the event was necessary. Those teachings arise through Paul and Restoration scripture. A critical reader can reasonably describe the mature doctrine as theological development rather than simple exposition of Genesis.

**The scientific objection:** Human genetics and paleoanthropology do not naturally suggest that every human descends biologically and exclusively from one couple who lived after a deathless terrestrial creation. Symbolic, representative, genealogical, or limited-population models may reduce the conflict, but some are not stated in the text and can become ad hoc if invoked only to evade evidence. See [[Were Adam and Eve real people]].

**The posterity tension:** Genesis 1:28 commands the humans to multiply before the transgression, whereas 2 Nephi 2:23 says they would have had no children without the Fall. A believer may distinguish a command from the conditions needed to fulfill it, but Genesis itself does not explain the issue in those terms.

**Gendered hierarchy and blame:** This objection is not only about later reception. Genesis 3:16 itself pronounces, as a stated consequence of the Fall, “thy desire shall be to thy husband, and he shall rule over thee” — a specific declaration of hierarchical rule between husband and wife, not merely vague relational friction. Later tradition has also often blamed Eve disproportionately, and 1 Timothy 2:13-14 has been used to subordinate women on that basis. Genesis 3:6 says Adam was with her and ate, and Romans 5 normally names Adam, not Eve, when explaining humanity's plight; Moses 5:11 gives Eve a positive theological voice. Whether Genesis 3:16 describes a fallen distortion to be overcome or prescribes an ongoing order remains genuinely disputed among interpreters, and that dispute belongs to the text itself, not only to its reception history.

**The “fortunate Fall” risk:** Praising benefits that follow the Fall can sound like praising disobedience or making evil necessary to God. The most careful reading says God brings redemptive goods from a real transgression; it does not say wrongdoing becomes good whenever beneficial consequences follow.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Scholarship
<!-- SG:BEGIN scholarship -->
Historical-critical scholarship commonly reads Genesis 2–3 first in its ancient literary setting rather than beginning with later doctrines of original sin. Claus Westermann's *Genesis 1–11: A Continental Commentary* treats the story as an account of basic human realities; James Barr's *The Garden of Eden and the Hope of Immortality* argues that mortality and access to the tree of life are more complex than the common claim that humans were straightforwardly immortal before eating. Scholars dispute sources, composition, and precise symbolism, but broadly agree that the later technical doctrine of the Fall should not simply be read back into every line of Genesis.

Elaine Pagels's *Adam, Eve, and the Serpent* traces the diversity and social consequences of early Christian interpretation. [[Irenaeus]] and [[Augustine of Hippo]] illustrate substantially different emphases: recapitulation and maturation in one case, inherited sin and culpability in the other. Modern Eastern and Western Christian accounts continue to differ over original guilt, corruption, and mortality.

John H. Walton's *The Lost World of Adam and Eve* offers a conservative Christian attempt to read Adam in ancient Near Eastern context while allowing significant compatibility with evolutionary science. Other scholars regard such harmonizations as insufficient because Paul and Genesis appear to make historical claims, or because the models import distinctions absent from the texts.

Believing Latter-day Saint interpretation gives independent revelatory authority to [[2 Nephi 2]], [[Alma 12]], [[Alma 42]], Moses 3–5, and Doctrine and Covenants 29. From that premise, the Book of Mormon and Pearl of Great Price restore a purposeful account largely missing from the surviving Genesis narrative. [[Fall of Adam and Eve (Gospel Topics)]] represents this official synthesis.

Critical and believing readings therefore part most sharply over source authority and historicity, not over whether the texts say different things. Both can observe that Genesis, Paul, the Book of Mormon, and Moses present distinct layers of interpretation. They disagree about whether the later Restoration layers are revelation recovering truth or modern theological development.

## Study Pathways
<!-- SG:BEGIN study-pathways -->
A careful study proceeds from narrative to interpretation rather than importing the finished doctrine into the first passage.

1. Read [[Genesis 2]] and [[Genesis 3]]. Mark only what the narrative explicitly says: the command, serpent, eating, responses, judgments, dust, expulsion, and guarded tree. Notice what it does not explicitly say about Satan, premortal planning, or inherited guilt.
2. Compare [[Moses 3]], [[Moses 4]], and [[Moses 5]]. List each addition to Genesis, especially agency, Satan's aims, sacrifice, redemption, and Adam and Eve's retrospective joy.
3. Study [[2 Nephi 2]] as a complete argument, not only verse 25. Trace law, opposition, creation, temptation, posterity, Messiah, freedom, and joy.
4. Follow Jacob's death-and-resurrection logic in [[2 Nephi 9]], then compare [[Mosiah 3]], [[Mosiah 16]], and [[Helaman 14]].
5. Read [[Alma 12]] before [[Alma 42]]. The first explains the guarded tree and probation; the second distinguishes temporal and spiritual death and integrates [[Justice and Mercy]].
6. Compare Paul's Adam–Christ reasoning in [[Romans 5]] and [[1 Corinthians 15]]. Ask where Paul agrees with Restoration scripture and where his vocabulary or emphasis differs.
7. Read Doctrine and Covenants 29:39-45 and then the official synthesis in [[Fall of Adam and Eve (Gospel Topics)]], [[Chapter 3 The Plan of Salvation]], and [[Fall (True to the Faith)]]. Keep scripture, official summary, and personal inference in separate notes.
8. Finally, study the unresolved historical questions through [[Were Adam and Eve real people]], [[How do faith and science fit together]], and [[How old is the earth according to the Bible]]. Do not make the theological coherence of the doctrine carry scientific or historical conclusions it cannot establish.
<!-- SG:END study-pathways -->

## Synthesis
<!-- SG:BEGIN synthesis -->
Genesis presents a compact and difficult narrative: humans cross a divine boundary, acquire knowledge, experience shame and conflict, face hardship and death, and lose access to Eden's tree of life. The text is foundational, but the full Latter-day Saint doctrine is not contained in Genesis alone.

Later scripture interprets the event in widening circles. Paul contrasts Adam with Christ. Lehi situates the Fall within opposition, agency, posterity, and joy. Jacob, Benjamin, Abinadi, Alma, and Samuel connect the fallen condition to atonement and resurrection. Moses adds premortal conflict, explicit agency, sacrifice, and Adam and Eve's gratitude. Together these texts yield a coherent Latter-day Saint synthesis: the Fall is a real transgression with painful consequences, a necessary transition into mortal experience, and the setting in which Christ's redemption becomes operative.

That synthesis is doctrinally strong within the canon. It does not, by itself, prove a particular model of human origins, a deathless biological world, exclusive descent from one couple, or the ancient provenance of Restoration expansions. Believers and critical scholars divide chiefly over those source and historicity judgments.

The most defensible conclusion preserves both tragedy and purpose. The Fall is not treated as good simply because good eventually comes from it. Nor is it treated as a disaster that defeated God's plan. Scripture portrays God as permitting genuine agency, containing the consequences within a probationary order, and answering death and separation through [[Jesus Christ]]. The center of the doctrine is therefore not speculation about the fruit; it is the movement from [[Creation]] through [[Agency]] and mortality to the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Resurrection]], and possible [[Eternal Life]].
<!-- SG:END synthesis -->
