# Alma 33 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 33]] · My notes: [[Alma 33 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Zenos taught that men should pray and worship in all places, and that judgments are turned away because of the Son—Zenock taught that mercy is bestowed because of the Son—Moses had lifted up in the wilderness a type of the Son of God. About 74 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The audience's questions (v. 1):** Must they believe in one God, how should they plant the word, and how should they begin exercising faith?
2. **Worship beyond the synagogue (vv. 2–11):** Alma rejects place-bound worship and quotes Zenos's prayers from varied settings and circumstances.
3. **Zenos and Zenock concerning the Son (vv. 12–17):** Alma applies their statements about judgment and mercy because of the Son.
4. **Moses's wilderness type (vv. 18–22):** Looking and living become Alma's analogy for believing in the Son.
5. **Return to the planted word (v. 23):** The word is to be nourished by faith, growing unto everlasting life.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Zenos and Zenock are presented as teaching that judgment is turned away and mercy bestowed because of the Son, joining [[Justice and Mercy]] to Christ's saving role. Verse 22 gives a compact account of the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], the Son's rising from the dead, [[Resurrection]], and [[Final Judgment]] according to works. Alma identifies faith with an initial willingness to believe and an ongoing practice of planting and nourishing the word. Verse 23 holds together a future hope of [[Eternal Life]] and present joy through the Son that may make burdens light.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated expressions “thou didst hear me” and “thou art merciful” unify Zenos's prayer. The locations change, but the divine response does not. Alma later organizes his appeal around simple bodily and agricultural verbs—look, plant, swell, nourish, spring up—which make faith concrete and active. “Because of thy Son” is the theological refrain joining mercy to Christ.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The Zenos quotation repeats declarations that God heard the speaker across wilderness, field, house, closet, congregation, affliction, and exclusion. The correspondence between people cast out of synagogues and a prayer from someone heard when cast out gives the citation a strong rhetorical fit. Alma then moves from looking and living to believing in the Son, before “plant this word” returns to the seed imagery of [[Alma 32]]. This coherence supports purposeful literary unity, but it cannot determine whether that unity arose through historical preaching, editorial shaping, or composition.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Compositional Coherence
> The chapter coherently integrates the hearers' questions about worship and planting the word with a scriptural argument about prayer, a typology of looking and believing, and a final return to the seed-and-tree imagery. This strongly supports purposeful literary unity across Alma 32–33.
> — confidence 1.0 · literary unity accepted; provenance unresolved · [[Compositional Coherence in Alma 33|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary And Rhetorical Coherence
> The selection of Zenos's statement about being heard after being cast out is closely tailored to an audience whose stated concern is exclusion from synagogues. This is strong evidence of deliberate rhetorical adaptation within the narrative.
> — confidence 1.0 · strong internal literary evidence · [[Literary And Rhetorical Coherence in Alma 33|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Linguistic/Textual *(tentative)*
> The phrase 'when I did turn unto my closet, O Lord, and prayed unto thee' (v.7) uses 'closet' for a private place of prayer, matching the King James English of Matthew 6:6 ('when thou prayest, enter into thy closet'), a distinctively Early Modern English idiom rather than a literal translation choice one would expect from an independent ancient source.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.6 · phraseological observation; significance disputed

> [!evidence] Textual/Source-Critical *(tentative)*
> That Zenos and Zenock are unattested outside the Book of Mormon is evidentially double-edged: it is consistent with the Book of Mormon's own claim that 'plain and precious' scripture was later removed from the biblical record, but equally consistent with these figures being literary constructions created to supply prophetic proof-texts for the book's own christological argument.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.7 · balanced but underdetermined
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why do the hearers equate exclusion from a synagogue with inability to worship?
- What is gained by listing so many locations of prayer instead of stating the principle only once?
- In verses 19–22, what exactly corresponds to looking: belief, repentance, attention to Christ, or the whole faithful response?
- How does judgment “according to their works” relate to the mercy and atonement emphasized in the same chapter?
- Does making burdens light describe changed circumstances, changed spiritual capacity, or potentially both?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Alma 32]] and [[Alma 33]] as successive parts of one teaching episode, noting the return to planting and nourishing in Alma 33:23. Compare the unnamed wilderness type with the raised object in [[Numbers 21]] and with its more explicit christological interpretations in [[John 3]] and [[Helaman 8]]. [[1 Nephi 17]] provides another Book of Mormon treatment of the Israelites' refusal to look. These are useful interpretive parallels; they do not independently establish the provenance or historicity of Alma's discourse.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
