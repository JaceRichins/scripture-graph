# Alma 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 1]] · My notes: [[Alma 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Alma 1 opens the reign of the judges with a test of the new legal order that took effect after Mosiah's death (see [[Mosiah 29]]). Nehor gains followers by preaching universal, unconditional salvation and a financially supported, popularity-seeking priesthood; when the aged Gideon—known for delivering the people of Limhi from bondage—resists him with "the words of God," Nehor kills him and is executed under the law Mosiah had established, for shedding innocent blood rather than for belief alone. His death does not end priestcraft: rivalry, persecution, and internal contention continue, even though the law "could have no power on any man for his belief" (v.17), punishing only lying, theft, robbery, and murder. The chapter closes by contrasting this disorder with a church community marked by shared labor, need-based generosity, and patient endurance (see [[Charity]], [[Humility]]); its resulting prosperity is morally qualified by the members' refusal to set their hearts on riches (vv.26–31; compare [[Alma 4]]). See also [[Government and Laws]], [[Pride]], and [[Work and Self-Reliance]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **New legal order and Nehor's rise (1–6):** Mosiah's acknowledged laws remain in force; Nehor's preaching brings followers, money, pride, and a new church.
2. **Violence, trial, and execution (7–15):** Nehor disputes with and kills Gideon, then is condemned under the law.
3. **Priestcraft and persecution continue (16–25):** Belief remains beyond the law's power, but persecution and retaliatory contention test the church; some withdraw while others endure.
4. **An alternative communal order (26–31):** Teachers and learners labor as equals, assist people in need, and prosper without making wealth their governing loyalty.
5. **Vice, enforcement, and public peace (32–33):** The narrator contrasts outsiders with the church and attributes increased restraint partly to enforcement.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter places doctrinal teaching within an ethical and institutional setting. Nehor's message is joined to popularity, financial gain, costly apparel, and eventually coercion. By contrast, approved ministry is linked to equality and shared labor (Alma 1:26). This supports study of [[Priesthood]], [[Humility]], and [[Work and Self-Reliance]], but it should not automatically be converted into a universal prohibition on every form of material support for religious ministers.

The treatment of wealth is similarly qualified. Abundance is praised only alongside care for the poor, impartial assistance, and hearts not fixed upon riches (Alma 1:27–30). The result is closer to [[Charity]] and responsible stewardship than to a promise that faith always yields financial success.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter itself supplies the operative features of "priestcraft": teachers seek popularity and support, adherents preach for riches and honor, and Nehor attempts to enforce the movement by the sword (Alma 1:3, 12, 16). Because the term carries this narrative cluster, it should not be applied indiscriminately to every paid ministry or theological disagreement.

The clause that law could have "no power on any man for his belief" (Alma 1:17) is unusually explicit. Its immediate context nevertheless distinguishes inward belief or professed teaching from independently punishable acts such as lying, theft, robbery, and murder.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is constructed through contrasts: Nehor's supported preaching versus laboring priests; popularity versus equality; costly apparel versus neat and comely dress; contention versus patience; and possessing riches versus setting one's heart upon them. These contrasts advance the narrator's moral interpretation of the events.

That design also calls for critical attention. Alma 1:32 gives outsiders a sweeping catalogue of vice, whereas steadfast insiders receive an idealized description. The passage is valuable as narrative theology, but its group portraits should not be mistaken for sociologically neutral reporting.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Legal-Narrative Evidence
> The chapter provides strong internal evidence that its narrator understands Nehor's execution as punishment for killing Gideon and attempting to enforce priestcraft by the sword, not merely for holding or preaching an unpopular belief. The same account expressly says that the law could have no power over a person for belief.

> [!evidence] Textual/Literary Parallel *(tentative)*
> Alma 1:26-31's description of the church's egalitarian economic practices and resulting prosperity is repeated in substantially similar language later in Alma 4 (8 overlapping verses per index data), suggesting either a deliberate recurring literary formula used by the record's compiler to mark cycles of church health, or simply habitual/formulaic phrasing reused to describe a recurring social condition.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · unverified 'near-verbatim' claim

> [!evidence] Textual/Legal Observation *(tentative)*
> Alma 1:17-18 can be read as an early legal articulation of a distinction between belief and conduct: Nephite law under the judges punished lying, theft, robbery, and murder, but not doctrinal error or preaching itself, so long as no crime was committed.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · disputed interpretation, well-hedged

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Which actions, rather than beliefs, does the narrative identify as the basis for legal punishment?
2. Does Alma's definition of Nehor's offense require doctrinal error, financial self-interest, coercion, or some combination of these?
3. Why does Nehor's execution fail to halt the spread of his ideas?
4. What separates the prosperous church in Alma 1 from the wealth-and-pride crisis later narrated in [[Alma 4]]?
5. How does the text evaluate members who retaliate against persecution, even when the persecution is real?
