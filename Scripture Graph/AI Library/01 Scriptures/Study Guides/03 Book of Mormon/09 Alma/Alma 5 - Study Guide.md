## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful rhetorical outline is:

1. **Communal memory (vv. 1–13):** Alma recalls baptism, captivity, deliverance, Abinadi's preaching, and the fathers' changed hearts.
2. **Personal judgment examination (vv. 14–25):** Questions about spiritual birth lead into an imagined appearance before God.
3. **Present durability of conversion (vv. 26–32):** Past experience is tested by present humility, purity, and conduct.
4. **Invitation and competing allegiances (vv. 33–42):** The tree of life, fruit, shepherd, sheep, and wages images contrast two ways of life.
5. **Authority and testimony (vv. 43–52):** Alma identifies his commission, fasting, prayer, revelation, and prophecy of Christ.
6. **Social application and final appeal (vv. 53–62):** Pride, wealth, persecution, and neglect of the poor are condemned; church members receive a command, while outsiders receive an invitation to [[Baptism]].

This outline describes the sermon's progression without requiring a rigid formal scheme.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Alma presents salvation through a cluster of related claims rather than a systematic formula. Faith in the Redeemer accompanies a mighty change of heart; garments are cleansed through Christ’s blood; and humility, repentance, righteous works, and faithfulness until the end are repeatedly required. These features are relevant to [[Grace]], [[Faith]], [[Repentance]], [[Justification and Sanctification]], and [[Endurance]], but the chapter does not explicitly settle whether works are causes, conditions, consequences, evidence of conversion, or some combination.

The question “can ye feel so now?” makes continuing [[Conversion]] a central concern. Alma does not dismiss an earlier spiritual experience, but neither does he treat it as immunity from present examination.

The sermon also applies repentance socially. Pride and envy appear beside costly apparel, claims of superiority, persecution, and withholding substance from the poor. Preparation for [[Final Judgment]] therefore includes conduct toward other people.

Alma’s testimony joins received witness with personally sought [[Revelation]]. He invokes the fathers’ words and his prophetic commission, while also saying that prolonged [[Fasting]] and [[Prayer]] preceded manifestation by the Holy Spirit.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated words “remember,” “imagine,” “now,” “come,” “repent,” and “hearken” trace the sermon's movement from inherited memory to present decision. Alma's many questions are not requests for information; they force hearers to supply an inward verdict before the future divine verdict.

Several paired contrasts organize the language: sleep/awakening, darkness/light, bondage/deliverance, stained/white garments, good/evil fruit, Christ's fold/the devil's fold, and names blotted out/names written in the book of life. These contrasts sharpen the appeal, though they should not automatically be read as a complete theory of moral psychology.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The garment image is compressed and paradoxical: bloodshed and guilt stain garments, while Christ’s blood makes garments white. The image holds culpability and redemptive cleansing within the same symbolic field.

The received English text also has numerous close biblical parallels: pure heart and clean hands with [[Psalm 24]]; corruption and incorruption with [[1 Corinthians 15]]; the ax and fruitless tree with [[Matthew 3]]; shepherd, voice, sheep, and fold with [[John 10]]; separation from unclean things with [[Isaiah 52]]; and blotted or written names with [[Revelation 3]]. These parallels strongly establish English-language intertextuality.

Their historical explanation remains disputed. The especially close Matthew and Romans wording postdates Alma’s internal narrative setting under conventional New Testament dating, creating a genuine difficulty for a simple claim that the precise English expressions reproduce pre-Christian wording. Direct or indirect dependence on the King James Bible during modern composition is one explanation; translation into conventional biblical English is another. Shared traditions may explain some imagery but are less sufficient for near-verbatim English phrasing. The parallels alone do not determine authorship, direction of dependence, or an underlying source language.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean to receive or retain God's “image” in one's countenance: visible conduct, covenant identity, inward transformation, or some combination?
- Why does Alma ask an already established church to revisit spiritual birth and baptismal identity?
- How do mercy offered freely and the sermon's strong demands for righteous works function together?
- Does the language about names in verses 57–58 refer primarily to earthly church records, heavenly judgment, or a deliberate movement between both?
- What specific practices today correspond to Alma's linked criticisms of pride, status display, persecution, and neglect of the poor?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
