# Alma 7 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 7]] · My notes: [[Alma 7 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Christ will be born of Mary—He will loose the bands of death and bear the sins of His people—Those who repent, are baptized, and keep the commandments will have eternal life—Filthiness cannot inherit the kingdom of God—Humility, faith, hope, and charity are required. About 83 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Reason for the visit and comparison with Zarahemla (vv. 1-6):** Alma explains his release from civic office and assesses the congregation's spiritual condition.

2. **The coming and saving work of the Son of God (vv. 7-13):** The prophecy moves from Christ's birth to his experience of suffering, death, infirmity, and sin-bearing.

3. **Required response (vv. 14-16):** Repentance, rebirth, [[Baptism]], covenant, and continuing obedience are joined together.

4. **Affirmation and warning (vv. 17-22):** Alma recognizes the people's faith while warning that uncleanness cannot enter the [[Kingdom of God]].

5. **Practices of enduring discipleship (vv. 23-27):** Character, prayer, [[Gratitude]], [[Faith]], [[Hope]], [[Charity]], good works, and a final blessing complete the address.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Virgin birth and incarnation** (7:10): Christ "shall be born of Mary... she being a virgin... [and] bring forth a son, yea, even the Son of God."
- **An expansive atonement** (7:11-13): Christ takes upon himself pains, afflictions, temptations, sicknesses, death, infirmities, and sin — broader than a purely legal description. The chapter assigns distinct outcomes to each without fully systematizing them: death is answered by the loosing of "the bands of death" (v.12), infirmity by mercy and succor (v.12), and sin by "deliverance" from transgression (v.13).
- **Succor through shared experience** (7:12): "that his bowels may be filled with mercy, according to the flesh, that he may know according to the flesh how to succor his people according to their infirmities" — grounding the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] in embodied experience alongside its more substitutionary aspects (v.13).
- **Conversion as an integrated response** (7:14-16): repentance, being born again, [[Baptism]], cleansing, faith in the Lamb of God, [[Covenants]], and ongoing obedience are presented together; the promise of [[Eternal Life]] in v.16 depends on keeping the commandments "from thenceforth," not on baptism in isolation.
- **Purity and the kingdom of God** (7:21): "he doth not dwell in unholy temples; neither can filthiness or anything which is unclean be received into the kingdom of God," tied explicitly to [[Final Judgment]] ("at the last day").
- **Faith, hope, and charity** (7:24) as the source of "good works," paired with a virtue list (7:23): [[Humility]], gentleness, patience, temperance, diligence, and [[Gratitude]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Alma marks a clear boundary around his own knowledge in verse 8: the Spirit has not told him whether Christ will visit his people during mortality, and he says plainly, "this thing I do not know" — set beside his firm testimony of what he does know. It's a useful example of prophetic conviction and acknowledged uncertainty in the same breath, relevant to how [[Revelation]] is understood to operate.

"Succor" (7:12) — from a root meaning to run to someone's aid — casts the Atonement as active rescue rather than passive payment.

The phrase "at Jerusalem which is the land of our forefathers" (7:10) admits more than one reading: "Jerusalem" may denote the city itself, identified by its surrounding ancestral land, or a broader region. The verse alone doesn't resolve which is intended, and the passage never names Bethlehem.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Path imagery threads through the sermon: the people are told to prepare the Lord's way and "walk in his paths, which are straight" (v.9); Alma later perceives them already "in the path which leads to the kingdom of God" (v.19), grounds that path in God's own unchanging "course" (v.20), and closes by urging them to "walk blameless before him" (v.22).

Verse 6 ("I trust that ye...," repeated four times) and verse 19 ("I perceive that ye...," repeated three times) form a real, smaller-scale echo — Alma's opening hopes becoming, by the sermon's end, a stated verdict — though the parallel is confined to those two verses rather than spanning all of vv.17-20.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual/Theological Comparison *(tentative)*
> The Christological detail in Alma 7:10-13 (virgin birth to Mary, vicarious suffering, taking upon himself sins/sickness/death) is presented as prophecy dated internally to about 83 B.C., roughly 83 years before the events described; the passage's precision and phrasing align closely with later New Testament Christian doctrine.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · disputed, well-hedged but evidentially thin

> [!evidence] Internal Textual/Geographic Consistency *(tentative)*
> Alma 7:10 states Christ 'shall be born of Mary, at Jerusalem which is the land of our forefathers' rather than naming Bethlehem, the town identified in the New Testament (Luke 2:4-7) and in Micah 5:2 as Jesus' birthplace; the verse's own qualifying phrase 'which is the land of our forefathers' suggests 'Jerusalem' is used here as a regional/land designation rather than the specific city, matching a broader Book of Mormon pattern of naming a 'land of [city]' region.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Alma open by explaining his political circumstances before preaching doctrine — what does this framing do for his authority with this audience?
- How does Alma's admission of limited knowledge in verse 8 affect the way his subsequent testimony should be read?
- What distinct purposes are assigned to Christ's suffering, death, assumption of infirmity, and bearing of sin in verses 11-13?
- Since Alma regards this audience as already believing and diligent, why does he still call them to repentance and warn them about uncleanness (v.21)?
- Does the promise in verse 16 describe a one-time ordinance, or a course begun in baptism and continued by keeping the commandments?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Alma 7:11-12 with [[Isaiah 53]] and [[Hebrews 4]]. Alma 7:11's specific wording — Christ will "take upon him the pains and the sicknesses of his people" — tracks the KJV New Testament's [[Matthew 8]]:17 ("himself took our infirmities, and bare our sicknesses," quoting Isaiah) more closely than it tracks Isaiah 53:4 itself ("borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows"). Likewise, Alma 7:10's "overshadowed...by the power of the Holy Ghost...the Son of God" closely echoes the specific KJV wording of [[Luke 1]]:35. These are among the standard textual observations raised about the King James Bible's influence on the Book of Mormon's English wording, worth naming specifically rather than left as a vague, unspecified 'parallel.'

Compare the faith/hope/charity triad (7:24) with [[Moroni 10]] and with its biblical source in 1 Corinthians 13:13 — a well-known Christian formula, not a distinctive link unique to these two chapters. Alma 7:20's "one eternal round" also has verified textual overlap with [[D&C 3]]. For the audience's likely location (the land of Gideon) and the "land of Jerusalem" question in v.10, see the surrounding chapters and [[Alma 5]] for the Zarahemla background referenced in vv.3-5.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
