# Alma 61 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 61]] · My notes: [[Alma 61 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Pahoran tells Moroni of the insurrection and rebellion against the government—The king-men take Zarahemla and are in league with the Lamanites—Pahoran asks for military aid against the rebels. About 62 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Epistle introduced (v. 1):** The narrator identifies the document as Pahoran's reply.
2. **The crisis reported (vv. 2–8):** Pahoran describes rebellion, his flight to Gideon, the gathering of freemen, the seizure of Zarahemla, and an alliance with the Lamanites.
3. **Censure and principles addressed (vv. 9–14):** He rejects personal anger, affirms liberty, and explains when he believes submission or armed resistance is justified.
4. **Operational plan (vv. 15–18):** Moroni is to leave most forces with Lehi and Teancum, gather reinforcements, join Pahoran, retake Zarahemla, and restore provisions.
5. **Deliberation and closing (vv. 19–21):** Pahoran discusses the justice of fighting fellow Nephites and closes with reassurance for the commanders.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Pahoran's account joins [[Faith]] with organized action. He expects divine deliverance, yet his plan requires delegated command, provisions, recruitment, movement, and redistribution of food (vv. 13, 15–18, 21).

His defense of [[Freedom and Liberty]] is conditional and subordinate to God. He says submission would be proper if divine justice required it, disclaims a desire to shed the blood of those who do not attack, and distinguishes evils resistible by words from rebellions he believes require swords (vv. 10–14). This supports neither unconditional pacifism nor unrestricted violence, but it is not a complete theory of just war.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The expression “stand fast in that liberty wherewith God hath made them free” (v. 21), together with “the yoke of bondage” (v. 12), closely resembles Galatians 5:1. This is strong evidence of an English textual relationship with biblical diction. Competing explanations include deliberate scriptural allusion, a translator's use of familiar King James language, and direct imitation by a modern author; the verbal parallel alone cannot decide among them.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is an embedded letter framed by the narrator in verse 1 and closed by Pahoran in verse 21. Within the letter, Pahoran moves from sympathy for Moroni's afflictions, through correction of his incomplete information, to refusal of anger and a proposal for joint action. This can be read as conflict repair, although the text does not determine whether his warmth reflects settled character, sincere brotherhood, urgent strategy, or some combination of these.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Which statements in the chapter are narrator-level assertions, and which are claims made by Pahoran in his own defense?
- What limits does Pahoran place on violence in verses 10–14, and where might readers disagree about applying those limits?
- How does the practical plan in verses 15–18 qualify any reading of deliverance as passive?
- Does Pahoran's gracious response demonstrate settled character, strategic rhetoric, or both?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
