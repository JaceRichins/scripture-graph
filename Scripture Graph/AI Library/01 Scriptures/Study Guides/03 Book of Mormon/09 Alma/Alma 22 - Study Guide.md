## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **Verses 1–4:** Narrative transition and Aaron's audience with the king.
- **Verses 5–11:** The king's questions and the identification of God with the “Great Spirit” understood as Creator.
- **Verses 12–14:** Scriptural exposition of creation, fall, redemption, atonement, and resurrection.
- **Verses 15–18:** The king's desire, Aaron's invitation, and the king's prayer.
- **Verses 19–23:** Crisis, competing interpretations of the king's collapse, restoration, and household conversion.
- **Verses 24–27:** The king pacifies the multitude and enables wider preaching.
- **Verses 27–34:** Geographic and strategic description.
- **Verse 35:** Explicit return to the larger missionary account.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Aaron's teaching makes the fall intelligible only in relation to the already-prepared plan of redemption. Humanity cannot independently merit release from its fallen condition, while Christ's sufferings and death answer both sin and death. Yet the response is not passive: Aaron calls for [[Faith]], [[Repentance]], and [[Prayer]]. The king's phrase “give away all my sins” gives repentance a relational purpose—knowing God—rather than depicting it merely as fear of punishment. His request to be raised from the dead also joins personal [[Conversion]] with hope in the [[Resurrection]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Great Spirit” functions as a point of contact rather than the endpoint of Aaron's explanation; Aaron immediately supplies the content “created all things.” The king's repeated conditional “if” is also significant: his prayer is sincere without pretending to knowledge he does not yet possess. The contrast between giving up possessions in verse 15 and giving away sins in verse 18 sharpens the distinction between costly external sacrifice and inward repentance. “As if he were dead” reports an appearance, not a medical diagnosis or a full explanation of the event.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several reversals and expansions give the chapter shape. Aaron bows before the king, after which the king bows before God. A private question becomes household conversion and then public preaching. The queen, servants, and Aaron interpret the same collapse from different amounts of knowledge, showing how ambiguity turns into fear and threatened violence. In the geographic section, “nearly surrounded” is repeated first for Nephite exposure to Lamanites and then for the lands' relation to water; the description culminates in control of the narrow northern passage.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Linguistic/Terminological *(tentative)*
> The term 'Great Spirit' used repeatedly for Deity in this chapter (as also in Ammon's teaching of Lamoni) matches a stock phrase common in 19th-century American discourse about Native American religion. Critics cite this as possible evidence of 19th-century cultural influence on the text's composition; a faithful reading holds it is simply Joseph Smith's translation idiom for a genuine underlying ancient concept. The term's mere presence does not decide between these explanations.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · Disputed and externally under-sourced

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Aaron begin with belief in God before moving to scripture, creation, fall, and redemption?
- What is gained—and what may be lost—when a missionary adopts an inquirer's existing religious term?
- Why does the king's offer change from surrendering his kingdom to surrendering his sins?
- How do unequal knowledge and fear drive the threatened violence in verses 19–22?
- Does the household's conversion represent individual conviction, the king's influence, or some combination the abbreviated account does not describe?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
