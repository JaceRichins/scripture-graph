# Alma 28 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 28]] · My notes: [[Alma 28 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Alma 28 closes the extended account of Ammon and his brethren ([[Alma 27]]–27) and immediately complicates any sense of triumph: after Nephite armies defend [[Jershon]], the Lamanites are driven back in a battle the narrator calls unprecedented "from the time Lehi left Jerusalem" (v. 2), but the account dwells instead on the human cost—mass casualties, national mourning, fasting, and [[Prayer]] (vv. 1–6). It then widens into a fifteen-year retrospective (vv. 9–12) and closes with the narrator's own reflection on inequality, diligence, sorrow, and joy (vv. 13–14). The chapter also functions as a hinge, closing out both the missionary account of Ammon and his brethren and the war narrative at the same chronological point—the end of the fifteenth year of the reign of the judges (vv. 7–9).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Battle and return (vv. 1–3):** [[Jershon]] is defended; both sides suffer heavy losses, and the Lamanites are driven back.
2. **Communal lament (vv. 4–6):** The narrator names specific bereaved relationships—widows and husbands, fathers and sons, daughters and brothers—and records a time of solemnity, fasting, and prayer.
3. **Nested closings (vv. 7–9):** The fifteenth year of the judges is closed (v. 7), the account of Ammon's mission is summarized and blessed (v. 8), and both the war record and the year are closed again (v. 9). This doubled formula may reflect two narrative threads being drawn together, though the text does not specify a compositional history.
4. **Fifteen-year reckoning (vv. 10–12):** The narrator surveys cumulative losses across all fifteen years of the judges and contrasts two responses to bereavement.
5. **Editorial lessons (vv. 13–14):** Twin "thus we see" statements draw explicit conclusions about sin, Satan's influence, diligent labor, sorrow, and joy.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verses 11–12 contrast two responses to the loss of loved ones: mourners who fear their dead are "consigned to a state of endless wo," and mourners who "truly mourn... yet... rejoice and exult in the hope, and even know... that they are raised to dwell at the right hand of God, in a state of never-ending happiness"—both said to rest on "the promises of the Lord." Notably, hope does not erase grief here: the second group still truly mourns.

Verse 13 attributes "the great inequality of man" to "sin and transgression, and the power of the devil, which comes by the cunning plans which he hath devised to ensnare the hearts of men" ([[Satan and Temptation]]). In its immediate context this most plausibly refers to the contrasting spiritual destinies just described in vv. 11–12, though the chapter does not rule out a broader reading tied to unequal wartime suffering—the text leaves this genuinely open.

Verse 14 answers this inequality with "the great call of diligence of men to labor in the vineyards of the Lord," and closes by pairing sorrow over death with joy in "the light of Christ unto life" ([[Light of Christ]]). Readers should be cautious not to use vv. 11–12 as a formula for judging the eternal state of any particular deceased person; the text records the narrator's theological framing of communal grief, not a general method of discernment.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 5 catalogs grief through a chain of specific kinship pairs—widow/husband, father/son, daughter/brother, brother/father—rather than a single generic statement, giving the mourning a cumulative rhythm. The chapter's scope widens in three steps: one battle, then fifteen years of bloodshed, then a general statement about humanity, turning a specific historical episode into moral reflection. The repeated "thus we see" formula in vv. 13–14 explicitly marks the narrator's own editorial lessons, closing with balanced, antithetical clauses—"sorrow because of death and destruction among men, and joy because of the light of Christ unto life"—that hold the chapter's two moods in tension rather than resolving them.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the narrator pause the war account to describe the mourning of individual families (vv. 4–6) rather than moving straight to a theological summary?

Does "the inequality of man" in verse 13 refer chiefly to the divergent spiritual destinies of vv. 11–12, or also to the unequal suffering of war itself—and does the text let us decide?

What does it mean that hopeful mourners in verse 12 both "truly mourn" and "rejoice and exult in the hope, and even know"? How might genuine grief and settled hope coexist without either one hollowing out the other?

Why might the record close the fifteenth year twice (vv. 7, 9), with the summary of Ammon's mission placed between the two notices?

How does the chapter move from a specific battle, to fifteen years of loss, to a general call to labor in the Lord's vineyard (v. 14)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
