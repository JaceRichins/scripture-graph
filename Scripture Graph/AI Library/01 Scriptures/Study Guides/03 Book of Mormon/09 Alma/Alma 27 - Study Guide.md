# Alma 27 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 27]] · My notes: [[Alma 27 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Alma 27 resolves a major phase of the mission begun in [[Alma 17]]. After violence is redirected toward the unarmed converts, Ammon proposes migration and inquires of the Lord. The community then leaves with its livestock, waits near the Nephite border, and sends representatives ahead. The Nephites collectively grant Jershon as an inheritance and provide military protection on condition that the newcomers contribute substance toward maintaining the armies. The chapter closes by naming them the people of Ammon and interpreting their transformed conduct through [[Repentance]], [[Faith]], [[Hope]], and [[Resurrection]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Persecution and proposed escape (vv. 1–5):** failed war against the Nephites is redirected toward the unarmed converts.

2. **Accountability, law, and revelation (vv. 6–13):** the king fears Nephite retribution and offers slavery; Ammon invokes the law against slavery and seeks the Lord's direction.

3. **Migration and reunion (vv. 14–20):** the refugees wait near the border while Ammon's party meets Alma; the narrative pauses over their joy.

4. **Public admission and reciprocal protection (vv. 21–26):** the voice of the people grants Jershon and establishes terms for defense.

5. **Narrative appraisal (vv. 27–30):** honesty, faithfulness, nonviolence, and hope in Christ characterize the people of Ammon.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter depicts repentance as more than regret. The converts acknowledge past murders, fear repeating them, accept displacement, contribute to those who defend them, and remain firm despite lethal danger. Yet the narrative does not portray repentance as self-destruction: Ammon rejects enslavement and leads them to seek mercy and safety.

Verses 28–29 connect courage before death directly to [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] and [[Resurrection]]. Death remains painful, but it loses its ultimate victory. This is a claim about hope amid suffering, not a denial of suffering's reality.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated word **brethren** is morally important. It describes former enemies as kin: the persecutors attack their brethren, the refugees appeal to Nephite brethren, and the Nephites protect their brethren. It also cautions against casually turning the converts' refusal into an unqualified theory of universal pacifism, since the stated abhorrence repeatedly concerns shedding the blood of their brethren.

The renaming from Anti-Nephi-Lehi to **people of Ammon** accompanies both incorporation and distinction. They are numbered among the Nephites and the church, yet retain a lasting group name.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The reunion in verses 16–19 forms an emotional center between danger and civic settlement. Ammon's joy exhausts his strength, while the others experience great joy without the same bodily collapse. The narrator pauses the action to associate such joy with penitence and humble seeking.

The migration can also be compared with an [[Exodus and Deliverance]] pattern: persecution, divine direction, departure with possessions, wilderness travel, and reception into a new land. This is a suggestive thematic resemblance, not evidence by itself of deliberate Exodus imitation or historical dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Narrative Characterization
> Within the narrative, the converts' former murders, present repentance, refusal to kill, honesty, faithfulness, and lack of terror before death form a coherent portrait of moral transformation.
> — confidence 1.0 · Broadly affirmed as narrative characterization · [[Narrative Characterization in Alma 27|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual And Ethical
> The chapter provides strong textual evidence that the people of Ammon maintain an enduring refusal to shed blood or take up arms, rooted in repentance and abhorrence of killing. Because the wording repeatedly concerns violence against their 'brethren' and this community's particular history, the passage alone does not settle whether the narrator teaches universal pacifism for every person and circumstance.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.9 · Strong textual observation; universal application disputed · [[Textual And Ethical in Alma 27|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the king imagine slavery as fitting restitution, and why does Ammon answer with both law and mercy?

- What responsibilities do the refugees and receiving society each assume in verses 22–24?

- Does the repeated phrase “their brethren” limit the scope of the converts' nonviolence, or deepen its universal moral force?

- Why does the narrator place the reunion and discourse on joy in the middle of a refugee-settlement narrative?

- How does the community remain distinct while also becoming part of the Nephites and the church?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Alma 24]] for the converts' covenant and burial of weapons, [[Alma 25]] for the violence preceding their departure, and [[Alma 28]] for the consequences of defending the people settled at Jershon. [[Alma 53]], [[Alma 57]], and [[Alma 58]] continue the history of their sons. Reading these chapters together clarifies both the converts' particular covenant and the military burden assumed by the Nephites.
