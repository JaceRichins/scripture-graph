# Alma 62 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 62]] · My notes: [[Alma 62 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Alma 62 resolves both fronts of the crisis created in [[Alma 60]] and [[Alma 61]]. Moroni and Pahoran suppress the rebellion at Zarahemla, reinforce endangered armies, recover Nephihah, and drive the remaining Lamanite force from Nephite territory. The cost remains visible in Teancum's death and in the narrator's summary of years of war, famine, dissension, and affliction. The chapter then devotes substantial attention to reconstruction: military command passes peacefully to Moronihah, civil offices resume, Helaman returns to preaching, the church is reestablished, and the people prosper without immediately succumbing to pride.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Internal restoration (vv. 1–11):** Moroni joins Pahoran, defeats Pachus, restores the judgment-seat, and enforces wartime law.
2. **Reinforcement and captive covenants (vv. 12–18):** armies and food are sent to threatened fronts; captured Lamanites covenant against renewed war and are resettled.
3. **Recovery of Nephihah (vv. 19–30):** reconnaissance, nighttime infiltration, flight of the defenders, and voluntary integration of prisoners.
4. **Final campaign and Teancum's death (vv. 31–38):** the Lamanite forces are concentrated and expelled after Teancum kills Ammoron and is killed.
5. **Moral assessment of the war (vv. 39–41):** the same prolonged suffering hardens some people and humbles others.
6. **Reconstruction (vv. 42–52):** fortification, succession, preaching, baptism, legal regulation, humble prosperity, and Helaman's death.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's clearest teaching about [[Adversity]] is its refusal to treat suffering as mechanically sanctifying. Alma 62:41 says prolonged war hardened many and softened many others. Affliction supplies a setting for response, but the text does not say that pain itself guarantees [[Humility]].

Verses 44–47 connect social recovery with [[Repentance]], [[Baptism]], preaching, church order, and civil regulation. Spiritual renewal does not replace material security or lawful government; it accompanies them. Verses 48–51 then present prosperity without immediate [[Pride]]. Remembering deliverance and practicing continual [[Prayer]] accompany continued humility.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Freedom language appears in several distinct settings. Citizens gather to prevent bondage (vv. 4–5); rebels are punished for fighting against the country and denying its freedom (vv. 9–10); Teancum is remembered as a friend to liberty (v. 37); and prisoners seek to become a free people (v. 27). These uses show that [[Freedom and Liberty]] includes national independence, political loyalty, and a change in social status. They also expose a difficult tension: the government protects freedom through compulsory wartime law and capital punishment.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter contains two nighttime operations that can be compared cautiously. At Nephihah, Moroni scouts the defenses, prepares cords and ladders, and coordinates an infiltration that the narrator says costs no Nephite lives (Alma 62:20–26). Later, while the armies decline to attempt a night stratagem, Teancum acts in anger, enters the enemy position, kills Ammoron, and is killed (Alma 62:35–36). The differing motives, scale, and outcomes make a contrast between coordinated strategy and individual action plausible, but the narrator does not explicitly state that this is the intended lesson. Teancum's eulogy in verse 37 also preserves a substantial heroic reading of his final act.

The ending compresses time. Detailed events close the thirty-first year in verse 39, while verse 52 advances to Helaman's death in the thirty-fifth year. Verses 48–51 also offer a notable instance of prosperity accompanied by remembrance, prayer, and humility rather than immediate pride; treating this as a deliberately constructed contrast with other chapters requires a broader literary comparison.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should readers distinguish the narrator's description of wartime executions as lawful and expedient from a universal endorsement of such policies?

Why do the same years of war harden some people but soften others (v. 41)? What role might agency, prior character, community, or remembered deliverance play?

What does the voluntary language in verses 27–29 contribute to the account of former prisoners joining the people of Ammon?

Why does the chapter pair the restoration of military security with renewed preaching, church organization, and legal regulation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

[[Alma 51]] supplies relevant background for the king-men conflict and for Teancum's earlier campaigns, but any claim that its nighttime episode was intentionally mirrored here should be treated as a literary interpretation.
