# Alma 11 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 11]] · My notes: [[Alma 11 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Nephite monetary system is set forth—Amulek contends with Zeezrom—Christ will not save people in their sins—Only those who inherit the kingdom of heaven are saved—All men will rise in immortality—There is no death after the Resurrection. About 82 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Legal and economic setting (vv. 1–20):** judicial wages, debt enforcement, reckoning and measures, and profit-driven corruption.
2. **Attempted entrapment (vv. 21–25):** Zeezrom offers six onties for a denial of God; Amulek accuses him of concealing his true intent.
3. **Questions about God and salvation (vv. 26–40):** the exchange addresses one God, the coming Son, salvation, and belief in Christ.
4. **Universal resurrection and judgment (vv. 41–45):** temporal death is overcome for all, body and spirit are reunited, and all appear before the divine bar.
5. **Audience response (v. 46):** the people are astonished and Zeezrom begins to tremble. The exchange continues in [[Alma 12]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Amulek distinguishes universal release from temporal death from nonuniversal salvation. All are raised and restored, including the wicked and righteous, but eternal life is associated with believing on Christ's name, and no unclean thing can inherit the kingdom of heaven. [[Resurrection]] therefore leads to, rather than eliminates, [[Final Judgment]].

The chapter also places several formulations about deity together. Amulek affirms one God, calls the Son the Eternal Father of heaven and earth, and later names Christ the Son, God the Father, and the Holy Spirit as one Eternal God. These statements are central to [[The Godhead]], but Alma 11 does not provide a technical explanation resolving every later theological question. Interpretations based on creation, covenantal fatherhood, identity, or other models should therefore be identified as interpretations rather than as the chapter's explicit formulation.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The principal reckoning values are organized around equivalent base units: a senine of gold equals a senum of silver; seon and amnor equal two base units; shum and ezrom equal four. Limnah and onti are each described as the value of the preceding units "all." Reading that phrase as their sum naturally yields seven base units, but the number seven is inferred rather than written in the text.

The smaller values proceed by halves: shiblon is half a senum, shiblum half a shiblon, and leah half a shiblum. An antion of gold equals three shiblons. Because the passage moves among pieces, reckoning, measurement, and grain, it does not establish that every named value was a minted coin or that shiblon was exclusively a silver unit.

Amulek's contrast between being saved **in** sins and Christ taking upon himself believers' transgressions is also important. His argument is not that Christ lacks power, but that salvation does not contradict the declared exclusion of uncleanness from heaven.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The reckoning table gives a possible internal benchmark for Zeezrom's offer. If an onti represents the sum of the preceding 1-, 2-, and 4-unit values, six onties equal 42 senums—42 days at the stated daily wage of a judge. This helps a reader perceive the offer as substantial within the chapter's own terms, though purchasing power and Zeezrom's income remain unknown.

A courtroom pattern can also be traced without assuming deliberate formal architecture. The chapter begins with human judges, evidence, debts, and sanctions; Zeezrom then attempts to create grounds against Amulek; and the discourse ends with all people arraigned before the divine bar. The narrative also reverses Zeezrom's position: the would-be examiner is accused of concealed intent, the audience is astonished, and Zeezrom begins to tremble.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Doctrinal Development *(tentative)*
> Read in isolation, Amulek's identification of the Son as "the very Eternal Father" (v39) sits in apparent tension with more fully differentiated later teaching that the Father and Son are distinct, separate personages. This passage is a genuine point of discussion regarding how Godhead doctrine is presented across different parts of the Restoration canon.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · Disputed theological interpretation

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Integration *(tentative)*
> On the chapter's own conversion table, Zeezrom's offer of six onties equals 42 senums. Since one senum is the stated daily wage of a judge, the offer is equivalent to 42 judge-days of pay. This strongly supports reading the monetary exposition as narrative preparation for understanding the magnitude of the attempted bribe, although it offers little independent evidence about the account's historical provenance.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.8 · Arithmetic plausible; narrative purpose interpretive
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the narrator place the reckoning system immediately before Zeezrom's offer? If "as great as them all" means the sum of the preceding values, how does the resulting amount affect the scene?

What distinctions does Amulek make among redemption, resurrection, eternal life, inheritance in heaven, and judgment?

How should Alma 11:39 and Alma 11:44 be read together when studying [[The Godhead]]?

How does the chapter's portrayal of profit-distorted human justice compare with its description of [[Final Judgment]]?

Does verse 46 imply that additional words were omitted, or does it merely qualify where the narrator's written excerpt ends?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Alma 12]] for the immediate continuation of the encounter with Zeezrom and further teaching about death, judgment, and redemption. Compare [[Alma 40]] for a fuller discussion of resurrection, bodily restoration, and the interval between death and resurrection. [[Alma 1]] supplies related institutional context for judicial compensation, while [[Mosiah 16]] also connects Christ's resurrection with humanity's resurrection and judgment. [[Alma 15]] continues Zeezrom's personal narrative. [[Revelation 22]] contains a close instance of the title sequence "the beginning and the end, the first and the last," though similar divine-title language occurs elsewhere in the biblical tradition and the parallel does not establish a specific literary dependence.
