# Alma 40 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 40]] · My notes: [[Alma 40 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Christ brings to pass the resurrection of all men—The righteous dead go to paradise and the wicked to outer darkness to await the day of their resurrection—All things will be restored to their proper and perfect frame in the Resurrection. About 74 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The appointed resurrection (vv. 1–5):** Christ brings resurrection, though its detailed schedule belongs to God.
2. **The question of the interval (vv. 6–10):** Alma redirects attention from the number of resurrection times to what happens between death and resurrection.
3. **Paradise and misery (vv. 11–14):** all spirits return to God, then experience contrasting temporary conditions.
4. **Meaning of the first resurrection (vv. 15–21):** Alma distinguishes a figurative use of resurrection from bodily reunion and qualifies his view of its timing.
5. **Restoration and judgment (vv. 22–26):** every person is bodily restored, judged according to works, and assigned a corresponding outcome.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter distinguishes [[Death and the Spirit World]] from both [[Resurrection]] and [[Final Judgment]]. Paradise is a genuine state of rest, but it is not yet the final embodied kingdom; misery is likewise described as continuing until resurrection. Resurrection restores the whole person rather than freeing a soul permanently from embodiment. The universal scope of resurrection does not erase moral accountability: verses 13, 21, and 26 connect choice, works, judgment, and consequence.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The word **restoration** governs verses 22–24 and means bodily reunion and completeness before it is developed more broadly in [[Alma 41]]. The paired expressions “mortal/immortality” and “corruption/incorruption” connect the chapter's language with [[1 Corinthians 15]]. That lexical parallel is secure; whether it reflects translation into familiar biblical idiom or direct literary dependence is a separate and disputed historical question.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Alma's repetitions are functional rather than merely redundant. “A time appointed,” “it mattereth not,” and references to what God knows narrow the argument toward the pastorally relevant point: an intermediate state exists and resurrection is assured even when its calendar is not disclosed. Verses 15–20 then stage a terminological correction—first conceding that one event “may be termed” a resurrection, then identifying the intended first resurrection as reunion of body and soul.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Epistemic Calibration
> The discourse carefully separates what Alma says he knows from unresolved chronology: he reports diligent inquiry and angelic disclosure, calls some mysteries known only to God, treats the number of resurrection times as immaterial, and labels the timing of the righteous resurrection as his opinion.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · Textual pattern broadly accepted · [[Textual Epistemic Calibration in Alma 40|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Consistency / Rhetorical Pattern *(tentative)*
> Alma's repeated hedging language across the chapter ('it mattereth not,' 'I do not say,' 'I give it as my opinion,' calling the topic a 'mystery') is internally consistent, distinguishing what he claims to know with certainty (angelic revelation about the intermediate state, vv. 11-14) from what he offers only tentatively (timing/order of bodily reunion, vv. 19-21).
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · Rhetorical pattern accepted but over-systematized
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Alma regard the existence of an appointed resurrection as sufficient even without knowing its complete chronology?
- How does the temporary paradise of verses 12–14 differ from the kingdom of God in verse 25?
- What interpretive limits should be respected when moving from “proper and perfect frame” to modern questions about age, disability, or physical appearance?
- What can Alma's phrase “I give it as my opinion” teach about distinguishing revelation from faithful inference?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the bodily-restoration wording with [[Alma 11]], the meaning of restoration with [[Alma 41]], and the first resurrection with [[Mosiah 15]]. [[D&C 138]] offers a later, more detailed account of the spirit world, but its additional details should not be retroactively treated as statements expressly made in Alma 40.
