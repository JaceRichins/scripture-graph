# Alma 47 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 47]] · My notes: [[Alma 47 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Amalickiah uses treachery, murder, and intrigue to become king of the Lamanites—The Nephite dissenters are more wicked and ferocious than the Lamanites. About 72 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Mobilization and concealed intent (vv. 1–8):** The king orders war; most of the people resist; Amalickiah receives command while planning to seize the throne.
2. **Absorption of Lehonti's army (vv. 9–20):** Four invitations culminate in negotiation, staged encirclement, gradual poisoning, and Amalickiah's succession to command.
3. **Regicide and redirected loyalty (vv. 21–31):** A token of peace is weaponized, the innocent servants are blamed, and the king's supporters are induced to pursue them.
4. **Manufactured legitimacy (vv. 32–35):** Complicit witnesses satisfy the queen; Amalickiah marries her and is acknowledged as king.
5. **Narratorial coda (v. 36):** The account closes with a broader judgment about Nephite dissenters.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter offers a sobering study of [[Agency]]. Amalickiah seldom removes choice entirely; instead, he manufactures danger, conceals relevant facts, and presents options calculated to elicit the decisions he wants. This does not erase the responsibility of other actors, but it shows why meaningful moral judgment must consider coercion and information asymmetry.

It is also a negative study of [[Honesty]] and [[Government and Laws]]. Institutions meant to provide order—military command, succession, diplomatic gestures, witness testimony, and royal marriage—can be exploited when participants judge appearances without access to independent evidence.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The narrator repeatedly separates appearance from reality: the king "supposed" Amalickiah had fulfilled his command; the servants bowed "as if" in reverence; Amalickiah "pretended" anger; and the conspirators treated flight as self-validating proof of guilt. These signals give the reader knowledge denied to the characters and make deception, rather than surprise, the source of tension.

The recurring expressions of Amalickiah's desire also organize the action. His intention is disclosed before each apparent reversal, and later events are marked as the very outcomes he wanted. The result is sustained dramatic irony: characters believe they are responding to events while the reader sees that Amalickiah has arranged those events.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The mount Antipas episode repeatedly describes Lehonti being asked to "come down." Literally, this is required by the terrain. Literarily, the repetition can also suggest an incremental loss of strategic advantage: Amalickiah advances toward the camp, reduces the apparent danger, permits guards, and secures the meeting he could not initially compel. That symbolic reading is useful but should remain an interpretation rather than a demonstrated formal device.

The peace-token scene is an especially sharp inversion. A customary act designed to communicate safety creates the physical proximity required for murder. Later, the queen's request for witnesses is similarly inverted: a safeguard for truth becomes effective cover because every witness brought before her is complicit.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. At what point does Lehonti's defensible negotiation become a dangerous concession, and what information available to the reader was unavailable to him?
2. Why does the narrator disclose Amalickiah's plan so early instead of preserving the coup as a surprise?
3. What makes testimony credible? In verses 33–34, the witnesses are numerous and their story is internally consistent, yet they are conspirators.
4. How should verse 36 be read—as a limited comment about the dissenters in this narrative, a broader theological pattern, or polemical generalization by the narrator?
5. Which institutions fail because their rules are defective, and which fail because people exploit otherwise useful customs?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
