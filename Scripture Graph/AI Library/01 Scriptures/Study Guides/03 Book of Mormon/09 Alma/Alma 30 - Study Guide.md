# Alma 30 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 30]] · My notes: [[Alma 30 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Korihor, the anti-Christ, ridicules Christ, the Atonement, and the spirit of prophecy—He teaches that there is no God, no fall of man, no penalty for sin, and no Christ—Alma testifies that Christ will come and that all things denote there is a God—Korihor demands a sign and is struck dumb—The devil had appeared to Korihor as an angel and taught him what to say—Korihor is trodden down and dies. About 76–74 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Aftermath and peace (vv. 1–5):** burial, mourning, prayer, obedience, and political calm.
2. **Legal setting and Korihor's message (vv. 6–18):** freedom of belief is explained before his teachings and their reported effects are summarized.
3. **Regional confrontations (vv. 19–29):** Jershon expels him; Gideon's authorities question and transfer him.
4. **Debate before Alma (vv. 30–45):** priestly gain, knowledge of God, evidence, and signs are contested.
5. **Sign and confession (vv. 46–55):** Korihor is struck dumb and gives a written account of his deception.
6. **Public reversal and death (vv. 56–60):** his followers repent, and his death receives an explicit narrator's moral.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Belief is not a crime, but the boundary is imprecise.** Nephite law punished murder, robbery, theft, and adultery, but not disbelief itself (30:7–11) — yet Korihor is bound and moved between Jershon, Gideon, and Zarahemla without a named charge, leaving the relationship between protected belief and punished conduct only partly explained. See [[Government and Laws]] and [[Agency]].
- **Priestcraft answered by personal example.** Alma refutes the charge of preaching for gain with a specific claim: years of self-supporting labor and no pay beyond lawful judgment-seat wages (30:32–35). This answers the financial accusation directly but does not by itself answer Korihor's broader claims about inherited tradition and priestly authority (30:23–28).
- **Evidence versus sign-seeking.** Alma sets the cumulative witness of personal and communal [[Testimony]], scripture, and the ordered natural world (30:39–45) against Korihor's demand for a specially produced sign — then gives one anyway (30:47–50). Readers should distinguish this punitive, protective sign from a philosophical proof of God; the chapter offers both an argument and a miracle, and they are not the same kind of evidence.
- **Deception through counterfeit revelation.** Korihor attributes his message to a being who appeared to him "in the form of an angel" (30:53), tying the chapter to [[Satan and Temptation]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several epistemic verbs organize the dispute: *know*, *believe*, *see*, *deny*, *testimony*, *witness*, and *sign*. Korihor first argues that people cannot know what they do not see, later says that he does not believe in God, and finally writes that he always knew God existed. That movement may portray deliberate dishonesty, self-deception, unstable conviction, or some combination of the three. The text itself supplies all three motifs—lying, deception by the devil, and eventual belief in the message he repeated.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly contrasts speech with imposed silence. Korihor succeeds through preaching, flattering words, accusations, and demands; the sign removes his utterance, forcing his confession into writing. His silencing also ends his capacity to spread the message. The sequence resembles [[Jacob 7]], where Sherem challenges prophecy concerning Christ, demands a sign, confesses, and dies. The resemblance is clear, but its historical or compositional explanation remains open.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Intertextuality
> Alma 30 and Jacob 7 exhibit a substantial narrative pattern: an opponent challenges prophecy about Christ, demands a sign, is struck by divine power, confesses deception, and dies. This is strong evidence of literary relationship or deliberate typological patterning, but it does not determine whether the similarity reflects repeated history, editorial shaping, a conventional cautionary narrative, or direct literary reuse.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · confirmed but ending parallel overstated · [[Literary Intertextuality in Alma 30|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Legal/Political Statement *(tentative)*
> The chapter's description of a law that criminalizes only conduct and not belief (Alma 30:7-11) is cited by some readers as evidence of a sophisticated ancient legal distinction, while critics note the passage's articulation of neutral, equal-grounds treatment of belief reads similarly to post-Enlightenment religious-liberty language current in Joseph Smith's own time and place.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, unresolved by text alone

> [!evidence] Internal Philosophical/Theological Argument *(tentative)*
> Alma's appeal to the order and motion of the created world as proof of God (Alma 30:44) is a teleological/design argument of a kind attested across many ancient and classical philosophical traditions; its presence here shows the text engaging serious natural-theology reasoning rather than assertion alone.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.6 · disputed, properly hedged in original proposal

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Verses 7–11 declare that belief cannot be legally punished, yet Korihor is bound and transferred between jurisdictions (30:19–29) without an identified charge. How should readers reconcile the stated principle with its apparent application?
- Does Alma's appeal to testimony, scripture, and the created order (30:40–44) answer Korihor's demand for evidence, or do the two men simply start from different standards of what counts as evidence?
- Korihor first argues that people cannot know what they do not see (30:15), later flatly denies God (30:38), then confesses "I always knew that there was a God" (30:52). How do lying, satanic deception, and self-deception interact in his account?
- What does Alma's personal financial disclosure (30:32–35) accomplish that a simple denial would not — and what does it leave unanswered?
- The sign given to Korihor is simultaneously evidence, punishment, and prevention (30:47–50). What ethical questions does a sign that is also a disabling punishment raise?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
