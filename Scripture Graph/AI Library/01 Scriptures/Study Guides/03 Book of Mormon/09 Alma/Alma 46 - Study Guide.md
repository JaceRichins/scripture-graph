# Alma 46 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 46]] · My notes: [[Alma 46 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Alma 46 begins immediately after a military victory but shifts attention to internal collapse. Amalickiah gathers an angry coalition, promises offices to power-seeking judges, and draws members away from the church. Moroni answers with prayer, a written standard, a public covenant, and armed mobilization. The resulting defense of [[Freedom and Liberty]] is inseparable from [[Covenants]], religious identity, family protection, and [[Government and Laws]]. The chapter also preserves an ethical difficulty: a movement organized around freedom later executes captured dissenters who refuse its covenant.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Dissension and editorial diagnosis (vv. 1–10):** Amalickiah seeks kingship, judges seek power, church members defect, and the narrator explains the crisis through forgetfulness, evil influence, and flattery.
2. **Symbol, prayer, and covenant (vv. 11–27):** Moroni creates the title of liberty, prays, summons the people, and interprets their torn garments through Joseph's coat and remnant imagery.
3. **Pursuit and enforcement (vv. 28–37):** Amalickiah retreats, Moroni intercepts most of his followers, a covenant of freedom is imposed on the captives, and standards are raised throughout Nephite territory.
4. **Peace, mortality, and hope (vv. 38–41):** Church order returns, but the narrative closes by acknowledging death from illness and age while affirming hope in Christ.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter treats liberty as a divine blessing that remains morally conditional. Moroni does not claim unconditional protection: he says destruction will come if the people bring it upon themselves through transgression. The people's enacted covenant makes that condition personal.

Christian identity is similarly active rather than merely nominal. True believers gladly take Christ's name, while the garment covenant treats being ashamed of that name as covenant failure. This theme closely parallels [[Mosiah 5]] and belongs with [[Covenants]], [[Faith]], and [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]].

The closing verses distinguish confidence from direct description. Some die believing their souls are redeemed; the narrator then says those dying in Christ are happy in him, adding "as we must needs suppose." That wording expresses theological assurance while retaining an explicit note of inference.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The English text is held together by the recurring language of **memory**, **rending**, and **remnants**. The title begins "In memory"; Moroni twice urges the people to remember; torn coats become signs of possible communal destruction; and Joseph's preserved garment becomes an image of a preserved lineage. The pattern is clear at the level of the received English text. Whether every sound or word association reflects the source language cannot be determined from this passage alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The title of liberty turns an ordinary damaged coat into a layered public symbol. Its meaning develops in stages: inscription, prayer, visual display, covenant ceremony, scriptural interpretation, military standard, and finally repeated installation on towers. This progression joins private devotion, communal memory, and state action.

The narrator also alternates between narration and explicit interpretation. Verses 8–10 tell readers what the crisis means; verses 23–27 preserve Moroni's interpretation of the garment ritual; and verses 39–41 interpret mortality through redemption. These voices should be distinguished from bare event reporting when assessing the chapter's historical and theological claims.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Pattern
> The passage develops a sustained garment pattern: Moroni rends his coat, the people rend theirs, the garments symbolize a covenant curse, and Moroni interprets the action through Joseph's rent coat and a preserved remnant. The recurrence strongly supports deliberate literary shaping of this episode, while the source or language level at which the pattern arose remains uncertain.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · literary pattern accepted; authorship implications disputed · [[Literary Pattern in Alma 46|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Textual Consistency / Terminology *(tentative)*
> The narrative uses the term 'Christians' for a group living roughly 73-72 B.C., decades before Jesus's birth in the Book of Mormon's own chronology. This is presented internally as a name given by unbelievers because of the group's belief 'in Christ who should come' (v.15), i.e. a forward-looking messianic title rather than a claim that the historical Jesus had already appeared.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.8 · textual observation accepted; linguistic significance disputed

> [!evidence] Intertextual Detail Not Present In The Base Biblical Account *(tentative)*
> Alma 46:23-24 describes Joseph's coat as 'rent by his brethren into many pieces,' a detail more specific than the Genesis narrative, which describes the brothers stripping Joseph's coat and dipping it in goat's blood to deceive Jacob (Genesis 37:31-33) but does not describe the coat itself being torn into multiple pieces.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.8 · difference affirmed; origin and significance unresolved

> [!evidence] Claimed Non-Biblical Source Material *(tentative)*
> The Jacob prophecy quoted in Alma 46:24-26 about a preserved remnant of Joseph's seed has no parallel in the canonical Genesis text; the Book of Mormon narrative frames such material as drawn from records on the brass plates, which Nephite writers repeatedly describe as containing content beyond the Hebrew Bible as it now exists.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.6 · speculative provenance
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the chapter use "liberty" primarily for individual choice, constitutional order, communal self-rule, covenant fidelity, or some combination of these?
- How should the executions in verse 35 be evaluated in light of the armed revolt, threatened alliance with the Lamanites, and the chapter's stated defense of free government?
- Why does the narrator emphasize one wicked leader in verses 9–10 after identifying broader institutional motives in verses 4–7?
- What does the followers' doubt about the justice of Amalickiah's cause suggest about the relationship between moral legitimacy and political power?
- Why does the chapter end its account of public peace with fevers, medicinal plants, old age, and death?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
