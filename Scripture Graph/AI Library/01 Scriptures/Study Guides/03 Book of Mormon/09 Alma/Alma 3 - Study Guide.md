# Alma 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 3]] · My notes: [[Alma 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Amlicites had marked themselves according to the prophetic word—The Lamanites had been cursed for their rebellion—Men bring their own curses upon themselves—The Nephites defeat another Lamanite army. About 87–86 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Aftermath of battle (vv. 1–3):** burial, civilian casualties, destroyed livestock and grain, and bodies cast into Sidon.
2. **Marks, ancestry, and communal identity (vv. 4–12):** descriptions of Lamanites and Amlicites lead into an explanation of how the narrator classifies Lamanites and Nephites.
3. **Return to the Amlicites (vv. 13–19):** the narrator explicitly resumes the interrupted subject, quotes a prophecy, calls the Amlicites' action unwitting fulfillment, and generalizes it into self-incurred condemnation.
4. **Renewed war and temporary peace (vv. 20–24):** a second invading army is expelled while the wounded Alma remains behind.
5. **Year-end theological summary (vv. 25–27):** chronological closure becomes reflection on death, judgment, works, and chosen obedience.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The repentance clause in Alma 3:14 is essential to the passage's theology. Separation is not presented without exception: turning to God makes mercy possible, connecting the chapter to [[Repentance]] and [[Justice and Mercy]]. Verses 19 and 26–27 emphasize [[Agency]] and accountability by describing condemnation as self-incurred and reward as corresponding to works and allegiance. The closing description of the dead entering the eternal world connects the year's warfare with [[Death and the Spirit World]] and [[Final Judgment]].

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter's vocabulary does not permit a simple equation of "mark," "curse," skin color, ancestry, and personal guilt. These terms overlap, but they perform different functions in different verses. The Lamanite mark is described as ancestral and embodied (vv. 6–9); the Amlicites paint a visible mark upon themselves (vv. 4, 13); "called" language makes affiliation and belief relevant to classification (vv. 10–11, 17); and the curse is finally moralized as self-incurred condemnation (vv. 18–19). Careful reading should preserve these distinctions rather than forcing every occurrence into one definition.

The English wording of Alma 3:27 also resembles Romans 6:16, 23 by joining obedience to a chosen master with the receipt of wages. That parallel is useful for studying the scriptural register of the translation, but alone cannot determine whether it reflects an ancient intertext, the translator's biblical idiom, or shared Christian language.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The editorial sentence "Now we will return again to the Amlicites" in verse 13 exposes the chapter's digressive construction: the narrator pauses the battle account to explain group distinctions, then deliberately resumes the Amlicites. A second expansion occurs at the close. The compressed military chronicle of verses 20–25 gives way to the universalizing language of verses 26–27, transforming one year's casualties into a meditation on judgment. The repeated emphasis on what people bring upon themselves and whom they choose to obey links the Amlicite episode to that conclusion.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the repentance exception in verse 14 qualify the surrounding language of enduring separation?
- Which features of identity in verses 8–17 are genealogical, and which depend on belief, conduct, naming, intermarriage, or affiliation?
- Why does the narrator interrupt the Amlicite story with an explanation of Lamanite and Nephite classification?
- How does verse 19's language of self-incurred condemnation relate to the descendant consequences described in verses 6–10?
- The Amlicites do not know they are fulfilling the prophecy, yet the narrator calls their conduct open rebellion. How does the text relate ignorance of fulfillment to moral responsibility?
- What might the unequal treatment of bodies in verses 1 and 3 reveal about logistics or the narrative's perspective?
- Does "good spirit or a bad one" in verse 26 denote spiritual beings, competing influences, moral dispositions, or deliberately broad categories?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
