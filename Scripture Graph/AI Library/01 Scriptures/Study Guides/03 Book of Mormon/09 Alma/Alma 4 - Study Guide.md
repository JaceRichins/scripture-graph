# Alma 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 4]] · My notes: [[Alma 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Alma baptizes thousands of converts—Iniquity enters the Church, and the Church’s progress is hindered—Nephihah is appointed chief judge—Alma, as high priest, devotes himself to the ministry. About 86–83 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **Alma 4:1–5 — Affliction, remembrance, and growth:** Material and human losses produce mourning; the people interpret their suffering as judgment, remember their duty, and seek baptism.

- **Alma 4:6–10 — Prosperity and internal collapse:** Riches acquired through industry become occasions for costly display, pride, scorn, and persecution. Church progress begins to fail.

- **Alma 4:11–15 — Inequality and a faithful contrast:** Some neglect the needy, while humble believers provide material aid and look forward to Christ's [[Resurrection]].

- **Alma 4:16–20 — A change in leadership strategy:** Nephihah receives the judgment-seat; Alma retains the high priesthood and turns wholly to the word and testimony.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**[[Pride]]:** The chapter traces the classic pride pattern in compressed form: prosperity (v. 6) leads to social display and contempt (vv. 6–9), producing internal persecution serious enough that the narrator says it exceeded the pride of those outside the church (v. 9), and it damaged the church's credibility with unbelievers (v. 10).

**[[Government and Laws]] / [[Priesthood]]:** Alma transfers the judgment-seat to Nephihah while retaining the high priesthood for himself (vv. 16–20), because he "saw no way that he might reclaim them save it were in bearing down in pure testimony against them" (v. 19). This shows Alma choosing preaching over civil administration for this particular crisis; the text does not state a general theory that law can never address spiritual conditions — Nephihah is simultaneously empowered to enforce laws against "wickedness and...crimes" (v. 16).

**[[Service]] and [[Repentance and Forgiveness]]:** Verse 14 links "retaining a remission of...sins" to the ongoing charitable conduct described in verse 13 (imparting substance, feeding the hungry). This describes the humble group's continuing spiritual state; it should not be read as a complete, formal mechanism of forgiveness.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two qualifications matter. First, Alma 4:3 says the people “believed” their losses were divine judgments. The verse clearly reports their interpretation, but it does not explicitly describe a revelation confirming it. Second, “seeing no way” in Alma 4:19 presents Alma's crisis assessment and motivation; it need not mean that testimony is the only legitimate response to every social problem.

The phrase “according to the voice of the people” (Alma 4:16) locates Nephihah's authority within the judgeship system, but the chapter gives no procedural details about nomination, voting, or ratification.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is organized around reversals: war ends but suffering remains; suffering produces reform; growth is followed quickly by pride; members become a worse example than outsiders; and the chief judge relinquishes political authority to increase his religious influence. The contrast in Alma 4:12–14 places two communities side by side—those who turn away from need and those who turn toward it—making treatment of vulnerable people the clearest test of the chapter's competing forms of life.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Narrative Causal Attribution
> Alma 4 gives strong textual evidence for the narrator's view that misconduct by professed believers can damage a religious community's public witness: church wickedness is called a stumbling-block, and Alma sees its example as influencing unbelievers toward further iniquity.
> — confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted, correctly scoped · [[Explicit Narrative Causal Attribution in Alma 4|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intra-Textual Ethical Contrast
> The contrast between wealth obtained by industry, prideful treatment of others, and voluntary aid to the needy supports the interpretation that the chapter's ethical critique centers strongly on pride and the social use of possessions, not simply on productive labor itself.

> [!evidence] Internal Literary Consistency *(tentative)*
> The wealth-catalogue formula in Alma 4:6 ('fine silks, and...fine-twined linen...gold and...silver') recurs in comparable form across multiple Nephite-record chapters describing the onset of pride (e.g., verified overlaps of related material appear near Mosiah 11), suggesting a consistent, possibly editorial, literary convention within the Book of Mormon for marking the transition from prosperity to pride.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does Alma 4:3 endorse the people's interpretation of their afflictions as divine judgment, or simply report their belief?
- Why does rapid church growth (v. 5) fail to prevent pride and persecution the very next year (vv. 6–10)?
- What distinguishes "retaining a remission of...sins" (v. 14) from receiving it once at baptism — is it an ongoing, conditional state, and if so, on what condition?
- What does verse 10 mean by the church becoming "a great stumbling-block" — declining baptisms, declining membership, or declining credibility with outsiders?
- What can legitimately be inferred from Alma separating the judgment-seat from the high priesthood (vv. 16–20), and what modern assumptions about church and state should not be read into it?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- [[Alma 5]] is the direct continuation: Alma's sermon to the church at Zarahemla puts into practice the preaching plan announced in verse 19.
- Mosiah 29 supplies institutional background for the judgeship and the "voice of the people" that Nephihah's appointment presupposes.
- [[Alma 1]] narrates an earlier appearance of pride, wealth, and mistreatment of the poor within the church, a useful (though not independently verified here) point of comparison with verses 6–13.
- Helaman 1 shows the chief-judge office recurring, and escalating into violent contention, a generation later — a contrast with Alma's orderly, voluntary transfer.
