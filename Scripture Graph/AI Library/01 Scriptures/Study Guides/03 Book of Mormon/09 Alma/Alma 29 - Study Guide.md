# Alma 29 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 29]] · My notes: [[Alma 29 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Alma desires to cry repentance with angelic zeal—The Lord grants teachers for all nations—Alma glories in the Lord’s work and in the success of Ammon and his brethren. About 76 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The angelic wish (vv. 1-2):** Alma imagines declaring repentance and redemption to every soul.

2. **Correction and theological explanation (vv. 3-8):** He accepts human limits, reflects on desire and accountability, and affirms God's provision of teachers among all nations and tongues.

3. **Calling, repentance, and remembrance (vv. 9-13):** Alma locates his glory in being God's instrument; others' repentance awakens memories of personal mercy and ancestral deliverance.

4. **Shared and eternal joy (vv. 14-17):** Attention moves from Alma's success to his brethren, their converts, and the hope of enduring life in the [[Kingdom of God]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents desire as morally consequential but not isolated from knowledge, justice, or divine judgment. Alma says that a person who does not know good from evil is blameless, whereas one who knows receives according to chosen desires (vv. 4-5). The passage thus contributes to [[Agency]] and [[Justice and Mercy]], although it does not offer a complete account of moral ignorance or final judgment.

Alma's humility is vocational rather than passive. He gives up the fantasy of being the sole voice to the world but continues to glory in God's command and to seek the repentance of souls (vv. 6-10). Contentment here means fidelity to one's calling, not indifference to suffering.

Verse 8 portrays revelation and teaching as distributed among nations and tongues. Its principal claim is divine wisdom in providing instruction; it does not specify that every nation receives identical information, at the same time, or through identical institutions.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three linked vocabularies organize the discourse. **Wish/desire/will** dominates verses 1-7 and frames the problem of aspiration and accountability. **Allotment/calling/command** redirects Alma toward assigned work in verses 3, 6, 9, and 13. **Joy/glory** then dominates verses 9-17, showing the emotional result of that redirection.

The repeated phrase "that same God" in verses 12-13 binds Israel's Egyptian deliverance, the deliverance of Alma's fathers, the establishment of the church, and Alma's calling into a continuous sacred memory. This is an interpretive reuse of exodus language, not independent proof about the historical details behind each remembered deliverance.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The central literary movement is not simply from ambition to resignation. It moves from an impossible wish for universal personal influence to a larger, communal vision of divine work. Alma first wants to speak to everyone himself; by the end, his fullest joy comes from his brethren's success and from the hoped-for salvation of their converts.

The chapter also repeatedly expands its horizon: one speaker, every people, all nations and tongues, Alma's own converts, his fathers, Israel in Egypt, his brethren, their converts, and finally eternal praise. This widening pattern makes the conclusion answer the opening: worldwide sorrow is not overcome by magnifying Alma alone, but through God's work among many peoples and servants.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What precisely does Alma judge sinful in his wish: missionary desire itself, dissatisfaction with his allotted calling, or the implied wish to override other people's agency?

- How does verse 8 answer the opening desire for one universally audible messenger?

- Why does seeing others repent cause Alma to remember both his own prayer and the captivity of his fathers?

- How does Alma's treatment of glory compare with Ammon's in [[Alma 26]]?

- Does the qualified phrase "as it were" in verse 16 signal metaphor, ecstatic experience, or deliberate uncertainty?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Alma's refusal to glory in himself (Alma 29:9) with Ammon's discussion of boasting and glorying in God in [[Alma 26]]. For the mission behind Alma's joy in verses 14-17, read [[Alma 17]] and the intervening mission narrative. Compare the remembrance of ancestral captivity and deliverance with [[Alma 5]], [[Alma 36]], and [[Exodus 3]]. Mosiah 23-24 may illuminate the possible recent deliverance behind 'the captivity of my fathers,' but Alma 29 does not make that identification explicit.
