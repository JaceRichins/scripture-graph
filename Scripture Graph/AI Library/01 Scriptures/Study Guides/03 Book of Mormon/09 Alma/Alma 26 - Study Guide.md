# Alma 26 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 26]] · My notes: [[Alma 26 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Ammon glories in the Lord—The faithful are strengthened by the Lord and are given knowledge—By faith men may bring thousands of souls unto repentance—God has all power and comprehends all things. About 90–77 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Joy and harvest (vv. 1–9):** Ammon celebrates the converts through images of light, harvest, gathering, storm, and divine custody.
2. **The boasting question (vv. 10–16):** Aaron objects; Ammon distinguishes self-glory from glorying in God.
3. **Mercy toward former enemies of the church (vv. 17–22):** Ammon recalls the missionaries' own deliverance and associates sacred knowledge with repentance, faith, works, and prayer.
4. **Destruction or mission (vv. 23–30):** Ridicule and a proposal for war are contrasted with a mission undertaken despite suffering.
5. **Claimed fruits of conversion (vv. 31–34):** Love, buried weapons, and voluntary exposure to death are offered as signs of sincerity.
6. **Concluding praise (vv. 35–37):** Ammon praises God's power and mercy, then expands his conclusion to every people and every land.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Humility]] and [[Gratitude]] rather than treating them as opposites. Ammon can rejoice intensely while denying that his own strength produced the result (vv. 10–16). His own history also makes [[Justice and Mercy]] personal: the missionary praising mercy is someone who believes he once merited destruction (vv. 17–20).

Verses 21–22 connect spiritual knowledge with [[Repentance]], [[Faith]], good works, and [[Prayer]]. The wording should not be expanded into a mechanical formula guaranteeing identical revelations or numerical missionary results to every faithful person.

The final claim that God remembers every people gives the chapter a geographically expansive theology of mercy (vv. 36–37). It does not teach universal salvation irrespective of response; verse 35 expressly speaks of salvation to those who repent and believe.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Ammon builds the speech from several linked image systems. Darkness gives way to light (vv. 3, 15); chains, an abyss, and a gulf give way to liberation and salvation (vv. 13–15, 20); ripe fields, sheaves, garners, storms, and the Lord of the harvest portray conversion and last-day preservation (vv. 5–7). These are metaphors within the speech and should not automatically be converted into a detailed chronology of the afterlife.

The phrase “natural man” in verse 21 occurs in an epistemic contrast: the natural person does not know “these things,” while the penitent may know them. The immediate context does not define a complete anthropology of human nature, and “these things” may refer particularly to the experienced realities of sin, mercy, and salvation described in verses 17–20.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Ammon's speech is built on two dominant image clusters: agricultural harvest (ripe field, sickle, sheaves, garners, storm/whirlwind, vv.5–7) and captivity/liberation (pains and chains of hell, everlasting darkness versus everlasting light, vv.13–15). The persecution catalogue in verse 29 (cast out, mocked, spit upon, smitten, stoned, bound, imprisoned) functions as a compact inventory of suffering; such hardship-catalogues are a recognizable convention in testimonial rhetoric generally, and their presence here does not require any specific literary source — a resemblance to Pauline hardship lists is possible but unprovable and should not be read as evidence of dependence.

The boasting exchange also has a literary shape worth noting: Aaron's rebuke (v.10) does not go unanswered by simple denial. Ammon does not claim his words merely sound like boasting when they are not; instead he relocates the object of the boast from himself to God (v.12), and by verses 35–36 he explicitly accepts the label — "if this is boasting, even so will I boast" — provided the boast glorifies God rather than himself. Repeated rhetorical questions and superlatives ("who can glory too much," "has there been so great love," "there never were men") communicate emotional intensity but are not independently verifiable historical comparisons; they are the emphatic idiom of praise, not claims subject to comparative proof.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Aaron perceive boasting where Ammon insists there is none — is this a genuine difference in reading Ammon's tone, or a rhetorical setup that lets Ammon articulate the distinction between self-glory and glorying in God?
- What specific reasons does Ammon give for treating his joy as worship rather than pride?
- Why does the narrative present love and nonviolence as evidence of sincerity? What can conduct reveal, and what can it not prove about inward motives?
- Does "he did not exercise his justice upon us" describe Ammon's personal experience of mercy, or is it intended as a complete account of how divine justice operates?
- What does "these things" most naturally denote in verse 21 — the mysteries of God broadly, or the specific realities of sin, mercy, and salvation just described in verses 17–20?
- Is the promise about bringing thousands to repentance (v.22) universal, conditional, or specific to this exceptional mission?
- How does the covenant against violence described in verses 32–34 relate to what later happens through the covenant-keepers' sons in [[Alma 56]], once Alma 53's account of the fathers' near-return to arms is also considered?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Alma 17]] for the mission's commission and the divine promise of success recalled in v.27, and [[Mosiah 28]] for the sons of Mosiah's original desire and permission to preach to the Lamanites. [[Mosiah 27]] supplies the missionaries' earlier opposition to the church that makes the mercy of vv.17–20 personal. See Alma 24 for the weapon-burial and covenant narrated in full behind vv.31–34, and [[Alma 56]] for how that covenant is later tested through the converts' sons (read alongside Alma 53, where the fathers nearly return to arms). Compare [[Alma 36]] for another first-person account organized around sin, mercy, darkness, light, and joy — a thematic resemblance, not a claim of dependence.
