# Alma 21 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Aaron teaches the Amalekites about Christ and His Atonement—Aaron and his brethren are imprisoned in Middoni—After their deliverance, they teach in the synagogues and make many converts—Lamoni grants religious freedom to the people in the land of Ishmael. About 90–77 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Jerusalem and its religious setting (vv. 1–4):** The narrator identifies the city's population and its Nehor-associated synagogues.
2. **Disputation in the synagogue (vv. 5–10):** An Amalekite challenges Aaron's knowledge and doctrine; Aaron expounds scripture concerning Christ, redemption, and resurrection; the audience rejects him.
3. **Movement, imprisonment, and renewed mission (vv. 11–17):** The missionaries move through Ani-Anti and Middoni, suffer imprisonment, receive deliverance, and resume preaching with greater success.
4. **Lamoni's reforms and Ammon's ministry (vv. 18–23):** Lamoni changes Ammon's status, sponsors assemblies, describes his people as free, protects worship, and supports continuing instruction.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Aaron's teaching joins several ideas tightly: the coming of the Son of God, the necessity of [[Repentance]], and redemption through Christ's suffering, death, and atoning blood, together with the [[Resurrection]] (vv. 7, 9). The Amalekite who confronts him appeals instead to his people's own sanctuaries, assemblies, and the belief that 'God will save all men' (v. 6) — including, in his own paraphrase of Aaron's message, a rejection of the idea that repentance is necessary ('except we repent we shall perish,' v. 6). The chapter does not explicitly stage Aaron's scriptural exposition as a point-by-point rebuttal of that claim, but it places the two positions side by side, and v. 17 records that many were later convinced of 'their sins, and of the traditions of their fathers, which were not correct' — without specifying whether this refers to the same audience.

The closing verses tie this teaching to lived practice: Ammon instructs Lamoni's people daily, and they respond by keeping the commandments (v. 23), portraying conversion as ongoing instruction and obedience rather than a single decision.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
**Naming and irony.** The Lamanite-built city named 'Jerusalem' (vv. 1–2) — explicitly named after 'the land of their fathers' nativity' — signals that these Amalekite- and Amulonite-led communities retained some memory of a shared Israelite background, even as the record elsewhere treats them as apostate.

**Escalating hardness.** Verse 3 explicitly grades hardness of heart among three groups (Lamanites less hardened than Amalekites and Amulonites), an unusually direct piece of comparative characterization.

**Access and liberty.** Early in the chapter, the missionaries' preaching depends on whether a synagogue or assembly will admit them (vv. 4, 16); by the chapter's end, Lamoni instead guarantees liberty of worship 'according to their desires' throughout his own realm (v. 22) — a shift from contested access to guaranteed liberty.

**A qualified reversal.** Middoni's 'few' believers (v. 12) are followed, after imprisonment and an expanded preaching campaign across 'every synagogue... or every assembly' (v. 16), by 'many' converts (v. 17) — though the text does not identify these as the same hearers, so this reads as the aggregate result of a broadened mission rather than a change of heart in the original audience.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Which parts of the Amalekite's challenge (vv. 5–6) are evasions, and which raise legitimate questions about evidence and about judging another's heart?
- Why does Aaron respond by opening the scriptures (v. 9) rather than addressing the request for a sign or an angelic appearance?
- Religious observance ('sanctuaries,' assembling 'to worship God,' v. 6) is claimed by people the narrator still calls hard-hearted (v. 3) — can religious practice coexist with the doctrine the text criticizes?
- How does Lamoni's grant of worship 'according to their desires' (v. 22) compare with other Book of Mormon statements on freedom of belief, given that it remains a royal, territorially bounded decree rather than a universal right?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Consider comparing the Amalekite's five rapid-fire rhetorical questions (vv5-6) with other recorded objections to prophetic authority in the Book of Mormon, and compare Lamoni's liberty declaration (vv20-22) with other Nephite-record statements on freedom of belief, to see whether Alma 21 represents an isolated case or part of a recurring pattern.
