# Alma 20 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 20]] · My notes: [[Alma 20 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Lord sends Ammon to Middoni to deliver his imprisoned brethren—Ammon and Lamoni meet Lamoni’s father, who is king over all the land—Ammon compels the old king to approve the release of his brethren. About 90 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Direction and preparation (vv. 1–7)** – Ammon receives the warning to go to Middoni instead of the land of Nephi; Lamoni offers diplomatic assistance through his friendship with Antiomno.
2. **Conflict on the journey (vv. 8–20)** – Lamoni's father, "king over all the land," voices anti-Nephite accusations, commands his son, and attacks first Lamoni and then Ammon.
3. **Negotiated reversal (vv. 21–27)** – Ammon disables the king's arm, then threatens him and negotiates: the brethren's release, Lamoni's kingdom, and his freedom to act on his own judgment — refusing the king's offer of half his kingdom.
4. **Deliverance and retrospective (vv. 28–30)** – Aaron, Muloki, and Ammah are freed at Middoni, described as suffering yet patient, having fared worse than Lamoni's people because of a harder-hearted local population.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Revelation]] as guidance that changes an intended journey and supplies information needed for service. It portrays [[Repentance]] as spiritually consequential in Ammon's warning about Lamoni and his father. It also places [[Agency]] within a difficult political setting: Lamoni resists paternal command, while his father eventually agrees to cease governing him. Readers should not automatically equate that negotiated concession with modern political liberty.

[[Justice and Mercy]] remain in tension. Ammon warns against innocent bloodshed and ultimately spares the king, but he also uses a credible death threat to obtain concessions. The narrator emphasizes his restraint and love without explicitly offering a general rule approving coercion.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase about blood crying from the ground in verse 18 recalls Genesis 4:10 and gives the threatened killing of an innocent son covenantal and judicial gravity. Repeated language about casting the brethren out of prison links the initial mission, Ammon's demand, the king's grant, and the eventual release. The chapter also repeatedly uses astonishment: Lamoni is surprised by his father's anger, while the old king is later astonished by Ammon's restraint, love, and words.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The encounter is structured as a reversal. The old king begins by calling Ammon a descendant of a liar and construing the mission as a scheme for theft. After being defeated, he offers up to half his kingdom. Ammon's refusal of enrichment undermines that accusation within the story, because his actual demands benefit the prisoners and Lamoni rather than himself. The reversal is morally complicated, however, because those demands are made under threat of death.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should Ammon's use of force be evaluated when the narrator combines self-defense, a death threat, limited demands, and mercy? What changes the old king most: fear, evidence that Ammon seeks no personal gain, love for Lamoni, or the words spoken? How does Lamoni's refusal of his father develop the portrait of his conversion begun in [[Alma 18]]? What does the chapter gain by ending with the prisoners' suffering rather than with the old king's invitation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Alma 21]] alongside verses 28–30 to compare its concise notice of imprisonment and release with this chapter's fuller retrospective. Then read [[Alma 22]] to follow the old king's expressed desire to learn. Genesis 4:10 is the clearest biblical comparison for the image of innocent blood crying from the ground.
