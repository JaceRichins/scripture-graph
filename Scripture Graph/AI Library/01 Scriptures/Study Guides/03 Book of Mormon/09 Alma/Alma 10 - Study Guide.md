# Alma 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Alma 10]] · My notes: [[Alma 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Amulek moves from private conversion to public witness. He identifies his ancestry and standing in Ammonihah, candidly admits his former resistance to God, and recounts the angelic command that led him to receive Alma. His testimony supplies a second witness, but the audience's astonishment quickly gives way to a legally framed effort to silence the preachers. The chapter then connects spiritual rebellion with institutional corruption: the people defend their laws, while Amulek argues that unrighteous lawyers and judges are actually undermining those laws.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Identity and former resistance (verses 1-6):** genealogy, reputation, wealth, and confession.
2. **Angelic commission and confirmation (verses 7-12):** Amulek receives Alma, experiences the promised blessing, and testifies as a second witness.
3. **Legal entrapment (verses 13-18):** lawyers question the preachers in hopes of creating a prosecutable contradiction.
4. **Collective accountability (verses 19-23):** Amulek invokes Mosiah's warning, calls for repentance, and says righteous prayers are delaying destruction.
5. **Dispute over law and corruption (verses 24-32):** the crowd accuses Amulek of attacking the law; he distinguishes the law from corrupt lawyers and judges, and Zeezrom emerges as the principal accuser.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Amulek's account joins [[Angels]], [[Obedience]], and [[Testimony]]: divine direction is followed by an action whose consequences he interprets as confirmation. His confession in verses 5-6 also portrays accountability as more than lack of information. He says he had knowledge but refused to acknowledge or act upon it.

Verses 22-23 give [[Prayer]] a communal dimension: the righteous pray within a corrupt society, and Amulek says those prayers delay judgment. The passage should be read as a claim about this particular prophetic warning, not automatically converted into a universal promise that righteous individuals will always prevent disaster.

The chapter's treatment of [[Government and Laws]] distinguishes a legal system from the morality of its administrators. Amulek claims that defending corrupt lawyers and judges can become a way of subverting the law one professes to honor.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase “I knew concerning these things, yet I would not know” in verse 6 describes chosen resistance rather than simple ignorance. The contrast between knowing and refusing to know is central to Amulek's self-portrait.

The repeated word “foundation” connects individual schemes to social consequences. In verse 17, lawyers lay “the foundations of the devil” through traps and snares; in verse 27, their unrighteousness lays “the foundation” of the people's destruction. The metaphor presents collapse as something gradually constructed by repeated choices.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
- **Self-correction:** Amulek's mid-sentence reversal ('I never have known much...but behold, I mistake,' 10:5) is a striking moment of first-person candor within the sermon.
- **Anaphora:** the repeated 'well doth he cry...by the voice of his angels' (10:20-21) builds rhetorical momentum before the angelic message is quoted directly.
- **Turned accusation:** Amulek's reply that he has 'spoken in favor of your law, to your condemnation' (10:26) inverts the lawyers' charge back onto them.
- **Witness and counter-witness:** the crowd recognizes Amulek as a second witness corroborating Alma (10:12), and the lawyers respond not with argument but by trying to manufacture contradiction — testimony and legal strategy are placed in direct opposition.
- **A New Testament echo:** 'Repent ye, repent, for the kingdom of heaven is at hand' (10:20) closely matches Matthew 4:17/3:2 in English wording — an interesting and disputed feature of the book's language (see uncertainties), not proof of either ancient anticipation or modern dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Legal-Cultural Coherence *(tentative)*
> The portrayal of a paid legal class that both administers and prosecutes law, and whose institutional interest lies in convictions ('get gain'), has been discussed by LDS legal scholarship (notably John W. Welch's work on Nephite/Book of Mormon law) as consistent with the kind of adversarial court procedure the Alma 10-14 narrative depicts, and as a coherent internal legal-cultural detail rather than an incidental aside.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · believing scholarship, honestly hedged

> [!evidence] Textual/Linguistic Parallel *(tentative)*
> The verbatim-style appearance of a King James Gospel phrase ('the kingdom of heaven is at hand') in a Nephite sermon dated centuries before the New Testament events it echoes is a recognized feature of Book of Mormon language, cited by critics as evidence of King James dependence and by believing scholars as either shared prophetic idiom about God's kingdom across dispensations or a function of the text being rendered into familiar biblical English during translation.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · disputed, well-hedged
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does Amulek's claim that he “perceived their thoughts” describe supernatural discernment, ordinary recognition of their strategy, or both? The chapter does not specify the mechanism.
- Why does Amulek defend the law while condemning its lawyers and judges? What criteria within the speech distinguish legitimate law from corrupt administration?
- How does the conditional language of verses 22-23 affect the interpretation of the later destruction of Ammonihah?
- What is gained by reading Amulek's version of his meeting with Alma beside the narrator's earlier version in [[Alma 8]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Alma 8]] for the earlier account of Alma and Amulek's meeting, [[Mosiah 29]] for Mosiah's warning about majority-supported iniquity, [[Alma 11]] for the narrative's fuller explanation of legal compensation, and [[Alma 16]] for the later destruction of Ammonihah. These connections clarify the chapter's narrative setting, but they do not independently verify every causal or theological interpretation assigned to the events.
