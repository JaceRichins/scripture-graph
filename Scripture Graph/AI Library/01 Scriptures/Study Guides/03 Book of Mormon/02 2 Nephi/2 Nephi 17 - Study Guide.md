## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Coalition and fear (vv. 1–2):** Syria and Ephraim threaten Jerusalem and the Davidic house reacts with fear.

2. **Reassurance and warning (vv. 3–9):** Isaiah declares that the coalition's plan will fail and warns that those who will not believe will not be established.

3. **Refused sign and Immanuel oracle (vv. 10–16):** Ahaz refuses to ask for a sign; the house of David is rebuked; and the child's development marks the approaching loss of the two hostile kings' lands.

4. **Assyrian judgment and altered land (vv. 17–25):** Foreign powers enter the land, humiliation follows, valuable vineyards become thorns, and intensive cultivation gives way to a sparsely inhabited pastoral landscape.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter links [[Faith]] with being established: God's reassurance ends with the warning, “If ye will not believe surely ye shall not be established” (v. 9). The audience and exact political scope of that warning are not fully specified, and the later Assyrian devastation prevents treating it as a general promise that belief averts every disaster.

The Immanuel oracle has contexts that should be distinguished without forcing them apart. Within [[2 Nephi 17]], the child's growth marks a deadline relevant to Ahaz's crisis. Matthew 1:22–23 applies the oracle to [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]. That canonical Christian application is explicit, but these passages do not by themselves settle whether the relationship is direct prediction, typology, fuller fulfillment, or multiple horizons.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Judah's hearts move like trees in the wind (v. 2), while the Lord declares that the enemy plan will not stand (v. 7). Verses 8–9 use parallel political formulas: Syria–Damascus–Rezin and Ephraim–Samaria–Remaliah's son. These lines identify the threatening kingdoms through their capitals and rulers; they may also rhetorically emphasize that the powers are bounded and mortal, though that effect is interpretive.

The hostile rulers are called “two tails of these smoking firebrands” (v. 4). Egypt and Assyria then appear as a summoned fly and bee (v. 18), and Assyria becomes a hired razor removing hair and beard (v. 20), an image plausibly conveying humiliation and stripping.

The wording shifts from “thy God” when Ahaz is invited to ask for a sign (v. 11) to Isaiah's “my God” in the rebuke (v. 13). The text does not explain the shift, so any suggestion that it marks Ahaz's alienation should remain tentative.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The closing oracle is organized by the repeated phrase “in that day” and a sequence of vivid images: fly and bee, hired razor, a small livestock holding, abundant milk, abandoned vineyards, briers, thorns, bows, and grazing animals. Together they depict invasion, humiliation, reduced population, and a shift away from intensive cultivation.

“Butter and honey” occurs with the sign-child in verse 15 and with everyone left in the land in verse 22. In the later setting, abundant milk coexists with ruined vineyards and expanded grazing land. The phrase can therefore suggest provision or pastoral abundance for survivors within a devastated landscape, rather than either uncomplicated national prosperity or necessarily bare starvation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Isaiah move from addressing Ahaz personally to addressing the house of David?

- What does verse 9 mean by being “established,” and whom does its plural warning address?

- Why is Ahaz rebuked after using apparently pious language about not tempting the Lord? What can the chapter establish about his action, and what remains unknown about his motive?

- How should readers relate the child's near-term role in verses 15–16 to Matthew's application of verse 14 to Jesus?

- How does the Assyrian devastation qualify the chapter's initial reassurance?

- Do butter and honey communicate provision, pastoral abundance, survival after devastation, or more than one of these?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Isaiah 7]] alongside this chapter to examine their sustained textual relationship, noting that close overlap alone does not determine a complete transmission or authorship model. Compare 2 Kings 16:5–9 for external narrative context about Ahaz's appeal to Assyria. Continue with [[2 Nephi 18]] or [[Isaiah 8]] for related child-sign, Damascus-Samaria, Assyria, and Immanuel themes, and with [[2 Nephi 20]] for a fuller portrayal of Assyria as an instrument of judgment. Matthew 1:22–23 is essential for understanding the oracle's explicit Christian application to Jesus.
