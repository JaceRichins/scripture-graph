# 2 Nephi 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Nephi 3]] · My notes: [[2 Nephi 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Lehi's final blessing to his youngest son Joseph is built around a long prophecy that Lehi attributes to Joseph of Egypt (2 Nephi 3:4-21) — material with no counterpart in the Genesis account of Joseph in the Hebrew Bible. Lehi introduces the prophecy in his own words (vv.1-4) before quoting Joseph's oracle, which itself contains direct first-person divine speech ("Thus saith the Lord," vv.7-8, 17), then returns to his own voice to close (vv.22-25). The oracle's central figure is a future "choice seer" of Joseph's line who will bring forth a record to work alongside "the fruit of the loins of Judah" (v.12). In the standard Latter-day Saint reading reflected in the official chapter heading, this seer is Joseph Smith and the record is the [[Book of Mormon (Gospel Topic)]] — an identification the chapter supports through descriptive clues (the double-name detail of v.15, the record-producing mission) but never states outright by name or title.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has three broad movements:

1. Verses 1–4: Lehi addresses Joseph, gives a conditional land blessing, promises preservation of his seed, and identifies their descent from Joseph of Egypt.
2. Verses 5–21: Lehi recounts Joseph of Egypt's prophecy concerning a separated branch, the Messiah, a choice seer, Moses, two writing lineages, a spokesman, and words proceeding from the dust.
3. Verses 22–25: Lehi applies the ancestral covenant to his son, promises that his seed will heed the book, describes a future mighty instrument, and directs the young Joseph to listen to Nephi.

The middle section contains nested voices—Lehi, Joseph of Egypt, and sayings attributed to the Lord—so some pronouns and role assignments require care.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Covenants]] to [[Records and Sacred Writings]]. The seer's work is valuable because it restores knowledge of ancestral covenants (verses 7, 11–12, 21), not merely because it produces a new text. The writings are assigned practical religious effects: confronting false doctrine, reducing contention, establishing peace, and reconnecting descendants with their fathers and covenants.

The chapter also frames restoration through [[Exodus and Deliverance]]. Moses delivers Israel from Egypt, while the future seer participates in a later deliverance from darkness, captivity, and covenant forgetfulness. This is an analogy of mission, not a claim that the two figures are identical.

Verse 2 preserves an important qualification: security in the promised land is conditional upon obedience. That condition should not be erased by reading the chapter's preservation promises as unrestricted guarantees to every individual descendant.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase "fruit of ... loins" (or "seed of thy loins") recurs across at least nine verses (2 Nephi 3:4-21), functioning as a rhetorical refrain that repeatedly foregrounds physical and covenantal descent. The oracle also organizes itself around paired contrasts — darkness/light, captivity/freedom, weakness/strength (vv.5, 13, 21) — and shifts registers between narration, first-person prophetic testimony ("Joseph truly testified, saying...", v.6), and quoted divine speech. The image of words crying "from the dust" (vv.19-20) echoes Isaiah's dust-speech imagery, though Isaiah 29's own context is a humbled, ghostly voice of judgment — a different valence from this chapter's positive image of faithful ancestral testimony reaching later readers.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 9-10 and 17-18 draw an explicit parallel to Moses: the seer is compared to Moses directly (v.9), and both figures are associated with divinely given writing and an appointed spokesman, recalling [[Exodus 4]] — though the two passages do not assign every role identically (verse 18 promises the later seer a spokesman but does not, unlike Exodus 4:10, say the seer himself lacks eloquence). Verse 12's image of two writings growing together also invites comparison with [[Ezekiel 37]], where Judah and Joseph are joined as inscribed sticks; the parallel is suggestive but not exact, since Ezekiel's immediate sign concerns two kingdoms reunited under one king, not two ongoing written records. Verse 15's formula — the seer's "name shall be called after me; and it shall be after the name of his father" — never names anyone explicitly, leaving the identification to depend on later interpretive tradition rather than the verse's own wording.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does "one mighty among them" in verse 24 continue describing the choice seer of verses 6-15, or introduce a separate, later figure? The text does not settle this.
- Who occupies each role in verse 18 — the person raised up, the writer, and the spokesman?
- How does the conditional promise of verse 2 relate to the seemingly unconditional preservation assurances of verses 3, 16, and 23?
- What is the relationship between this chapter and the parallel prophecy in Joseph Smith's translation of Genesis (JST Genesis 50), produced after the Book of Mormon's 1830 publication?
- Which conclusions follow directly from the chapter's own wording, and which depend on the later identification of the seer and the two records within Latter-day Saint tradition?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare verses 9-18 with [[Exodus 4]] for both similarities and differences in the Moses/spokesman pattern. Compare verses 19-20 with [[Isaiah 29]] for the "cry ... from the dust" imagery, keeping in mind Isaiah's own context is a voice of judgment rather than faithful testimony. Compare verse 12 with [[Ezekiel 37]], while keeping Ezekiel's national-reunification context distinct from the two-writings reading commonly applied to this chapter. For the closest biblical genre-parallels — a father's deathbed blessing on Joseph — see [[Genesis 49]] and [[Deuteronomy 33]]. A substantially parallel prophecy of Joseph also appears in Joseph Smith's translation of Genesis (JST Genesis 50), produced after the Book of Mormon's 1830 publication; comparing the two texts, without assuming either explains the other, is a natural next step.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
