# 2 Nephi 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Nephi 15]] · My notes: [[2 Nephi 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
This chapter reproduces the vineyard song and woes of [[Isaiah 5]]. The vineyard is explicitly the house of Israel, with Judah as its pleasant plant. Although the owner has carefully prepared it, it produces bad fruit—explained as oppression rather than judgment and a cry rather than righteousness. Six woes then expose concentrated possession, heedless revelry, determined sin, moral inversion, self-satisfied wisdom, and corrupt justice. The chapter ends with desolation, captivity, and a foreign military force, making [[Scattering of Israel]] more explicit here than restoration.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The vineyard song (vv. 1-7):** careful cultivation, failed fruit, an appeal for judgment, announced desolation, and an explicit interpretation.

2. **Woes and consequences (vv. 8-17):** accumulation of property and heedless feasting are followed by scarcity, captivity, death, and the humbling of social rank.

3. **Further woes (vv. 18-23):** the oracle condemns attachment to sin, taunting demands for divine action, moral reversal, intellectual pride, and reward-driven injustice.

4. **Judgment (vv. 24-30):** rejection of divine law leads to consuming judgment, a recurring anger refrain, and the summoning of a formidable foreign force.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects holiness with righteousness and judgment rather than treating worship as detached from public ethics (vv. 7, 16, 23). Its central failures include oppression, indifference to God's work, [[Pride]], and corrupted adjudication. The vineyard owner's extensive preparation places human failure—not divine neglect—at the center of the metaphor. At the same time, the text emphasizes judgment more than an explicit invitation to [[Repentance]] or a developed explanation of mercy.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter builds through paired contrasts: cultivated grapes/wild grapes, judgment/oppression, righteousness/a cry, exalted/humbled, evil/good, darkness/light, and bitter/sweet (vv.7,20) — moral disorder rendered as inverted categories. Verse 25's refrain, "his anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still," is often read devotionally as an offer of mercy, but its grammar ("but") coordinates it with continuing anger, not comfort. Underlying the English, verse 7 is traditionally understood to preserve a Hebrew sound-play (mishpat/"judgment" vs. mispach/"oppression"; tsedaqah/"righteousness" vs. tse'aqah/"a cry") that reinforces betrayed expectation with sound as well as sense; this wordplay is invisible in translation, and whether it was present in the brass-plates source text cannot be verified from the English Book of Mormon alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The opening song invites "the inhabitants of Jerusalem, and men of Judah" to judge between the owner and his vineyard (v.3) before verse 7 turns the figure back on the audience, identifying them as the vineyard itself. The sixfold "Wo unto..." sequence (vv.8,11,18,20,21,22) broadens the charge from property and appetite to defiance, moral inversion, pride, and judicial corruption — a recognized prophetic "woe-oracle" pattern used elsewhere by Isaiah and other prophets. Verse 25's refrain recurs at the close of related passages in [[2 Nephi 19]] and [[2 Nephi 20]], linking this material into a larger connected sequence of Isaiah's judgment oracles. The closing verses (26-30) accelerate through images of readiness, weapons, horses, and roaring lions, describing an invading force — not, in its immediate context, a peaceful gathering.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- The vineyard song (vv.1-7) and Jacob 5's olive-tree allegory both use a divine-husbandman image for Israel — how does comparing the two illuminate what each emphasizes about divine patience versus judgment?
- Verse 20's condemnation of calling "evil good, and good evil" is often applied broadly today — what does the immediate context (vv.18-23: defiance, self-conceit, bribery) suggest about the specific behaviors originally in view?
- Why might the speakers in verse 19 demand that the Lord "make speed" and "hasten his work" — is this disbelief, mockery, defiance, or some combination?
- The ensign in verse 26 summons an invading, prey-seizing force, the reverse of later "ensign" passages describing Israel's gathering — what does this contrast suggest about how the same image can carry opposite meanings depending on context?
