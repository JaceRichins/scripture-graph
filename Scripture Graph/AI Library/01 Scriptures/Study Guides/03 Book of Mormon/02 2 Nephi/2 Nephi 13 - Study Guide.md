# 2 Nephi 13 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Nephi 13]] · My notes: [[2 Nephi 13 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
2 Nephi 13 presents judgment upon Jerusalem and Judah as an unraveling of the whole society. Food, water, experienced leaders, and ordinary social trust disappear (vv. 1–7). The prophecy identifies rebellion against the Lord as the cause (vv. 8–12), brings rulers into judgment for exploiting the poor (vv. 13–15), describes the humiliation of the haughty daughters of Zion (vv. 16–24), and ends with military defeat and a personified city mourning in desolation (vv. 25–26).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Removal of social supports (vv. 1–3):** Sustenance and experienced leaders are taken away.

2. **Disordered rule and social relations (vv. 4–7):** Inexperienced rulers, mutual oppression, and unwillingness to govern signal collapse.

3. **Cause and moral consequence (vv. 8–12):** Speech and conduct against the Lord lead to ruin; the righteous and wicked receive the fruit of their actions.

4. **Judgment of leaders (vv. 13–15):** Elders and princes are charged with consuming the vineyard and crushing the poor.

5. **Oracle against the daughters of Zion (vv. 16–24):** Haughtiness and elaborate display give way to exposure, loss, and mourning.

6. **War and desolation (vv. 25–26):** Men fall by the sword, and the city sits mourning on the ground.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects [[Sin]] with consequences affecting institutions, economic security, and communal order. Its clearest ethical accusation concerns leaders who enrich themselves while injuring the poor (vv. 13–15), placing public power under divine judgment. Verses 10–11 teach moral accountability through the image of eating the fruit of one's actions, although they should not be converted into a simplistic promise that every righteous person always prospers immediately. The chapter also treats [[Pride]] as more than an inward attitude: it becomes visible in conduct and status display.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The paired expressions “stay and staff” and “bread” and “water” emphasize the removal of everything that supports ordinary life. Verses 18–23 use an accumulating catalog of adornments; several English item names are archaic, so their exact ancient referents should not be inferred from modern usage alone. Verse 24 then employs repeated “instead of” contrasts to reverse fragrance, clothing, hair, and beauty into signs of defeat and mourning.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several literary techniques structure the chapter: anaphoric cataloging, used twice — once for the leaders and resources removed (vv.1-3) and once for the ornaments removed from the daughters of Zion (vv.18-23) — to convey exhaustive, itemized loss. Verse 24 closes the ornament catalog with a tight five-part 'instead of' reversal formula, each mark of former glamour answered by its degraded opposite. Verses 13-15 use courtroom language — the Lord 'stands to plead' and 'enter[s] into judgment' — a common prophetic image for divine accusation, though the chapter does not develop the fuller formal elements of a technical 'covenant lawsuit' genre. The chapter closes by personifying Jerusalem as a mourning woman sitting on the ground (v.26), extending the feminine imagery introduced earlier with the 'daughters of Zion.'
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the prophecy place exploitation of the poor at the center of the Lord's case against the rulers?

- How does the loss of competent leadership relate to the mutual oppression described in verse 5?

- What cultural assumptions make rule by children, babes, and women function rhetorically as a sign of disorder, and which conclusions should modern readers avoid drawing from that rhetoric?

- Does the placement of the luxury catalog after the accusation concerning the poor imply an economic connection, or only a thematic one?

- How can the individual recompense of verses 10–11 be understood within a prophecy of collective catastrophe?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the chapter line by line with [[Isaiah 3]], distinguishing exact correspondence from genuine variation before assigning significance to any difference. Historical translations and specialized lexicons may help identify the ornaments in verses 18–23 and clarify the humiliation imagery in verses 17 and 24. Such lexical work should acknowledge uncertainty where the underlying object or custom cannot be identified securely.
