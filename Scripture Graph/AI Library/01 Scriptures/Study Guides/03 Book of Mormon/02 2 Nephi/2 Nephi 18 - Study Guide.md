# 2 Nephi 18 — Study Guide

## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Recorded oracle and child-timetable (vv. 1–4):** A roll, named witnesses, a birth, and a divinely assigned name accompany the prediction concerning Damascus and Samaria.

2. **Shiloah and the Assyrian flood (vv. 5–10):** Quiet Shiloah is refused; the king of Assyria is depicted as overwhelming water that reaches into Judah. Verse 9 repeats “ye shall be broken in pieces” three times, while verse 10 declares that hostile counsel and speech will fail because “God is with us.”

3. **The object of fear and the Lord's twofold imagery (vv. 11–15):** The speaker is told not to share the people's fear but to fear the Lord of Hosts, who is described as sanctuary and as a stone, rock, gin, and snare.

4. **Testimony, waiting, and prophetic signs (vv. 16–18):** The testimony is bound, the law sealed among disciples, and the speaker waits for the hidden Lord. He and his children are signs and wonders in Israel.

5. **Competing counsel and a dark conclusion (vv. 19–22):** Familiar spirits and wizards are contrasted with seeking God and with the law and testimony. The chapter ends with hunger, cursing, anguish, and darkness.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter redirects fear rather than merely demanding its suppression. The speaker is told not to share the people's fear but to sanctify the Lord of Hosts and let him be the object of fear and dread (vv. 11–13). This supports study of [[Faith]] while leaving room for prudent attention to real danger.

The Lord is described as both sanctuary and stumbling stone (vv. 14–15). The immediate subject is the Lord of Hosts. Romans 9:32–33 and 1 Peter 2:6–8 later apply the stone imagery in arguments centered on Christ, providing a canonical basis for connection with [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]. That later application should not be treated as proof that every feature of the oracle was originally a direct prediction of Christ.

Verses 19–20 contrast familiar spirits and wizards with seeking God and with speech that accords with the law and testimony. The passage therefore supports discernment grounded in [[Revelation]], [[Testimony]], and [[Records and Sacred Writings]]. Applying it to every modern spiritual claim is a reasonable devotional extension, not the only possible statement of its original scope.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly develops meaning through paired expressions: quiet Shiloah and the strong river; the people's fear and fear of the Lord; sanctuary and stumbling stone; law and testimony and occult muttering; light and the darkness of the conclusion.

“Immanuel” in verse 8 and “God is with us” in verse 10 form a strong nearby echo. The connection reinforces the theme of divine presence, but the chapter does not settle whether Immanuel functions here as a personal name, symbolic title, direct address, or personification.

The strong river is explicitly identified as “the king of Assyria and all his glory” (v. 7). More specific claims about Shiloah representing quiet faith, Jerusalem's legitimate order, or the Davidic promise remain interpretations requiring historical and lexical support.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter moves through several linked image systems: writing and naming, quiet and overflowing water, sanctuary and stumbling stone, authoritative testimony and occult muttering, then light and darkness. These shifts give the oracle literary force without establishing a hidden structure or an otherwise undocumented birth-announcement convention.

Repetition intensifies the judgment. “Ye shall be broken in pieces” occurs three times in verse 9; verse 10 follows with a distinct pair of failure declarations concerning counsel and speech. Verse 15 accumulates verbs—stumble, fall, be broken, be snared, and be taken—while verse 22 closes with trouble, darkness, dimness, and anguish. The unresolved darkness becomes an important point for comparison with [[2 Nephi 19]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why are Uriah and Zechariah named immediately after the command to write, and precisely what were they expected to attest?

- What does refusing Shiloah represent: rejection of divine provision, rejection of Jerusalem's established order, political sympathy with Rezin and Remaliah's son, or some combination?

- Does the flood reaching the neck imply limited survival for Judah, or simply intensify the picture of near destruction?

- Who is included in “this people,” and what is meant by “a confederacy” in verses 11–12?

- How should the passage's immediate description of the Lord of Hosts as a stumbling stone be related to the later Christological use in Romans 9 and 1 Peter 2?

- How far may the law-and-testimony standard in verse 20 responsibly be extended beyond its immediate contrast with occult consultation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[2 Nephi 17]], [[2 Nephi 18]], and [[2 Nephi 19]] as a continuous unit, tracking sign-names, the Immanuel theme, the Assyrian threat, and the movement from darkness toward light. Compare [[Isaiah 8]] verse by verse with [[2 Nephi 18]] to distinguish the verified chapter-level correspondence from any wording differences.

For later Christian reception, compare Romans 9:32–33 and 1 Peter 2:6–8 with 2 Nephi 18:14–15, noting that Romans also draws upon Isaiah 28:16. Hebrews 2:13 may be compared with 2 Nephi 18:17–18 as a later application of the speaker-and-children language. Deuteronomy 18:10–12 and Leviticus 19:31 provide relevant legal background for the rejection of familiar spirits.
