## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
Three "Hearken unto me" calls (vv.1, 4, 7) open the chapter, addressed in turn to those who "follow after righteousness," to "my people... my nation," and to those who "know righteousness" and carry God's law "written" in their hearts. Three "Awake, awake" imperatives (vv.9, 17, 24) organize the rest:

1. **vv.1-8 — Hearken:** identity rooted in Abraham and Sarah; divine salvation and righteousness outlast the created order.
2. **vv.9-16 — Awake, O arm of the Lord:** an appeal to a remembered deliverance through the sea (Rahab, the dragon, "the great deep"), followed by God's reassurance against fear of mortal oppressors and a covenant commission ("thou art my people," v.16).
3. **vv.17-25 — Awake, O Jerusalem / O Zion:** the cup of the Lord's fury is taken from Jerusalem's hand and given to her afflicters, and the captive city is commanded to rise, dress, and loose her bonds.

Zion and Jerusalem are addressed jointly rather than as strictly separate stages (v.24 names both in the same verse), so this is one useful outline among others rather than the chapter's only structure.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verses 1-2 root the hearers' identity in the calling and blessing of [[Abrahamic Covenant|Abraham and Sarah]], and the promised return of "the redeemed of the Lord" to Zion (v.11) ties [[Salvation]] to the [[Gathering of Israel]] — though the chapter itself gives only a brief appeal to these ancestors, not a systematic exposition of the covenant. The recurring release-from-captivity imagery (the "captive exile" of v.14, the loosed "bands of thy neck" in v.25) situates deliverance within the pattern of [[Exodus and Deliverance]]. Verses 6 and 8 contrast the permanence of divine salvation and righteousness with the transience of the heavens, earth, and mortal life — not a denial that oppression, exile, and loss are real, but a claim that the Lord's care outlasts them (vv.12-13). Verse 16 briefly touches [[Creation]] language ("plant the heavens... lay the foundations of the earth") in describing the commissioned figure's protection.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The poetry relies on parallelism and personification: the "arm of the Lord" is addressed as though a separate agent ("Art thou not he..."), and Jerusalem's suffering is figured as drinking a "cup of trembling" that is later transferred to her oppressors. "Rahab" and "the dragon" appear beside the sea and "the great deep" (vv.9-10), evoking a wider Hebrew poetic idiom for chaos-combat (as elsewhere in Job and Psalms) fused, in the same breath, with the historical Exodus deliverance through the sea (vv.10-11). The text itself does not specify whether Rahab denotes a mythic chaos-figure, a poetic name for Egypt (compare Isaiah 30:7), or both at once — that question is genuinely disputed and not resolved here.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter contains a notable difference from its Isaiah parallel. Isaiah 51:19–20 refers to “two things,” whereas 2 Nephi 8:19–20 has “two sons” and “save these two.” That difference is textually clear, but its meaning is not. Since the verse names desolation, destruction, famine, and sword and does not identify either son, confident identification of two specific prophetic figures goes beyond this chapter.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the oracle root the hearers' identity in a quarrying metaphor (v.1) before naming Abraham and Sarah — what does 'being hewn from a rock' add?
- Do the Rahab/dragon/sea images in vv.9-10 recall one event or fuse a mythic chaos-combat tradition with the historical Exodus — and does the text itself decide the question?
- Who is addressed by the singular pronouns in verse 16 — a prophet, Israel, Zion, or another servant figure?
- What can the "two sons" of vv.19-20 (KJV Isaiah: "two things") establish from this text alone, and what would depend on outside sources?
- How does Christ's reuse of this chapter's closing summons in 3 Nephi 20 reshape its original audience and referent?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
