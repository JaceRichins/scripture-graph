# 2 Nephi 19 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Nephi 19]] · My notes: [[2 Nephi 19 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
2 Nephi 19 closely parallels [[Isaiah 9]] and falls into two sharply contrasting movements. Verses 1-7 promise light replacing darkness, joy, liberation from oppression, and a royal child given exalted titles (v.6) who will reign forever on David's throne "with judgment and with justice" (v.7). [[Matthew 4]] quotes the opening light/Galilee imagery (vv.1-2) and applies it to the start of Jesus's Galilean ministry. The official heading and wider Christian and Latter-day Saint tradition identify this child as [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]], though the chapter's own wording presents him through titles and Davidic promises rather than naming Jesus directly — a passage some scholars instead relate primarily to an eighth-century Judean king. Verses 8-21 pivot to a judgment oracle against proud, unrepentant Ephraim and Samaria (vv.9-10, 13), building through three strophes that each close with the refrain "his anger is not turned away, but his hand is stretched out still" (vv.12, 17, 21) toward corrupt leadership and internal fratricide among Manasseh, Ephraim, and Judah (vv.14-21). The same refrain recurs in [[2 Nephi 20]], showing the judgment oracle continues past this chapter's boundary.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Reversal and hope (vv. 1-3):** afflicted lands see light, and sorrow gives way to joy.

2. **Reasons for joy (vv. 4-7):** oppression is broken, battle gives way to burning, and a royal child establishes enduring government and peace.

3. **Pride and external enemies (vv. 8-12):** Ephraim and Samaria answer loss with boastful rebuilding; the first judgment refrain follows.

4. **Corrupt leadership (vv. 13-17):** the people do not seek the Lord, while leaders and a lying prophet cause error; the refrain returns.

5. **Self-consuming violence (vv. 18-21):** wickedness spreads like fire, hunger becomes insatiable, and related tribes attack one another; the refrain returns again. The sequence continues into [[2 Nephi 20]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The child's titles in verse 6, especially "The Mighty God" and "The Everlasting Father," raise a Godhead question: how can Christ be called Father without denying he is the Son? [[Mosiah 15]] (see 15:1-4) addresses this by explaining the sense in which Christ is both. The promise of an everlasting government "upon the throne of David... with judgment and with justice" (v.7) ties legitimate government to moral order rather than mere absence of conflict — its ruler is called the Prince of Peace. The judgment half emphasizes failed [[Repentance]]: calamity alone does not produce turning to the Lord (v.13), and verses 14-16 assign real responsibility to corrupt leaders and false [[Prophets]] without excusing those who follow them.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The refrain in verses 12, 17, and 21 controls the reading of the second half: “his anger is not turned away” explains the accompanying stretched-out hand as continued judgment in this immediate context. Reading the phrase here as an extended offer of mercy reverses its local syntax, even though hand imagery can have other meanings elsewhere.

The chapter repeatedly turns abstractions into physical images: oppression is a yoke, staff, and rod; wickedness is a spreading fire; social violence is ravenous eating; and government rests upon a shoulder. These images connect political disorder, moral corruption, and bodily suffering.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's contrast is deliberately severe: light, harvest joy, liberation, and peace in verses 1-7 give way to darkness, fire, hunger, and fratricidal conflict in verses 8-21. Verses 14-15 use the merism "head and tail, branch and rush" — glossed by the text itself as the elder and the lying prophet — to express judgment on Israel's leadership. Verses 9-10 pair rebuilding images (bricks/hewn stones, sycamores/cedars) to dramatize defiance rather than repentance. The threefold refrain is the chapter's clearest structural device, tying its three judgment cycles together and linking into [[2 Nephi 20]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- In its original eighth-century setting, did Isaiah's audience understand the "child... born" (v.6) as a living or soon-to-be-born Judean king, an idealized future king, or something more directly messianic — and how does that horizon relate to the fulfillment [[Matthew 4]] identifies in Jesus?
- What does it mean for Christ to be called "The Everlasting Father" (v.6) without denying that he is also the Son?
- Why does an oracle of messianic hope (vv.1-7) sit immediately beside an oracle of Israel's self-destruction (vv.8-21) in the same chapter?
- Is "the flesh of his own arm" (v.20) literal famine language, figurative language for consuming one's own kin, or deliberately both?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the chapter line by line with [[Isaiah 9]], noting both its sustained dependence and any wording differences. Read [[2 Nephi 18]] to recover the distress and political setting presupposed by “Nevertheless” in verse 1, and continue through [[2 Nephi 20]] because the repeated judgment refrain crosses the chapter boundary. [[Matthew 4]] is important for studying how the opening light-and-Galilee oracle was applied to Jesus in early Christian scripture.
