# 2 Nephi 12 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Nephi 12]] · My notes: [[2 Nephi 12 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
2 Nephi 12 is Nephi's quotation of [[Isaiah 2]] (the chapter heading itself says 'Compare Isaiah 2'). It opens with a vision of many nations ascending to the exalted 'mountain of the Lord's house' to receive instruction, resulting in the cessation of war (vv. 2-4). It then turns to indict the house of Jacob for foreign influence, soothsaying, wealth, military strength, and idolatry (vv. 5-9), before unfolding into a sweeping oracle in which every proud and lofty thing — human, natural, and man-made — is brought low so that 'the Lord alone shall be exalted in that day' (vv. 10-21), closing with a maxim on human mortality (v. 22). The official chapter heading reads this judgment as the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]] and the resulting peace as [[Millennium|millennial]], though the chapter's own language speaks more generally of 'the last days' and 'the day of the Lord.' Themes of [[Pride]], the [[Gathering of Israel]], and [[Zion]] run throughout.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- v. 1 — superscription: Isaiah's vision concerning Judah and Jerusalem
- vv. 2-4 — the exalted mountain of the Lord's house; nations flow to it; law goes forth from Zion; universal peace
- v. 5 — transitional call: 'come ye and let us walk in the light of the Lord'
- vv. 6-9 — indictment: soothsaying, wealth, horses and chariots, idols, unrepented pride
- vv. 10-21 — the 'day of the Lord' oracle, framed by the identical refrain 'the Lord alone shall be exalted in that day' (vv. 11, 17), cataloging what will be humbled: proud men, cedars and oaks, mountains and hills, towers and walls, ships and pleasant things, and idols
- v. 22 — closing maxim: cease from trusting in mortal man

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter contrasts human [[Pride]] with the exaltation of God alone, teaching that at the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]] all that is 'lifted up' among men — whether wealth, military power, or idols — will be humbled. Verses 2-3 are read as prophecy of a latter-day [[Temples|temple]] and the [[Gathering of Israel]] to [[Zion]], while verse 4's vision of repurposed weapons anticipates [[Millennium|millennial]] peace ([[War and Peace]]).

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Height organizes the chapter: the Lord's house is properly exalted (v. 2), while proud persons, mountains, hills, towers, and walls are improperly 'lifted up' and brought low (vv. 10-17). The identical refrain 'the Lord alone shall be exalted in that day' brackets this catalog at verses 11 and 17. A second pattern contrasts movement toward God with flight from him: nations openly ascend to learn (vv. 2-3), while the judged retreat into rocks and caves (vv. 10, 19, 21) that offer no real refuge from divine judgment. The chapter's close correspondence with [[Isaiah 2]] is explicit from its attribution to Isaiah in verse 1; its opening oracle also parallels Micah 4:1-3, though that overlap cannot settle which prophetic book has priority.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> The Book of Mormon's singular 'forgive him not' in 2 Nephi 12:9, in place of the King James plural 'forgive them not,' is sometimes cited by Latter-day Saint scholars as a grammatically smoother reading consistent with the verse's singular subjects, and occasionally offered as a possible authentic variant from an underlying non-Masoretic text; the observation is real, but its evidentiary weight for an independent ancient source is weak.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does “Zion” function as a poetic counterpart to Jerusalem in verse 3, or does the line distinguish two locations or roles?
- Are the mountains, towers, ships, and walls primarily literal objects of judgment, symbols of political and economic power, or both?
- How does the command to “cease” from mortal man in verse 22 summarize the preceding criticism of pride and handmade idols?
- What interpretive assumptions are required to identify the Lord's house specifically with a modern temple or the day of the Lord specifically with the Second Coming?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Because 2 Nephi 12 is Nephi's quotation of [[Isaiah 2]], compare directly with that chapter and with the closely related oracle in Micah 4:1-3. The 'day of the Lord' theme recurs in Joel 2 and in judgment oracles against nations elsewhere in Isaiah and Jeremiah. Readers interested in Book of Mormon Isaiah textual questions may compare 2 Nephi 12:9 and 2 Nephi 12:16 against the King James Isaiah 2:9 and 2:16, where small wording differences have drawn scholarly attention; any specific historical-textual claims (e.g., about Septuagint variants) should be checked against primary sources rather than taken on secondhand authority.
