# 2 Nephi 20 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Nephi 20]] · My notes: [[2 Nephi 20 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The destruction of Assyria is a type of the destruction of the wicked at the Second Coming—Few people will be left after the Lord comes again—The remnant of Jacob will return in that day—Compare Isaiah 10. About 559–545 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Unjust government condemned (vv. 1-4):** Written decrees deprive vulnerable people of justice.
2. **Assyria commissioned but self-directed (vv. 5-11):** The empire is called a rod of anger, although its own purpose is broad conquest.
3. **The instrument judged for pride (vv. 12-19):** Imperial boasting is answered by the ax-and-worker analogy and images of consuming fire.
4. **A remnant returns (vv. 20-23):** Survivors transfer their reliance from the power that struck them to the Lord.
5. **Zion reassured through deliverance patterns (vv. 24-27):** Egypt and Midian provide precedents for the breaking of Assyria's yoke.
6. **Threatening advance and sudden reversal (vv. 28-34):** The army approaches Jerusalem, but the Lord fells the high forest.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Justice for the vulnerable.** Verses 1-2 condemn rulers who use decrees and written rules to deny judgment and rights to the poor, widows, and fatherless. The chapter therefore treats injustice as institutional as well as personal (see [[Government and Laws]] and [[Justice and Mercy]]).

**Power does not erase accountability.** Assyria is called the Lord's rod, yet verse 7 distinguishes Assyria's destructive intention from the divine purpose, and verses 12-16 announce punishment for the king's pride and self-exaltation. The text supports accountability for motives and pride; it does not explicitly describe a precisely limited military mandate that Assyria violated.

**A remnant changes its reliance.** The remnant does more than survive: it ceases to rely on the power that struck it and stays upon the Lord 'in truth' (vv.20-21). This joins the remnant theme with [[Gathering of Israel]] while resisting any claim that all covenant people automatically escape judgment.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated words **rod** and **staff** trace control and reversal. Assyria begins as a rod in the Lord's hand (v. 5), absurdly boasts like a tool above its user (v. 15), strikes Zion with a rod (v. 24), and finally faces the Lord's rod raised against it after the manner of Egypt (v. 26).

The verb **stay** in verse 20 carries the sense of lean upon or rely upon. Its repetition makes the change of allegiance visible: Israel will no longer stay upon the one who smote it but will stay upon the Lord.

The phrase 'because of the anointing' in verse 27 is textually clear but interpretively difficult. The immediate result is liberation from burden and yoke; the verse itself does not identify who or what is anointed.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly turns an image against Assyria. The empire that treats conquered wealth like unattended eggs is itself reduced to a nearly empty forest. The conqueror that imagines itself autonomous is compared to an ax, saw, rod, or wooden staff—objects whose movement depends on another.

The itinerary in verses 28-32 slows the threat into a sequence of named locations and then brings it within sight of Jerusalem. At that apparent climax, the subject changes: the Lord, not the invader, performs the decisive action by lopping the high boughs. The next chapter's growth from Jesse's cut-down stem makes the sequence from [[2 Nephi 20]] to [[2 Nephi 21]] especially significant: arrogant growth falls, while new divinely endowed growth emerges.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- When laws are formally enacted but systematically harm the vulnerable, what standard of justice does verses 1-2 apply to them?
- How does the difference between the Lord's purpose and Assyria's purpose in verses 5-7 clarify the relationship between providence and moral responsibility?
- Why is the remnant's object of reliance emphasized alongside its survival?
- Does the command not to fear in verse 24 promise avoidance of suffering, or confidence that oppressive power has a limit?
- What changes when the forest imagery at the end of this chapter is read directly beside the shoot-from-Jesse imagery in [[2 Nephi 21]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the chapter with [[Isaiah 10]], then read [[2 Nephi 19]] and Isaiah 7 for the judgment refrain, 'mighty God,' and remnant-return connections. Compare verses 24-27 with Exodus 14 and Judges 7 for the Egypt and Midian deliverance patterns. Romans 9:27 later cites Isaiah 10:22's remnant language within Paul's own argument; that later use should be studied without assuming it exhausts the oracle's original meaning. Finally, read [[2 Nephi 20]] and [[2 Nephi 21]] consecutively to examine the contrast between the felled forest and the new shoot from Jesse.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
