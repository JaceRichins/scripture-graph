## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves through five compact stages. Verses 1-2 state the covenant premise: God will "set his hand again" to recover Israel and fulfill his promise to Nephi and Lehi that their seed's words would go forth. Verses 3-6 quote the anticipated objection ("A Bible! ... there cannot be any more Bible," v. 3) and rebuke Gentile ingratitude toward the Jews who transmitted it. Verses 7-10 argue from God's rule over "more nations than one," his unchanging nature ("the same yesterday, today, and forever," v. 9), and the claim that one spoken word does not preclude another. Verses 11-13 describe a universal command to write God's words and a reciprocal exchange of records among Jews, Nephites, and the lost tribes. Verse 14 closes by pairing the gathering of Israel with the gathering of God's word, again invoking the covenant with Abraham that opened the chapter (v. 1) — a loose thematic bracket built from the chapter's sustained subject rather than confirmed evidence of a deliberately engineered literary frame.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter is a key textual basis within Latter-day Saint teaching for continuing [[Revelation]]: "my work is not yet finished" (v. 9) and the Bible does not contain "all my words" (v. 10). It teaches that God deals with "all nations" impartially (vv. 7-8) rather than favoring one people's record, and that [[Records and Sacred Writings|written records]] play a role in [[Final Judgment]] ("I will judge the world... according to that which is written," v. 11) — though the verse does not specify whether written records are the sole ground of accountability or one expression of God's complete knowledge. The conclusion ties this theology of records to [[Covenants]]: God remembers Israel and the [[Abrahamic Covenant]] made with Abraham, and the gathering of scripture accompanies the [[Gathering of Israel]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated cry “A Bible!” functions as a compressed slogan of scriptural sufficiency in verses 3 and 6. The response repeatedly widens the frame: from one Bible to the Jews who transmitted it, from one nation to all nations, from one word to further words, and from one record to a network of exchanged records. “The same words” in verse 8 should not automatically be read as verbatim identity; the surrounding argument can support the more modest sense of a consistent divine witness. Likewise, “all men” in verse 11 may express universal scope through peoples and regions rather than requiring that every individual personally produce scripture.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The Lord answers using direct address to a hypothetical objector — quoting the objection verbatim (vv. 3, 6) and answering "O fools" (v. 4), "Thou fool" (v. 6), "O ye Gentiles" (v. 5) — a rhetorical device that personifies the anticipated objection rather than arguing against it in the abstract. Rhetorical questions carry much of the argument in roughly verses 4-8, before turning to flat declarative claims in verses 9-10 ("my work is not yet finished," "ye need not suppose"). Verses 12-13 use anaphora — "I shall speak unto... and they shall write it," repeated for the Jews, the Nephites, the other tribes, and all nations — echoed by a parallel "shall have the words of" construction, though the two lists are not perfectly symmetric: verse 12's fourth category ("all nations") is not among the records exchanged in verse 13, which names only Jews, Nephites, and the lost tribes.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Verse 8 speaks of "the testimony of two nations" — does this mean specifically Jewish and Nephite records, or state a general principle extending to any pair of scriptural witnesses?
- What does "the same words" (v. 8) require: identical text between national records, or simply a consistent divine witness?
- What does it mean for the world to be judged "according to that which is written" (v. 11) — is the written record itself the standard, or a record of deeds that happen to be written?
- How does this chapter's claim (more nations means more scripture, not less) relate to modern claims of continuing revelation through living [[Prophets]]?
