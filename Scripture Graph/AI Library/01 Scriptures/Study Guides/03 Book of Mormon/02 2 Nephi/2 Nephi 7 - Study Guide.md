# 2 Nephi 7 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Nephi 7]] · My notes: [[2 Nephi 7 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jacob continues reading from Isaiah: Isaiah speaks messianically—The Messiah will have the tongue of the learned—He will give His back to the smiters—He will not be confounded—Compare Isaiah 50. About 559–545 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Israel's estrangement and the Lord's power (1-3):** Divorce and debt imagery assign covenant responsibility to Israel; sea-drying and cosmic imagery assert divine power to redeem.

2. **The instructed and suffering servant (4-6):** The servant hears, speaks, obeys, and accepts humiliation.

3. **Resolve and judicial vindication (7-9):** Repeated confidence that the Lord will help leads into a courtroom challenge to every adversary.

4. **Two responses to darkness (10-11):** One audience fears the Lord and hears the servant despite lacking light; another manufactures its own fire and receives sorrow.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds accountability and hope together. Israel's sins have real covenant consequences, but the Lord's hand is not too short to redeem. This supports [[Repentance]] without depicting alienation as irreversible.

The servant models [[Obedience]] and [[Endurance]], yet his confidence is explicitly grounded in divine help rather than willpower alone. Verse 10 is especially pastorally important: a person may fear God and obey while still experiencing darkness. The text therefore resists simplistic equations between hardship, uncertainty, and unfaithfulness.

The suffering servant can be read in relation to [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] and the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], especially through passion parallels. That application is meaningful, but the chapter should not be made to say more than it does: it does not explain the mechanics of atonement or explicitly identify the servant.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several word clusters carry the argument. In verse 1, **divorcement**, **creditors**, **sold**, **iniquities**, and **transgressions** combine family and debt metaphors. In verses 2-3, **redeem** and **deliver** are supported by imagery associated with [[Exodus and Deliverance]]. In verses 8-9, **justifieth**, **contend**, **adversary**, and **condemn** create a judicial setting.

The repeated ear-and-tongue language in verses 4-5 connects learning with receptivity: the servant speaks effectively because he first hears. The English text also contains differences from the King James form of [[Isaiah 50]], including direct references to the house of Israel and the added strength-of-the-mouth statement. Their existence is observable; their origin remains disputed.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is organized by contrasts: divine power versus Israel's failure to answer, an opened ear versus rebellion, public shame versus future vindication, mortal accusers versus a moth-eaten garment, and darkness endured faithfully versus sparks manufactured as substitutes for light.

The legal imagery also develops across the chapter. Verse 1 opens with documentary and creditor questions; verses 8-9 stage a direct contest with adversaries. Between those scenes stands the servant whose obedience and suffering form the central testimony.

The placement between [[2 Nephi 6]] and [[2 Nephi 8]] matters. Jacob's surrounding discourse concerns Israel's scattering, recovery, and Zion's consolation, making covenant restoration a stronger contextual frame than an isolated reading of the suffering imagery alone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Correspondence
> 2 Nephi 7 follows Isaiah 50 in the same sequence and with extensive verbal correspondence. This firmly establishes a literary relationship—direct copying, translation from a related source, or dependence on a common textual tradition—but does not by itself determine the direction, date, or precise mechanism of dependence.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · Firmly established textual relationship · [[Textual Correspondence in 2 Nephi 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Variants
> The English text of 2 Nephi 7 is not wholly identical to the King James Version of Isaiah 50. Among its differences are added direct addresses to the house of Israel in verses 2 and 4 and the statement about smiting an adversary with the strength of the servant's mouth in verse 8. These are strong evidence of a distinct English form but, without corroborating ancient witnesses, weak evidence for any particular underlying Hebrew reading.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 1.0 · Variants established; historical significance disputed · [[Textual Variants in 2 Nephi 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Messianic And Typological Interpretation
> A Christian messianic reading is meaningfully supported by the servant's willing suffering, rejection, confidence in divine help, and anticipated vindication. The official chapter heading adopts this reading. Nevertheless, the chapter itself never explicitly calls the speaker the Messiah or Jesus Christ, so the evidence does not establish an exclusive original referent.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · Traditional Christian reading; exclusive original referent disputed · [[Messianic And Typological Interpretation in 2 Nephi 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical-Critical *(tentative)*
> 2 Nephi 7 corresponds verse-for-verse (per the verified 12-verse text overlap) to Isaiah 50:1-11 in the KJV tradition. Isaiah 50 falls within Isaiah 40-55, a block that a substantial body of biblical scholarship ('Deutero-Isaiah') dates to the Babylonian exile period (mid-6th century BC), which would place at least some of it after Lehi's departure from Jerusalem (traditionally c. 597-587 BC). This raises a question about how such material could already appear on the brass plates Lehi's family carried out of Jerusalem.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Historical-critical issue is real; formulation requires correction

> [!evidence] Literary-Critical Categorization *(tentative)*
> 2 Nephi 7:4-9 (=Isaiah 50:4-9) is conventionally categorized in biblical scholarship as the third of four 'Servant Songs' within Isaiah 40-55 (alongside 42:1-4, 49:1-6, and 52:13-53:12), a literary grouping first proposed by Bernhard Duhm (1892) based on shared first-person servant speech and themes of suffering and vindication.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · Conventional category; rationale and boundaries disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Who is speaking in verses 1-3, 4-9, and 10-11? What textual signals mark each transition?

2. What does verse 10 imply about the assumption that faithful people should always experience clarity or consolation?

3. How does the repetition of the Lord's help in verses 7 and 9 qualify the servant's flint-like resolve?

4. Where do the passion parallels support a messianic reading, and where does the text leave the servant's identity open?

5. How do the surrounding chapters change the meaning of the divorce, redemption, and darkness imagery?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare 2 Nephi 7 line by line with [[Isaiah 50]], recording both agreements and differences before assigning significance to them. Then compare the sea-drying imagery with Exodus 14 and the continuation of the redemption theme in [[2 Nephi 8]]. Romans 8:33-34 is useful for studying the later scriptural reception of the courtroom language. Matthew 26 and Matthew 27 provide the clearest passion parallels, but comparison should distinguish verbal or thematic correspondence from proof of exclusive prophetic fulfillment.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
