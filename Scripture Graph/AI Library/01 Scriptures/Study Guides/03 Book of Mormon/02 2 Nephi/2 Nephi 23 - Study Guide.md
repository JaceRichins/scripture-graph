# 2 Nephi 23 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Nephi 23]] · My notes: [[2 Nephi 23 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The destruction of Babylon is a type of the destruction at the Second Coming—It will be a day of wrath and vengeance—Babylon (the world) will fall forever—Compare Isaiah 13. About 559–545 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verse 1:** Superscription naming Babylon and Isaiah as the oracle's source.
2. **Verses 2–5:** The Lord summons and musters an army from distant kingdoms.
3. **Verses 6–13:** The day of the Lord brings terror, moral judgment (pride and haughtiness abased), and cosmic disorder — darkened sun, moon, and stars; the shaking of heaven and earth.
4. **Verses 14–16:** Flight, social disintegration, and wartime atrocities, including harm to children and women.
5. **Verses 17–22:** The Medes are named as the specific instrument of judgment; Babylon falls and is left desolate, compared to the overthrow of Sodom and Gomorrah; the chapter closes by contrasting mercy toward 'my people' with the fate of the wicked.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The stated moral targets of judgment include evil, iniquity, arrogance, and haughtiness (verse 11), making [[Pride]] central to the oracle. Yet the chapter's depictions of children killed and women ravished require moral seriousness; they should not be softened into decorative apocalyptic imagery. The text attributes the mustering of the conquerors to the Lord, but it does not offer a philosophical explanation of divine responsibility for every atrocity it depicts.

Verse 22 places [[Justice and Mercy]] together in one closing contrast: mercy is promised to "my people," while the wicked perish. The verse does not define that community in detail or explain all difficult cases within the preceding destruction.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter shifts scale repeatedly: from a banner on a mountain, to gathered kingdoms, to the whole world and heavens, and finally back to the Medes and Babylon. Similes of a chased roe and an abandoned sheep portray helpless flight, while the comparison with Sodom and Gomorrah expresses catastrophic reversal. The ruined city's occupation by wild creatures replaces its former description as "the glory of kingdoms," sharpening the movement from splendor to desolation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Correspondence
> The verified chapter-wide textual correspondence with Isaiah 13 is strong evidence that 2 Nephi 23 presents that Isaianic oracle. The correspondence establishes a literary relationship much more securely than it establishes any particular account of authorship or translation.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strong textual match; authorship/dating separately disputed · [[Textual Correspondence in 2 Nephi 23|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Predictive-Specificity / Composition-Dating *(tentative)*
> Verse 17's naming of 'the Medes' as Babylon's destroyer matches the historical fact that Babylon fell in 539 B.C. to combined Medo-Persian forces under Cyrus the Great. Taken as an 8th-century-B.C. prediction (per the traditional authorship claimed in v.1, 'Isaiah the son of Amoz'), this is a specific and historically accurate detail delivered well over a century before the events (Babylon was not yet even the dominant regional power in Isaiah's lifetime).
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · authorship/dating of Isaiah 13 genuinely disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the cosmic language in verses 10–13 describe literal end-time events, conventional prophetic imagery for political collapse, or both through typology?

- How does naming the Medes in verse 17 constrain interpretations that treat Babylon only as a symbol of the latter-day world?

- What does the text affirm—and what does it leave unexplained—when it presents invading armies as instruments of divine judgment?

- Who are "my people" in verse 22 within the chapter's immediate setting and within Nephi's larger use of Isaiah?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Open questions worth sitting with rather than resolving prematurely:
- How literally should the 'never inhabited...from generation to generation' language (vv.20-22) be read, given that ancient Babylon was in fact inhabited for centuries after its 539 B.C. conquest?
- What is gained, and what is risked, by reading a historically-specific oracle (naming the Medes, a real 6th-century empire) as *also* a type of the Second Coming — where does the typology start and the literal history end?
- Mainstream biblical scholarship widely dates the Babylon oracle in Isaiah 13-14 to the exilic or post-exilic period rather than to the 8th-century Isaiah ben Amoz named in v.1; the Book of Mormon's placement of this full text on the brass plates by c. 600 B.C. takes a clear position on that contested question worth studying both LDS and non-LDS scholarship on.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
