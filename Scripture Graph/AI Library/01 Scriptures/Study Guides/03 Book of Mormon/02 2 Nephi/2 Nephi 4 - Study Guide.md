# 2 Nephi 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Nephi 4]] · My notes: [[2 Nephi 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Lehi counsels and blesses his posterity—He dies and is buried—Nephi glories in the goodness of God—Nephi puts his trust in the Lord forever. About 588–570 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Joseph's prophecies (vv. 1–2):** Nephi closes the preceding discussion by locating Joseph's prophecies on the plates of brass.
2. **Lehi's final blessings (vv. 3–11):** Lehi addresses the descendants of Laman and Lemuel, the household of Ishmael, and Sam.
3. **Death and renewed conflict (vv. 12–14):** Lehi dies; soon afterward, members of the family become angry with Nephi over his admonitions.
4. **Editorial explanation (vv. 14–16):** Nephi distinguishes the more historical record from the plates devoted to the things of his soul and to scripture.
5. **Lament and remembrance (vv. 17–25):** Nephi admits moral distress but rehearses God's past support and revelations.
6. **Questions, self-exhortation, and prayer (vv. 26–35):** He challenges his despair, commands his soul to awake, petitions God, and renews his trust.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Lehi's blessings hold together personal obedience, parental influence, inherited consequences, and divine mercy. The passage should be read carefully: it does not provide a complete theory of moral accountability, nor does it say that descendants are incapable of choosing differently from their parents.

Nephi's prayer presents remembrance as a response to discouragement. He recalls concrete acts of divine support before exhorting himself to rejoice and pray. His requested deliverance is both external and internal: escape from enemies accompanies a desire to recoil from sin, walk a straight path, and be encircled in divine righteousness.

Verse 26 turns from remembered experience to argument: having witnessed "the Lord in his condescension unto the children of men" visit men "in so much mercy," Nephi treats continued despair as inconsistent with what he already knows — part of what licenses the self-command "Awake, my soul!" in verse 28, though the whole recollection of verses 20-25, not [[Condescension of God]] alone, supplies that premise.

The closing rejection of the "arm of flesh" (v34) concerns the object of ultimate trust; it need not be read as a prohibition on receiving legitimate human help.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several phrases in this chapter track closely with familiar King James idiom used elsewhere in the Bible: 'O wretched man that I am!' (v17) matches Romans 7:24; 'brought up in the way ye should go... will not depart from it' (v5) matches Proverbs 22:6; 'cursed is he that... maketh flesh his arm' (v34) matches Jeremiah 17:5; 'the rock of my salvation' (v30) resembles the 'Rock of his salvation' epithet in Deuteronomy 32:15 (though rock-as-deliverer imagery is common in biblical poetry generally); and 'God will give liberally to him that asketh' (v35) echoes James 1:5. Taken individually, each is a modest resemblance; taken together, they form a genuine pattern worth noting for readers interested in how the English text's phrasing relates to the King James Bible — a question about translation and idiom that the text itself does not settle, and for which direct nineteenth-century dependence on the KJV is as live a possibility as translation of an ancient idiom or shared biblical vocabulary.

Nephi's plea 'my heart is broken and my spirit is contrite' (v32) also echoes biblical penitential language. The closer verbal match — matching both word order and pairing — is Psalm 34:18 ('a broken heart, and a contrite spirit') rather than Psalm 51:17, which reverses the pairing ('a broken spirit... a broken and a contrite heart').
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 17–35 are reasonably described as psalm-like because they combine lament, recollection of deliverance, self-address, rhetorical questions, petition, praise, enemies, paths, and rock imagery. Comparisons with [[Psalm 18]] and [[2 Samuel 22]] clarify these shared conventions. The description "psalm-like" is safer than claiming a demonstrated formal genre or direct dependence on one biblical composition.

The most important movement is internal: "my heart sorroweth" gives way to "Awake, my soul," and then to direct petitions beginning "O Lord." Remembered experience functions as the hinge between lament and renewed resolve.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> The five-stage movement of 2 Nephi 4:16-35 (address/praise, complaint, remembered deliverance, self-exhortation, petition-and-vow) parallels the classic form-critical shape of the individual lament psalm found repeatedly in the Hebrew Psalter (e.g., Psalms 13, 22, 42-43), a genre pattern long recognized by biblical form critics and applied to this chapter by LDS scholars under the label 'the Psalm of Nephi.'

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What particular sins or temptations trouble Nephi? The text deliberately or incidentally leaves them unspecified.
- Are the "enemies" in the prayer Laman, Lemuel, and the sons of Ishmael, other opponents, spiritual adversaries, or a mixture? The preceding narrative makes a family connection plausible but not certain.
- Does the mountain experience in verse 25 refer to the vision narrated in [[1 Nephi 11]] (which uses nearly identical wording, 'exceedingly high mountain'), another recorded experience, or an otherwise undisclosed revelation?
- How should Lehi's statements about consequences falling on parents be reconciled with individual agency and accountability? The chapter supplies a blessing and promise, not a systematic explanation.
- Lehi blesses the sons of Ishmael (v10) yet they are named among those angry with Nephi only a few verses later (v13) — what does this juxtaposition suggest about the limits of a blessing to change behavior?
- Why might Nephi choose to record his private spiritual struggle (vv17-19) rather than only triumphant testimony — what does that choice suggest about the purpose of "the things of my soul" (v15)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
