# 2 Nephi 26 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Nephi 26]] · My notes: [[2 Nephi 26 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Christ will minister to the Nephites—Nephi foresees the destruction of his people—They will speak from the dust—The Gentiles will build up false churches and secret combinations—The Lord forbids men to practice priestcrafts. About 559–545 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- vv. 1-6: Christ's future ministry to the Nephites, and the destruction awaiting the wicked before it
- v. 7: Nephi's lament for the slain, followed by submission to divine justice ('Thy ways are just')
- vv. 8-11: The righteous who look to Christ are promised healing and peace for several generations; then the Spirit's eventual withdrawal and 'speedy destruction'
- vv. 12-13: Jews and Gentiles alike to be convinced Jesus is the Christ
- vv. 14-19: A last-days prophecy — a people dwindled in unbelief and besieged, whose words are nevertheless written, sealed, and preserved
- vv. 20-22: Gentile pride, false churches, and secret combinations
- vv. 23-28: Rhetorical questions insisting God excludes no one (the fourfold Q&A pattern runs vv. 25-28, building on the premise stated in vv. 23-24)
- vv. 29-33: Priestcraft defined and forbidden; [[Charity]] commanded; universal moral law; the closing declaration that all are alike unto God
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Priestcraft** is explicitly defined here (v. 29) rather than left to inference: preaching and setting oneself up 'for a light unto the world' in order to get gain and worldly praise, without seeking the welfare of [[Zion]]. The definition centers on motive and self-promotion rather than on compensation alone; v. 31's warning that laborers in Zion who 'labor for money... shall perish' sharpens the same concern without settling every question about legitimate support for religious labor.

**Charity** is defined in-text as love, and its absence as amounting to nothing (v. 30) — a formulation this record develops further elsewhere (compare [[Moroni 7]]).

**Universal invitation and moral law**: vv. 23-28 repeatedly deny that God excludes anyone from worship or salvation, offering it 'without money and without price.' Verse 32 lists prohibited conduct — murder, lying, stealing, taking the Lord's name in vain, envy, malice, contention, and sexual immorality — and v. 33 closes by declaring that God denies none who come to him, 'black and white, bond and free, male and female,' and counts Jew and Gentile alike. The verse's explicit subject is the invitation to receive Christ's goodness; readers should be cautious about extending it, without further argument, to settle every later question about ecclesiastical practice.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The 'voice from the dust' passage in vv. 15-18 closely parallels [[Isaiah 29]] (compare Isaiah 29:3-5): siege and being brought low, a voice speaking 'out of the ground' and 'low out of the dust,' chaff, and sudden destruction all appear in nearly the same sequence, applied here to the preserved words of a destroyed people rather than to a besieged city. The stubble/'Lord of Hosts' burning language in vv. 4-6 similarly parallels [[Malachi 4]]. The two parallels are not on equal historical footing: Isaiah was available to Nephi on the brass plates, while Malachi is conventionally dated about a century after Lehi left Jerusalem, which makes the Malachi correspondence harder to explain as ancient allusion and easier to read as reflecting familiar King James wording carried into the English text through the translation process. Verse 25's invitation to 'buy milk and honey... without money and without price' echoes Isaiah 55:1's 'buy wine and milk without money and without price'; the 'milk and honey' substitution may or may not be a deliberate allusion to the Exodus promised-land formula, but the text alone does not settle authorial intent.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 25-28 build a repeated question-and-answer pattern — 'Behold, doth/hath he...? Behold, I say unto you, Nay' — that makes God's openness the direct antithesis of the darkness, profit-seeking, and exclusion criticized in vv. 20-22. 'Saith the Lord of Hosts' recurs three times in vv. 4-6 to punctuate a list of judgments. The devil's method in v. 22 is compressed into a single image: a 'flaxen cord' — initially slight and easily underestimated — that tightens until it 'bindeth them with his strong cords forever,' figuring temptation as gradual rather than sudden bondage.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> The language of the coming day consuming the proud and wicked as stubble closely corresponds to Malachi 4:1. The verbal relationship is clear, while its historical or translational explanation remains disputed.

> [!evidence] Scriptural Intertextuality
> The sequence of siege, being brought low, speech from the ground, whispering from the dust, chaff, and sudden destruction closely follows Isaiah 29:3-5. This is strong evidence of a literary relationship between the passages.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation · [[Scriptural Intertextuality in 2 Nephi 26|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual-Parallel
> 2 Nephi 26:16's imagery of a destroyed people whose speech is 'low out of the dust' and whose voice is 'as one that hath a familiar spirit' closely parallels the well-known KJV language of Isaiah 29:4, which describes a humbled Jerusalem/Ariel speaking 'low out of the dust' with a voice 'as of one that hath a familiar spirit, out of the ground.' Nephi elsewhere quotes Isaiah 29 at length (2 Nephi 27), making direct literary dependence between these passages highly plausible.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation · [[Intertextual-Parallel in 2 Nephi 26|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual-Parallel *(tentative)*
> Verse 25's invitation, 'Come unto me all ye ends of the earth, buy milk and honey, without money and without price,' echoes the structure and closing phrase of Isaiah 55:1's invitation to 'buy wine and milk without money and without price,' but substitutes 'milk and honey' for Isaiah's 'wine and milk' — a change some readers take as a deliberate allusion to the 'land flowing with milk and honey' (Exodus/promised-land imagery) layered onto an Isaiah-based invitation.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.7 · speculative

> [!evidence] Doctrinal-Historical *(tentative)*
> The universal, non-exclusionary language of v. 33 sits in notable tension with the LDS Church's own history of restricting priesthood ordination and temple ordinances for members of Black African descent from the mid-19th century until a 1978 revelation lifted the restriction. The verse itself does not specify whether 'alike unto God' concerns priesthood office, temple ordinances, or the general gospel invitation, and it has been read differently at different periods.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · true but requires citation before wider use

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean that Christ's own spoken words, not only his atonement, are called 'the law which ye shall do' (v. 1)?
- How does Nephi's grief in vv. 7, 10-11 shape the way the surrounding prophecies of judgment should be read?
- What distinguishes priestcraft (v. 29) from legitimate religious labor, and how do motive, compensation, and concern for Zion's welfare (v. 31) each play a role?
- How does the universal invitation of vv. 24-28 prepare for the paired categories named in v. 33 — and what is, and isn't, settled by that verse alone?
- What changes when the siege imagery of [[Isaiah 29]] is reapplied here to a people's surviving record rather than to a city?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
