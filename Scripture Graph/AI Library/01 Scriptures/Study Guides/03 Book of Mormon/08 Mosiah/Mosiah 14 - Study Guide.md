# Mosiah 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mosiah 14]] · My notes: [[Mosiah 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Mosiah 14 is not narrative but quotation: on trial before the priests of King Noah, Abinadi recites in full what is elsewhere catalogued as [[Isaiah 53]] — the most sustained of Isaiah's "servant songs." The unnamed servant is unimpressive in appearance and rejected (vv.1-3); the speakers come to attribute his suffering to their own transgressions and iniquities (vv.4-6); he suffers and dies in silence despite his innocence (vv.7-9); and he is afterward promised seed, prolonged days, and a portion with the great (vv.10-12). The chapter itself never names the servant. That identification — and Abinadi's further claim that this sufferer is the same being who is both Father and Son — is supplied by the discourse immediately following in [[Mosiah 15]], not by this chapter on its own. Isaiah's servant has been read very differently across interpretive traditions (an individual Messiah, a specific historical figure, or collective/exiled Israel), and Abinadi's messianic-individual reading, while consistent with later New Testament usage, does not settle that older exegetical question.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter is a single unbroken poetic unit rather than a narrative with formal divisions, built substantially (though not exclusively) on Hebrew-style parallelism. A working outline: vv.1-3 (unbelief and the servant's lowly, rejected appearance), vv.4-6 (the speakers reinterpret his suffering as connected to their own wrongdoing), vv.7-9 (silent affliction, death, burial, and asserted innocence), vv.10-12 (offering for sin, promised seed, justification, and intercession). The first-person plural voice ("we hid our faces," "we esteemed him not") already appears in v.3; the real turn at vv.4-5 is not the introduction of "we" but the shift to causal, self-implicating language ("wounded for our transgressions... bruised for our iniquities"). The chapter's placement matters: it serves as Abinadi's scriptural text, which he expounds doctrinally in the immediately following [[Mosiah 15]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter supplies concentrated language later drawn on for the doctrine of the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]: a righteous servant bears others' griefs, sorrows, transgressions, and iniquities; his suffering is tied to healing and to justifying "many"; his soul becomes "an offering for sin"; and he "made intercession for the transgressors." This strongly supports a representative or vicarious reading of his suffering, but the chapter does not by itself specify a complete mechanism — penal-substitution, moral-influence, and other theological models are all compatible with this language. That further theological development belongs to later doctrine and to [[Mosiah 15]]'s own exposition, not to this chapter's text alone.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verse 1's "arm of the Lord" is a Hebrew idiom for God's power made visible; the question of who has seen it revealed sets up the chapter's central irony — that this power appears in a form (vv.2-3) with "no form nor comeliness." The poem's imagery moves across several domains: plant and root (v.2), illness and wounds (vv.3-5), wandering sheep and sacrificial lamb (vv.6-7), legal detention and judgment (v.8), grave and death (v.9, v.12), cultic sin offering (v.10), travail (v.11), and military spoil (v.12). The pronouns carry much of the argument: "he" stands against "we," "our," and "us" throughout, until vv.10-12 shift toward divine declarations of what the servant will accomplish.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The poem repeatedly overturns appearances. The servant lacks visible desirability yet becomes the agent of healing; observers suppose that God has struck him yet confess their own implication; an innocent person receives treatment associated with evildoers; and language of death is followed by seed, prolonged days, success, and victory. This reversal pattern supports a vindication reading. A resurrection reading is coherent within the Book of Mormon's christological interpretation, but resurrection is not named explicitly in this chapter.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> Mosiah 14 reproduces the twelve-verse sequence of Isaiah 53 in closely corresponding English and explicitly introduces it as Isaiah's words. This is conclusive evidence of an intended literary relationship between the two chapters.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted · [[Explicit Quotation And Textual Overlap in Mosiah 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Interpretive *(tentative)*
> The identity of the 'servant' in this song is genuinely disputed in biblical scholarship: Christian tradition (ancient and modern) has predominantly read it as an individual, suffering Messiah, while much of the Jewish interpretive tradition has read the servant as a figure for Israel collectively, suffering among/for the nations. Abinadi's reading in Mosiah 15 is a messianic-individual reading consistent with the Christian tradition, but it does not settle the question for readers evaluating the text on independent historical-critical grounds.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · disputed, needs scholarly sourcing to firm up

> [!evidence] Textual-Critical *(tentative)*
> The extremely close, near-verbatim correspondence between Mosiah 14 and the King James Version's rendering of Isaiah 53 (spanning all twelve verses) is a well-documented feature of Book of Mormon Isaiah quotations generally, and its significance is read very differently by different observers.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · disputed, common-ancestor explanation overstated

> [!evidence] Textual Variant *(tentative)*

If that recollection is correct, this is a small but notable wording difference in the Book of Mormon's quotation of Isaiah 53 worth flagging for text-critical study rather than treating the two texts as letter-for-letter identical.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- If read without Abinadi's commentary in [[Mosiah 15]], what in this chapter itself points to who the servant is?
- Why does the speakers' account turn in vv.4-6 from disregarding the servant to confessing that his suffering was "for our transgressions"?
- What is gained, and what is risked, by treating the corporate (Israel) and individual (messianic) readings of the servant as mutually exclusive rather than layered?
- How should the servant's death (vv.8-9, 12) relate to his "seeing his seed" and prolonging his days (v.10)? Does the text itself support a resurrection reading, or only later interpretation?
- Why would Abinadi choose this particular passage — an unnamed sufferer — to make his case to hostile priests, rather than a more overtly triumphant messianic text?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare directly, verse by verse, against [[Isaiah 53]], then read [[Mosiah 15]] for Abinadi's own interpretation. For the passage's reception in the New Testament, compare [[Matthew 8]], [[Luke 22]], [[John 12]], [[Acts 8]], and [[1 Peter 2]] — each applies part of this song to Jesus. Keep two questions separate as you read: how later scripture applies this poem, and what the servant may have meant in Isaiah's own historical setting — a question this chapter's quotation does not itself resolve.
