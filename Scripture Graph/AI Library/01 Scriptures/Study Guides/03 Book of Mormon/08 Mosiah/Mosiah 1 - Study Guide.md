## Overview

> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> King Benjamin teaches his sons the language and prophecies of their fathers—Their religion and civilization have been preserved because of the records kept on the various plates—Mosiah is chosen as king and is given custody of the records and other things. About 130–124 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter can be read in four movements:
1. **Private instruction (vv. 2-8):** Benjamin teaches his sons language, prophecy, and the contents of the brass plates and plates of Nephi.
2. **Succession decided (vv. 9-14):** Benjamin selects Mosiah, orders a proclamation and gathering, and announces a new, conditionally-permanent name for the people.
3. **Transfer of custody (vv. 15-17):** the kingdom's affairs and four inherited items — brass plates, plates of Nephi, sword of Laban, and [[Liahona (compass)|ball or director]] — pass to Mosiah.
4. **Public execution (v. 18):** Mosiah proclaims the gathering, leading directly into [[Mosiah 2]].

Movements 1 and 3 both concern records, which invites reading the chapter as bracketing the succession material between two record-focused sections. That is a reasonable thematic observation, but it falls short of a formal inclusio — the chapter doesn't repeat a distinctive framing phrase between the two sections, and the underlying logic may simply be chronological (teach the heirs, choose the successor, hand over responsibility, execute the order).
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Two threads run through the chapter. First, **records as a safeguard for belief**: Benjamin states that without the brass plates 'even our fathers would have dwindled in unbelief' (v.5). But the chapter does not present writing as sufficient by itself — Benjamin also commands his sons to 'search them diligently' (v.7) and to keep God's commandments, and he credits divine preservation ('kept and preserved by the hand of God,' v.5) alongside the records themselves. Records, literacy, teaching, diligent study, and obedience together form the preservation mechanism the text describes — not records alone.

Second, **conditional covenant identity**: the new name Benjamin will give the people 'never shall be blotted out, except it be through transgression' (v.12), and a wicked, 'adulterous' people would be delivered up to weakness (v.13). The chapter's prosperity language (v.7) is likewise conditioned specifically on searching the records and keeping the commandments — it should be read within that covenant framework, not generalized into an unconditional promise that record-possession or relic-custody itself guarantees safety or prosperity (contrast the famine of v.17, which the text attributes to unfaithfulness, not to lacking the objects).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated vocabulary of **fathers**, **children**, **remember**, **records**, and **teach** creates an intergenerational chain. Mosiah 1:2 and 1:4 should not be collapsed carelessly: the sons learn “the language of his fathers,” while Lehi is separately said to have learned “the language of the Egyptians.” The chapter does not define either phrase closely enough to prove that they denote the same linguistic system.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The movement from private instruction to public transfer and assembly makes preservation the chapter's organizing concern: teachings pass from father to sons, records and objects pass from king to successor, and the people are summoned to receive a public message. Benjamin's own argument leans on the records' visibility ('we can know of their surety because we have them before our eyes,' v.6) — a character's rhetorical claim, not the narrator's independent verification.

The 'ball or director' of verse 16 is left unnamed and unexplained here; a fuller account of how it operated 'according to the faith and diligence' given it appears later, in Alma 37:38-47. That said, the director was already introduced and described operating on faith and diligence back in 1 Nephi 16:28-29, so Mosiah 1 is not the reader's first encounter with the concept, and it would overstate the case to call the later Alma passage a deliberately withheld 'payoff' of a detail invented here — it is a continuation of an already-established motif.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- The chapter distinguishes possessing records, reading them, and 'profiting' from them (v.7) — what does Benjamin think closes the gap between the first and the last?
- Why are the records, the sword of Laban, and the [[Liahona (compass)|ball or director]] transferred together with governmental responsibility (vv.15-16)? Does the text treat them as necessary to legitimate rule, or simply as inherited stewardship alongside it?
- What can legitimately be inferred from 'the language of the Egyptians' (v.4), and what remains unstated about Nephite language and script?
- Benjamin's account of Lamanite unbelief (v.5) comes from an opposing ruler with no Lamanite voice represented — how should that shape how much weight the description carries?
- The text distinguishes 'the people of Zarahemla' from 'the people of Mosiah' even while gathering them as one audience (v.10) — what does this suggest about how fully those two groups had merged by Benjamin's day?
- Why does the chapter postpone the actual content of the promised name beyond this chapter?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- [[Omni 1]] for the earlier account of the Mulekite/Nephite political union presupposed by 'the people of Zarahemla, and the people of Mosiah' (v.10), and for its own record-and-language-loss narrative paralleling Benjamin's argument here.
- [[2 Nephi 5]] for Nephi's original acquisition and use of the brass plates, sword of Laban, and director.
- 1 Nephi 16:28-29 for the director's earlier, faith-and-diligence-conditioned operation, and Alma 37:38-47 for its fullest explanation and naming as the [[Liahona (compass)]].
- [[Mosiah 28]] for the next transfer of records and preserved objects, this time from Mosiah to Alma the younger (verify the specific items named there before treating it as an exact repeat of Mosiah 1:16's list).
- Stephen D. Ricks, 'Kingship, Coronation, and Covenant in Mosiah 1-6,' and Terrence L. Szink and John W. Welch, 'King Benjamin's Speech in the Context of Ancient Israelite Festivals,' both in *King Benjamin's Speech: "That Ye May Learn Wisdom"* (FARMS, 1998) — believing scholarship reading this chapter's succession-and-covenant pattern against ancient Israelite ceremony. The parallel is suggestive but not distinctive: similar patterns (aging king, public succession, conditional national blessing) appear throughout the King James Old Testament material available to a 19th-century author, so this should be treated as a low-strength comparative argument, not evidence of antiquity.
