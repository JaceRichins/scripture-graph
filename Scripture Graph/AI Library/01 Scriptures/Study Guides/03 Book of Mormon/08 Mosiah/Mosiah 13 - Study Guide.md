# Mosiah 13 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mosiah 13]] · My notes: [[Mosiah 13 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter moves from confrontation to exposition. King Noah orders Abinadi's death, but Abinadi remains under divine protection just long enough to finish delivering his message (vv. 1-10). He then recites the remainder of the [[Ten Commandments]] (vv. 11-24) and indicts the priests for failing to teach the people to keep them (vv. 25-26). In the final movement he distinguishes the [[Law of Moses]]'s temporary, instructional role from the redemption that only an [[Atonement of Jesus Christ|atonement]] God himself will make can provide (vv. 27-32), closing by appealing to Moses and 'all the prophets' as having already testified that God would come down in the form of man, be afflicted, and bring about the [[Resurrection]] (vv. 33-35).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Threat and temporary protection (vv. 1-10):** Noah orders Abinadi's death; the priests cannot lay hands on him because 'the Spirit of the Lord was upon him' (v.5), and Abinadi insists he must finish his message — noting that what is later done to him will be 'a type and a shadow of things which are to come' (v.10).
2. **Commandments and indictment (vv. 11-26):** Abinadi recites the Decalogue, closely following the wording of Exodus 20, and asks whether the priests have taught the people to obey it.
3. **Law, atonement, and Messiah (vv. 27-35):** The law remains expedient 'as yet,' but salvation depends on the atonement God himself will make (vv. 27-28, 32); Abinadi calls the law's performances and ordinances 'types of things to come' (v.31) and closes by grounding the coming Messiah, his suffering, and the resurrection in the testimony of Moses and 'all the prophets' (vv.33-35).

The phrase 'type... of things to come' appears at both v.10 (applied to Abinadi's own treatment) and v.31 (applied to the law); the two uses are thematically related, but the chapter does not specify a single referent for v.10, so reading them as a deliberate structural frame should remain tentative.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Abinadi's argument preserves several qualifications rather than rejecting the law outright. The law is still expedient 'as yet' (v. 27), so he is not dismissing obedience; salvation does not come by the law 'alone' (v. 28), so his claim concerns the law's insufficiency rather than its worthlessness. The law reminds Israel of God and duty and functions typologically (vv. 29-31), while the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] and divine redemption supply what the law cannot accomplish by itself (vv. 28, 32-35). Within the commandments themselves, the text pairs [[Justice and Mercy]]: God 'visiting the iniquities of the fathers upon the children... unto the third and fourth generations of them that hate me,' but 'showing mercy unto thousands of them that love me and keep my commandments' (vv. 13-14).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three limiting expressions guard against overreading the passage: protection lasts only 'at this time' (v. 3); the law remains expedient 'as yet' (v. 27); and Abinadi says Israel did 'not all' understand the law (v. 32) — each preventing an overclaim (permanent immunity, immediate abolition of the law, or blanket Israelite ignorance). Separately, the commandments themselves are rendered in wording close enough to the KJV Exodus 20 text — including the specific item order of the covetousness clause (v.24, matching Exodus 20:17 rather than Deuteronomy 5:21) and the creation-based Sabbath rationale (v.19, matching Exodus 20:11 rather than Deuteronomy 5:15's exodus-based rationale) — that the chapter functions as a close English-language reproduction of the Exodus form of the Decalogue specifically.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The shining-face detail in v.5 casts Abinadi in a Moses-like prophetic role — compare the shining face Moses receives after speaking with the Lord in [[Exodus 34]] — just before Abinadi recites Moses' own commandments and then reinterprets their ultimate purpose. The chapter also uses 'type' language twice: Abinadi calls what will be done to him a 'type and a shadow' (v.10), then calls the Mosaic performances and ordinances 'types of things to come' (v.31). The two uses are thematically related, but this chapter does not specify what future event v.10 refers to; that question is best tested against [[Mosiah 17]] and Abinadi's subsequent fate rather than assumed.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why are the commandments rendered in wording so close to Exodus 20 rather than paraphrased?
- What is the full referent of Abinadi's statement that his treatment will be 'a type and a shadow of things which are to come' (v.10) — does [[Mosiah 17]] resolve it?
- Does 'not written in your hearts' (v.11) describe the priests' inward disposition, their failure to teach the people (vv.25-26), or both?
- How do the mnemonic and typological purposes assigned to the law (vv.29-31) relate to the claim that it cannot save 'alone' (v.28)?
- What does verse 32's qualification that Israel did 'not all' understand the law prevent readers from claiming?
