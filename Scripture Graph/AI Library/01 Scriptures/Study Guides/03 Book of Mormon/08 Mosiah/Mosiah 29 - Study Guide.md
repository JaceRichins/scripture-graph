# Mosiah 29 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mosiah 29]] · My notes: [[Mosiah 29 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Mosiah proposes that judges be chosen in place of a king—Unrighteous kings lead their people into sin—Alma the younger is chosen chief judge by the voice of the people—He is also the high priest over the Church—Alma the elder and Mosiah die. About 92–91 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Succession crisis (vv. 1–10):** Aaron and Mosiah's other sons refuse the throne; Mosiah anticipates disputed claims, faction, and bloodshed.
2. **Argument against monarchy (vv. 11–24):** Mosiah grants that consistently righteous kings would be desirable but argues that no society can guarantee them. King Noah supplies the negative precedent.
3. **Proposed safeguards (vv. 25–36):** judges are selected by the people's voice, unjust judgments may be reviewed, and public responsibility replaces the concentrated burden of kingship.
4. **Ratification and transition (vv. 37–47):** the people accept the argument, select judges, and appoint Alma chief judge. The deaths of Alma the elder and Mosiah close the chapter and the monarchical era.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The political argument rests on a theology of judgment: "it is better that a man should be judged of God than of man, for the judgments of God are always just, but the judgments of man are not always just" (29:12) — a reminder that no human office can substitute for divine justice, which shapes Mosiah's preference for distributing power rather than concentrating it in one fallible ruler. The chapter also engages [[Agency]] and accountability along two related but distinct lines: a civic one, where "the burden should come upon all the people, that every man might bear his part" (29:34) — citizens now share the practical work of self-government instead of leaving it to one man — and a moral one, where under kings "the sins of many people have been caused by the iniquities of their kings" (29:31), but under judges each person "shall be answered upon their own heads" (29:30), a redistribution the people embrace when "every man expressed a willingness to answer for his own sins" (29:38). King Noah's reign supplies the negative precedent, but the recollection of his people's bondage also follows a pattern of [[Repentance]], [[Humility]], and [[Prayer]] within a larger arc of [[Exodus and Deliverance]]: after bondage they humble themselves, cry to God, and receive mercy (29:18-20). The chapter ties [[Freedom and Liberty]] to equality: Mosiah desires "that this inequality should be no more in this land" and "every man may enjoy his rights and privileges alike" (29:32) — a promise the text itself qualifies by "so long as the Lord sees fit that we may live and inherit the land."
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase “voice of the people” describes both collective preference and the procedure for public business, but the chapter does not define an electorate or voting method. “Every man” similarly expresses equal opportunity and accountability within the text without establishing the inclusion standards expected of modern universal suffrage.

The terminology also resists a simple modern classification. Judges are appointed “to rule over” the people, and the era is called the “reign of the judges” (vv. 41, 44). Alma is simultaneously chief judge and high priest (v. 42). Thus labels such as democracy, republic, separation of church and state, or independent judiciary require qualifications not supplied by the chapter.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Mosiah's written address uses conditional reasoning rather than a simple slogan: if rulers could always be just, kingship would be expedient; because that condition cannot be guaranteed, power should be redistributed (29:12-17). The phrase "voice of the people" (or "voice of this people") then recurs as a structuring keyword at the chapter's key turns — the original request for a king (29:2), the institution of judges (29:25-26), and the mechanism for checking judges (29:29) — binding the abstract argument to its narrated implementation. King Noah's reign turns the abstract risk of tyranny into a remembered sequence of wickedness, bondage, repentance, and deliverance (29:18-20). The chapter concludes with rapid institutional and generational closure: judges are appointed, Alma and Mosiah die, and the narrator declares both the reign of kings and Alma the elder's days ended (29:41-47).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What assumptions about majority behavior underlie Mosiah's claim that the people's voice does not commonly choose what is wrong, and how does verse 27 qualify that confidence?
- Does distributing the burden among the people reduce injustice, or chiefly redistribute accountability for it?
- How should Alma's simultaneous roles as chief judge and high priest affect modern attempts to classify the new government?
- Which elements of the reform are genuine restraints on power, and which details necessary to evaluate those restraints are absent from the text?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Mosiah 11]] alongside verses 17–24 to test how accurately Mosiah's summary selects the features of King Noah's reign relevant to tyranny. Compare [[Mosiah 22]] with verses 18–20 for the fuller deliverance narrative. Then follow the institutional experiment into [[Alma 1]], [[Alma 2]], and [[Alma 51]], where religious conflict, an attempted kingship, and the king-men controversy expose pressures not resolved merely by replacing kings with judges.
