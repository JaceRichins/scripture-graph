# Mosiah 16 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mosiah 16]] · My notes: [[Mosiah 16 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> God redeems men from their lost and fallen state—Those who are carnal remain as though there were no redemption—Christ brings to pass a resurrection to endless life or to endless damnation. About 148 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Universal disclosure and the fallen condition (vv. 1-5):** all will acknowledge God's just judgments; the Fall explains humanity's lost condition, while persistent rebellion leaves a person “as though there was no redemption made.”

2. **Christ defeats death (vv. 6-9):** two theological counterfactuals describe the necessity of Christ's coming and rising, followed by the emphatic affirmation, “But there is a resurrection.”

3. **Resurrection and judgment (vv. 10-12):** mortals put on immortality and stand before God's bar; the text contrasts life and happiness with damnation and emphasizes repeated refusal of offered mercy.

4. **Appeal and commission (vv. 13-15):** Abinadi calls his hearers to repent and commands them to teach the [[Law of Moses]] as a shadow pointing to redemption through Christ.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Humanity cannot escape its fallen condition without God's redemption, and resurrection depends upon Christ (vv. 4, 6-9, 13, 15). At the same time, the chapter treats people as accountable: they persist in rebellion, follow carnal desires, and repeatedly refuse mercy, warning, and the command to repent (vv. 2, 5, 12). Thus [[Salvation]] originates in Christ while [[Final Judgment]] takes works and human response seriously.

Verse 5 qualifies verse 2 by describing the persistent sinner as **“as though”** no redemption had been made. This supports the interpretation that redemption exists but its saving benefit is refused; it does not answer every broader question about grace, knowledge, or capacity.

Verses 10-11 express judgment through a binary contrast between life and damnation. They do not enumerate the [[Kingdoms of Glory]], but silence about gradations is not evidence that Abinadi rejected them.

The title “very Eternal Father” should be studied with [[Mosiah 15]] and [[The Godhead]]. It strongly affirms Christ's divinity, but these verses alone do not exhaustively define the relationship between the Father and the Son.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verse 6 comments on its own prophetic presentation: Abinadi is “speaking of things to come as though they had already come.” This resembles what is often called the prophetic perfect, but an English description of the phenomenon does not demonstrate Hebrew source grammar or ancient authorship.

The repeated refusals in verses 2 and 12 give the warning cumulative force: the wicked do not hearken, call upon the Lord, depart from iniquity, or repent. This rhetoric strongly emphasizes responsibility, though conclusions about the complete justice of punishment remain interpretive.

The phrase “carnal, sensual, and devilish” also appears in Moses 5:13. The correspondence is noteworthy, but without corpus analysis it should be treated as shared Restoration-era wording rather than evidence of ancient literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Mosiah 16 contains several conspicuous scriptural correspondences. Verse 1 combines Isaiah 52:8's “eye to eye” language with the universal sight of salvation in Isaiah 52:10, returning to material quoted by the priests in [[Mosiah 12]]. Verses 7-10 closely parallel 1 Corinthians 15:53-55 in their language of mortality, immortality, corruption, incorruption, death's sting, and the grave's lost victory. The imagery also has earlier antecedents in Isaiah 25:8 and Hosea 13:14. Verses 10-11 resemble John 5:29's contrast between resurrections of life and damnation. Verse 14 parallels both Hebrews 10:1 and, more closely in wording, Colossians 2:17 on the law as a shadow of things to come. Mosiah 16:9's “light and the life of the world” has a particularly close internal parallel in [[3 Nephi 9]].

These comparisons securely establish relationships in the English wording. They do not establish antiquity or modern composition by themselves. Because Paul, John, Hebrews, and Colossians postdate Abinadi's reported setting, a direct attribution of their wording to Abinadi would be chronologically problematic; possible explanations include shared earlier traditions, later abridgment, translation choices, or modern composition.

Verse 12's repeated “they would not” is an emphatic rhetorical pattern, not evidence of a formal chiasm.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Textual Intertextuality

Deliberate scriptural intertextuality in the English wording is highly likely, although the direction and mechanism of dependence remain unresolved.

> [!evidence] Intertextual Phrase Match *(tentative)*
> The phrase 'see eye to eye' in Mosiah 16:1 matches a rare KJV idiom concentrated in Isaiah 52:8 ('for they shall see eye to eye, when the LORD shall bring again Zion'), a chapter Abinadi has just finished quoting at length in Mosiah 12-14. This raises the possibility of a deliberate echo of Isaiah's watchmen-of-Zion imagery, but the evidence is thin.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.7 · Verbal parallel accepted; intentionality disputed

> [!evidence] Christological Title Analysis *(tentative)*
> Verse 15 calls the redeeming Christ 'the very Eternal Father,' part of a recurring pattern in Mosiah-Alma discourse (echoed in Alma 11, per the verified text-overlap data) where Christ is called 'Father.' Taken in isolation this title is theologically striking and requires careful handling.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · Title established; doctrinal explanation disputed

> [!evidence] Cross-Canon Phrase Match *(tentative)*
> The unusual three-word collocation 'carnal, sensual, and devilish' in Mosiah 16:3 also occurs describing the identical post-Fall condition of humanity in Moses 5:13, a Restoration-scripture text outside the Book of Mormon. This is a specific enough phrase to be notable, but its significance is ambiguous.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does Mosiah 16:1 answer the priests' quotation of Isaiah 52 in [[Mosiah 12]]?

- Why does Abinadi say the unrepentant are “as though there was no redemption made”? How does that qualification shape the relationship among [[Agency]], [[Grace]], and judgment?

- What does the movement from universal resurrection to divergent judgment suggest about the distinction between [[Resurrection]] and [[Salvation]]?

- Does the binary language of verses 10-11 offer a complete map of final conditions, or is it sermonic compression?

- How should “the very Eternal Father” be read alongside [[Mosiah 15]] without forcing the phrase to settle every later question about [[The Godhead]]?

- Why does the sermon end with instructions about teaching the [[Law of Moses]], and how does that instruction contribute to Abinadi's indictment of the priests?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
