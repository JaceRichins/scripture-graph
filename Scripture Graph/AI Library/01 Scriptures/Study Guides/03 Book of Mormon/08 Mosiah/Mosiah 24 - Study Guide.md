# Mosiah 24 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mosiah 24]] · My notes: [[Mosiah 24 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Mosiah 24 continues directly from [[Mosiah 23]]: Amulon, a former priest of king Noah, gains favor with the Lamanite king Laman and is appointed to teach the language of Nephi throughout Lamanite territory — but explicitly without religious instruction: neither the knowledge of God, the law of Moses, nor Abinadi's words (vv. 1-7). The chapter then turns personal: Amulon recognizes Alma as the priest who once believed Abinadi and fled, and persecutes his community, outlawing audible prayer on pain of death (vv. 8-11). Alma's people respond with silent, heartfelt prayer; the Lord first strengthens them to bear their burdens and later promises deliverance, which comes as they depart under cover of a divinely caused sleep upon their captors, evade pursuit, and arrive after twelve days in Zarahemla, where king Mosiah receives them with joy (vv. 12-25). The official chapter heading assigns this account a broad range of about 145–120 B.C., a Nephite-internal reckoning rather than an externally corroborated date. The chapter engages [[Government and Laws]], [[Exodus and Deliverance]], [[Freedom and Liberty]], [[Prayer]], [[Covenants]], and [[Adversity]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1–7:** Amulonite teachers spread the language of Nephi, literacy, and commerce while withholding religious teaching.

2. **Verses 8–12:** Amulon imposes labor and suppresses audible prayer; the people turn to silent petition.

3. **Verses 13–17:** The Lord remembers the covenant, lightens burdens, and promises release.

4. **Verses 18–25:** The people prepare, escape, give thanks, evade pursuit, and arrive in Zarahemla.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Within the narrative, effective [[Prayer]] does not depend upon audible speech: God knows the people's inward petitions. Divine help also appears in more than one form. The Lord first grants strength to endure continuing oppression and later provides escape. That sequence makes the chapter relevant to [[Endurance]] without reducing deliverance to passive acceptance. The episode culminates in [[Gratitude]], with the whole speaking community participating in praise.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The language of voice and silence gives the episode cohesion. The people first "cry mightily"; Amulon suppresses their cries; they pour out their hearts without raising their voices; the voice of the Lord answers twice; and, after escape, their voices rise again in praise. This progression marks a movement from coerced silence to communal worship.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter juxtaposes two kinds of power: Amulon's royal appointment, teachers, taskmasters, guards, and a death penalty against the Lord's direct communication, strengthening of the burdened, disabling of pursuers, and guidance through the wilderness. A second contrast places practical learning beside religious and moral absence — literacy and trade increase, but the narrator explicitly says the teachers withheld knowledge of God, the [[Law of Moses]], and Abinadi's words (vv. 4-6). The passage should therefore be read as a critique of learning severed from covenantal morality, not of literacy or commerce themselves. The escape narrative also parallels [[Mosiah 22]]: both describe a Nephite group's flight from Lamanite bondage via incapacitated captors, a night departure, wilderness travel, and arrival before king Mosiah in Zarahemla — a resemblance worth studying, though whether it reflects deliberate editorial shaping or two independently remembered escapes cannot be settled from the text alone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Which covenant does the Lord remember in verse 13? [[Mosiah 18]]'s baptismal covenant (including the promise to bear one another's burdens) is the nearest narrative candidate, though the text does not name it explicitly, and the two burden-images are not identical.
- What does it mean that the people 'could not feel' their burdens (v. 14)? Does this describe a physical lightening of the load, or only a strengthened capacity to endure it?
- Why are the Lamanites given language and literacy but explicitly withheld from religious teaching (vv. 4-6) — what does this suggest about Amulon's motives or the Lamanite king's priorities?
- Why does the Lord initially ease the burden rather than remove it, and what does this suggest about the purpose of affliction?
- How does the twelve-day wilderness journey and the naming of the valley of Alma function to establish Alma's authority as a leader, distinct from his earlier role as a priest?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Mosiah 18]] for the covenant and burden-bearing language, [[Mosiah 23]] for the occupation and Amulon's rise, and [[Mosiah 22]] for the parallel escape of Limhi's people — especially useful since both communities reach Zarahemla, though the narratives emphasize different combinations of planning, providence, and revelation. For the broader wilderness-journey idiom (pitching tents, traveling by stages), compare 1 Nephi 16.
