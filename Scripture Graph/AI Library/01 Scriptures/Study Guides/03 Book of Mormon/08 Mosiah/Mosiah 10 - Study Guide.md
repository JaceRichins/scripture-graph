# Mosiah 10 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> King Laman dies—His people are wild and ferocious and believe in false traditions—Zeniff and his people prevail against them. About 187–160 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Peace, preparedness, and prosperity (vv. 1–5):** weapons, guards, agriculture, and textile production accompany twenty-two years of peace.
2. **Renewed invasion (vv. 6–11):** King Laman dies; his son mobilizes for war; Zeniff uses spies and assembles those able to fight.
3. **Ancestral grievance (vv. 12–18):** Zeniff recounts the Lamanite explanation of the division originating with Nephi.
4. **Battle and return (vv. 19–21):** Zeniff rallies his people, they drive out the invaders, and ordinary labor resumes.
5. **Succession (v. 22):** Zeniff transfers the kingdom to a son and ends his account, leading into [[Mosiah 11]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter associates deliverance with trust in the Lord while still depicting extensive human preparation: weapons are made, guards and spies are deployed, families are protected, and soldiers are organized (vv. 1–2, 7, 9–10, 19). Thus [[Faith]] is not presented here as a substitute for prudence or labor. Conversely, the victory report by itself cannot demonstrate that every strategic choice or every element of Zeniff's account received divine endorsement.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase 'for the space of twenty and two years' is repeated verbatim in v.3 and v.5, underscoring the length and stability of the peace before it breaks in v.6. The word 'again' recurs as a structural marker in two distinct ways: in vv.1, 20-21 it marks the cycle of resettlement, victory, and resumed labor, while in vv.13, 15-16 'And again' introduces each new item in the list of Lamanite grievances — two different rhetorical uses of the same word, not one unified pattern. The martial vocabulary of v.8 (bows, arrows, swords, cimeters, stones, slings) is notably specific; 'cimeter' in particular has drawn attention in debates over Book of Mormon weaponry and translation vocabulary, though the text itself specifies neither the material nor the curvature of the weapon, so claims about its historical identity go beyond what v.8 states.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 12–18 form an explanatory digression inside the battle narrative. They are especially important because they present an opposing community's collective memory through Zeniff's voice. The passage preserves the content of a counter-story—Nephi as usurper and robber—while simultaneously framing that story as ignorance, hardened hearts, hatred, and deception. Comparison with [[2 Nephi 5]] reveals continuity in the reported events but opposition in their moral interpretation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Is the detailed Lamanite grievance tradition in vv.11-17 a genuine attempt to represent the Lamanite perspective, or a rhetorical device that explains (and perhaps justifies) Nephite hostility toward them? The chapter offers no independent Lamanite voice against which to test Zeniff's account.
- Which parts of vv.12-17 report the tradition Zeniff attributes to the Lamanites, and which parts are his own evaluation of it (e.g., calling them 'wild, and ferocious, and a blood-thirsty people')?
- Why does Zeniff recount this inherited grievance tradition immediately before saying he 'stimulate[d]' his people to battle (v.19) — is the retelling itself part of how he rallies them?
- What does the specific weapons and dress catalog in v.8 — especially the disputed term 'cimeter' — suggest about the record's composition and translation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
