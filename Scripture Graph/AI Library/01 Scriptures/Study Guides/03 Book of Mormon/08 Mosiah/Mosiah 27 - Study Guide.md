# Mosiah 27 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mosiah 27]] · My notes: [[Mosiah 27 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Mosiah forbids persecution and enjoins equality—Alma the younger and the four sons of Mosiah seek to destroy the Church—An angel appears and commands them to cease their evil course—Alma is struck dumb—All mankind must be born again to gain salvation—Alma and the sons of Mosiah declare glad tidings. About 100–92 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Law, equality, and renewed peace (vv. 1–7):** Civil and ecclesiastical leaders respond to persecution; equality is tied to humility and self-support.
2. **Opposition and angelic confrontation (vv. 8–18):** Alma and the sons of Mosiah secretly seek to destroy the church until an angel calls them to remember earlier deliverance.
3. **Communal intercession and Alma's rebirth (vv. 19–31):** Fasting and prayer surround Alma's recovery and testimony about universal rebirth, redemption, and judgment.
4. **Repair and proclamation (vv. 32–37):** Former persecutors accept persecution themselves, repair injuries, explain scripture, and publish peace.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Alma's account joins [[Conversion]], [[Repentance]], and [[Grace]]. Rebirth is a change from a fallen condition into righteousness through divine redemption, producing "new creatures" who become God's sons and daughters (vv. 24–26). Alma does not treat repentance as painless: he describes tribulation, bitterness, bondage, darkness, and torment before mercy and light (vv. 27–29).

The chapter also links [[Prayer]] with communal solidarity. The father's faithful prayer precedes the angel's appearance, while priests fast and pray during Alma's helplessness (vv. 14, 22–23). The story attributes deliverance to God without reducing the surrounding community to passive spectators.

Repentance has outward consequences. Alma and his companions confess, teach what they had rejected, and strive to repair their injuries (vv. 32–35). Their conduct supplies a concrete model of restitution, although the text does not specify how every injury was repaired.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The principal conversion speech is organized through contrasting terms: **carnal/fallen → righteous**, **bondage → redeemed**, **darkest abyss → marvelous light**, and **torment → pain no more** (vv. 25, 29). This vocabulary makes salvation both relational and transformational.

The command to "remember" the captivities at Helam and in the land of Nephi (v. 16) connects Alma's inward bondage with the communal deliverances narrated earlier in [[Mosiah 24]] and related chapters. That is a strong literary and theological connection; it is not independent proof that the narrated events occurred historically.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's most pronounced narrative device is reversal. Alma moves from persuasive persecutor to incapacitated witness, then to persecuted preacher. His companions likewise move from secretly destroying the church to publicly repairing it (vv. 8–10, 19, 32–35).

The conclusion echoes [[Isaiah 52]]: "publish peace," "good tidings," and "the Lord reigneth" appear together in Mosiah 27:37 and Isaiah 52:7. The echo casts their preaching as an announcement of divine kingship. Its literary presence is strong, but it cannot determine whether the wording reflects an ancient source, later editorial use, or the English translation's biblical register.

[[Alma 36]] retells the conversion from Alma's own perspective and gives much more attention to his internal anguish and deliverance. Reading the two accounts together shows how a later autobiographical discourse expands the shorter communal narrative.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Scriptural Intertext

> [!evidence] Literary Type-Scene Parallel *(tentative)*
> The angelic confrontation of Alma the younger while he actively persecutes the church—resulting in his physical incapacitation and subsequent transformation into a missionary—structurally resembles Saul's conversion on the road to Damascus (Acts 9): a persecutor is stopped by a divine manifestation, temporarily disabled, and becomes an advocate for the faith he opposed.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.7 · Recognizable but nondiagnostic literary resemblance

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the text prescribe both a royal proclamation and a separate command within the churches (vv. 2–3)?
- How do humility, neighbor-regard, and labor define the chapter's stated ideal of equality (vv. 3–5)?
- Why is the memory of collective deliverance from captivity chosen as the angel's message to Alma (v. 16)?
- What does the attempt to "repair all the injuries" add to the chapter's understanding of [[Repentance]] (v. 35)?
- How should the dramatic intervention of the angel be understood alongside the residual language of personal will in verse 16?
