# Jacob 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jacob 4]] · My notes: [[Jacob 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Jacob 4 moves from the physical making of scripture to the theological purpose of scripture. Jacob preserves a small selection of his ministry so later generations may know that their ancestors hoped in Christ long before his coming. He then connects the [[Law of Moses]], the [[Sacrifice of Isaac (Akedah)]], prophecy, creation, [[Grace]], the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], and [[Resurrection]] in a single Christ-centered argument. The chapter closes with the problem that leads into [[Jacob 5]]: how can a covenant people who rejected the sure foundation ever build on it again?
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful progression is:

1. **Jacob 4:1–3 — Making and transmitting the record.** Durable plates require difficult, selective engraving for future readers.
2. **Jacob 4:4–7 — Ancient hope in Christ.** Prophets, the law, Isaac's offering, revelation, and grace function as witnesses.
3. **Jacob 4:8–10 — Divine word and human limits.** Creation demonstrates God's power; human beings should receive rather than dictate divine counsel.
4. **Jacob 4:11–13 — Salvific center.** Reconciliation, atonement, resurrection, hope, and intelligible prophecy are brought together.
5. **Jacob 4:14–18 — Rejection and promised restoration.** Looking beyond the mark leads to stumbling, the foundation stone is rejected, and Jacob promises to explain how restoration remains possible.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Jacob does not treat the [[Law of Moses]] and faith in Christ as competing systems. Within his account, the law is sanctified because it points the soul toward Christ. Reconciliation comes through the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], while resurrection comes through Christ's resurrection power. Even miraculous prophetic power remains dependent on [[Grace]] and the [[Condescension of God]], because God reveals the servants' weakness as well as granting their power.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The contrast between “counsel the Lord” and “take counsel from his hand” is an economical statement of epistemic humility. Likewise, “looking beyond the mark” is defined by its immediate context: despising plain words while desiring what cannot be understood. Yet Jacob also values mysteries and promises to unfold one. The chapter therefore contrasts receptive pursuit of revealed depth with a preference for obscurity that displaces plain revelation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Jacob's stone passage draws together several recognizable scriptural motifs: the sure foundation of [[Isaiah 28]], the rejected stone that becomes the head of the corner in [[Psalm 118]], and the later application of that saying in [[Matthew 21]]. This is strong evidence of literary participation in a biblical stone tradition, but the English text alone cannot determine whether the relationship reflects ancient allusion, translation into familiar biblical language, modern composition, or later Christian synthesis.

The repeated word “stumble” is also noteworthy. Jacob describes the Jews' stumbling and later worries that his own anxiety might cause him to stumble. Because the first is asserted and the second hypothetical, the repetition may create a self-qualifying echo, but deliberate literary design is uncertain.

Jacob's closing question and promise to “unfold this mystery” form a clear transition into [[Jacob 5]], whose olive-tree allegory addresses Israel's loss, preservation, and restoration. The chapters are continuous in argument even though Jacob 5 is also a distinct embedded quotation attributed to Zenos.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Biblical Intertextuality
> The convergence of rejected-stone, sure-foundation, and cornerstone language is strong evidence of scriptural intertextuality. It supports intentional participation in a recognizable biblical tradition, while leaving the direction and historical mechanism of dependence unresolved.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · strong intertext; mechanism disputed · [[Biblical Intertextuality in Jacob 4|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Philological/Interpretive Dispute *(tentative)*
> The exact referent of 'looking beyond the mark' (Jacob 4:14) is disputed among commentators: some read it as a straightforward archery metaphor (overshooting or missing a target through overreach), while a minority of LDS scholars have proposed it alludes to a weaver's beam/marker based on a hypothesized underlying Hebrew idiom. Neither reading is textually certain within the Book of Mormon itself.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.4 · underdetermination accepted; philology unverified

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Thematic Parallel *(tentative)*
> Jacob 4:11's phrase 'first-fruits of Christ' applied to believers being presented to God echoes New Testament firstfruits language, but reverses its most familiar Pauline application (where Christ himself is called 'the firstfruits,' 1 Corinthians 15:20), making this a thematic parallel rather than a demonstrable quotation.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.5 · speculative and incomplete
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What is the “mark” in Jacob's argument: Christ specifically, revealed plainness more generally, or both? How should readers distinguish diligent searching into divine mysteries from the pursuit of incomprehensibility criticized in verse 14? In what sense are faithful people presented as the “first-fruits of Christ”? Does verse 6 summarize exercised prophetic powers, potential authority, or conventional miracle imagery? How should Jacob's sweeping portrayal of “the Jews” be read as internal polemic without turning it into a general judgment about Jewish people, ancient or modern?
