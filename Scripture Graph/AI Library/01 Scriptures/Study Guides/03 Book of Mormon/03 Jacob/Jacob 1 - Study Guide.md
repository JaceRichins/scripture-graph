# Jacob 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jacob 1]] · My notes: [[Jacob 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jacob and Joseph seek to persuade men to believe in Christ and keep His commandments—Nephi dies—Wickedness prevails among the Nephites. About 544–421 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The record commission (vv. 1-4):** Jacob receives the plates, a selective content policy, and a duty to preserve the record through his descendants.

2. **The ministry's purpose (vv. 5-8):** Revelation about Christ and anxiety for the people lead Jacob and his associates to labor to bring people to Christ and God's rest.

3. **Succession and memory (vv. 9-12):** Nephi appoints a successor, the people preserve his name through numbered designations for later rulers, and Nephi dies.

4. **Narrative nomenclature (vv. 13-14):** Jacob lists several named groups but announces that he will ordinarily classify them by friendship or hostility toward Nephi's people.

5. **Crisis and commission (vv. 15-19):** Marital misconduct, wealth-seeking, and pride prompt a temple warning grounded in Jacob's claimed divine errand and his accountability as a teacher.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays sacred record keeping as stewardship: Jacob is instructed to preserve what is most precious, especially sacred preaching, revelation, and prophecy. In this sense, [[Records and Sacred Writings]] serves the work of bringing people to Christ.

Knowledge of Christ and concern for the people's future lead Jacob and his associates to diligent persuasion rather than prediction alone (Jacob 1:5-8). They invite people to partake of God's goodness and enter his rest.

Jacob 1:19 makes ministerial accountability conditional on fulfilling the duty to teach diligently. Jacob and Joseph say they would bear responsibility if they neglected that duty; the verse does not provide a complete theory of responsibility for every subsequent choice made by their hearers.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The expression “engraven the heads of them” (Jacob 1:4) likely means recording principal points or summaries, fitting the instruction to include only a few precious matters. The phrase does not reveal the exact extent of Jacob's later abridgment.

“Enter into his rest” and the surrounding language in Jacob 1:7 closely track the KJV wording of [[Psalms 95]] as quoted in [[Hebrews 3]]. Because Hebrews is reusing the Psalm, these are one underlying biblical complex rather than two independent parallels. The English wording alone cannot determine whether the relationship reflects an ancient tradition, translation into familiar biblical idiom, or modern composition.

In Jacob 1:13-14, Nephite and Lamanite operate as Jacob's broad narrative classifications based on friendship or hostility. That convention should not automatically be treated as a complete genealogical definition or projected unchanged onto every later narrator.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Jacob 1 repeatedly joins commission with fulfillment. Nephi commands Jacob concerning the plates (Jacob 1:1-4), and Jacob takes upon himself the duty to fulfill that commandment (Jacob 1:8). Later, Jacob reports an errand from the Lord, recalls his consecration by Nephi, and describes the accountability attached to teaching (Jacob 1:17-19). Record keeping and public preaching are thus presented as entrusted stewardships.

Two biblical comparisons merit different levels of confidence. Jacob 1:7 has a strong English-language correspondence with the provocation–wilderness–wrath–rest sequence in [[Psalms 95]] and [[Hebrews 3]], although the mechanism behind that wording remains disputed. Jacob 1:15-16 thematically resembles [[Deuteronomy 17]], where multiplying wives and accumulating silver and gold appear together. The latter connection is suggestive rather than demonstrative and may also reflect broader traditions about David, Solomon, and royal excess.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> Jacob 1:7's phrase 'as in the provocation in the days of temptation while the children of Israel were in the wilderness' closely echoes the King James wording of Psalm 95:8 and its quotation in Hebrews 3:8 ('as in the provocation, in the day of temptation in the wilderness'), raising a question about the relationship between Book of Mormon phrasing and specific KJV biblical texts.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · strong English-language parallel; mechanism disputed · [[Verbal/Textual Parallel in Jacob 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Coherence
> Jacob's distinction between sacred small-plate material and fuller history coheres with earlier explanations of the two records and with the later account of preserving the small plates. This is substantial evidence of internal literary continuity across the Book of Mormon's record narrative.
> — confidence 0.9 · internal coherence accepted; historical significance limited · [[Internal Narrative Coherence in Jacob 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Biblical Intertext
> Jacob 1:7 closely invokes the scriptural complex found in Psalms 95 and Hebrews 3: provocation, temptation in the wilderness, divine wrath, and failure to enter God's rest. The clustered vocabulary and sequence make a literary relationship highly probable, while leaving its precise mechanism unresolved.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · strong verbal relationship; origin disputed · [[Biblical Intertext in Jacob 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Legal And Thematic Intertext *(tentative)*
> Jacob places the multiplication of wives and concubines beside the accumulation of gold and silver under a king. These are also adjacent dangers in the law for Israelite kings in Deuteronomy 17. The pairing is meaningful evidence of engagement with biblical royal-law themes, though Jacob does not explicitly cite that law.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.8 · meaningful thematic parallel; dependence disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Jacob state his selection rules before describing what he knows about Christ and the people's future?

- Does “second Nephi, third Nephi” describe a formal royal title, popular commemorative usage, or Jacob's narrative shorthand?

- How is the “second king” of Jacob 1:15 counted, and can the passage securely identify him with “second Nephi”? The wording permits more than one reading.

- How should Jacob's explicit naming convention affect interpretation of Nephite and Lamanite elsewhere without assuming that every narrator uses the terms identically?

- Why are plural wives, wealth, and pride introduced together? Compare Jacob 1:15-16 with [[Deuteronomy 17]] and [[Jacob 2]].

- What was the specific errand Jacob obtained from the Lord before teaching in the temple (Jacob 1:17)? The chapter does not say.

- What exactly does Jacob 1:19 make teachers responsible for, and what does it leave unstated about their hearers' responses?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Jacob's editorial mandate with [[1 Nephi 6]], [[1 Nephi 9]], and [[Words of Mormon 1]] to trace the stated purpose and preservation of the small plates. Read Jacob 1:7 alongside [[Psalms 95]] and [[Hebrews 3]], remembering that Hebrews quotes the Psalm, and compare how each text uses wilderness rebellion and divine rest. Compare Jacob 1:15-16 cautiously with [[Deuteronomy 17]], then follow the fuller treatment of wealth and marriage in [[Jacob 2]]. For ministerial accountability, compare Jacob 1:19 with [[Ezekiel 3]], [[Ezekiel 33]], [[Mosiah 2]], and [[Acts 20]].
