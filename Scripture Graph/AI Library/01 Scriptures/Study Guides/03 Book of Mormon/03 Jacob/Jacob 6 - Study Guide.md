## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Lord will recover Israel in the last days—The world will be burned with fire—Men must follow Christ to avoid the lake of fire and brimstone. About 544–421 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Prophetic interpretation (vv. 1–3):** Zenos's allegory will be fulfilled; the final recovery and vineyard labor precede the end.
2. **Mercy and invitation (vv. 4–6):** God remembers Israel and extends his mercy; Jacob calls for wholehearted repentance today.
3. **Warning through vineyard imagery (vv. 7–10):** Evil fruit, rejection of revelation, resurrection, judgment, and fire form an escalating warning.
4. **Concluding appeal (vv. 11–13):** Enter the strait gate, continue toward [[Eternal Life]], act wisely, and prepare to meet God.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
God's mercy precedes the chapter's judgment warning: he remembers Israel, stretches forth his hands, and invites the hearers to cleave to him. Jacob nevertheless calls for a response—do not harden the heart, repent with full purpose, and continue in the narrow way. This supports a relationship between offered mercy and accountable response without supplying a complete theory of grace or culpability.

Verse 9 joins the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Resurrection]], and [[Final Judgment]] by saying that the power of redemption and resurrection in Christ brings people before God's bar. Verses 9–10 then connect shame, guilt, punishment, and justice. Verse 11 presents [[Eternal Life]] as the destination of entering the strait gate and continuing in the narrow way, strongly emphasizing [[Endurance]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Jacob 6 uses several recognizable scriptural formulations. The Lord setting his hand again a second time to recover his people closely parallels [[Isaiah 11]]. The appeal to hear God's voice today and not harden the heart follows the formula in [[Psalms 95]], also repeated in [[Hebrews 3]] and [[Hebrews 4]]. The strait gate and narrow way parallel [[Matthew 7]] and [[3 Nephi 14]].

The outstretched-hands language is a composite resemblance: Isaiah 65:2 describes hands spread out all day to a rebellious people, while [[Romans 10]] uses 'stretched out' and 'gainsaying people.' Jacob combines comparable elements; 'stiffnecked' also belongs to broader biblical diction, including [[Acts 7]].

These parallels establish meaningful relationships in the English wording but do not determine provenance. Possible explanations include inherited scriptural tradition, translation into King James-style English, editorial shaping, deliberate modern biblical imitation, common religious formulas, or different explanations for different phrases.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Jacob turns a communal allegory into personal exhortation. The vineyard first concerns the history and recovery of Israel, but verse 7 addresses the hearers as people nourished by the good word who may still produce evil fruit. Its language of nourishment, fruit, cutting, and fire tightly connects the chapter to [[Jacob 5]]. This establishes deliberate literary continuity without establishing when or by whom the material was composed.

The warning intensifies through successive questions: Why will the hearers die? Will nourishment yield evil fruit? Will they reject the words, the prophets, Christ, God's power, the Holy Ghost, and redemption? Verse 12—'O be wise; what can I say more?'—then compresses the argument into an urgent final appeal. Verse 13 closes with a solemn anticipated meeting before God's bar; it creates accountability without assigning Jacob an unstated formal role as witness.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean in practice to cleave to God in response to his cleaving to us?
- Why does Jacob move from the collective history of Israel to the fruit produced by individual hearers?
- How does the word “today” change the force of a prophecy about the last recovery of Israel?
- Why can Jacob call God's bar pleasing after describing shame and dread before that same bar?

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
