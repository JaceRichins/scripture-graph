# Jacob 7 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jacob 7]] · My notes: [[Jacob 7 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Sherem denies Christ, contends with Jacob, demands a sign, and is smitten of God—All of the prophets have spoken of Christ and His Atonement—The Nephites lived out their days as wanderers, born in tribulation, and hated by the Lamanites. About 544–421 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Sherem introduced (vv. 1–5):** persuasive speech, public success, and a stated effort to overthrow the doctrine of Christ.
2. **Competing claims to knowledge (vv. 6–12):** Sherem appeals to the law and the unknowability of the future; Jacob appeals to scripture, prophets, experience, and the Holy Ghost.
3. **Sign and reversal (vv. 13–23):** Sherem asks for a sign, falls, publicly recants, and dies; the people turn from his words to searching scripture.
4. **Unresolved communal sorrow (vv. 24–27):** missionary efforts toward the Lamanites fail, warfare continues, and Jacob closes with an exile lament and transfers the plates to Enos.

The movement from flattery to plain confession, from hearts led away to restored peace, and from Sherem's power of speech to God's power gives the account a pronounced reversal pattern.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Jacob presents the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] as universally necessary: without it, all mankind would be lost (v. 12). He cites scripture, prophetic witness, angelic ministry, the Lord's voice, personal manifestation, and the Holy Ghost as converging grounds for [[Testimony]]. His assertion that the prophets testify of Christ answers Sherem's claim that worship of a future Christ perverts the [[Law of Moses]]; compare the explicit combination of Mosaic observance and hope in Christ in [[2 Nephi 25]].

When Sherem demands a sign, Jacob objects to tempting God, conditionally identifies a possible judgment, and submits the outcome to divine will (v. 14). Sherem's later fear that he has committed the unpardonable sin remains his own assessment. The narrator supplies no revealed verdict about his eternal state.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The verb *know* is contested throughout the exchange. Sherem claims to know what Jacob teaches (v. 6), says future events cannot be known (v. 7), and then says he knows Christ never will exist (v. 9). Jacob, by contrast, grounds his knowledge in multiple forms of witness (vv. 5, 10–12). The argument therefore concerns not only conclusions but also what counts as adequate grounds for knowledge.

The word *power* likewise changes allegiance across the narrative: Sherem's rhetorical power is associated with the devil (v. 4), while Jacob's answer and the ensuing events are associated with the Spirit, the Holy Ghost, the Lord, and God (vv. 8, 12–15, 21).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Sherem's final speech reverses important features of his introduction. The man initially associated with flattery and rhetorical power ultimately speaks "plainly" and confesses Christ, the Holy Ghost, and angelic ministry—categories invoked across Jacob's preceding witness. His public recantation is followed by a communal movement from hearkening to Sherem toward searching the scriptures.

The close prevents the episode from functioning as an uncomplicated triumph narrative. Jacob turns immediately from restored peace to failed reconciliation, continuing war, aging, exile, and mourning; his description of life passing like a dream gives the farewell an elegiac tone.

The denial-and-sign account in [[Alma 30]] is a useful narrative parallel, but it is not a verified verse-level textual overlap. Korihor is struck dumb and later dies by trampling, whereas Sherem's collapse and death form a more continuous episode. The resemblance supports recognition of a recurring narrative pattern, not a conclusion about historical corroboration, dependence, authorship, or the pattern's origin.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- If Sherem genuinely believed the scriptures, what interpretive assumptions allowed him to deny their witness of Christ?
- Why does the account contrast Sherem's earlier flattery with the plainness of his final confession?
- Does the public sign produce conviction, fear, or both among the multitude?
- Why does Jacob place the unsuccessful effort to reclaim the Lamanites immediately after the restoration of internal peace?
- How does Jacob's final lament qualify the apparent victory over Sherem?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Sherem's claim that Christ-centered worship perverts the law (Jacob 7:7) with the combination of Mosaic observance and hope in Christ in [[2 Nephi 25]]. Compare the denial, sign request, affliction, confession, and death sequences in [[Alma 30]], noting both their similarities and their different causal details. Read [[Enos 1]] after Jacob's transfer of the plates to Enos in Jacob 7:27. Also trace the possible antecedents of "this thing" in Jacob 7:22 before deciding what Jacob says he had requested in prayer.
