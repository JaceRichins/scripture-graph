# Jacob 2 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jacob denounces the love of riches, pride, and unchastity—Men may seek riches to help their fellowmen—The Lord commands that no man among the Nephites may have more than one wife—The Lord delights in the chastity of women. About 544–421 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Commission and reluctant boldness (vv. 1–11):** Jacob explains his office, anxiety, temple setting, and concern for wounded listeners.

2. **Riches, [[Pride]], and equality (vv. 12–21):** unequal prosperity produces costly display, superiority, and persecution; Jacob redirects wealth toward relief and grounds equality in creation.

3. **Transition to a “grosser crime” (vv. 22–24):** offenders use David and Solomon as scriptural precedents.

4. **Marriage command and its rationale (vv. 25–34):** the reported oracle gives a one-wife rule, prohibits concubines, includes the compressed qualification in verse 30, and says God hears women's cries.

5. **Measured consequences (v. 35):** Jacob identifies broken hearts, lost confidence, corrupt examples, and deep wounds.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The riches discourse joins inward priority to outward practice. Jacob places seeking the [[Kingdom of God]] before seeking riches and permits seeking them only with hope in Christ and an intent to do good: clothing the naked, feeding the hungry, liberating captives, and relieving the sick and afflicted (Jacob 2:18-19). Verse 17 urges generous sharing so others may be "rich like unto you," while verse 21 declares each person equally precious before God. These teachings have a strong egalitarian and charitable force, but the chapter does not prescribe a complete economic institution.

The marriage discourse makes the stated rule clear: one wife and no concubines (Jacob 2:27). Verse 30 is commonly read as describing a possible departure only when God commands it for the purpose of raising up seed. That reading is plausible but disputed; the verse may instead function as a hypothetical reinforcement of the present prohibition. It does not authorize private exceptions based on desire or appeal to prestigious ancestors. The discourse also gives substantial attention to [[Family]] harm, including women's sorrow, children's lost confidence, and the consequences of bad examples, while continuing to ground the indictment in divine command.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Jacob contrasts two kinds of divine “word.” The audience hoped for the “pleasing word of God” that heals a wounded soul (v. 8), but the “strictness of the word of God” comes down against offenders and leaves already injured hearts pierced (vv. 9, 35). The same word can console the receptive and expose conduct that has wounded others.

The bodily imagery is sustained: Jacob is weighed down, shrinks with shame, speaks of stiff necks and high heads, invokes God's piercing glance, and closes with broken and pierced hearts. These images make pride visible as posture and family injury visible as wounds.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The recurrence of wound imagery near both the beginning and end gives the sermon a coherent emotional frame. Verses 7–10 introduce tender listeners, wounded souls, daggers, and broken hearts; verse 35 returns to broken hearts, sobbing, piercing, and deep wounds. This is good evidence for deliberate thematic unity, though repetition alone cannot prove a formal inclusio or anything about the text's date of composition.

A second organizing movement runs from vertical distinction to common origin: costly apparel and “high heads” mark social elevation in verse 13, whereas verse 21 brings “all flesh” back to the dust and to a shared divine purpose.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How does Jacob distinguish receiving riches through providence from turning riches into a claim of superiority? Does “rich like unto you” require economic equality, generous sufficiency, or an unattained ideal? Why does Jacob explain the emotional cost of preaching before he identifies the crimes? What controls the exception language in verse 30: communal revelation, prophetic command, reproductive purpose, or all three? How should [[Jacob 2]] be read alongside biblical texts that regulate plural marriage or portray aspects of David's marital history differently?

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the kingdom-first sequence with [[Matthew 6]], the restriction on royal wives with [[Deuteronomy 17]], and Solomon's history with [[1 Kings 11]]. Also consider [[2 Samuel 12]] and [[Deuteronomy 21]], which complicate claims that every biblical passage presents plural marriage in the same way as Jacob 2. Read [[Jacob 1]] for Jacob's explanation of his office and accountability. For comparable teachings about imparting substance and equality, examine [[Mosiah 4]]. For another Book of Mormon treatment of sexual wrongdoing and damage to others' trust, compare [[Alma 39]].
