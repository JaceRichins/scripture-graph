# Mormon 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mormon 6]] · My notes: [[Mormon 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Nephites gather to the land of Cumorah for the final battles—Mormon hides the sacred records in the hill Cumorah—The Lamanites are victorious, and the Nephite nation is destroyed—Hundreds of thousands are slain with the sword. About A.D. 385.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Gathering and preservation (vv. 1–6):** The final battlefield is arranged, the people gather, and Mormon secures the records.
2. **Battle and survivors (vv. 7–15):** Fear, weapons, casualties, named military groups, and limited exceptions to the destruction are reported.
3. **Lament and judgment (vv. 16–22):** Historical narration becomes direct address, moving from grief and failed [[Repentance]] to [[Resurrection]], judgment, justice, and mercy.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter places divine invitation before judgment. Jesus is pictured with open arms ready to receive the people (v. 17), but their rejection does not cancel moral accountability (v. 21). Mormon nevertheless leaves their precise state to the Father's knowledge (v. 22). This combination resists both despair that mercy was unavailable and presumption that judgment is unnecessary.

Verse 21 teaches bodily [[Resurrection]] through the paired transformations of mortal to immortal and corruptible to incorruptible. Its wording closely parallels [[1 Corinthians 15]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter narrows its lens in stages: an entire people gathers, military bodies fall, twenty-four survivors remain, and finally one father cries over named human relationships. Repetition of “O ye fair ones” and the sequence of questions in verses 17–20 create a formal lament. The family catalog in verse 19 interrupts the military arithmetic with sons, daughters, parents, husbands, and wives, making the cost of war personal.

The detailed roll of “ten thousand” groups has the character of a casualty register, but the passage does not explain whether the expression is an exact count, a rounded figure, or a military-unit designation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Evidence
> The account adopts a strongly situated survivor perspective: Mormon reports being wounded and passed over, names Moroni among the twenty-four survivors, and describes viewing the dead from Cumorah on the following day. This is strong evidence for deliberate eyewitness-style narration but only weak evidence by itself for the account's external historicity.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · narrative posture secure; historicity significance weak · [[Internal Narrative Evidence in Mormon 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Numerical And Military Terminology
> The repeated expression 'ten thousand' supports the conclusion that the text portrays casualties on an enormous scale. It is less certain whether every occurrence is intended as an exact headcount, a rounded number, or the designation of a military body.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.9 · large scale secure; exact interpretation disputed · [[Numerical And Military Terminology in Mormon 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Literary/Narrative Consistency *(tentative)*
> Mormon's practice of exchanging a formal epistle with an enemy king before battle (Mormon 6:2-3) is internally consistent with other Nephite-Lamanite wartime correspondence recorded elsewhere in the record (e.g., the epistles in Alma 54 and Alma 56), which supports the record's internal narrative consistency but does not by itself establish that such diplomatic exchanges reflect an actual ancient historical practice.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.7 · broad literary consistency plausible; examples conflated
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Mormon emphasize that the Lamanite king granted his request to gather at Cumorah?
- How do Mormon's roles as commander, record keeper, survivor, and father shape the account?
- What changes when verses 16–22 are read as lament rather than only as doctrinal exposition?
- Why does Mormon confidently affirm resurrection and judgment while leaving the dead person's state to the Father?
- What conclusions about Cumorah's geography are warranted by “many waters, rivers, and fountains,” and which conclusions exceed the text?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
