# Mormon 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mormon 5]] · My notes: [[Mormon 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Mormon 5 is a hinge chapter. The military narrative that has followed Mormon since his youth reaches its final, hopeless phase (vv. 1-7): he resumes command of armies he does not expect to save, and the war ends in the Nephites' rout in the 380th year. From verse 8 on, the narrative voice pulls back into an extended editorial and prophetic address aimed at readers centuries removed from the battle — the remnant of Mormon's own people, the unbelieving Jews, and the Gentiles who will one day possess this record. Mormon leads his people 'without hope' (v. 2), yet the very record he writes is aimed at a future generation's hope: a deliberately partial 'small abridgment' (v. 9), hidden up to 'come forth in his own due time' (v. 13) so it can testify that Jesus is the Christ, further the [[Gathering of Israel]], and call the Gentiles themselves to [[Repentance]].

> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Mormon again leads the Nephite armies in battles of blood and carnage—The Book of Mormon will come forth to convince all Israel that Jesus is the Christ—Because of their unbelief, the Lamanites will be scattered, and the Spirit will cease to strive with them—They will receive the gospel from the Gentiles in the latter days. About A.D. 375–84.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter divides into three movements:

1. **Failed defense and flight (vv. 1–7):** Mormon resumes command; temporary defense of the city of Jordan and allied strongholds gives way to overwhelming Lamanite numbers and a rout in the 380th year.
2. **Why Mormon writes (vv. 8–14):** He explains his restraint in recounting the carnage, names the record's future audiences, and states its Christ-centered purpose.
3. **Scattering, covenant, and warning (vv. 15–24):** Images of a people without a shepherd give way to the promised remembrance of the [[Abrahamic Covenant]] and a direct warning to the Gentiles to repent.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter refuses to treat military or covenant advantage as security against judgment. The Nephites struggle to survive without repenting or calling on God (v. 2), and centuries later the Gentiles — who will possess the promised land and even become the Lord's instrument for scattering Israel (vv. 19-20) — are themselves commanded to repent or face justice (vv. 22-24). Underlying this is a stated sequence: the Lord reserves Israel's blessings for the Gentiles (v. 19), the Gentiles drive and scatter Israel (v. 20), and only afterward does the Lord 'remember the covenant which he made unto Abraham and unto all the house of Israel' (v. 20) together with 'the prayers of the righteous' offered on Israel's behalf (v. 21) — an outline of the [[Abrahamic Covenant]] worked out across [[Scattering of Israel]] and [[Gathering of Israel]]. Mormon ties this covenant history to a Christological purpose: the record exists, in part, 'that they may be persuaded that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of the living God' (v. 14).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verses 16–18 build a sequence of lost-direction images: chaff has no control over the wind, and a vessel without sail, anchor, or steering cannot choose a course or remain fixed. These images sharpen the contrast with verse 17, where Christ is a shepherd and God leads the people.

Verse 15 requires unusual care. Its description of a people as 'dark,' 'filthy,' and 'loathsome' is the narrator's morally charged language, not a neutral ethnographic classification. The verse itself supplies its own explanation, attributing the condition explicitly to 'their unbelief and idolatry' — grounding the language in a moral and spiritual framework rather than settling any claim about physical description. The text supplies no warrant for extending this language to any modern ethnic population.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Mormon interrupts the war narrative to reflect on the limits and purpose of his own record-making (vv. 8-9): he must make the destruction known but declines to reproduce its full horror, framing his abridgment as a self-conscious, selective act of testimony (see [[Records and Sacred Writings]]).

Two metaphor clusters carry the chapter's second half. First, a shepherd-and-vessel contrast: the fathers of the remnant 'once' had 'Christ for their shepherd' (v. 17), but are now driven 'as chaff... before the wind' and tossed 'as a vessel... without sail or anchor' (v. 18) — guidance lost figured as both pastoral abandonment and navigational helplessness. Second, the closing warning to the Gentiles states that 'a remnant of the seed of Jacob shall go forth among you as a lion, and tear you in pieces, and there is none to deliver' (v. 24). This closely echoes Micah 5:8, but an even closer and more probable source lies within Mormon's own record: the resurrected Christ quotes this same Micah material almost verbatim in [[3 Nephi 20]] ('the remnant of Jacob shall be among the Gentiles... as a lion among the beasts of the forest,' 3 Nephi 20:16), a chapter Mormon himself abridged. Mormon is most plausibly echoing his own earlier compiled material rather than independently drawing on Micah. The nearby image of the earth 'rolled together as a scroll' (v. 23) recalls similar apocalyptic language in Isaiah 34:4 (heavens) and Revelation 6:14, though the wording is looser and any borrowing cannot be pinned to a single source.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Mormon calls himself 'without hope' for his people (v. 2) yet still accepts command — what does the chapter suggest about the relationship between hope and duty?
2. Why might Mormon choose to withhold 'the full account' of what he witnessed (v. 9) rather than record it in detail, given his stated belief that 'all things which are hid must be revealed' (v. 8)?
3. Why are the Gentiles described first as beneficiaries of Israel's reserved blessings and the instrument of Israel's scattering (vv. 19-20), and then directly warned that they too must repent (vv. 22-24)?
4. What is gained by describing the remnant's spiritual condition with three different images — shepherdless flock, wind-driven chaff, and an unsteerable vessel — rather than just one?
5. Verse 15 describes a future people in harsh terms tied explicitly to 'unbelief and idolatry.' How can a reader take the verse's warning seriously without turning its language into a claim about any real ethnic group?
