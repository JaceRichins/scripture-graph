# 3 Nephi 21 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[3 Nephi 21]] · My notes: [[3 Nephi 21 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Israel will be gathered when the Book of Mormon comes forth—The Gentiles will be established as a free people in America—They will be saved if they believe and obey; otherwise, they will be cut off and destroyed—Israel will build the New Jerusalem, and the lost tribes will return. About A.D. 34.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The sign and its purpose (vv. 1–7):** Christ's teachings pass to the Gentiles and from them to the remnant, signaling that covenant fulfillment has begun.
2. **The message and servant (vv. 8–11):** Isaiah's astonished kings and marred-servant language frames the bringing forth of Christ's words; rejection invokes Moses' warning.
3. **The remnant and judgment (vv. 12–21):** [[Micah 5]] supplies the lion image and a sequence of judgments. Verses 19–20 focus the warning on concrete evils and refusal to repent.
4. **Covenant cooperation and New Jerusalem (vv. 22–25):** repentant Gentiles join the remnant, assist in building the city, and participate in gathering.
5. **Worldwide gathering (vv. 26–29):** the Father's work extends to all dispersed Israel and all nations, closing with language from [[Isaiah 52]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins conversion to gathering. Coming to Christ, baptism, entering the covenant, and calling on the Father in Christ's name accompany gathering to the New Jerusalem and lands of inheritance. Repentant Gentiles may be “numbered among” Israel and assist the remnant; this portrays covenantal incorporation without requiring the conclusion that lineage distinctions disappear. Judgment is conditional upon refusal to repent, but it also has a collective dimension: the adapted oracle speaks of lands, cities, strongholds, institutions, and communal evils as well as individual response.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter substantially reuses earlier prophetic language. [[Isaiah 52]] contributes the astonished kings, the marred-servant image, and the assurance that the gathered will not flee because God goes before them and guards their rear. The marred language is notably reapplied to an unnamed servant spoken of by Christ in the third person. [[Micah 5]] supplies the remnant-as-lion image and the ordered sequence involving horses, chariots, cities, strongholds, divination, images, groves, and vengeance. The sustained order strongly supports purposeful intertextual organization in the extant English text. It does not by itself determine whether the underlying production was ancient translation, revealed or familiar KJV-style phrasing, editorial mediation, or direct nineteenth-century composition.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What precisely does “these things” include in verses 2–7: this discourse, the Nephite record generally, or both?
- Are the servant of verse 10 and the man empowered in verse 11 necessarily the same figure?
- How should the “already commenced” work in verse 7 be distinguished from the work that will “commence” in verses 26–28?
- Does the lion imagery describe literal conflict, irreversible divine judgment, political ascendancy, or prophetic symbolism?
- Which details about the New Jerusalem can be established from this chapter itself, without importing conclusions from later texts?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read the chapter alongside [[3 Nephi 20]], which provides the immediate covenant setting and the fuller citation of Moses' promised prophet. Then compare 3 Nephi 21:8, 10, and 29 with [[Isaiah 52]], and verses 12–21 with [[Micah 5]]. Track who acts in each stage: the Father prepares and gathers, Christ speaks and promises his presence, the Gentiles transmit and may assist, and the remnant recognizes, builds, and gathers. This helps distinguish textual observations from later identifications of particular nations, servants, dates, or locations.
