# 3 Nephi 12 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[3 Nephi 12]] · My notes: [[3 Nephi 12 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter opens the [[Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)]] by connecting the testimony of twelve chosen ministers, authorized [[Baptism]], remission of sins, and the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]] (vv. 1–2). Jesus then gives the [[Beatitudes]] (vv. 3–12), describes his followers as salt and light (vv. 13–16), speaks of fulfilling the [[Law of Moses]] in himself while requiring obedience to his present commandments (vv. 17–20), and teaches about anger, lust, reconciliation, truthful speech, retaliation, generosity, and enemy-love (vv. 21–48). The discourse repeatedly joins outward conduct with transformed motives and relationships.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. Verses 1-2: chosen witnesses, authorized baptism, and future believers.
2. Verses 3-12: the beatitudes.
3. Verses 13-16: salt, city, candle, and public good works.
4. Verses 17-20: fulfillment of the law in Christ and obedience to his commandments.
5. Verses 21-45: six ethical units—anger, lust, divorce, oaths, retaliation, and enemies.
6. Verses 46-48: old things fulfilled, newness, and the call to perfection.

This organization describes visible transitions in the discourse; it does not prove that every unit was composed independently or that the outline captures all possible literary patterns.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verses 1–2 connect belief in Christ and the testimony of chosen witnesses with humility, [[Baptism]], visitation by fire and the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], and remission of sins. Verses 17–20 hold fulfillment and obligation together: the law is fulfilled in Christ, yet hearers must repent, come to him with a broken heart and contrite spirit, and keep his commandments. The ethical teachings address anger, contempt, desire, reconciliation, truthful affirmation, retaliation, generosity, and enemy-love. They support a doctrine of inward conversion without making outward conduct unimportant. The chapter does not by itself define a complete theology of priesthood offices, confirmation, public justice, or the timing of sanctification.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several differences from [[Matthew 5]] are significant at the level of the received texts. The poor in spirit are qualified as those “who come unto me” (v. 3); those seeking righteousness are filled “with the Holy Ghost” (v. 6); persecution is “for my name's sake” (v. 10); and the hearers are made the light “of this people” (v. 14). Verse 26 uses the Book of Mormon monetary term “senine” and adds a question about paying while imprisoned; [[Alma 11]] describes a senine, but this does not make it economically equivalent to Matthew's smallest payment. Verse 48 adds Jesus alongside the Father as an exemplar. These differences fit the chapter's narrative setting, but textual fit does not identify whether they arose through an ancient speaker, record transmission, translation, editing, or modern composition.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's extensive verbal and sequential relationship with [[Matthew 5]] is primary literary evidence. Within the beatitudes, verses 3 and 10 repeat the promise that “theirs is the kingdom of heaven,” framing the third-person sequence before verses 11–12 continue the persecution theme in direct address. Verses 21–45 can be outlined as six treatments of inherited teachings: anger, lust, divorce, oaths, retaliation, and enemies. The repeated contrast between earlier teaching and Jesus' instruction gives the section a cumulative rhythm, although the formula is not identical in every unit.

The chapter also adapts the Matthew-like material through references to coming to Christ, the Holy Ghost, “this people,” the senine, fulfillment in Christ, and Jesus alongside the Father in verse 48. These coordinated differences support adaptation to the chapter's setting. Neither the similarities nor the adaptations establish who performed that adaptation, when it occurred, or whether the underlying discourse is ancient or modern.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How do verses 17-20 and 46-47 distinguish fulfillment from simple abolition? Why is reconciliation required when the worshipper remembers that a brother has something against him, without first assigning blame (vv. 23-24)? Are the commands concerning retaliation and enemies intended as exhaustive civil rules, personal discipleship ideals, rhetorical examples, or some combination? How does the promise to later believers in verse 2 shape the chapter's view of testimony? What aspects of "perfect" are defined by the immediate discussion of indiscriminate love, and what aspects remain unstated?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the chapter line by line with [[Matthew 5]], recording both exact correspondences and meaningful differences before drawing conclusions about dependence or historicity. Read it within [[3 Nephi 11]], [[3 Nephi 13]], and [[3 Nephi 14]] to see how baptism, coming to Christ, prayer, judgment, and covenant obedience frame the ethical teachings. The relationship between verses 17-20 and 46-47 also merits study alongside the broader Book of Mormon treatment of the [[Law of Moses]].
