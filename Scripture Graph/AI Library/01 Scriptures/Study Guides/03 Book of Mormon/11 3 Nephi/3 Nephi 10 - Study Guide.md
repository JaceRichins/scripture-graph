# 3 Nephi 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[3 Nephi 10]] · My notes: [[3 Nephi 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter moves from stunned silence to a renewed divine lament, then from mourning to thanksgiving as the three days of darkness end. Christ portrays his relationship with Israel through the image of a hen gathering and nourishing her young. The threefold wording—what he has done, what he would have done, and what he will do—joins persistent divine initiative with human refusal and a continuing invitation to repent. The narrator then interprets the destruction through [[Prophecy]], [[Records and Sacred Writings]], and the conduct of those who received rather than killed the prophets. The final verses bridge the destruction narrative of [[3 Nephi 8]] to Christ's bodily manifestation in [[3 Nephi 11]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Silence after the voice (vv. 1–2):** Astonishment temporarily stops the people's lamentation.

2. **The gathering lament (vv. 3–7):** Christ addresses the house of Israel through past gathering, refused gathering, and conditional future gathering.

3. **End of the three days (vv. 8–10):** Grief resumes, the darkness and upheaval cease, and lamentation becomes praise.

4. **Prophetic interpretation (vv. 11–17):** The narrator identifies the survivors, invites readers to search scripture, and names earlier prophetic witnesses.

5. **Transition to Christ's ministry (vv. 18–19):** The narrator previews Christ showing his body and ministering to the spared people.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The gathering image brings [[Gathering of Israel]], [[Repentance]], [[Agency]], and [[Covenants]] into one compact passage. Christ is portrayed as initiating, sheltering, and nourishing, while “ye would not” makes the people’s refusal meaningful. The future gathering remains conditional upon repentance and return “with full purpose of heart,” even as verse 7 anticipates fulfillment of the covenant to Israel’s fathers. This holds individual response together with a continuing corporate covenant purpose; it does not promise the same outcome to every unrepentant individual.

The survivors are called the “more righteous part,” not a sinless group. Verse 12 identifies their distinguishing conduct as receiving the prophets, not stoning them, and not shedding “the blood of the saints.” This is the narrator’s theological interpretation of this particular destruction. It should not be generalized into a rule that disaster survival always measures righteousness or that every casualty was less righteous than every survivor.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated sequence “how oft have I,” “how oft would I,” and “how oft will I” gives the gathering lament its force. It spans remembered care, rejected opportunity, and future invitation. The hen image is both protective and domestic: gathering under wings communicates refuge, while nourishment adds sustained care rather than mere rescue.

The language closely parallels [[Matthew 23]], especially Matthew 23:37. That textual relationship is clear, but its explanation remains debated. The parallel can establish intertextual affinity; it cannot by itself determine the direction of dependence or the mechanics of translation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter carefully stages silence, renewed speech, renewed lamentation, and final praise. Mourning first ceases because the people are overwhelmed; it later ceases because darkness and upheaval have ended and lamentation has been redirected into thanksgiving. This creates a movement from stunned interruption to interpreted worship without denying the intervening grief.

Verses 11–17 also turn the audience from witnesses into readers. “Let him search” invites examination of earlier records, particularly traditions associated with Zenos and Zenock. [[1 Nephi 19]] provides a strong internal parallel by connecting those prophetic traditions with Christ's death, three days of darkness, and upheavals among the house of Israel.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Verbal And Thematic Intertext
> The lament over Jerusalem and the image of gathering children as a hen gathers her chickens closely parallel Matthew 23:37. The relationship is especially clear in the shared combination of Jerusalem, repeated desire to gather, the hen image, and human refusal; 3 Nephi expands the saying into past, counterfactual, and future gathering statements.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong parallel accepted; direction and mechanism disputed · [[Verbal And Thematic Intertext in 3 Nephi 10|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Intertextual Coherence
> The appeal to Zenos and Zenock as prophets of events associated with Christ's coming forms a substantial internal connection with 1 Nephi 19, which also attributes signs surrounding Christ's death—including three days of darkness and disruptions of the earth—to these prophetic traditions. This is strong evidence of deliberate intertextual coherence within the Book of Mormon.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Internal correspondence accepted; historical significance disputed · [[Internal Intertextual Coherence in 3 Nephi 10|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Verbatim Textual Parallel
> 3 Nephi 10:5 reproduces, almost verbatim, Jesus' lament over Jerusalem recorded in Matthew 23:37, including the closing clause 'and ye would not.' The wording match is close enough to be a direct textual echo rather than a general thematic similarity.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Parallel accepted; explanation disputed · [[Verbatim Textual Parallel in 3 Nephi 10|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Unattested Source *(tentative)*
> 3 Nephi 10:16 asserts that the prophets Zenos and Zenock 'testified particularly concerning us, who are the remnant of their seed,' but no writings attributed to Zenos or Zenock survive outside quotations embedded within the Book of Mormon itself, so this specific claim of individualized prophecy cannot be checked against an independent record.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.7 · Internal claim verified; external absence not independently sourced

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the text repeat the gathering image in past, counterfactual, and future forms? What changes between the first cessation of mourning in verse 2 and the second in verse 10? How does the phrase “more righteous part” limit conclusions that might otherwise be drawn about suffering and moral worth? What evidence can an internal prophetic correspondence establish, and what historical questions must remain open?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the gathering lament with [[Matthew 23]], then trace its expansion from rejected gathering to a conditional future promise. Read [[1 Nephi 19]] alongside verses 14–17 to examine the book's internal use of Zenos and Zenock. Finally, compare the destruction in [[3 Nephi 8]], the voice in [[3 Nephi 9]], this chapter's transition, and the manifestation in [[3 Nephi 11]] as one sustained narrative movement.
