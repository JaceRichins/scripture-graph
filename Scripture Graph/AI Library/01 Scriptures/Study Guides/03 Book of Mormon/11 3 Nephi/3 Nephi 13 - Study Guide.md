# 3 Nephi 13 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[3 Nephi 13]] · My notes: [[3 Nephi 13 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
3 Nephi 13 develops two closely related concerns: undivided devotion to God and trust in the Father. Almsgiving, prayer, and fasting must not become performances for human approval (vv. 1-18). Treasure, perception, and service must be oriented toward heaven rather than Mammon (vv. 19-24). Jesus then turns explicitly to the twelve chosen to minister and tells them to place God's kingdom ahead of anxiety over temporal provisions (vv. 25-34). The chapter closely parallels [[Matthew 6]] while adapting the discourse to the Nephite ministry setting.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Devotion before the Father (vv. 1-18):** almsgiving (vv. 1-4), prayer (vv. 5-15), and fasting (vv. 16-18). Each practice is contrasted with action performed to be seen by people.

2. **Undivided allegiance (vv. 19-24):** earthly and heavenly treasures, the single or evil eye, and the impossibility of serving both God and Mammon.

3. **Instruction to the twelve (vv. 25-34):** a narrative transition identifies the immediate audience. Birds and lilies illustrate providence; seeking God's kingdom supplies the governing priority.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays the Father as one who sees hidden devotion (3 Nephi 13:4, 6, 18), knows needs before they are voiced (vv. 8, 32), forgives those who forgive (vv. 14-15), and provides for creation (vv. 26, 30). [[Prayer]] is neither public theater nor a technique for overcoming divine ignorance. [[Repentance and Forgiveness]] has an interpersonal dimension: the petitioner who asks mercy is also accountable to extend it. The final counsel joins [[Faith]] to the priority of the [[Kingdom of God]], especially in the ministry of the twelve.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated vocabulary of being seen, secrecy, and reward unifies the almsgiving, prayer, and fasting units. The shift from “debts” in the prayer to “trespasses” in its explanation shows how the text applies the debt metaphor to wrongdoing. “Take no thought” concerns food, clothing, and tomorrow in its immediate context; reading it as a prohibition of every kind of planning goes beyond what the chapter states. The “single” and “evil” eye imagery stands between teachings about treasure and competing masters, favoring a reading concerned with moral perception and allegiance, though the full historical nuance of the idiom remains debatable.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The opening portion uses a threefold pattern for almsgiving, prayer, and fasting: an assumed religious practice is contrasted with conduct performed for human notice, the hypocrites are said to have their reward, and the Father sees devotion offered in secret (vv. 1-18). Verses 19-24 move from treasure and the heart through the single or evil eye to the impossibility of serving both God and Mammon; allegiance is a plausible unifying theme, though the sayings need not form a tightly developed argument. Verse 25 is a narrative hinge that identifies the chosen twelve as the immediate audience of the teaching about temporal provision. The exact phrase “take no thought” recurs in verses 25, 31, and 34, with the related wording “why take ye thought” in verse 28. Comparison with [[Matthew 6]] reveals extensive inherited correspondence as well as notable variation, especially the explicit ministerial frame and the shorter form of the model prayer; neither feature identifies the historical mechanism behind the relationship.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Extended Textual Parallel
> The sustained agreement in sequence, imagery, and wording between 3 Nephi 13 and Matthew 6 strongly establishes a close literary relationship. Verse 25 also contextualizes the material by inserting an explicit address to the twelve chosen to minister among the Nephites.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · relationship certain; mechanism disputed · [[Extended Textual Parallel in 3 Nephi 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism / Translation Phenomenon *(tentative)*
> The presence of this KJV-matching doxology in 3 Nephi 13:13 is a text-critical data point worth flagging honestly: most New Testament textual critics regard the Matthew 6:13 doxology as a later liturgical addition, absent from the earliest extant Greek manuscripts and from the shorter version of the prayer in Luke 11:2-4. A comparable Old Testament doxological formula does exist ('thine is the kingdom, O LORD... thine is the greatness, and the power, and the glory,' 1 Chronicles 29:11), which the Nephites could plausibly have known from the brass plates, offering a possible ancient liturgical antecedent independent of New Testament transmission history. Neither possibility is established by the chapter itself.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · important but insufficiently sourced
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does secrecy function as an absolute rule, or as a forceful safeguard against seeking human praise?

- How does the explicit address to the twelve in verse 25 affect later application of verses 25-34 to disciples generally? No audience change occurs within the chapter.

- What significance, if any, should be assigned to the omission of the petitions for the kingdom to come and daily bread when the prayer retains the KJV-form doxology? The chapter itself gives no explanation, and the textual history of [[Matthew 6]] must be considered before proposing one.

- How do treasure, the eye, and the two masters illuminate one another? Does the eye image concern generosity, undivided devotion, general moral perception, or overlapping meanings?

- Does “take no thought” prohibit preparation, or address consuming concern over provisions and tomorrow? The immediate ministerial context is clear, but the chapter does not present a complete doctrine of labor or planning.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the chapter closely with [[Matthew 6]], noting both agreements and differences rather than assuming a mechanism for the relationship. [[Luke 12]] provides another form of the teaching about birds, lilies, and the kingdom. Read [[3 Nephi 12]] and [[3 Nephi 14]] to follow the larger [[Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)]]. Useful thematic paths include [[Prayer]], [[Fasting]], [[Service]], [[Pride]], [[Faith]], [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], and [[Kingdom of God]].
