# 3 Nephi 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[3 Nephi 6]] · My notes: [[3 Nephi 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Nephites prosper—Pride, wealth, and class distinctions arise—The Church is rent with dissensions—Satan leads the people in open rebellion—Many prophets cry repentance and are slain—Their murderers conspire to take over the government. About A.D. 26–30.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has two sharply contrasting halves:

1. **Restoration and order (vv. 1–9):** resettlement, peaceful reintegration, equitable laws, prosperity, reconstruction, and transportation networks.
2. **Stratification and revolt (vv. 10–30):** wealth-based ranks, persecution, church fracture, willful rebellion, prophetic warning, illegal executions, and conspiracy against the government.

A smaller contrast joins the halves. In verse 3, a covenant turns former robbers toward peace and productive labor; in verses 28–30, another covenant turns legal and religious elites against justice. This covenant contrast gives the chapter moral as well as chronological unity.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The account holds [[Agency]] and [[Satan and Temptation]] together. Satan stirs, tempts, and leads (3 Nephi 6:15–17), yet the people remain accountable because they knew God's will and rebelled willfully (v. 18).

The prophets' warning is centered on [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]. They testify not only against sin but also of redemption, the [[Resurrection]], and Christ's death and sufferings (v. 20). The chapter therefore presents prophetic rebuke as part of proclaiming deliverance, not merely condemnation.

Verse 13 also links [[Humility]], [[Repentance]], and [[Endurance]]: some believers refuse to answer abuse with abuse and remain penitent before God.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The repeated year notices make the reversal conspicuously rapid: peace continues through the twenty-eighth year, disputes begin in the twenty-ninth, and the people are described as being in "awful wickedness" at the commencement of the thirtieth.

The chapter also uses "covenant" for associations with opposite purposes. Former robbers covenant to keep peace and receive means to subsist (3 Nephi 6:3), while the judges' allies enter a covenant described by the narrator as devil-administered and directed against righteousness and justice (3 Nephi 6:27–30). The contrast is observable, but deliberate literary design cannot be established from the juxtaposition alone.

[[Alma 4]] provides a useful thematic comparison involving prosperity, pride, inequality, persecution, and church disruption. [[Alma 46]] similarly illuminates the chapter's concern with kingship ambition and liberty. These parallels may reflect recurring editorial vocabulary and themes; they do not by themselves establish deliberate cross-reference, ancient authorship, or historicity.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the narrator place roads, cities, equitable laws, and prosperity immediately before the account of collapse? What distinguishes prosperity itself from the pride and ranking that follow it?

What does the contrast between the peace covenant in verse 3 and the destructive covenant in verses 28–30 suggest about the moral neutrality of organized cooperation?

Why are the converted Lamanites the principal group described as remaining firm when most of the church breaks apart?

How does verse 18 qualify the strong language about Satan's power in verses 15–17?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
