# 3 Nephi 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[3 Nephi 14]] · My notes: [[3 Nephi 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus commands: Judge not; ask of God; beware of false prophets—He promises salvation to those who do the will of the Father—Compare Matthew 7. About A.D. 34.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful outline is:

1. **Self-aware judgment (vv. 1-5):** reciprocal judgment and the mote and beam.
2. **Discretion and prayer (vv. 6-11):** guarding holy things, then asking the Father for good gifts.
3. **A summary ethic (v. 12):** treat others as one wishes to be treated.
4. **Contrasting outcomes (vv. 13-27):** two ways, two kinds of prophets and fruit, two kinds of claimants, and two builders.

The final movement becomes progressively more searching: choose the way, test fruit, do the Father's will, and build by doing Jesus's sayings.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter links [[Prayer]] with confidence in the Father's goodness, but describes his answer as “good things,” not as automatic fulfillment of every desired request. It links entry into the [[Kingdom of God]] with doing the Father's will and closes by defining a secure response to Christ as both hearing and doing. In the judgment scene, claimed prophecy, exorcism, and wonderful works do not substitute for righteousness; the passage therefore warns against using religious speech or reported spiritual power as self-authenticating proof.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several verbs govern the chapter's moral logic: **judge**, **ask**, **seek**, **knock**, **know**, **hear**, and **do**. The movement from knowing to doing is especially important. False prophets are “known” by fruit, but disciples are compared to wise builders only when they “do” what they hear. The English diction and sequence are extensively shared with [[Matthew 7]], a clear textual observation whose implications for the translation process remain disputed.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The teaching relies on memorable paired contrasts: mote/beam, bread/stone, fish/serpent, wide/strait, sheep/wolves, good/corrupt trees, profession/obedience, wise/foolish builders, and rock/sand. These are not decorative images; each makes an unseen moral condition visible through an outcome. The storm falls on both houses, while the foundation determines survival. Within the comparison, faithful action—not the absence of testing—marks the wise builder.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How can readers obey “Judge not” while also recognizing teachers by their fruits? The chapter suggests self-examination and attention to outcomes, but it does not supply an exhaustive test for fruit.

What counts as a “good thing” given by the Father, especially when a sincere request is not granted in the expected form?

Are the speakers in verses 22-23 reporting genuine miracles, mistaken interpretations, or false claims? The passage does not decide; its point is that such claims cannot replace doing the Father's will.

Does the storm signify ordinary adversity, final judgment, or both? The immediate context makes judgment plausible, but the comparison itself does not explicitly identify the storm.
<!-- SG:END questions -->
