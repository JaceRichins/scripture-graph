# 3 Nephi 11 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[3 Nephi 11]] · My notes: [[3 Nephi 11 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
At the temple in Bountiful, a multitude first struggles to understand a heavenly voice and then hears the Father identify his Beloved Son. Jesus descends, identifies himself, and relates his suffering to the sins of the world and the Father's will. The people examine his wounds individually and worship him collectively. Jesus then authorizes baptizers, prescribes the manner of [[Baptism]], rejects anger-driven contention, and states a doctrinal foundation centered on [[Repentance]], belief in Christ, baptism, divine witness through the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], and becoming as a little child.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline follows the chapter's changes in speaker and action:

1. **Gathering and preparation to hear (vv. 1–6):** The people gather at the temple, discuss recent events, and hear the voice three times before understanding.
2. **The Father presents the Son (v. 7):** The voice identifies the Beloved Son and commands the multitude to hear him.
3. **Appearance, identification, and witness (vv. 8–17):** Jesus descends, identifies himself, invites examination of his wounds, and receives the multitude's worship.
4. **Authority and baptismal procedure (vv. 18–28):** Nephi and others receive power to baptize, followed by a prescribed formula and immersion.
5. **Contention and Christ's doctrine (vv. 29–41):** Jesus rejects angry contention, declares his doctrine, warns against redefining it, and commissions its proclamation.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter gives unusually explicit attention to the form of [[Baptism]]: authority comes from Jesus Christ, the candidate is called by name, the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost are named, and immersion is directed (vv. 21–27). Its doctrinal summary joins [[Faith]], repentance, baptism, divine witness, and childlikeness (vv. 31–39).

The Father, Son, and Holy Ghost are both distinguished and called one (vv. 27, 32, 35–36). This is important evidence for studying [[The Godhead]], but the chapter alone does not settle later metaphysical questions about the meaning of that oneness — Latter-day Saint readings typically take it as a unity of purpose and testimony rather than unity of substance, though the verses themselves do not specify.

Verses 28–30 forbid disputation, especially disputation "concerning the points of my doctrine" (v. 28); verse 29 identifies the source of such contention as satanic and names anger as its driving spirit. Verse 28's own wording is not explicitly limited to angry disputes, so readers should be cautious about narrowing the prohibition to hostile disagreement alone — the text clearly supports rejecting anger-driven contention, and a broader caution against doctrinal factionalism somewhat less certainly.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The account coordinates several modes of perception: the people hear but initially do not understand (vv. 3–6), look toward heaven and see Jesus (vv. 5, 8), then see and feel his wounds (vv. 14–15), culminating in knowing and bearing record (v. 15). The sequence makes acquired witness — not mere spectacle — a central concern.

Forms of **one** recur in several distinct settings: the people approach "one by one" (v. 15), respond "with one accord" (v. 16), and hear that the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost "are one" (vv. 27, 36). The recurrence is noteworthy, though treating all three as a single deliberate wordplay would require more evidence than the English text alone can supply.

The discourse also carries a Johannine cadence in translation: the doubled formula "verily, verily I say unto you" appears three times in a short span (vv. 32, 35, 39), and the Father's declaration in v. 7 ("Behold my Beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased... hear ye him") closely echoes the Transfiguration voice of Matthew 17:5. Whether this reflects an ancient tradition rendered in familiar King James idiom, or the language of composition itself, is not something the chapter's wording can settle.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter has close New Testament parallels: the Father's declaration resembles Matthew 17:5 (the Transfiguration); the invitation to examine Jesus's wounds parallels John 20:27 (Thomas), though here extended to the whole multitude going forward "one by one" rather than a single doubter; the baptismal formula parallels Matthew 28:19; and the "rock" and "sandy foundation... floods... winds" image in vv. 39–40 parallels the Parable of the Wise and Foolish Builders (Matthew 7:24–27) — though the accompanying phrase "the gates of hell shall not prevail" (v. 39) / "stand open to receive" (v. 40) instead echoes Matthew 16:18, so the passage actually merges two distinct Matthean sayings. The historical explanation for these correspondences (deliberate literary use, translation into biblical idiom, editorial shaping, or — within a believing reading — a consistent divine voice) remains disputed and is not settled by the parallels themselves.

Two smaller structural features are also present: the baptismal instructions are bracketed by the prohibition "there shall be no disputations among you" (vv. 22, 28), forming a loose inclusio around the ordinance; and the same three requirements — repent, be baptized, become as a little child — are repeated in vv. 37–38 with their order varied rather than fixed. The refrain "this is my doctrine" recurs at vv. 32, 35, and 39 (not v. 40, which instead warns against declaring "more or less than this... for my doctrine").
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What changes between the first two hearings of the voice and the third, and does the text portray understanding as requiring attentive participation?
- Why does the narrative emphasize that every person approaches Jesus individually before the multitude responds collectively?
- What does this chapter explicitly say about becoming as a little child, and which common definitions actually come from other passages?
- Does the warning against declaring "more or less" prohibit additional teaching, or more narrowly prohibit establishing additions and subtractions as Christ's foundational doctrine?
- How can readers distinguish candid doctrinal discussion from the anger-driven contention condemned in verses 28–30?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the divine declaration in [[Matthew 17]], the wound-recognition scene in [[John 20]], the baptismal commission in [[Matthew 28]], and the two-foundations conclusion in [[Matthew 7]]. Comparison should begin with exact wording and narrative function before drawing conclusions about dependence or authorship. [[3 Nephi 12]] is also necessary context: Jesus immediately proceeds to teach much more, which cautions against reading 3 Nephi 11:40 as a ban on all doctrine or instruction beyond the brief foundation stated here.
