# 3 Nephi 24 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[3 Nephi 24]] · My notes: [[3 Nephi 24 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Jesus commands that Malachi's words be written and then expounds them (v.1). The preserved text moves from the Lord's coming and purification to judgment, covenant return, [[Tithing|tithes]] and offerings, the apparent prosperity of the wicked, and God's remembrance of those who fear him. Because the chapter closely corresponds to [[Malachi 3]] (verified across all 18 verses), its placement within Christ's ministry makes the act of transmitting and interpreting scripture part of the chapter's own message. Malachi is conventionally dated after Lehi's family left Jerusalem (c. 600 BC), so having the risen Christ personally relay these words is internally consistent with the record's account of the brass plates' limited contents — though this shows internal consistency only, not proof of ancient origin, since a modern author aware of the same chronology could construct the same narrative device.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Commission and coming (vv. 1–4):** Malachi's words are written; a messenger prepares the way; the Lord comes to his temple and purifies the sons of Levi.
2. **Judgment and return (vv. 5–7):** judgment includes religious, sexual, economic, and social wrongdoing; Israel is invited to return.
3. **Tithes and covenant blessing (vv. 8–12):** withholding is called robbery, while full contribution is associated with provision and protection.
4. **Complaint and remembrance (vv. 13–18):** claims that service is vain are answered by the Lord's attention to the faithful and a coming distinction between those who serve him and those who do not.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter integrates [[Tithing]] with broader covenant life rather than treating it as an isolated financial rule. The same address calls for [[Repentance]], condemns exploitation, and anticipates [[Final Judgment]]. Its promised blessings use the corporate and agricultural world of the passage; applying them as an unconditional promise of individual wealth goes beyond what the text says.

The refining imagery connects divine presence with purification. The purpose is constructive: purified worshipers become capable of offering "in righteousness" (v. 3). Judgment and mercy remain together—the Lord is a swift witness against wrongdoing (v. 5) yet promises to spare those who are his (v. 17), a useful conjunction for studying [[Justice and Mercy]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three exchanges employ a compact accusation-question-answer form: "Wherein shall we return?" (v. 7), "Wherein have we robbed thee?" (v. 8), and "What have we spoken against thee?" (v. 13). The questions expose a gap between divine assessment and the people's self-understanding. Images then make the consequences concrete: fire and soap purify, a storehouse holds food, a devourer threatens crops, a book preserves remembrance, and jewels signify valued possession.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's strongest secure intertext is [[Malachi 3]]; that relationship is explicit in 3 Nephi 24:1 and verified across all 18 verses. This establishes deliberate textual dependence but does not, by itself, resolve historical questions about translation or composition. Malachi's oracle style is also a staged dialogue — the Lord asserts, the audience's own words of denial are quoted, and the Lord answers (vv.7-8, 13-14) — a pattern recognized in Old Testament scholarship as disputation speech. [[D&C 128]] later draws on this chapter's offering-in-righteousness and record-keeping language, illustrating its continuing use in scripture, though the exact distribution of its imagery across D&C 128's verses has not been mechanically checked here.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Interdependence
> The chapter-length correspondence with Malachi 3, reinforced by the explicit attribution to Malachi, is strong evidence of a direct literary relationship. The parallel alone cannot determine whether that relationship arose through an ancient citation rendered in familiar biblical language, modern dependence on the King James text, or another transmission process.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-calibrated, separates fact from mechanism · [[Textual Interdependence in 3 Nephi 24|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual / New Testament Citation
> The New Testament Gospels apply verse 1's 'messenger' who 'shall prepare the way before me' to John the Baptist preparing for Christ's ministry (Matthew 11:10; Mark 1:2; Luke 7:27 each quote this verse in that context), while 'the messenger of the covenant' who 'shall suddenly come to his temple' is read as Christ himself — a reading already implicit in Jesus quoting this text to the Nephites about his own mission.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · well-attested typology; Christ-as-messenger is traditional, not textual · [[Intertextual / New Testament Citation in 3 Nephi 24|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Narrative Framing
> The command to write the passage and the statement that Jesus expounded it provide strong evidence that the narrative deliberately presents Malachi's words as authoritative and relevant to the assembled people. This literary function can be identified without deciding whether the narrated event is historical.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · appropriately scoped to literary function · [[Narrative Framing in 3 Nephi 24|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal-Consistency / Textual-Critical *(tentative)*
> 3 Nephi 24:1's framing addresses a chronological problem: Malachi is conventionally dated to the Persian/post-exilic period (after Jerusalem's temple was rebuilt, c. 516 BC), which is after Lehi's family left Jerusalem c. 600 BC and took the brass plates (whose prophetic contents are described in 1 Nephi 5 as running only 'down to the commencement of the reign of Zedekiah'). Since Malachi could not have been on the brass plates, having the resurrected Christ dictate Malachi's words directly to the Nephites in A.D. 34 is internally consistent with the record's own claims about the brass plates' limits, rather than requiring the Nephites to have anachronistically possessed a later prophet's text.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · weak internal-consistency evidence only

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the list in verse 5 broaden judgment beyond private ritual observance to economic and social conduct?
- Why does the chapter place the tithing disputation between an invitation to return and a complaint that serving God is unprofitable?
- Does the answer in verses 16–18 promise immediate reversal, future judgment, or both? What in the wording limits certainty?
- What does verse 1 add by emphasizing that the words were written before they were expounded?
