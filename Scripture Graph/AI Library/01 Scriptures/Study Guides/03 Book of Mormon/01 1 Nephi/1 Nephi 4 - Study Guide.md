# 1 Nephi 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Nephi 4]] · My notes: [[1 Nephi 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Nephi slays Laban at the Lord’s command and then secures the plates of brass by stratagem—Zoram chooses to join Lehi’s family in the wilderness. About 600–592 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Renewed appeal (vv. 1–4):** Nephi invokes Moses, the Red Sea, and the angelic visitation.
2. **Unplanned entry (vv. 5–9):** He enters alone, says he does not know beforehand what to do, and finds Laban drunk.
3. **Resistance and decision (vv. 10–18):** The command is repeated; Nephi weighs Laban's conduct and the future need for the law before acting.
4. **Disguise and acquisition (vv. 19–27):** Laban's clothing, armor, and voice allow Nephi to enter the treasury and obtain the plates.
5. **Recognition crisis and oath (vv. 28–37):** Both the brothers and Zoram react to mistaken identity; reciprocal assurances and Zoram's oath resolve the danger.
6. **Departure (v. 38):** The group leaves with both the plates and Zoram.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's theological logic is specific rather than abstract: the Lord's command concerns obtaining records that preserve the [[Law of Moses]], and obedience is connected to the future faithfulness of Nephi's descendants. The text also depicts [[Revelation]] as sometimes incremental: Nephi claims spiritual guidance while lacking a complete plan. Neither feature supplies a general rule permitting violence whenever a person believes a desirable religious outcome will follow. The account itself marks the act as exceptional through Nephi's reluctance and the repeated command.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The expression "delivered him into thy hands" recurs in verses 11, 12, and 17, linking opportunity, divine providence, and Nephi's final explanation. Verse 13's one-person-versus-a-nation formulation states the narrative's consequential rationale with unusual sharpness. In verses 32–37, oath language governs the transition from fear to trust: Nephi invokes lives as guarantees, Zoram makes his own oath, and the group's fear ceases. These observations establish literary and argumentative functions; they do not by themselves prove a particular ancient legal background.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Laban's sword receives unusual material detail before it becomes the instrument of his death — a gold hilt, fine workmanship, and a blade of 'the most precious steel' (v.9). The chapter turns repeatedly on reversals of weakness and power: Nephi enters Jerusalem alone and unarmed but leaves with the records, and Laban's own sword and clothing become the means of overcoming his household's defenses. Nephi's disguise as Laban then organizes the second half of the chapter through misrecognition — it secures Zoram's full cooperation (v.21) but also causes Nephi's own brothers to flee in terror, supposing Laban had killed him too (v.28), until he calls out and they recognize him. Nephi's Exodus appeal (vv.1-3) is not a one-time device; he returns to the same Moses/Red Sea argument later when his brothers again murmur (see [[1 Nephi 17]]), and its language has some overlap with Joshua's own covenant-renewal recollection of the Red Sea deliverance (see [[Joshua 24]]), though the connection is verbal rather than clearly intentional allusion. Separately, v.13's maxim that one man's death can spare a nation from unbelief echoes, in English wording, the high priest Caiaphas's statement in John 11 — a striking parallel worth noting, though it may reflect shared King James-English idiom as much as deliberate echo, and John's own narrative complicates any simple moral contrast between the two speakers.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the account's extended justification answer the moral problem of killing Laban, or chiefly show that the narrator recognized the problem?
- How should claims of private divine direction be evaluated when the commanded act would ordinarily be gravely wrong?
- Is Laban's earlier threat relevant as punishment, self-protection, evidence of character, or some combination of these?
- Could the law have been preserved orally, and what does Nephi mean by saying his descendants could not keep it unless they had it?
- How voluntary is Zoram's oath after he has been physically restrained and offered survival conditionally?
- What ethical distinction, if any, does the narrative make between obtaining the records by impersonation and obtaining them by force?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[1 Nephi 3]] for the immediate background to Laban's threats, confiscation of property, and the angelic intervention. Compare [[Exodus 14]] with 1 Nephi 4:1–3, noting both the explicit parallels and the differences between national deliverance and an individual's mission. [[1 Nephi 17]] provides a later and fuller use of the exodus tradition in Nephi's disputes with his brothers. A focused study of [[Records and Sacred Writings]], the [[Law of Moses]], [[Agency]], and [[Covenants]] can clarify the chapter's competing concerns without resolving its ethical tensions prematurely.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
