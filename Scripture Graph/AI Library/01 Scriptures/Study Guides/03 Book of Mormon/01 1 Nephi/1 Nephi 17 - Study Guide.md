# 1 Nephi 17 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Nephi 17]] · My notes: [[1 Nephi 17 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Nephi is commanded to build a ship—His brethren oppose him—He exhorts them by recounting the history of God’s dealings with Israel—Nephi is filled with the power of God—His brethren are forbidden to touch him, lest they wither as a dried reed. About 592–591 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Journey and preservation (vv. 1–6):** eight years of affliction end at a fruitful seashore.
2. **Command and preparation (vv. 7–16):** Nephi receives the shipbuilding command, finds ore, and makes tools.
3. **Competing readings of the journey (vv. 17–22):** the brothers portray departure from Jerusalem as foolish and destructive.
4. **Israel's history as Nephi's answer (vv. 23–47):** Exodus, wilderness rebellion, covenant, conquest, and Jerusalem's wickedness form an extended rebuttal.
5. **Divine sign and redirected worship (vv. 48–55):** the brothers are restrained and shaken, acknowledge God's power, and are told to worship God.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's teaching on [[Obedience]] includes both grace and effort. God prepares the way, but Nephi must pray, seek ore, make tools, and work. Covenant privilege is also presented as morally conditional: 1 Nephi 17:34–35 denies that Israel's ancestors were inherently more deserving than the peoples they displaced. The governing assertion is that God esteems all flesh alike and favors righteousness. This principle should be read alongside, rather than erased by, the chapter's difficult claims about conquest and destruction.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several repeated terms organize the argument. The phrase **“ye know”** presses the brothers to reason from a history they already accept. References to God's **word** in verses 26, 31, and 46–51 connect creation, deliverance, prophetic authority, and the shipbuilding command. Verse 45 contrasts hearing with feeling: repeated manifestations have not produced responsiveness because the brothers are described as “past feeling.”
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The most prominent literary feature is sustained Exodus typology. Nephi does more than mention Moses: he gathers bondage, prophetic command, divided waters, manna, water from rock, divine light, murmuring, serpents, and promised land into a single interpretation of his family's crisis. This makes the speech an argument by scriptural analogy: rejecting the apparently impossible shipbuilding command repeats Israel's rejection of divine guidance. The observation is strong; whether it reflects ancient Nephite composition or modern biblical imitation remains a separate and disputed historical question.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Intertextuality
> The sustained concentration of Exodus motifs is strong evidence that the chapter deliberately frames the Lehite journey as analogous to Israel's deliverance. It is literary evidence for purposeful scriptural interpretation within the narrative, not independent proof that the narrated events occurred.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · strong literary evidence; historical implications disputed · [[Literary Intertextuality in 1 Nephi 17|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Geographic/Archaeological Correlation *(tentative)*
> Some LDS-affiliated researchers (notably Warren Aston) have proposed that Bountiful and Irreantum correspond to a real Arabian coastal site (commonly identified with Khor Kharfot/Wadi Sayq in Oman's Dhofar region), citing matching features such as fruit trees, freshwater, timber suitable for shipbuilding, and coastal access.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · minority LDS geographic proposal

> [!evidence] Internal Prophecy/Narrative Consistency *(tentative)*
> Within the narrative's own internal chronology (set about 592-591 B.C., per the chapter heading), verse 43 reads as a prophecy of Jerusalem's fall and Judah's Babylonian captivity, which the Old Testament (2 Kings 25) records as occurring in 586 B.C.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.5 · internal correspondence, historically non-independent
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does Nephi answer a technical objection about shipbuilding with an extended account of Israel's history? What is gained—and what may be obscured—when present suffering is interpreted through a sacred national story? How does verse 35's impartial moral principle affect the reading of verses 32–38 about conquest? Why does a dramatic sign produce acknowledgment in verse 55 when earlier signs had not produced durable faith?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
