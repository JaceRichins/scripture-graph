# 1 Nephi 13 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Nephi 13]] · My notes: [[1 Nephi 13 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Nephi's vision, begun in chapter 11, continues here with a sweeping panorama of Gentile history: the rise of "a great and abominable church" (vv4-9), the crossing of "many waters" to a promised land, and — pivoting at verse 20 — the transmission, corruption, and eventual restoration of sacred writings (vv20-42). The official chapter heading (dated about 600-592 B.C.) summarizes this as the church of the devil, the discovery and colonizing of America, the loss of plain and precious biblical content, gentile apostasy, the Restoration, latter-day scripture, and the building up of [[Zion]].

Two cautions belong up front. First, the vision itself names no institutions, individuals, or dates — traditional identifications (the unnamed man of v12 as Columbus, the "mother Gentiles" conflict of vv17-19 as the American Revolution) are long-held interpretive readings layered onto symbolic language, not statements the text makes explicitly; label them as tradition, not observation. Second, the chapter is morally more complex than a simple "Gentiles favored, Israel judged" reading suggests: the prospering Gentiles are described as "white, and exceedingly fair and beautiful" (v15) even as the seed of Nephi's brethren are "scattered... and smitten" (v14) — a racially coded contrast worth naming rather than passing over. The vision itself goes on to record that the Gentiles fall into blindness because of withheld truth (vv29, 32) and are shown mercy only after that judgment (vv33-36): divine aid to one group is not the same as endorsement of everything that group later does.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter divides into two panels joined by the recurring image of "many waters":

1. **A political/historical panorama (vv1-19).** The nations of the Gentiles; the rise of the abominable church and its exploitation of "the seed of thy brethren"; Gentiles crossing the waters, colonizing the promised land, warring with their "mother Gentiles," and prospering by the power of God.
2. **A textual/sacred panorama (vv20-42).** A record proceeding "out of the mouth of a Jew" — naturally read as the Bible, though the chapter never uses that word — passes through the abominable church, which strips it of "plain and precious" content (vv26-29), and is later supplemented by "other books" (v39) that restore what was lost and reconcile the records as one under "one God and one Shepherd" (v41).

The hinge is v20: "I beheld that they did prosper in the land; and I beheld a book" — the vision turns from national fortune to the fate of scripture. Within the second panel, vv30-36 mark a further turn from judgment (on the remnant, and on Gentiles blinded by the loss of plain truths) to promised mercy on both.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Loss and restoration of scriptural truth.** The chapter grounds the doctrine that a record proceeding from the Jews originally contained "the fulness of the gospel" (v24) but later lost "plain and precious" parts and covenants (vv26-29) at the hands of the abominable church, and that latter-day scripture restores rather than replaces what was lost (vv34-40). See [[Restoration of the Gospel]] and [[Apostasy]]. This is the chapter's own visionary/theological assertion about loss; it does not itself specify which passages, when, or by what process — it should not be treated as a manuscript-critical claim the text substantiates on its own.
- **Scriptural unity, not competition.** V41 states the Bible and the latter-day record "shall be established in one" under "one God and one Shepherd," framing multiple scriptural witnesses as converging testimony rather than rivals.
- **Judgment answered by mercy.** The remnant is visited "in judgment" (v34) and the Gentiles themselves stumble because of withheld truth (vv29, 32), yet the Lord promises mercy — to the Gentiles through restored gospel truth (vv33-36) and to the remnant through preservation ("the Lord God will not suffer that the Gentiles will utterly destroy the mixture of thy seed," v30). See [[Justice and Mercy]].
- **Universal reach of the gospel.** V40's charge that "all men must come unto him, or they cannot be saved," and v42's promise that Christ will manifest himself to "all nations, both unto the Jews and also unto the Gentiles," extend covenant promises beyond a single lineage. See [[Jews and Gentiles]].

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase family "plain and precious"/"plain and most precious" is the chapter's central refrain, appearing repeatedly across vv26-40 (including twice within a single verse at v34) to name what is lost from scripture and later restored. "Captivity" and deliverance "by the power of God" recur at vv9, 13, 16, 18-19, though not always with the same subject — v9 concerns the saints persecuted by the abominable church, while vv13-19 concern the colonizing Gentiles — so the resemblance to an Exodus pattern is thematic rather than a single tightly repeated formula. "Many waters" (vv10, 12, 13, 17, 29) is the vision's recurring geographic image separating Gentile and Israelite peoples. The interrogative-response device — "What beholdest thou? And I said: I behold..." (v2) — echoes the catechetical pattern used earlier in the same vision (1 Nephi 11), consistent with chapters 11-14 forming one continuous instructional vision.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter builds through repetition rather than plot: "great and abominable church" and "plain and precious" both function as refrains inviting the reader to track a single entity and a single loss across scattered verses. The designation of the church as "the mother of harlots" (v34) resembles Revelation 17:5's "MOTHER OF HARLOTS"; 1 Nephi 14 later ties this vision explicitly to John's Revelation, suggesting the two are meant to be read together, though the resemblance is best treated as a literary echo rather than proof of direct textual dependence. A firmer parallel is v37's "publish peace, yea, tidings of great joy, how beautiful upon the mountains," which closely echoes Isaiah 52:7 ("how beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace") — most plausibly explained as shared King James idiom rather than Nephi quoting a text not yet written in his own narrative timeframe. The closing "the last shall be first, and the first shall be last" (v42) echoes a familiar gospel saying (compare Matthew 19:30) and repurposes it from individual reward to describe the sequence of God's dealings with Jew and Gentile.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What in the text itself (as opposed to later tradition) supports or resists identifying the man of v12, the Gentiles of v13, or the conflict of vv17-19 with specific historical events?
- Does "taken away" (vv26-29) necessarily mean physical deletion from a written text, or could it include suppressed covenants, lost oral teaching, or obscured understanding?
- How should the vision's evident approval of the Gentiles' arrival and prospering (vv15-16) be read alongside its own acknowledgment that the seed of Nephi's brethren were "scattered... and smitten" (v14) and its racially coded description of the Gentiles as "white, and exceedingly fair and beautiful" (v15)? Does divine aid to one people imply approval of everything that people later does?
- What would it mean for the "great and abominable church" to be read as a moral/spiritual condition (per vv7-9, 26-27) recurring across many institutions, rather than one fixed historical identity?
- What does it mean for two bodies of scripture to be "established in one" (v41) while remaining textually distinct?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
