# 1 Nephi 21 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Messiah will be a light to the Gentiles and will free the prisoners—Israel will be gathered with power in the last days—Kings will be their nursing fathers—Compare Isaiah 49. About 588–570 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The servant's call and apparent failure (vv. 1–4):** The servant is called from the womb, prepared as a concealed weapon, named Israel, and allowed to voice discouragement.
2. **A widened commission (vv. 5–7):** Restoring Jacob is only part of the mission; the servant becomes a light to the Gentiles and salvation to the ends of the earth.
3. **Liberation and return (vv. 8–13):** Prisoners emerge, travelers are fed and guided, barriers become roads, and creation celebrates God's mercy.
4. **Zion's complaint and God's answer (vv. 14–21):** Zion feels forgotten, but divine remembrance and the unexpected return of children reverse her desolation.
5. **Gentile assistance and divine deliverance (vv. 22–26):** Nations carry Zion's children home; oppressors lose their captives; recognition of the Lord closes the oracle.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter depicts salvation at several scales. The servant restores Jacob, brings light to the Gentiles, releases prisoners, and participates in a covenant that renews desolate inheritances. Zion's restoration therefore includes reconciliation to God, freedom from bondage, communal rebuilding, and the recovery of family. Nephi's explanation in [[1 Nephi 22]] makes [[Gathering of Israel]] the controlling Book of Mormon application, while leaving room for spiritual and historical dimensions rather than reducing gathering to a single event.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The poem builds meaning through linked images rather than abstract definitions. Sword and arrow imagery presents the servant as prepared and potent; prisoner and journey imagery casts restoration as [[Exodus and Deliverance]]; maternal and family imagery makes covenant fidelity intimate; city and bridal imagery makes restored people Zion's adornment. The geographical horizon also expands—from Jacob and Israel, to the Gentiles, to the ends of the earth, and finally to “all flesh.”
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The emotional center is Zion's direct objection: she believes she has been forsaken and forgotten. The text does not suppress that complaint; it answers it through increasingly expansive images—maternal compassion, engraved palms, remembered walls, returning children, a bride's ornaments, and an overcrowded restored land. A second productive tension concerns the servant: verse 3 says “Israel,” while verse 5 gives the servant a mission to restore Israel. Representative identity offers one coherent explanation, but the ambiguity should remain visible in interpretation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Narrative And Thematic Context
> The chapter's opening address to scattered Israel, its return imagery, and Nephi's immediate exposition provide strong evidence that the quotation functions in 1 Nephi as a prophecy of covenant scattering and gathering.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · strongly established narrative function · [[Narrative And Thematic Context in 1 Nephi 21|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual Dependence
> The sustained sequence and close wording across all twenty-six verses constitute very strong evidence that 1 Nephi 21 is a conscious quotation or adaptation of Isaiah 49.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · textual dependence established · [[Intertextual Dependence in 1 Nephi 21|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Literary Analysis *(tentative)*
> The servant in this chapter is identified in v.3 as 'Israel' yet elsewhere is described as an individual sent to restore and gather Israel (vv.5-6, 8), creating an internal tension between collective and individual readings of the servant figure.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · textual tension accepted; interpretation disputed

> [!evidence] Literary/Typological Reading *(tentative)*
> Verse 16's image of being 'graven upon the palms of my hands' is sometimes read devotionally as a foreshadowing of Christ's nail-pierced hands, but in its Isaianic context it is a covenant-remembrance metaphor (like an engraved seal or tattoo marking ownership/memory), not an explicit reference to crucifixion wounds.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · contextual reading plausible; typology speculative

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical-Geographic *(tentative)*
> The identity of 'Sinim' (v.12) is disputed among scholars; proposed identifications include a southern region (possibly Aswan/Syene in Egypt) or, less commonly, an association with China, but the text itself gives no geographic markers to adjudicate between readings.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.7 · uncertainty sound; proposed locations insufficiently sourced

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does verse 5 change or complicate the identification of the servant in verse 3?
- Which details in verses 8–12 make the gathering resemble a new exodus?
- Why might the prophecy answer Zion's theological fear through maternal, bodily, architectural, and family images?
- How does the role of the Gentiles change between verses 6 and 22–23?
- Does the judgment language in verses 24–26 describe literal retaliation, poetic reversal, divine justice, or some combination?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the full chapter with [[Isaiah 49]], noting both its extensive agreement and the expansions in 1 Nephi 21:1 and 13. Then read [[1 Nephi 22]] for Nephi's immediate explanation and [[2 Nephi 6]] for Jacob's quotation and application of verses 22–23. Acts 13:47, 2 Corinthians 6:2, Luke 2:32, and Revelation 7:16–17 show how several images from the servant passage were reused in early Christian writings.
