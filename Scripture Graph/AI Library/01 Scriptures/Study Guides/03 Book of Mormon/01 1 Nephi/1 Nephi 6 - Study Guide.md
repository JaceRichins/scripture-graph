# 1 Nephi 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Nephi 6]] · My notes: [[1 Nephi 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Nephi writes of the things of God—Nephi’s purpose is to persuade men to come unto the God of Abraham and be saved. About 600–592 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The six verses form a compact progression:

1. **Omission and source (vv. 1–2):** Nephi declines to duplicate his father's genealogy but preserves the essential claim of descent from Joseph.
2. **Constraint and priority (v. 3):** limited plate space is reserved for the things of God.
3. **Controlling purpose (v. 4):** the record seeks to persuade people to come unto God and be saved.
4. **Selection and succession (vv. 5–6):** Nephi contrasts divine and worldly approval, then makes his editorial standard binding on his descendants.

The central purpose in verse 4 explains both the omissions before it and the selection policy after it.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verse 4 joins persuasion, coming unto God, and salvation. The title “God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob” uses a common biblical patriarchal formula also found in [[Exodus 3]]. It places Nephi's invitation in continuity with Israel's ancestral religious language, although this chapter does not specify particular covenant promises or establish dependence on one biblical passage. [[2 Nephi 25]] later gives a fuller, explicitly Christ-centered account of Nephi's persuasive purpose.

The chapter also presents sacred record keeping as purposeful and selective. Nephi reserves limited room for material intended to bring readers to God and commands later custodians not to occupy the plates with material lacking worth for humanity. This concerns the function of this record; it should not be turned into a general declaration that genealogy, history, or all temporal subjects are worthless. See [[Records and Sacred Writings]], [[Salvation]], and [[Scripture Study]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Nephi's repeated first-person statements—“I do not give,” “I desire,” “mine intent,” and “I shall give commandment”—make authorial choice unusually visible. “For” supplies reasons in verses 2–4, while “wherefore” in verses 5–6 draws practical consequences. The language therefore moves from decision, to rationale, to policy.

“Pleasing unto the world” and “pleasing unto God” form an ethical contrast. In context, however, “not of worth” should not automatically be equated with every nonsacred subject: Nephi's concern is what deserves the scarce space of this particular record.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter functions as metanarrative: the narrator pauses the story to explain how and why the story is being written. Its references to another family record, finite plate capacity, and future custodians create a coherent internal picture of a layered record-making project. That coherence is useful literary evidence, but by itself it cannot determine whether the account is ancient history or a successfully constructed narrative persona.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary Evidence
> The chapter provides strong internal evidence that its narrator is portrayed as a self-conscious editor: he identifies a source he will not duplicate, explains a material constraint, states a theological objective, and prescribes a policy for for later custodians.

> [!evidence] Scriptural Intertextuality *(tentative)*
> The patriarchal formula in verse 4 is strong evidence of deliberate continuity with biblical religious language, but it is not diagnostic of when or how the wording entered the text.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · overlap accepted; deliberate continuity disputed

> [!evidence] Literary/Genre Parallel Argument *(tentative)*
> Some LDS scholars have argued that Nephi's recurring statements about what he will and will not record function similarly to scribal 'colophons' (opening/closing statements of authorship, scope, or purpose) attested in ancient Egyptian and Near Eastern texts, and have cited this chapter as an example of that genre feature.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.4 · minority apologetic proposal; source application unverified

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should Nephi's declared purpose in verse 4 shape interpretation of the narratives and sermons he chooses to preserve?
- What does the omission of genealogy reveal about the difference between this record and Lehi's record?
- Does “things which are pleasing unto the world” name a fixed category, or material that fails the saving purpose stated in verse 4?
- How do Jacob and later plate keepers understand and apply Nephi's command in verse 6?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare this chapter with [[1 Nephi 5]], [[1 Nephi 9]], [[1 Nephi 19]], and [[Jacob 1]] to trace references to genealogy, distinct records, and the editorial policy inherited by later plate keepers. Compare verse 4 with [[Exodus 3]] and [[Matthew 22]] as examples of the shared patriarchal divine title, while remembering that the formula is widespread and serves different purposes in different passages. Finally, read [[2 Nephi 25]] for Nephi's fuller account of the persuasive and Christ-centered purpose of his writings.
