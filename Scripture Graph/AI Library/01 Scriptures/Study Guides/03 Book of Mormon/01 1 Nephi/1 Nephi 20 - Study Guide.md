# 1 Nephi 20 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Nephi 20]] · My notes: [[1 Nephi 20 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Lord reveals His purposes to Israel—Israel has been chosen in the furnace of affliction and is to go forth from Babylon—Compare Isaiah 48. About 588–570 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Claimed identity without fidelity (vv. 1–2).** Israelite identity and religious speech are contrasted with truth, righteousness, and reliance on God.
2. **Advance declaration against idol-credit (vv. 3–8).** God announces events beforehand so Israel cannot attribute them to an image.
3. **Anger restrained; Israel refined (vv. 9–11).** Preservation is grounded in God's name and glory, while the furnace can convey both discipline and refining.
4. **Creator and ruler of history (vv. 12–17).** The Lord announces action against Babylon. The 'him' of verses 14–15 is commonly associated with Cyrus in the broader Isaianic context, while the transition to the sent 'me' in verse 16 remains difficult.
5. **A lament over disobedience (vv. 18–19).** Peace, righteousness, and abundant posterity describe what might have followed from hearkening.
6. **Departure and renewed exodus (vv. 20–22).** Jacob leaves Babylon, receives desert provision, and encounters a final warning that wickedness yields no peace.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds divine restraint and accountability together. God does not cut Israel off despite treachery (vv. 8–11), yet inherited identity and religious speech do not excuse falsehood (vv. 1–2). Redemption from Babylon likewise does not erase the warning to the wicked (v. 22). These themes relate to [[Grace]], [[Obedience]], and [[Justice and Mercy]].

The 'furnace of affliction' connects with [[Adversity]], portraying Israel's corporate affliction as a setting of discipline, refining, and choosing. It should not be universalized into a claim that God directly causes every affliction or that suffering automatically proves righteousness or divine favor.

Verses 20–21 connect release from Babylon with [[Exodus and Deliverance]] through departure, desert guidance, and water from rock.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Commands and forms of hearing organize the chapter: 'Hearken and hear' (v. 1), 'Hearken unto me' (v. 12), and 'O that thou hadst hearkened' (v. 18). The repetition links Israel's failure with refusal to receive and answer divine speech.

The current text has two notable differences from traditional KJV [[Isaiah 48]]. Verse 1 includes 'or out of the waters of baptism,' a phrase absent from the 1830 Book of Mormon and introduced in Joseph Smith's 1840 revision. It therefore gives the current verse an explicit baptismal interpretation but should not be attributed to Nephi's underlying text without evidence. Verse 10 omits the KJV phrase 'but not with silver.' That omission simplifies the English sentence, but internal evidence cannot establish whether it was intentional or which reading is textually prior.

Other images include an iron sinew and brass brow for resistance (v. 4), a furnace for affliction (v. 10), river and sea waves for peace and righteousness (v. 18), sand and gravel for abundant posterity (v. 19), and rock-water for desert provision (v. 21).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 3–8 stage a conflict between rival explanations of history. God announces events beforehand so Israel cannot credit idols after those events occur. Prophecy therefore functions here as theological attribution as well as prediction.

Verses 20–21 place the departure from Babylon beside desert leading and water from a split rock. This strongly evokes Israel's earlier wilderness deliverance and supports a 'renewed exodus' or 'second exodus' reading. That expression is a literary interpretation, not terminology used by the chapter itself.

Water imagery also appears in verses 1, 18, and 21, but a unified compositional water motif is uncertain—especially because the baptism wording in the current verse 1 entered the printed Book of Mormon in 1840.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Extended Literary Dependence
> The sustained correspondence with Isaiah 48 strongly supports the conclusion that this chapter deliberately incorporates and applies an Isaianic oracle. The correspondence alone cannot determine the historical mechanism by which the wording entered the Book of Mormon.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · Broadly accepted with mechanism disputed · [[Extended Literary Dependence in 1 Nephi 20|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Ambiguous Prophetic Voice Reading *(tentative)*
> The unnamed 'me' sent by the Lord and his Spirit in verse 16 has been read variously as the prophet Isaiah, as Cyrus (the addressee of the surrounding Babylon/Chaldeans material), or, in later Christian/LDS devotional reading, as a Christological voice; the chapter itself does not resolve the referent.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · Disputed reception history

> [!evidence] Bom/Kjv Textual Divergence *(tentative)*
> Omitting 'but not with silver' removes a phrase whose meaning is disputed among Isaiah scholars (some KJV translators/readers take it as 'not for silver's sake'), leaving 1 Nephi 20:10 as a simpler, less ambiguous refining metaphor.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.6 · Reader effect plausible; cause disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the Lord ground restraint in his own name and glory (vv. 9–11), and how does this relate mercy to Israel's guilt?
- How does the broader context of [[Isaiah 44]] and [[Isaiah 45]], where Cyrus is named, clarify the 'him' of verses 14–15? Does it also resolve the identity of the sent 'me' in verse 16?
- What interpretive effect does the baptism phrase give the current form of verse 1, and how should its introduction in the 1840 revision limit claims about Nephi's source text?
- Does 'transgressor from the womb' describe corporate Israel's history, prophetic hyperbole, or something else? What prevents it from serving as a complete doctrine of inherited guilt?
- How does verse 22 qualify the proclamation of Jacob's redemption in verses 20–21?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare 1 Nephi 20 verse by verse with [[Isaiah 48]], distinguishing verified correspondence from conclusions about how that correspondence arose.
- Compare the 1830 and 1840 Book of Mormon forms of verse 1 to trace the history of 'or out of the waters of baptism.'
- Read [[Isaiah 44]] and [[Isaiah 45]] for the named Cyrus context behind the action against Babylon, while keeping the speaker transition in 1 Nephi 20:16 distinct.
- Compare the rock-water imagery with [[Exodus 17]] and [[Numbers 20]].
- Examine other biblical uses of seed or sand imagery before treating verse 19 as a specific quotation of the Abrahamic promise.
