# 1 Nephi 16 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Nephi 16]] · My notes: [[1 Nephi 16 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The wicked take the truth to be hard—Lehi’s sons marry the daughters of Ishmael—The Liahona guides their course in the wilderness—Messages from the Lord are written on the Liahona from time to time—Ishmael dies; his family murmurs because of afflictions. About 600–592 B.C.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Hard teachings and temporary humility (vv. 1–6):** Nephi answers his brethren's complaint and expresses hope after they humble themselves.
2. **Marriage and departure (vv. 7–16):** The families intermarry, Lehi receives a command to depart, and the brass ball guides their route.
3. **Broken bow and restored food (vv. 17–32):** Hunting equipment fails, hunger produces murmuring, Nephi makes replacement equipment, Lehi seeks guidance, and the party obtains food.
4. **Death, revolt, and repentance (vv. 33–39):** Ishmael dies at Nahom; grief and deprivation intensify into a proposed return and murder plot before divine chastening produces another repentance.

This organization reveals a recurring sequence: hardship, murmuring, correction, humility, and provision. Because the sequence is narrated selectively, it should be treated as a literary-theological pattern rather than a complete psychological account of every traveler.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's treatment of [[Revelation]] is conditional and participatory. The pointers work according to faith, diligence, and heed, while Nephi must still construct equipment, travel, climb, and hunt. The narrative therefore joins [[Faith]], [[Obedience]], and [[Work and Self-Reliance]] rather than presenting guidance as a substitute for action.

The chapter also complicates simplistic views of prophetic leadership. Lehi murmurs, is chastened, and then resumes his revelatory role by inquiring of the Lord. Correction and [[Repentance]] are thus applied even to the group's senior prophet.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Material objects carry much of the chapter's argument: a brass ball directs travel, failed bows expose the group's vulnerability, a handmade bow enables renewed action, and changing writing interprets events. Nephi then states the governing moral directly: small means can bring about great things.

Laman's speech in verses 37–38 provides a significant countervoice. He interprets Nephi's claimed revelations as deception masking political ambition. The narrative rejects that interpretation through the divine intervention of verse 39, but historically cautious reading still distinguishes Laman's reported accusation, Nephi's framing of it, and any conclusion about the participants' actual motives.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the statement that the pointers worked according to faith describe their physical operation, the travelers' ability to interpret them, or both? The chapter does not fully distinguish these possibilities.
- Why does Nephi ask Lehi where to hunt after Lehi has murmured? The scene may express respect for Lehi's role, invite him to repent through renewed inquiry, or serve both purposes.
- How should readers evaluate the daughters of Ishmael's complaint? The narrator calls it murmuring, but their stated grief, hunger, thirst, fatigue, and fear are substantial rather than trivial.
- What exactly is meant by the writing changing from time to time? The text reports the change but does not explain its material mechanism.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
The identification of Nahom (v. 34) with the ancient South Arabian tribal name NHM/Nihm — attested in votive inscriptions on altars near Marib, Yemen, associated with the Nihm tribal confederation — is frequently cited as external evidence for the Book of Mormon's Arabian setting. The inscriptions attest a tribal or personal designation, not a site explicitly named "Nahom," and connecting it to Lehi's route requires further linguistic, chronological, and geographic inference that this verse alone cannot supply. Readers should weigh this alongside the alternative explanation that "Nahom" may function as a Hebrew wordplay on the root n-ħ-m ("to mourn/comfort"), placed immediately before the account of the daughters of Ishmael mourning their father (v. 35) — a literary reading that requires no ancient toponym transmission at all. Claims connecting Nahom to an external site should be treated as disputed and interesting rather than settled.

The "fine steel" bow (v. 18) is best studied alongside general archaeometallurgical findings on early carburized-iron technology in the ancient Near East. Such findings show steel-like iron was not unknown in the broader region well before 600 B.C., but they do not by themselves establish that a bow of this material was available to a family of Lehi's era and setting, nor do they resolve whether "steel" reflects a translation choice rather than a strict material claim. Compare the compass episode with [[1 Nephi 18]], where rebellion again affects its operation, and with Alma 37, which names the instrument Liahona and develops it as a spiritual type.
