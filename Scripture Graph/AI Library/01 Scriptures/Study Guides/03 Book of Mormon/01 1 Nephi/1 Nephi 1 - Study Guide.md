# 1 Nephi 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Nephi 1]] · My notes: [[1 Nephi 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter serves simultaneously as a preface to Nephi's record and as the call narrative of Lehi. Nephi introduces his education, experience, language, and responsibility for the record (vv. 1–3); establishes the Jerusalem setting (v. 4); recounts Lehi's prayer and revelations (vv. 5–15); explains his selective use of his father's writings (vv. 16–17); and describes Lehi's public testimony and rejection (vv. 18–20). Its final statement about tender mercies and deliverance anticipates the movement into [[1 Nephi 2]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Nephi's record-making preface (vv. 1–3):** education, language, truth claim, and personal authorship.

2. **Historical crisis and intercession (vv. 4–5):** prophetic warnings at Jerusalem prompt Lehi's prayer.

3. **Lehi's revelations (vv. 6–15):** pillar of fire, throne vision, heavenly figures, book, judgment oracle, and praise.

4. **Editorial disclosure (vv. 16–17):** Nephi identifies Lehi's writings as a source but expressly gives only an abridgment.

5. **Public witness and rejection (vv. 18–20):** Lehi proclaims judgment, Messiah, and redemption; the people mock and threaten him; Nephi states the theme of divine deliverance.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects [[Prayer]], [[Revelation]], and prophetic service: Lehi prays for his people, receives knowledge, and then publicly declares it. It also holds judgment and mercy together. Jerusalem faces destruction because of wickedness, yet Lehi praises God's goodness and mercy. The promise that those who come to God will not perish should be interpreted cautiously; within a passage that predicts temporal deaths, it need not promise that every faithful person will escape physical harm.

The reference to a Messiah and "the redemption of the world" makes redemption central from the opening chapter, but no detailed theory of the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] is supplied here. The concluding statement associates [[Faith]] with divine choosing and deliverance without explaining the full relationship among those ideas.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verse 2's parallel phrases—"the learning of the Jews" and "the language of the Egyptians"—should not be made more precise than the text permits. "Learning" may concern cultural, religious, or scribal formation, while "language" may concern speech or writing; the verse itself does not settle those options. Verse 3 likewise combines strong attestation with epistemic limitation: Nephi says the record is true and personally made, but also says it is written "according to my knowledge."
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The vision narrows in stages: from a pillar of fire, to an opened heaven and throne, to one radiant figure and twelve others, and finally to a book placed directly before Lehi. Reading leads first to a judgment oracle and then to praise, after which private revelation becomes public proclamation. This resembles prophetic-call narratives in [[Isaiah 6]] and [[Ezekiel 1]], while the written commission also invites comparison with [[Ezekiel 2]] and [[Jeremiah 36]]. These parallels clarify the narrative's biblical literary setting but cannot by themselves determine date, dependence, or historicity.

Nephi also exposes the chapter's layered narration: readers receive Nephi's abridgment of Lehi's written account, including summaries of what Lehi saw, heard, read, wrote, prophesied, and said. Because the underlying record is not reproduced in full, the exact boundary between Lehi's wording and Nephi's summary cannot always be recovered.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary And Intertextual Pattern
> Lehi's account has several features of biblical prophetic-call and commission narratives: divine glory, an opened-heaven throne scene, a written heavenly message, proclamation of judgment, and resistance from the audience. Isaiah 6 and Ezekiel 1-3 provide particularly relevant comparisons. These correspondences strongly support reading the episode as deliberately framed in biblical prophetic terms, but they provide little leverage for determining when or how the account was composed.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · accepted observation / disputed evidentiary weight · [[Literary And Intertextual Pattern in 1 Nephi 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Synchronism
> The reference to Zedekiah and the anticipated Babylonian captivity is consistent with the late-Judah political crisis summarized in 2 Kings 24:10-17. This constitutes a coherent historical synchronism within the narrative, but only modest evidence beyond internal plausibility because the same setting is readily available in the Bible.
> — strength 0.1 · confidence 1.0 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation · [[Historical Synchronism in 1 Nephi 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary Form / Genre Parallel *(tentative)*
> Lehi's vision (prayer, heavens opening, God enthroned amid a heavenly host, a commissioning message) follows the recognizable ancient Near Eastern 'prophetic call / throne theophany' pattern also seen in Isaiah 6, Ezekiel 1-2, and 1 Kings 22:19-23.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · disputed interpretation

> [!evidence] Historical Plausibility *(tentative)*
> The claim that 'many prophets' were active in Jerusalem warning of destruction at the start of Zedekiah's reign is broadly consistent with the biblical record of prophetic activity in that era, including Jeremiah, whose ministry is dated across the reigns of Josiah, Jehoiakim, and Zedekiah.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · plausible but imprecise

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does "perish" in verse 14 refer to physical death, ultimate spiritual loss, or both?

- How are the pillar-of-fire experience and the later throne vision related: one continuous revelation or two distinct experiences?

- Which details in verses 13 and 19 reproduce the heavenly book, and which are Nephi's summary of Lehi's broader visions and preaching?

- On what textual grounds may the One and twelve be identified, and where does explicit observation end and Christian interpretation begin?

- What does "according to my knowledge" contribute to Nephi's claim of truth and to the acknowledged limits of his account?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the throne and commission scenes in [[Isaiah 6]], [[Ezekiel 1]], and [[Ezekiel 2]]; the judgment-scroll setting in [[Jeremiah 36]]; and the rejection of prophetic messengers in [[2 Chronicles 36]]. [[2 Kings 24]] supplies biblical context for Zedekiah's accession and Babylon's pressure on Jerusalem. Within the Book of Mormon narrative, [[1 Nephi 2]] develops the promised deliverance, while [[1 Nephi 5]] later revisits the danger to Jerusalem and the preservation of Lehi's family.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
