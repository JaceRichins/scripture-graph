# D&C 64 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 64]] · My notes: [[D&C 64 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 64 was given through Joseph Smith at Kirtland, Ohio (traditionally dated September 11, 1831), during the early effort to gather the Saints to [[Zion]] and organize the Church's temporal affairs. It opens as a call to the elders to forgive one another, acknowledging that both accusers and the accused — including Joseph Smith himself — have sinned, while making forgiveness a universal requirement and reserving final judgment to God (vv.1-14). The Lord then addresses several named individuals directly — forgiving Isaac Morley, promising conditional forgiveness to Edward Partridge, and giving property and business instructions to Sidney Gilbert, Frederick G. Williams, and Newel K. Whitney — before pivoting at verse 23 to frame the present as a 'today' of [[Sacrifice]] and [[Tithing]] set against a coming 'tomorrow' of judgment (vv.15-33). It closes with a vision of [[Zion]] as a flourishing, self-correcting community that will become an ensign to the nations (vv.34-43).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful, though not perfectly clean, outline:

1. **Mercy, accusation, and forgiveness (vv.1-17):** divine forgiveness (including of Joseph Smith, v.7), the command to forgive all people (v.10), compassionate church accountability for the unrepentant (vv.12-14), and individualized treatment of Ezra Booth, Isaac Morley, and Edward Partridge (vv.15-17).
2. **Stewardship on the way to Zion (vv.18-33):** property and business instructions for Sidney Gilbert, Frederick G. Williams, and Newel K. Whitney, framed by an urgent 'today'/'tomorrow' eschatological contrast (vv.23-25) and the maxim that 'small things' build toward something great (v.33).
3. **The character and future of Zion (vv.34-43):** willing obedience versus rebellion, Zion's internal accountability (even for an unfaithful bishop), and its promised glory among the nations.

Verses 22-25 function as a bridge and could reasonably be grouped with either the second or third movement, so this division is a teaching aid rather than a structure the text marks out explicitly.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter resists reducing forgiveness to a simple rule: disciples are required to forgive 'all men' (v.10), and withholding forgiveness is called 'the greater sin' (v.9) — yet forgiving does not preclude bringing an unrepentant, unconfessing person before the church (vv.12-14), an action the text insists should come from compassion, not its absence. The Lord reserves his own prerogative to 'forgive whom I will forgive' (v.10), distinct from what is required of the elders. The 'keys of the mysteries of the kingdom' promised to Joseph Smith are explicitly conditioned on his obedience and bounded to his lifetime (v.5) — a specific conditional promise about those keys, not a general theory of prophetic authority. [[Zion]] is framed as requiring both an inward 'willing mind' and outward obedience (v.34), with standing tied — ambiguously, literally or figuratively — to being 'of the blood of Ephraim' (v.36). Verse 23's 'day...for the tithing of my people' is a notably early use of tithing language in the Doctrine and Covenants, years before the fixed one-tenth law of [[Tithing]] was formalized; what exactly it required in 1831 is not settled by this verse alone.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The words **today** and **tomorrow** in verses 23-25 are explicitly figurative or divine-perspective language: verse 24 says it is speaking “after the manner of the Lord.” The passage contrasts the present opportunity for sacrifice, tithing, and labor with future judgment; it should not automatically be converted into a literal twenty-four-hour timetable.

The term **tithing** in verse 23 is clearly connected to sacrifice and protection at the coming judgment. This chapter alone, however, does not define the amount, method, or institutional administration intended by the term.

The noun **agents** in verse 29 occurs amid debt, property, stores, and provisioning. That setting favors the sense of entrusted representatives or stewards, although it does not exclude broader reflection on moral agency.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 24's stubble-and-burning imagery closely reproduces [[Malachi 4]] 4:1 — sharing 'proud,' 'do wickedly,' 'stubble,' 'burn...up,' and the title 'Lord of Hosts' in the same order — making this the chapter's strongest identifiable textual echo. Verse 34's 'the willing and obedient shall eat the good of the land' closely reproduces Isaiah 1:19; its companion phrase 'a willing mind' also appears in 1 Chronicles 28:9, though that resemblance is looser and may reflect common KJV idiom rather than a deliberate second allusion. Verse 11's 'let God judge between me and thee' echoes a KJV appeal-to-divine-judgment formula found in Genesis 16:5 and 1 Samuel 24:12; the Genesis instance is followed by Sarai dealing harshly with Hagar, so it does not cleanly model non-retaliation the way David's appeal to Saul does, and the parallel is best read as shared idiom rather than an exact ethical template. Verse 33's compact aphorism, 'out of small things proceedeth that which is great,' stands out stylistically from the surrounding practical instruction and reads best in its immediate context of sustained, patient labor toward Zion (vv.30-34) rather than as a general promise about all small actions.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Internal Textual Juxtaposition

> [!evidence] Immediate Literary Context
> The surrounding discussion of delayed fulfillment, sustained labor, Zion, and willing obedience is strong contextual evidence that the saying about great things proceeding from small things encourages persistent participation in building Zion, rather than promising that every minor undertaking will become great.

> [!evidence] Textual Ambiguity
> Verse 36 states that 'the rebellious are not of the blood of Ephraim,' tying continued standing in Zion to lineage language; this can be read either as describing literal descent or as figurative/adoptive covenant status, and the verse itself does not disambiguate which reading is intended.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · affirmed, genuinely ambiguous in the text · [[Textual Ambiguity in D&C 64|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should verses 10-14 be understood together — what is the line between the personal duty to forgive and the church's compassionate but real accountability for unresolved sin? What did 'tithing' most plausibly mean to those who received this revelation in 1831, years before the fixed one-tenth law was given? Is 'the blood of Ephraim' in verse 36 lineage, covenant status, or both, and how would 19th-century readers most likely have understood it? What might 'terrible ones' mean in verse 43's picture of Zion? Does the rebuke of Ezra Booth alongside Isaac Morley (vv.15-16) shed real light on Booth's departure from the Church not long afterward, or is that connection only circumstantial hindsight?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
