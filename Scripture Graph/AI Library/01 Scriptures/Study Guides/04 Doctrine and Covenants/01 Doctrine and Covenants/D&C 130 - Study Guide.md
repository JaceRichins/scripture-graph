# D&C 130 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 130]] · My notes: [[D&C 130 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 130 is a compact collection of teachings rather than a single sustained argument. Its subjects include the embodied Savior, continuing sociality, [[Angels]], revelatory knowledge, the transformed earth, the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]], [[Resurrection]], [[Education and Learning]], [[Obedience]], and [[The Godhead]]. The chapter is especially valuable for its explicit distinctions: personal appearance versus metaphorical indwelling (v. 3), reported revelation versus uncertain interpretation (vv. 15–17), and embodied Father and Son versus the Holy Ghost as a personage of Spirit (vv. 22–23).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious topical outline is:

- Verses 1–3: Christ's appearance, social continuity, and interpretation of John 14:23.
- Verses 4–11: divine time, angels, God's dwelling place, the transformed earth, and Urim-and-Thummim imagery.
- Verses 12–13: prophecy concerning South Carolina and possible conflict over slavery.
- Verses 14–17: a conditional answer about seeing the Son of Man, followed by explicit interpretive uncertainty.
- Verses 18–21: enduring intelligence and the relationship of law, obedience, and blessings.
- Verses 22–23: the embodied Father and Son and the Holy Ghost as a personage of Spirit.

The abrupt transitions counsel against assuming that every adjacent statement forms one continuous argument.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
D&C 130 teaches that the Father and the Son each have 'a body of flesh and bones as tangible as man's,' while the Holy Ghost is 'a personage of Spirit' without such a body — a distinction the text ties to the Holy Ghost's capacity to dwell within a person (v.22), though the passage does not explain the underlying mechanism, and separately distinguishes a passing 'descent' of the Holy Ghost from one that 'tarries' (v.23). Blessings are said to be governed by an eternal, irrevocable law to which obedience is the condition (vv.20-21); the text affirms moral order but does not promise that every act of obedience yields an immediate or visible reward. Likewise, intelligence and knowledge gained through mortal diligence and obedience 'will rise with us in the resurrection' and carry lasting advantage (vv.18-19) — a claim about the enduring worth of learning tied to obedience, not an endorsement of formal credentials or the idea that knowledge alone secures salvation. The sanctified earth is described as becoming a Urim and Thummim revealing 'all things pertaining to an inferior kingdom' to its inhabitants (vv.8-9), while an individual white stone functions as a higher Urim and Thummim for those in the [[Kingdoms of Glory|celestial kingdom]] (vv.10-11). Whether v.22's explicit embodiment language reflects a late clarification relative to earlier, more open-ended Restoration teaching on [[The Godhead]] is a question best explored by comparison with earlier texts outside this chapter, not something this chapter alone settles.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly marks degrees of certainty. Verse 12 uses the strong formula “I prophesy,” while verse 13 qualifies the proposed cause with “may probably.” In verses 16–17, “without being able to decide” and “I believe” distinguish uncertainty and personal inference from the words reported in verse 15. These verbal signals should be preserved when interpreting the predictions; flattening them into equally categorical declarations misrepresents the text.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter reuses two images from Revelation, reworked through a Latter-day Saint cosmology: the 'sea of glass and fire' before God (v.7) echoes the sea of glass before the throne in Revelation 4:6 (compare also Revelation 15:2, which likewise joins glass and fire), and the 'white stone' bearing a hidden 'new name' (vv.10-11) is drawn directly from Revelation 2:17. Verses 8-10 also build a local pattern around the term 'Urim and Thummim': God's dwelling place is 'a great Urim and Thummim' (v.8), the sanctified earth will become one for its inhabitants (v.9), and the individual white stone becomes one for higher knowledge (v.10) — consistently associating the term with manifested or disclosed knowledge, though the text does not say whether the imagery is meant literally or symbolically. Verses 14-15 embed a short first-person revelatory formula ('Joseph, my son... thou shalt see the face of the Son of Man') within Smith's own narrated account, which he immediately follows with an admission of interpretive uncertainty (vv.16-17).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical/Predictive
> The 'eighty-five years' statement (D&C 130:14-17) is explicitly conditional ('if thou livest') and Joseph Smith himself immediately states he could not decide what kind of appearing was meant and merely 'believed' the Second Coming would not be sooner than that date; Smith died in 1844 at age 38, decades short of 85.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · text accurately preserves its own hedges · [[Historical/Predictive in D&C 130|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Predictive-Prophecy Datum *(tentative)*
> As an evidentiary datum, the prediction's combination of a named location and a possible cause is more testable than a generic forecast of future conflict. Its evidentiary weight for supernatural foreknowledge remains limited without independent analysis of provenance, recording date, contemporary political expectations, transmission, and the criteria used to identify fulfillment.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · disputed; needs provenance framing

> [!evidence] Historical/Predictive *(tentative)*
> Joseph Smith's December 1832 prophecy that sectional bloodshed 'previous to the coming of the Son of Man' would begin in South Carolina, arising 'through the slave question,' is cited by believers as anticipating the Civil War (which began at Fort Sumter, South Carolina, in 1861, over slavery and secession).
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Does 'sociality' in v.2 refer to specific relationships (family, marriage) or a more general capacity for social existence, and what changes when it is 'coupled with eternal glory'? How should the explicit uncertainty Joseph Smith expresses in vv.16-17 about the nature of the promised 'coming' — and his own labeling of his conclusion as belief rather than certainty — shape how vv.14-15 are read as prophecy? What does 'intelligence' encompass in vv.18-19, and how does it differ from mere information? In what sense can the Holy Ghost 'dwell in us' (v.22) — locally, relationally, covenantally? Does the direct, unqualified language on divine embodiment in v.22 represent a late clarification of earlier, more open-ended teaching on [[The Godhead]], and what would that imply about how revelation unfolds over a prophet's ministry? Which elements of the cosmological imagery in vv.6-11 (globe, sea of glass and fire, crystal, Urim and Thummim) are required by the text itself, and which are later extrapolation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare verses 12–13 with [[D&C 87]], while keeping two questions separate: whether later events resemble the prediction and whether the wording and date have independently demonstrable provenance. Compare verse 3 directly with John 14:23 and verses 10–11 with Revelation 2:17 to see how D&C 130 interprets biblical language. For doctrine, trace the chapter's connections to [[Angels]], [[Kingdoms of Glory]], [[Resurrection]], [[Education and Learning]], [[Obedience]], and [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]].
