# D&C 103 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 103]] · My notes: [[D&C 103 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 103 answers a practical question—how Church leaders should act for displaced brethren—with a theological interpretation and a mobilization plan. It condemns the aggressors while also attributing the Saints' chastisement to incomplete [[Obedience]]. That is the revelation's account of this crisis, not independent proof that displaced people generally cause their own suffering.

The promised redemption of [[Zion]] is concrete: return, restoration to inheritances, rebuilding, and establishment under revealed laws. Yet nearly every major promise is qualified by conduct. The plan combines [[Exodus and Deliverance]] imagery with [[Consecration]], land purchase, recruitment, leadership, and [[Prayer]]. Its cursing, vengeance, generational judgment, and willingness-to-die language also make the chapter ethically difficult and prevent a simple pacifist reading.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1-4 — Crisis interpreted:** Enemies are condemned for violence, while the Saints' incomplete obedience is given as the reason for chastisement.
2. **Verses 5-10 — Conditional vocation:** Obedience leads to prevailing; disobedience leads to defeat. Light, savior, and salt imagery defines responsibility toward the world.
3. **Verses 11-14 — Restoration and renewed risk:** Return and establishment are promised, but pollution of the inheritances would bring another overthrow.
4. **Verses 15-21 — New-exodus pattern:** Zion's redemption is compared with deliverance under Moses, and Joseph Smith is identified with the servant in the earlier vineyard parable.
5. **Verses 22-28 — Property, opposition, and mortal cost:** Consecrated funds and lawful purchases stand beside cursing, vengeance, and required willingness to die.
6. **Verses 29-35 — Recruitment under incomplete participation:** Five hundred is preferred, three hundred and one hundred are fallback goals, and one hundred is the minimum.
7. **Verses 36-40 — Means and assignments:** Victory is linked with diligence, faithfulness, and prayer; specific travel partnerships are assigned; the residue is left in divine hands.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter distinguishes an announced divine purpose from contingent human participation. Decree, promise, angels, and divine presence stand beside repeated conditions involving [[Obedience]], purity, diligence, organization, and prayer. Verse 31 makes the tension explicit: human beings do not always do God's will. The reduced recruitment targets then embody that contingency rather than merely discussing it.

The new-exodus pattern joins [[Abrahamic Covenant]], [[Gathering of Israel]], and [[Exodus and Deliverance]]. The comparison is textually explicit, but it does not license transferring every feature of ancient Israel's exodus into this nineteenth-century setting.

Calling the Saints "saviors of men" is best read within the accompanying light-and-salt imagery as an appointed vocation of [[Service]]. The wording does not present them as independent sources of salvation or as equivalents of Jesus Christ.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"By power" is central but underdefined. Its immediate associations include a stretched-out arm, angels, divine presence, organized companies, consecrated funds, land purchases, leadership, hostile resistance, and mortal danger. The context supports a composite reading more readily than either an exclusively military or exclusively inward definition.

The repeated "inasmuch as" clauses make conditionality conspicuous. They qualify prevailing and possession even while the revelation uses emphatic language of decree and promise.

The third-and-fourth-generation wording in verse 26 belongs to the covenant-judgment idiom of the [[Ten Commandments]]. Recognizing that source clarifies the register of the language but does not remove the moral difficulty of applying multigenerational judgment.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The dominant literary pattern is explicit exodus typology: bondage, Moses, Israel, Abraham, power, the stretched-out arm, angels, divine presence, and a good land. Other biblical echoes include savorless salt, finding life by laying it down, and judgment to the third and fourth generation. These correspondences establish a dense scriptural frame; they do not by themselves establish conscious literary design, divine origin, or a single direction of dependence, since familiar King James Bible diction offers an important alternative explanation.

A second pattern juxtaposes sovereignty and contingency. “I have decreed” stands beside “men do not always do my will”; an ideal company of five hundred gives way to lower contingencies; detailed human assignments conclude by leaving the residue in God's hands. The chapter presents this tension rather than resolving it.

The canonical sequence is also useful. [[D&C 101]] supplies the vineyard parable referenced in verse 21, D&C 103 gives mobilizing instructions, and [[D&C 105]] later addresses postponement or nonfulfillment. This sequence supports reading D&C 103 as preparation rather than as a narrative of completed redemption, while leaving believing and skeptical explanations of the later outcome open.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Which elements surrounding "by power" support divine, institutional, economic, coercive, or military senses, and which readings exclude too much of the context?
2. How should "no more to be thrown down" in verse 13 be read beside the renewed-loss condition in verse 14?
3. Does the move from five hundred to three hundred to one hundred depict divine accommodation, ordinary contingency planning, a test of collective willingness, or some combination?
4. How do commanded land purchases constrain a purely military reading, and how do verses 24-28 constrain a purely pacifist reading?
5. What safeguards are needed to prevent the section's explanation of one community's chastisement from becoming a general doctrine that victims caused their suffering?
6. What ethical authority, if any, should modern readers assign to the cursing and multigenerational vengeance language?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[D&C 101]] for the vineyard parable and earlier land-purchase program, then [[D&C 105]] for the later postponement and interpretation of Zion's redemption. This sequence is essential before making claims about fulfillment or failure.

Compare D&C 103:17 with Exodus 6:6; D&C 103:10 with Matthew 5:13 and 3 Nephi 12:13; D&C 103:26 with Exodus 20:5 and Deuteronomy 5:9; and D&C 103:27 with Matthew 10:39. These comparisons establish meaningful literary relationships but do not independently establish inspiration, historicity, or ethical application.
