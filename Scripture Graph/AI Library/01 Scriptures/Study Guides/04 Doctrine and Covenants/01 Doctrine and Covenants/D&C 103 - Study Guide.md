# D&C 103 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 103]] · My notes: [[D&C 103 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 103 addresses how the Saints should act concerning brethren driven from Zion. It condemns the aggressors' iniquity while also presenting the Saints' chastisement as a consequence of incomplete [[Obedience]]. That is the revelation's theological account of the crisis, not an independent historical demonstration that suffering generally results from disobedience.

The chapter promises restoration while making prevailing and continued possession conditional. It frames Zion's redemption through [[Exodus and Deliverance]] imagery, then joins that imagery to practical measures: [[Consecration]], land purchase, recruitment, leadership, revealed laws, and [[Prayer]]. Verses 24–28 also retain severe cursing, vengeance, multigenerational judgment, and willingness-to-die language; these features prevent an uncomplicated pacifist reading and remain ethically difficult. The chapter commands preparation but does not narrate the expedition's outcome.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1–4 — Crisis and chastisement:** The scattered Saints' enemies are condemned, while the Saints' failure to hearken fully is also named.
2. **Verses 5–10 — Conditional vocation:** Obedience leads to prevailing; disobedience leads to defeat. Light and salt imagery defines a responsibility to serve others.
3. **Verses 11–14 — Restoration with a proviso:** Return and establishment in [[Zion]] are promised, but pollution of the inheritance would bring renewed loss.
4. **Verses 15–21 — New-exodus interpretation:** Redemption is compared to Israel's deliverance under Moses, and Joseph Smith is connected with the servant in the earlier vineyard parable.
5. **Verses 22–28 — Land, opposition, and costly discipleship:** The churches are to fund land purchases; renewed expulsion would bring judgment; participants must accept mortal risk.
6. **Verses 29–35 — Recruitment with contingency:** Five hundred is preferred, three hundred and one hundred are fallback goals, and one hundred is the minimum.
7. **Verses 36–40 — Means and assignments:** Diligence, faithfulness, and prayer are emphasized, travel partnerships are assigned, and the unresolved remainder is entrusted to God.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's covenant logic resists an unconditional reading of [[Zion]]. Divine decree and promise stand beside repeated conditions concerning obedience, purity, diligence, and prayer. The text can therefore be read as distinguishing an announced divine purpose from the contingent participation and conduct of human agents.

Its new-exodus pattern links [[Abrahamic Covenant]], [[Gathering of Israel]], and [[Exodus and Deliverance]]. That typology is explicit as a literary and theological pattern, but it does not by itself prove that every feature of ancient Israel's exodus should be transferred to this nineteenth-century setting.

Verses 31–36 also hold [[Agency]] and divine aid together. Human beings may fail to do God's will, plans accommodate incomplete response, and victory requires human effort; yet prayer, promised presence, and leaving the residue in God's hands prevent the account from becoming one of unaided self-reliance.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“By power” is a key but underdefined expression. Its immediate setting includes the stretched-out arm, angels, and divine presence, but also an organized company, consecrated funds, land purchases, leadership, and the possibility of mortal danger. The phrase should therefore not be reduced confidently either to military force alone or to an entirely inward spiritual power.

“Saviors of men” likewise requires restraint. In context it describes the Saints' appointed service to the world and is paired with light and salt imagery. The chapter does not define the phrase as making them independent sources of salvation or equivalent to Jesus Christ.

The repeated “inasmuch as” clauses make conditionality conspicuous. They qualify prevailing and secure inheritance even while the text uses strong language of promise and decree.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The dominant literary pattern is exodus typology. Compare the earlier Zion material and vineyard parable in [[D&C 101]], then the later reassessment of the expedition in [[D&C 105]]. Read together, these chapters can distinguish the commanding rhetoric of preparation from the historical and theological interpretation of what followed.

A secondary pattern moves between divine sovereignty and human contingency: “I have decreed” stands near “men do not always do my will,” while the closing appeal to leave the residue in God's hands follows detailed assignments. The juxtaposition produces tension rather than a simple formula.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Does “the redemption of Zion must needs come by power” refer primarily to divine intervention, institutional authority, organized collective action, physical force, or some combination? Which details in verses 15–23 constrain the answer?
2. How should the promise of establishment in verse 13 be read alongside the renewed-loss warning in verse 14?
3. What does the progression from five hundred to three hundred to one hundred reveal about the relationship between divine commands and incomplete human participation?
4. How do land purchase in verses 22–23 and avenging language in verses 24–26 qualify competing purely peaceful or purely military readings?
5. How can the chapter's explanation of chastisement be studied without turning it into a general claim that victims of displacement caused their own suffering?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[D&C 101]] for the earlier tribulation promise and vineyard parable to which this chapter appears to refer. Then compare [[D&C 105]] for the later instructions associated with the attempted redemption of Zion. This sequence is necessary before drawing conclusions about fulfillment, failure, or the meaning of “redemption by power.”

For the chapter's imagery, compare Exodus 6:6 with D&C 103:17 and Matthew 5:13 with D&C 103:10. These comparisons establish literary relationships, not conclusions about authorship or historicity.
