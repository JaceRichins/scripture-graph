## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
D&C 2 directly links four elements: Elijah, [[Priesthood Restoration]], the planting of intergenerational promises, and the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]]. It does not itself mention temples, genealogy, sealing keys, or [[Baptism for the Dead]] — those connections are made explicit only in later revelations. [[D&C 110]] presents Elijah's 1836 appearance in the Kirtland Temple as committing 'the keys of this dispensation' and fulfilling this prophecy. [[D&C 128]] quotes the fathers-and-children promise and calls it a 'welding link,' applying it to baptism for the dead. [[D&C 138]] similarly ties Elijah's mission to redemption of the dead and temple work. These later texts are canonical development and interpretation, not restatements of content already explicit in D&C 2.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
D&C 2:2 does not merely reword [[Malachi 4]] 4:6 — it drops the first half of Malachi's couplet (the fathers' hearts turning to the children) and keeps only the second half (the children's hearts turning to the fathers), replacing the missing half with the image of Elijah 'planting' the fathers' promises in the children. This is a real wording change, not just paraphrase, though whether it reflects revealed correction, retrospective memory (the history was recorded roughly fifteen years after the reported 1823 event), or deliberate compression is disputed and not settled by the text alone. Both 'Priesthood' and 'the promises made to the fathers' are left undefined in the chapter itself.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The three verses move from divine action (v.1), to inward change (v.2), to cosmic consequence (v.3), ending on an unusually stark warning — 'the whole earth would be utterly wasted' — for such a short passage. 'Plant' is a generative metaphor: promises are placed in hearts with an expectation of a later response, though the chapter doesn't say how quickly or uniformly that response occurs. Related language recurs, with variation, in [[Malachi 4]], [[3 Nephi 25]], [[Joseph Smith—History 1]], [[D&C 110]], [[D&C 128]], and [[D&C 138]] — though these are best described as related reuses and developments of the same motif rather than one unbroken refrain, since several rework rather than repeat the wording.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Who are 'the fathers' in this immediate wording — Israel's patriarchs, one's own ancestors generally, or both?
- What promises are intended, and where does the wider canon go on to identify them?
- What does 'utterly wasted' mean concretely — literal destruction, or a covenantal/spiritual desolation?
- Why does D&C 2:2 depart from the KJV wording of Malachi 4:6 — revealed clarification, retrospective memory variation (the history was written about fifteen years after the reported 1823 event), or deliberate compression? The text alone doesn't decide this.
- Which conclusions come from D&C 2 alone, and which depend on later texts like [[D&C 110]], [[D&C 128]], or [[D&C 138]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
