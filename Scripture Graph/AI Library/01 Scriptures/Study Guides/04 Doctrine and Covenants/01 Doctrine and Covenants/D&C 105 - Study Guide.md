# D&C 105 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 105]] · My notes: [[D&C 105 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
[[D&C 105]] explains why Zion's immediate redemption is deferred and what the text says must precede it. Its condemnation is corporate but qualified: disobedience, neglect of the poor, and disunity are attributed to the church, while the text states the first elders "are not all under this condemnation" (v.7) — a partial, not blanket, exemption. The resulting program includes instruction, experience, [[Obedience]], [[Consecration]], [[Humility]], sanctification, temple endowment, lawful land purchase, legal redress, and peace. The promised endowment is conditioned on faithfulness and humility (v.12), and the difficult march is described as "a trial of their faith" (v.19) — a theological framing that need not rule out the expedition also having fallen short of its immediate objective. These are the chapter's stated explanations and directives; the chapter alone does not establish that communal failings were the only historical cause of the delay.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is: (1) diagnosis of the delay and the requirements of Zion (vv. 1–10); (2) promised endowment and evaluation of the assembled company (vv. 11–19); (3) interim instructions concerning residence, discretion, gathering, and legal redress (vv. 20–27); (4) land purchase and conditional future possession (vv. 28–32); and (5) Kirtland endowment, choosing and sanctification, followed by an emphatic peace commission (vv. 33–41). The near-identical clause "wait for a little season...for the redemption of Zion" brackets verses 9 and 13, straddling the first two sections and framing the intervening explanation of endowment and preparation (vv. 10–12). This outline describes the progression without claiming a formal literary pattern.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter links [[Zion]] with [[Consecration]], [[Obedience]], unity, care for the poor and afflicted, and sanctification. Geographic possession remains important, but land, gathering, and organized strength are presented as insufficient without the required communal practices. Chastening and delay are described here as means of learning and preparation, without implying that every participant is individually condemned or that all suffering has the same explanation. Promised power is associated with humility, worthiness, following counsel, and the endowment to be received in the Kirtland house, connecting [[Temples]] with preparation for Zion. Verses 35–36 further distinguish a past "day of calling" from a coming "day of choosing," in which specific individuals are to be identified by revelation and sanctified; later Latter-day Saint tradition has associated this with the 1835 organization of the Quorum of the Twelve and the Seventy, though the text itself names no such body and this remains a retrospective inference.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter links a cluster of expressions concerning waiting, power, blessing, and endowment: the elders must "wait for a little season," be "endowed with power from on high," receive a promised "great endowment," and later receive their "endowment from on high" in the Kirtland house. These are related expressions, not one phrase repeated verbatim throughout. The text also juxtaposes martial terms—army, warriors, towers, watchmen, vengeance, and banners—with civic and conciliatory terms—law, justice, redress, favor, and peace. That contrast should not be reduced either to uncomplicated militarism or to absolute pacifism. Verse 30 closes with "unto the third and fourth generation of them that hate me," a near-verbatim reuse of the Decalogue's covenant-curse formula (compare [[Exodus 20]]; [[Deuteronomy 5]]), here redirected from covenant-breaking Israelites toward external enemies of Zion. Verse 31's "fair as the sun, and clear as the moon...terrible unto all nations" echoes, with sun and moon transposed, the bridal-military imagery of [[Song of Solomon 6]]; whether this reflects deliberate literary allusion or a shared conventional idiom is not something the text itself settles.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The most important tension is between martial and peaceful language. Verses 30–32 imagine an enlarged, sanctified army and forceful possession, whereas verses 25 and 38–40 prescribe legal redress, an ensign of peace, and proposals for peace. The sequence matters: purchase, growth, and sanctification precede the future action, while peace is commanded in the immediate setting; this clarifies but does not fully resolve the tension, since verse 26's "until" allows the interim peace policy to be read as strategic as well as principled. A second, subtler feature appears in verse 27, where the Lord promises to "soften the hearts of the people, as I did the heart of Pharaoh" — an inversion of the more familiar Exodus motif of a hardened heart. Readers might connect this to Pharaoh's temporary relentings during the plague narrative, but the text does not specify an episode, so the allusion's precise referent is uncertain. Verse 31 also personifies the army's standards as feminine ("her banners"), echoing Song of Solomon's bridal imagery, though whether this reflects deliberate bridal personification of Zion or simply inherited wording is not established by the verbal parallel alone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should the corporate condemnation in verses 2–6 be distinguished from individual accountability, especially given verse 7's "not all under this condemnation"?
- What do care for the poor, unity, and celestial law contribute to the chapter's understanding of [[Zion]]?
- Which martial statements describe conditional future action, and which instructions govern the immediate setting?
- Does the command to seek peace function as a temporary strategy, a durable ethic, or both?
- How do the Kirtland endowment and the movement from calling to choosing reshape the chapter's interpretation of the expedition's outcome?
- Does chronological sequencing resolve the tension between peace and coercion, or merely postpone it?
- What episode, if any, does verse 27's image of a "softened" Pharaoh have in view, given that Exodus more often depicts a hardened heart?
- Is the Song of Solomon-like imagery in verse 31 a deliberate allusion, or a coincidence of shared scriptural idiom?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[D&C 103]] alongside this chapter to trace the movement from mobilization to waiting, and [[D&C 104]] for the economic obligations surrounding [[Consecration]]. Compare D&C 105:30 with [[Exodus 20]] and [[Deuteronomy 5]] to examine how the third-and-fourth-generation formula is reused, and compare D&C 105:31 with [[Song of Solomon 6]] to weigh whether the shared "fair...clear...terrible...banners" imagery reflects deliberate allusion. Study D&C 105:11–12 and 33 together when considering the connection between promised power and the Kirtland house. The "day of choosing" language of verses 35–36 is worth studying alongside the traditional account of the February 1835 organization of the Twelve Apostles and Seventy, while keeping in mind that this connection is a later inference rather than a claim the text itself makes.
