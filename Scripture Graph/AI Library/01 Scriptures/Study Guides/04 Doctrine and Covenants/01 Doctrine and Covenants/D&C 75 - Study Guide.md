# D&C 75 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 75]] · My notes: [[D&C 75 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 75 records the Lord speaking as "Alpha and Omega, your Lord and your God" (v. 1) to elders who had "given their names" for [[Missionary Work]]. The chapter falls into three parts, each closed with "Even so. Amen." (vv. 12, 22, 36). The first addresses William E. McLellin: an earlier commission is revoked, he is chastened for "the murmurings of his heart" and called a sinner, then immediately forgiven and sent with Luke Johnson to the south countries (vv. 6-12). The second sends further named companionships east, west, and south, and instructs them on itinerant preaching and on how to respond to households that receive or reject them, including sober judgment language (vv. 13-22). The third turns to the elders of the church generally: the church has a duty to help support missionary [[Family|families]], a man still obligated to provide for his own family will not lose his reward for doing so, and idleness has no place in the church except the idler repents — followed by a further list of seven ministry companionships without stated destinations (vv. 23-36). In all, at least twenty-four men are named as missionary companions in this single chapter.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **Verses 1-5**: General charge to all who have given their names for missionary service — go without delay, proclaim boldly, expect reward for faithfulness.
- **Verses 6-12**: William E. McLellin's commission is revoked; he is chastened and forgiven, and reassigned with Luke Johnson to the south countries.
- **Verses 13-22**: Further named pairs (Orson Hyde/Samuel H. Smith; Lyman Johnson/Orson Pratt; Asa Dodds/Calves Wilson; Major N. Ashley/Burr Riggs) sent east, west, and south, with shared instructions on itinerant proclamation and on responding to reception or rejection.
- **Verses 23-29**: General instruction to the elders on family support — a church duty, a personal duty for those still providing at home, and a warning against idleness.
- **Verses 30-36**: A concluding list of seven additional companionships "united in the ministry," without destinations specified.

The three "Even so. Amen." markers (vv. 12, 22, 36) are the clearest textual signal of structure; they show three literary units but do not by themselves establish that these were originally separate revelations later compiled together.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Chastening, forgiveness, and renewed service** (vv. 6-9): McLellin's fault is named plainly ("the murmurings of his heart") and called sin, and forgiveness is immediate and paired with a new, changed assignment (east becomes south). The text reports the Lord's forgiveness and McLellin's restoration to duty; it does not narrate a confession or repentance process on McLellin's part.
- **Conditional promises of divine presence** (vv. 11, 13, 16, 22): Several — though not all — of the promises that the Lord will be with the missionaries "even unto the end" are explicitly tied to faithfulness, prayer, or "fainting not."
- **Church and personal responsibility for [[Family|family]] support held together** (vv. 24-29): The church has a duty to help support missionary families where members are willing; a man still obligated to provide for his own family may do so without forfeiting his "crown"; but "the idler shall not have place in the church, except he repent." The chapter states these as duties without specifying an administrative mechanism.
- **Guidance through the Comforter** (vv. 10, 27): Missionaries are told to call on the Lord for the Comforter's teaching, and later to "ask" and "knock" for guidance "made known... by the Comforter." The antecedent of "whither they shall go" in verse 27 is ambiguous — it may refer to where the missionaries themselves should go rather than exclusively to family placement — so this verse is best read as guidance for missionary service generally.
- **Testimony and judgment for rejection** (vv. 20-22): Rejecting the messengers carries courtroom-toned consequence ("judges," "condemn," comparison to "the heathen"), though the chapter does not explain the mechanics of that judgment.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The instructions in verses 18-20 and 22 — going house to house, blessing receptive homes, departing and "shak[ing] off the dust of your feet as a testimony" against unreceptive ones, and the comparative "more tolerable... in the day of judgment" — closely track the missionary-charge pattern of [[Matthew 10]] (a verified textual overlap) and echo the same comparative idiom found in [[Matthew 11]]. Verse 21's added statement that the elders will be "judges of that house" and "condemn" it goes beyond that specific Matthew 10 pattern and may reflect a related but distinct New Testament judgment motif. Verse 27's "ask... receive... knock... opened" echoes the familiar request-and-answer idiom of the Sermon on the Mount, and the Comforter's teaching role in verse 10 recalls similar New Testament language about the Spirit as teacher — though neither specific comparison is confirmed by mechanical text-overlap for this chapter, only by recognizable shared KJV idiom. These parallels show D&C 75 drawing on familiar biblical missionary and revelatory language; on their own they do not establish independence from the King James Bible or settle questions of authorship or antiquity.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is built from short, formulaic units — "And again, verily I say unto my servant..." — repeated with minor variation across more than a dozen individual or paired callings, giving the text a list-like, roll-call cadence appropriate to a record of assignments. Three larger sections are marked off by the closing "Even so. Amen." (vv. 12, 22, 36); this shows a clear three-part literary structure, though it does not by itself prove the units were originally separate revelations later compiled together. Legal and courtroom imagery clusters in verses 20-22 (testimony, judging, condemning, "more tolerable... than"), heightening the stakes of ordinary hospitality. The refrain "even unto the end" (vv. 11, 13, 14) is explicitly conditioned on faithfulness in some occurrences (v. 13) but not repeated with an explicit condition in every instance (v. 14), so it is best read as generally, not uniformly, conditional.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What is the significance of the Lord naming McLellin's specific fault ("the murmurings of his heart") rather than treating the revocation as purely administrative?
- What scope should be given to the statement that the elders will be "judges of that house, and condemn them" (v. 21)? The immediate context concerns testimony against rejection, but the chapter does not explain the mechanics or limits of that judgment.
- Does "the heathen" in verse 22 identify a specific population, or function rhetorically as a comparison for those with less opportunity to receive the message?
- How does the chapter's balance of church-level and personal responsibility for family support (vv. 24-29) relate to later Church teaching on welfare and self-reliance, given that no administrative procedure is specified here?
- Whose direction is being sought in verse 27 — the missionaries' own destination, the placement of their families, or both?
