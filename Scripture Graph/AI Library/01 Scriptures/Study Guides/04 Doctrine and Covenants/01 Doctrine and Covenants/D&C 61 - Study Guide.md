# D&C 61 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 61]] · My notes: [[D&C 61 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 61 combines immediate travel directions with broader instruction about [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], [[Missionary Work]], discernment, and preparation for the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]]. Its movement is pastoral as well as corrective: the elders are forgiven and chastened, divided into assignments, warned of danger, permitted limited judgment in choosing routes, and finally assured that the Lord has not forsaken them.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful outline is: (1) divine identity, forgiveness, and chastening (vv. 1-8); (2) division of company and practical arrangements (vv. 9-12); (3) teaching and warnings concerning land and waters (vv. 13-20); (4) differentiated routes and Spirit-directed exceptions (vv. 21-29); (5) preaching assignments (vv. 30-35); and (6) reassurance and eschatological vigilance (vv. 36-39). The repeated formula “what I say unto one I say unto all” helps mark the two broadly applicable portions: warning in verse 18 and reassurance in verse 36.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The elders are told that their sins are forgiven, and the Lord associates mercy with confession offered in [[Humility]] (v. 2); the grammar supports this association without stating a formal condition. Chastening is directed toward unity and deliverance from continuing in wickedness (v. 8), followed by withdrawn anger and reassurance (vv. 20, 36-37).

The travel instructions combine direct commands with limited, person-specific discretion. Gilbert and Phelps may later proceed by land or water according to judgment (vv. 21-22), while another person empowered to command the waters is to act as directed by the Spirit (vv. 27-28). These passages can illuminate [[Revelation]] and [[Agency]], but they should not be turned into an unrestricted general rule.

The conclusion joins watchfulness, sobriety, and [[Prayer]] with readiness for the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]] (vv. 38-39).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The waters and land are arranged in a striking reversal: waters blessed in the beginning are described as cursed in the last days, while land cursed in the beginning is blessed in the last days for the Saints (vv. 14, 17). The journey is then explicitly cast in the pattern of [[Exodus and Deliverance]], with the Saints pitching tents like the children of Israel (v. 25). The chapter also balances danger with reassurance: warnings about destruction stand beside promises of preservation, divine presence, and blessings for the humble.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Textual Qualification
> The chapter’s internal qualifications strongly support reading its water instructions as differentiated rather than as an unqualified prohibition on every journey by water: faithful persons receive a promise of preservation, some missionaries may later choose land or water, and a person empowered to command the waters is to follow the Spirit.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Qualifications explicit; scope remains disputed · [[Internal Textual Qualification in D&C 61|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Scriptural Allusion *(tentative)*
> The statement that the waters were cursed through “my servant John” plausibly alludes to the water judgments in Revelation, especially Revelation 8 and 16. The allusion is probable, but D&C 61 does not identify one exact passage.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · Probable Johannine background; exact referent disputed

> [!evidence] Literary/Thematic Parallel *(tentative)*
> The blessed/cursed reversal in D&C 61:14,17 plausibly draws rhetorical force from Genesis creation language (God blessing the waters and living things, and cursing the ground after the Fall), repurposed to authorize new latter-day travel restrictions and to frame Zion as a now-blessed land.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · Plausible but unconfirmed Genesis resonance
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should the broad, future-oriented warnings in verses 15-19 be read alongside the narrow permissions and exceptions in verses 22-28?
- Does “my servant John” in verse 14 refer chiefly to a particular water judgment in [[Revelation 8]] or [[Revelation 16]], or to Johannine judgment imagery more generally? The chapter does not identify an exact passage.
- Who or what is “the destroyer” in verse 19? The text does not specify.
- What conduct lies behind the statement that the Lord was angry “yesterday” (v. 20)? The chapter mentions chastening but does not identify a discrete incident.
- What was the unstated “wise purpose” for keeping Reynolds Cahoon and Samuel H. Smith together (v. 35)?
- How does concern for inhabitants along the route affect the relationship between efficient travel and [[Missionary Work]] (v. 3)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare the water judgments attributed to John in [[Revelation 8]] and [[Revelation 16]] with D&C 61:14-19, noting both the thematic similarities and the absence of an explicit passage citation.
- Compare D&C 61:38-39 with [[Joseph Smith—Matthew 1]], especially its commands to watch and its warning that the Son of Man comes unexpectedly.
- Read [[D&C 60]] alongside this chapter to trace how the neighboring revelations coordinate travel with [[Missionary Work]].
- Examine the children-of-Israel simile in D&C 61:24-26 as a limited journey comparison, without assuming that every feature of the biblical Exodus has a latter-day counterpart.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
