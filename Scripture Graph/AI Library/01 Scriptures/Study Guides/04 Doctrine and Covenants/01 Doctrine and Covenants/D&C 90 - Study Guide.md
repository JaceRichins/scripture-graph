# D&C 90 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 90]] · My notes: [[D&C 90 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 90 was given to Joseph Smith in March 1833 and combines personal reassurance with practical church administration. It opens by declaring Joseph's sins forgiven "according to thy petition" (v.1) and confirms that he bears "the keys of the kingdom," which "shall never be taken from you, while thou art in the world, neither in the world to come" (vv.2-3). His counselors, Sidney Rigdon and Frederick G. Williams, receive the same forgiveness and are "accounted as equal" with him "in holding the keys of this last kingdom" (v.6) — the chapter holds this shared keyholding together with Joseph's individual, non-transferable promise without fully explaining how the two relate. The revelation ties this authority to the newly commanded School of the Prophets (v.7) and a sweeping missionary vision: the gospel goes "unto the Gentiles first," then "they shall turn unto the Jews," until "every man shall hear the fulness of the gospel in his own tongue" (vv.9-11). Receiving these "oracles of God" is framed as a stewardship rather than a possession — those who treat them as "a light thing" risk stumbling "when the storms descend" (vv.4-5). The chapter then turns to practical matters: household and financial order for the Kirtland leaders, including a warning against "high-mindedness and pride" (v.17) alongside directions for debt, housing, and the bishop's storehouse (vv.12-27); an individual commandment for Vienna Jaques to gather to Zion and receive an inheritance (vv.28-31); and a closing message to the Saints in Zion, Missouri, acknowledging that they "begin to repent" while naming specific individuals — William E. McLellin and Sidney Gilbert — who still have "many things to repent of," and promising that the Lord will "contend with Zion" and "chasten her until she overcomes," yet "she shall not be removed out of her place" (vv.32-37).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- vv.1-6: Forgiveness pronounced on Joseph Smith, then on Sidney Rigdon and Frederick G. Williams; keys of the kingdom confirmed, with Joseph alone promised keys that "shall never be taken" from him
- vv.7-11: The School of the Prophets and a worldwide missionary vision — Gentiles first, then the Jews, until all hear the gospel "in his own tongue"
- vv.12-18: Charge to preside, receive revelation, study and learn, and beware of pride
- vv.19-27: Household, housing, and financial order for the Kirtland leaders, including the bishop's search for an agent to discharge debts
- vv.28-31: Individual commandment to Vienna Jaques to gather to Zion
- vv.32-37: Message to the Saints in Zion — partial repentance, named rebukes (McLellin, Gilbert), and a promise that the Lord will chasten Zion "until she overcomes," paired with assurance that she will not be removed from her place

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter treats [[Priesthood]] keys and [[Revelation]] as inseparable from accountability: those who "receive the oracles of God" risk "condemnation" if they hold them lightly (v.5), and even the newly confirmed leaders are warned against "high-mindedness and [[Pride]]" in the same breath as their authority is confirmed (v.17). Rigdon and Williams are "accounted as equal" with Joseph Smith "in holding the keys" (v.6), yet verse 3 promises Joseph personally that his keys will never be taken from him — the text holds shared keyholding and an individual, non-transferable assurance together without fully explaining how they relate; reading this as a settled hierarchy or complete succession theory goes beyond what these verses state. [[Consecration]] appears in miniature in the commandment to Vienna Jaques, whose temporal resources are folded into both personal support and communal offering (vv.28-30). The closing promise that [[Zion]] "shall not be removed out of her place" (v.37) follows, rather than cancels, a threat of chastening (v.36) — and within months the Missouri Saints were in fact driven from Jackson County. Later revelations (D&C 101, 103, 105) attribute that loss to the Saints' own failures and treat Zion's redemption as deferred rather than void; D&C 90 itself does not resolve how its promise relates to that later history, and this study guide holds the assurance and the outcome in tension rather than presenting either as simply fulfilled or broken.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The word **administration** links several movements in the chapter: leaders administer the school and the word, other ministers carry that word outward, and the Comforter administers revelation (vv. 7-11). The repeated vocabulary makes ministry appear mediated and relational rather than merely individual. In verse 24, the word **if** is interpretively important: the assurance that all things work together for good is not stated here as an unconditional promise of favorable circumstances.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 5's storm-and-house warning — "the winds blow, and the rains descend, and beat upon their house" — closely parallels the parable of the wise and foolish builders in [[Matthew 7]]:24-27 ("the rain descended, and the floods came, and the winds blew, and beat upon that house"), applying the familiar image to responsible reception of revelation rather than to hearing Christ's words generally. Verse 1's "thy sins are forgiven thee" echoes the same formula Jesus uses in [[Mark 2]]:5; the shared phrase is a genuine lexical parallel, though Mark's narrative goes on to demonstrate authority to forgive through healing, not a subsequent grant of governing keys, so the two passages should not be read as a matched sequence. "The keys of the kingdom" (vv.2-3) reuses the distinctive phrase from [[Matthew 16]]:19. The promise that "every man shall hear the fulness of the gospel in his own tongue" (v.11) resonates with Pentecost in [[Acts 2]]:6 ("every man heard them speak in his own language"), though D&C 90 frames this as a future, ongoing missionary condition rather than a single miraculous day. Verse 24's assurance that "all things shall work together for your good" echoes [[Romans 8]]:28, with D&C 90 adding an explicit covenantal condition absent from the Pauline verse. These parallels demonstrate reuse of familiar biblical diction and imagery; by themselves they do not establish literary dependence beyond ordinary King James familiarity, nor do they decide questions of revelatory origin.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should Rigdon and Williams being "accounted as equal" in holding the keys (v.6) be reconciled with the promise, addressed to Joseph individually, that his keys "shall never be taken" from him (v.3)?
- Why does the revelation pair confirmation of authority (vv.1-9) so closely with warnings against pride and household disorder (vv.17-18)?
- What does "oracles" mean in verses 4-5 — revelations, authorized divine communication, scripture, or some broader category — and who or what is "another" in verse 4?
- Does verse 11 describe miraculous speech, learned language ability, translation, or simply the eventual fruit of inspired missionary labor?
- Why single out Vienna Jaques by name (vv.28-31) within a section otherwise devoted to the presiding brethren's households, and what does her case show about how individual consecration was handled?
- Given that the Missouri Saints were driven from Jackson County within months of this revelation, how should the promise that Zion "shall not be removed out of her place" (v.37) be understood — geographically, covenantally, or as an assurance later revelations treat as deferred rather than broken?
