# D&C 41 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 41]] · My notes: [[D&C 41 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 41 prepares a community to receive divine law. Its movement is practical and theological: the elders must assemble and pray; the law will teach them how to govern; discipleship will be measured by obedience; and specific arrangements are made for Joseph Smith, Sidney Rigdon, and Edward Partridge. The conclusion places the entire revelation under the horizon of [[Final Judgment]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful reading divides the chapter into four movements:

1. **Hear and assemble (vv. 1–2):** those who hear are contrasted with empty profession, and the elders are summoned to agree upon the Lord's word.
2. **Receive and do the law (vv. 3–6):** faithful prayer is associated with receiving law; the law orders the church; and doing it distinguishes discipleship from profession.
3. **Put the community in order (vv. 7–11):** three directives introduced by “And again” address Joseph Smith, Sidney Rigdon, and Edward Partridge. Only verses 7–8 use the fuller formula “it is meet that”; verse 9 directly calls Partridge to be appointed by the church's voice and ordained a bishop.
4. **Guard the words (v. 12):** the recipients are made accountable for how they hold the revelation.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects [[Revelation]], [[Prayer]], [[Obedience]], and [[Government and Laws]]. Revelation is sought communally through “the prayer of your faith” (v. 3), but receiving it is incomplete without doing it (v. 5). Christ remains the ultimate ruler (v. 4), while an ordained bishop works within assigned laws (vv. 9–10). Edward Partridge's appointment “by the voice of the church” also provides an important textual basis for [[Sustaining Church Leaders]]: divine calling and community participation appear together rather than as rivals.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter's controlling verb is **hear**: “Hearken and hear” opens the revelation, followed by a contrast between those who hear and those who do not (v. 1). Verse 5 sharpens that contrast through paired verbs, **receive** and **do**. Thus hearing develops into reception, and reception must become action. The repeated adjective **pure** also joins Partridge's heart (v. 11) to the words of the revelation (v. 12).

The dogs, pearls, and swine of verse 6 closely parallel Matthew 7:6. Here the saying is attached by “For” to the preceding rule about excluding a professed recipient who refuses to do the law. The imagery is severe; the immediate context concerns protection of “the things which belong to the children of the kingdom,” but the passage does not specify every circumstance in which exclusion is warranted.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The final text permits a coherent progression: hearing leads to assembly and prayer; prayer is associated with receiving law; receiving must become doing; and the administrative instructions put governance into concrete form before the closing warning of judgment. This is a useful literary reading, though shared vocabulary, practical continuity, or editorial arrangement could also contribute to the effect.

Two biblical connections are especially useful. D&C 41:6 securely echoes Matthew 7:6 through its distinctive dogs, pearls, and swine imagery. D&C 41:11 more explicitly invokes Nathanael and the description “in whom there is no guile” from John 1:47. These connections establish biblical reuse or allusion but do not, by themselves, determine the mechanism of composition or authorship.

Verses 3 and 10 also anticipate law still to be received, making [[D&C 42]] an important canonical continuation. The sequence supports textual and institutional continuity but does not establish prophetic foresight, divine origin, or a particular composition history.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does “agree upon my word” in verse 2 require: unanimity, shared commitment, or something else?
- How does verse 5 distinguish a verbal profession of discipleship from enacted discipleship?
- What safeguards should govern the severe exclusion language of verses 5–6?
- Why are divine calling, appointment by the church's voice, and ordination all named in Edward Partridge's case?
- What does verse 12 imply about the responsibilities of people who claim to receive sacred words?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[D&C 42]] after this chapter and track how its laws answer the expectations created in D&C 41:3 and 10. Compare D&C 41:5 with James 1:22, D&C 41:6 with Matthew 7:6, and D&C 41:11 with John 1:47. These comparisons illuminate the chapter's use of biblical language without, by themselves, resolving questions of composition or authorship.
