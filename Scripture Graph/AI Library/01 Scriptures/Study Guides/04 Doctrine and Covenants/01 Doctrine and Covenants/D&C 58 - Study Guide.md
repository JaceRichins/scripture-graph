# D&C 58 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 58]] · My notes: [[D&C 58 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 58 combines promise with administration. It begins by interpreting present tribulation in light of future glory, then moves into concrete directions for establishing [[Zion]]: leadership, law, land, money, labor, gathering, discipline, and preaching. The chapter repeatedly joins divine direction with accountable human action. God commands and promises, while disciples must deliberate, act voluntarily, repent, purchase land, organize their work, and bear [[Testimony]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. Verses 1–5: tribulation, limited present sight, and promised glory.
2. Verses 6–13: purposes for coming to the land and imagery of a universal divine feast.
3. Verses 14–25: Edward Partridge's mission, judgment, law, and residence.
4. Verses 26–33: initiative, [[Agency]], obedience, and forfeited blessings.
5. Verses 34–43: money, inheritances, named correction, and [[Repentance and Forgiveness]].
6. Verses 44–58: preaching, fundraising, land purchase, labor, orderly gathering, and dedication.
7. Verses 59–65: testimony during the return journey, discipline, conference, worldwide proclamation, and the coming of the Son of Man.

This outline describes shifts in subject matter; it does not demonstrate an elaborate formal literary pattern.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's teaching on [[Agency]] is bounded rather than autonomous: disciples should do good without waiting for detailed commands, yet remain accountable to commandments (verses 26–30). Its teaching on repentance emphasizes both divine forgiveness and observable change—confession and forsaking (verses 42–43). Those verses offer a concise test for recognizing repentance in this discourse, but the text does not say they exhaust every pastoral, emotional, or restorative dimension of repentance.

The land instructions also connect [[Consecration]] with practical administration. Money, property, a storehouse, printing, skilled labor, and counsel are treated as parts of building [[Zion]], not as incidental secular matters.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The feast language in verse 8 closely echoes Isaiah 25:6, while verses 9–11 combine a universal supper, marginalized guests, and the marriage of the Lamb. These images resonate with biblical banquet passages, but their precise relationship should be described as intertextual reuse or adaptation unless a stronger historical or theological argument is supplied.

In verses 31–33, unfulfilled promises are explained through covenantal conditionality: disobedience precedes revocation of the blessing. This explanation applies to the scenario articulated in the passage; it should not be used automatically to blame sufferers whenever an expected blessing does not occur.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
D&C 58:8 closely echoes Isaiah 25:6 KJV ("a feast of fat things...of wine on the lees well refined"), applying that prophetic banquet image to the promised supper for Zion's poor (vv. 8-9). The "marriage of the Lamb" and "supper of the Lord" language of v. 11 likewise closely parallels Revelation 19:9. The guest sequence in vv. 10-11 (rich, learned, wise, and noble first; poor, lame, blind, and deaf afterward) resembles the reversal in Jesus' parable of the great supper ([[Parables of Jesus]], cf. Luke 14:15-24), though D&C 58 does not narrate any refusal by the first-invited group as Luke does, so the resemblance is best treated as thematic rather than demonstrated literary dependence. Taken together, these are best described as reuse or adaptation of familiar KJV prophetic and New Testament banquet language, not proof of a deliberately constructed literary design.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Is the "feast of fat things" banquet imagery (vv. 8-9) a deliberate literary allusion to Isaiah 25:6, or simply shared reliance on common KJV idiom?
- What does the invitation order in vv. 10-11 (rich/learned/wise/noble, then poor/lame/blind/deaf) signify — missionary chronology, social hierarchy, judgment imagery? The text itself does not resolve this.
- How should the specific, unflattering personal corrections of Harris and Phelps (vv. 39, 41) be weighed as evidence about the revelation's origin, given that they cut against the interests of the text's own recipients?
- How should the civil-obedience instruction in vv. 21-23 be applied when civil and religious duties conflict, given that the text asserts they will not conflict for the faithful but does not address the hypothetical case where they do?
- Why does the revelation favor a gradual, counseled gathering over haste or flight (v. 56)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[D&C 57]] for the immediately related identification of the Missouri center place and leadership assignments. Compare D&C 58:34–56 with D&C 42:30–36 on property, inheritances, care for the poor, and the storehouse. Isaiah 25:6, Luke 14:13–24, and Revelation 19:7–9 provide the clearest biblical contexts for the banquet imagery in verses 8–11. Romans 13:1 is useful for comparing the phrase about subjection to governing powers, while attention should also be given to the qualifying language within D&C 58:22 itself.
