# D&C 38 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 38]] · My notes: [[D&C 38 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 38 pairs an extended divine self-identification with concrete, practical commands. Christ opens by naming himself 'the Great I AM, Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end' (v.1) — titles that echo [[Exodus 3|Exodus 3:14]]'s divine self-naming and [[Revelation 22|Revelation 22:13]]'s Alpha-and-Omega language — and claims a role in creation and in interceding for believers 'by the virtue of the blood which I have spilt' (vv.2-4). The revelation then turns to a hidden threat 'in secret chambers' (vv.13, 28), a call to equality illustrated by the parable of a father's twelve sons (v.26) and the unity mandate 'be one...or ye are not mine' (v.27), the commandment to gather 'to the Ohio' and receive an endowment 'with power from on high' (vv.31-33), and the appointment of men to care for the poor and administer church property (vv.34-38). It closes with a warning against pride 'as the Nephites of old' (v.39) and a purity charge echoing [[Isaiah 52|Isaiah 52:11]] (v.42).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. **Divine identity and judgment (vv. 1–12):** Christ identifies himself and contrasts protection of believers with judgment upon hardened wickedness.
2. **Hidden danger and promised mercy (vv. 13–17):** the audience is warned of danger but told not to fear.
3. **Inheritance, government, and unity (vv. 18–27):** promised land and divine rule lead into repeated commands for equal regard and oneness.
4. **Preparation and gathering (vv. 28–33):** local danger supplies immediate context for gathering to Ohio, receiving law, and being endowed with power.
5. **Poor relief, property, and labor (vv. 34–42):** appointed administrators coordinate relief and property while all members labor and preach meekly.

This is a thematic outline, not a claim that the chapter has a formally marked literary structure.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter associates salvation with Christ's advocacy through his shed blood (v. 4), while also demanding purification and responsive discipleship (vv. 6–9). Grace and obligation therefore appear together rather than as competitors.

Its account of unity is demanding: "be one" is reinforced by a parable condemning discriminatory treatment among obedient children (vv. 24–27). The following arrangements for the poor suggest that [[Zion]]-like unity has material consequences. Yet the text does not specify complete equality of possessions, so it should not be made to answer every later question about [[Consecration]].

Preparation includes spiritual, social, and practical dimensions: purity, wisdom, gathering, poor relief, stewardship of property, manual labor, and meek proclamation. This breadth connects the chapter with [[Gathering of Israel]], [[Service]], [[Missionary Work]], and [[Work and Self-Reliance]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The opening titles deliberately use biblical idiom. "Great I AM" recalls [[Exodus 3]], while "Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end" parallels [[Revelation 22]]. The closing instruction about being clean while bearing the Lord's vessels echoes [[Isaiah 52]]. These observations securely establish intertextual language; they do not, without further evidence, determine whether the relationship is revelatory continuity, literary reuse, or both.

The word "enemy" shifts attention from cosmic powers of darkness (vv. 11–12) to a threat operating in secret chambers and within the hearers' own land (vv. 13, 28–30). The text itself does not identify the persons involved.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 1-4 use a theophanic self-identification formula ('I am the same which...'). Whether this recurs as a deliberate opening convention across other early Doctrine and Covenants revelations is suggestive — several sections surface as semantically similar — but unverified here and should not be treated as an established pattern. The chapter's most distinctive literary feature is the parable of a father with twelve sons who clothes one in robes and another in rags while claiming impartiality (v.26), used to expose the gap between professed and practiced equality; no close parallel to this specific parable has been confirmed elsewhere in the standard works, though that has not been exhaustively checked. Verse 6's triple 'wo, wo, wo' follows a biblical pattern of intensified woe-pronouncements (compare Revelation 8:13's triple 'Woe, woe, woe'). The doubled instruction 'let every man esteem his brother as himself' (vv.24-25) and the doubled address 'verily, verily' (v.7) use repetition for emphasis.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What is the specific historical referent, if any, behind 'a thing which is had in secret chambers' (vv.13, 28) — a known conspiracy, a general warning, or apocalyptic rhetoric borrowed from Book of Mormon 'secret combinations' language? What distinguishes those called 'clean' (v.10) from those called 'guilty' (v.14) — the same people, or different ones? Does the unnamed 'land of promise' in vv.18-20 refer to Ohio specifically, a broader future Zion, or an eternal inheritance? How should the future markers 'in time' (v.21) and 'when I come' (v.22) limit any application of the no-king/no-law language to the Saints' present circumstances? How much economic specificity should be drawn from the twelve-sons parable and the poor-relief instructions that follow — common ownership, or simply impartial and adequate care? In what sense is 'endowed with power from on high' (v.32) meant here — general spiritual empowerment, or something anticipating the later Kirtland Temple endowment?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
