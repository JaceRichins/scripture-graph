# D&C 45 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 45]] · My notes: [[D&C 45 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 45 moves through four movements. Verses 1–10 present Christ as the advocate who pleads with the Father on behalf of believers, appealing to his own sinless suffering and shed blood (vv. 3–5), and describe his everlasting covenant as a light and standard for the world. Verses 11–15 briefly recall Enoch's city, received up and "reserved until a day of righteousness" — an earlier gathered community that anticipates the [[Zion]] built later in the chapter. Verses 16–59 retell, in Christ's own first-person voice, a prophecy he says he gave to his disciples "as I stood before them in the flesh" (v. 16): the fall of the Jerusalem temple, the scattering and later gathering of Israel, wars and cosmic signs, the fig-tree parable, the resurrection of sleeping saints, and Christ's appearing to the Jews who recognize his wounds. Verses 60–75 turn from prophecy to assignment: further understanding awaits the translation of the New Testament, and the elders are commanded to gather westward, preach repentance, pool resources, and build the New Jerusalem, called [[Zion]], as a refuge from the wars described earlier in the chapter.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv. 1–10** — Exhortation to hearken; Christ as advocate pleading with the Father (vv. 3–5); Christ as Alpha and Omega, light of the world, reworking language from John 1; the everlasting covenant as light and standard.
- **vv. 11–15** — Enoch and his city, received up and reserved for "a day of righteousness," introduced ahead of the chapter's later Zion material.
- **vv. 16–30** — Christ retells prophecy given "as I stood before them in the flesh": destruction of the Jerusalem temple, scattering of the Jews, the times of the Gentiles, and the gospel's rejection by many.
- **vv. 31–59** — Calamities and contrasting responses; the fig-tree parable; cosmic signs; the resurrection of sleeping saints; Christ's appearing to the Jews, who recognize his wounds; the parable of the ten virgins; millennial conditions.
- **vv. 60–75** — Translation of the New Testament tied to further understanding; command to gather westward and build the New Jerusalem; temporary confidentiality until the work is accomplished.

This outline tracks clear shifts in topic and speaker focus but should not be read as a strict timetable — the referent of "that generation" (vv. 21, 30, 31, 38) shifts in ways the text does not fully resolve, and "this place" (v. 43) is not identified within the chapter.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Christ as advocate**: vv. 3–5 depict Jesus pleading with the Father on the basis of his own sinlessness, suffering, and shed blood — salvation presented as relational and intercessory, culminating in the promise that believers "may come unto me and have everlasting life." See [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]].
- **Readiness as spiritual, not just predictive**: the "wise" of the ten-virgins parable are defined in v. 57 as those who "received the truth" and "taken the Holy Spirit for their guide" — readiness for the Second Coming is framed as ongoing spiritual fidelity, not merely the ability to identify disaster signs.
- **Zion as a demanding, gathered community**: Enoch's city (vv. 11–14) and the New Jerusalem (vv. 64–71) both belong to a pattern of a people gathered out and protected, though the chapter itself does not explicitly define how the two relate. Building the later Zion requires deliberate human effort — preaching, gathering resources, purchasing an inheritance (vv. 64–67) — alongside divine protection; its people are also described as uniquely free of internal war (vv. 68–69).
- Two points deserve caution rather than confident doctrinal statement: v. 54's promise that those who "knew no law" will find their redemption merely "tolerable" does not itself specify a tiered system of final glory, and v. 55's statement that Satan will "have no place in the hearts of the children of men" describes a loss of influence over people without settling how that relates to any broader binding described elsewhere.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
D&C 45:16–59 forms a dense scriptural mosaic reworking earlier biblical language into Christ's first-person voice. The temple prediction, wars, fig-tree parable, cosmic signs, clouds, angels, and watchfulness align closely with the Olivet-discourse tradition shared with [[Matthew 24]], [[Luke 21]], and [[Joseph Smith--Matthew 1]] (the latter two confirmed by verified verse-level text overlap). The named parable of the ten virgins (v. 56) recalls Matthew 25:1–13, and the Lord's foot cleaving the mount in twain (v. 48) recalls Zechariah 14:4, though that specific parallel is unverified here and should be checked before being asserted with high confidence. Elsewhere the chapter draws closely, sometimes near-verbatim, on other biblical texts: vv. 7–8 recast John 1:5 and 1:11–12 in first-person divine speech ("I came unto mine own, and mine own received me not"); v. 13 quotes Hebrews 11:13 almost verbatim ("strangers and pilgrims on the earth"); vv. 51–52 apply Zechariah 13:6's wounded-hands question directly to the resurrected Christ; and v. 2's warning echoes Jeremiah 8:20's "harvest is past, the summer is ended" (with the clauses reordered). These correspondences are strong literary observations; they do not by themselves decide whether the relationship reflects revelatory expansion, deliberate composition, or Joseph Smith's general immersion in KJV language.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Paraphrastic Parallel
> D&C 45:37-38 restates the Olivet Discourse's fig-tree parable ('when they begin to shoot forth, and their leaves are yet tender, that summer is now nigh at hand') in wording close to Matthew 24:32.

> [!evidence] Near-Verbatim Intertextual Dependence
> D&C 45:7-8 closely reworks John 1:5 and John 1:11-12, recasting third-person narration about the Logos into first-person divine speech by Christ ('I came unto mine own, and mine own received me not... gave I power... to become the sons of God').
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted · [[Near-Verbatim Intertextual Dependence in D&C 45|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Near-Verbatim Quotation Applied Typologically
> D&C 45:51-52's exchange—'What are these wounds in thine hands and in thy feet?' answered 'These wounds are the wounds with which I was wounded in the house of my friends'—closely parallels Zechariah 13:6, applying the prophecy explicitly to the resurrected Christ's wounds being recognized by the Jews.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation · [[Near-Verbatim Quotation Applied Typologically in D&C 45|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary Intertextuality
> The sustained convergence of temple destruction, wars, false security, cosmic signs, the fig tree, watchfulness, clouds, angels, and gathering provides strong evidence that D&C 45:16–59 is literarily related to the New Testament Olivet discourse and associated judgment parables.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted · [[Literary Intertextuality in D&C 45|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Prophecy Vs. Historical Event *(tentative)*
> D&C 45:18-21 presents Jesus foretelling the destruction of the Jerusalem temple ('thrown down that there shall not be left one stone upon another'), an event historically fulfilled when Roman forces destroyed the Second Temple in AD 70.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What is the relationship between the prophecy Christ says he gave "as I stood before them in the flesh" (v. 16) and the canonical Gospels' Olivet Discourse — restatement, expansion, or independent revelation?
- Does "that generation" (vv. 21, 30, 31, 38) refer to one group throughout, or does the discourse shift between the disciples' own generation and a later one?
- What location does "this place" (v. 43) designate? The chapter does not identify it explicitly.
- How does the promise to Enoch's community (vv. 11–14) frame the later command to establish Zion — precedent, pattern, or something more specific?
- Why does the chapter repeatedly pair physical signs with warnings about hardened hearts, deception, and love waxing cold, rather than treating the signs alone as sufficient?
