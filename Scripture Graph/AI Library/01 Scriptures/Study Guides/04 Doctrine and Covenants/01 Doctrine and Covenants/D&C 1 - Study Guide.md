# D&C 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 1]] · My notes: [[D&C 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 1 functions as a theological preface to a collection of commandments. It opens with a universal summons and warning (vv. 1–16), explains why Joseph Smith and other servants were called (vv. 17–23), describes revelation working through limited but teachable human servants (vv. 24–28), reviews the Book of Mormon and the church's establishment (vv. 29–33), and closes with approaching judgment and a command to search the revelations (vv. 34–39). Its central concerns include [[Revelation]], [[Prophets]], [[Restoration of the Gospel]], [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], and [[Scripture Study]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful five-part reading is:

1. **Universal summons and commissioned warning (vv. 1–6):** the Lord's voice reaches the world through chosen disciples, and the text identifies itself as a preface.
2. **Judgment and apostasy (vv. 7–16):** recompense is reciprocal, prophetic voices are rejected, ordinances and covenant are abandoned, and worldly idols perish with Babylon.
3. **Purposes of the Restoration (vv. 17–23):** weak servants are called so that reliance on human strength may diminish, faith may increase, the covenant may be established, and the gospel may be proclaimed.
4. **Human weakness, correction, and institutional founding (vv. 24–33):** divine commandments come through human language; error, sin, humility, translation, church organization, and forgiveness are addressed.
5. **Renewed worldwide address and closing attestation (vv. 34–39):** judgment approaches, readers are told to search the commandments, and the enduring word is affirmed.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verses 24–28 hold divine authority and human limitation together. The commandments are said to be from God, yet the servants receive them in weakness and in the manner of their language. Error can be exposed, wisdom increased, sin chastened, and humble recipients strengthened. This makes ongoing correction part of the chapter's account of [[Revelation]], not necessarily evidence against it.

Verses 31–33 similarly hold judgment and mercy together. Sin receives no allowance, but repentance and obedience bring forgiveness. The placement of these statements discourages reading either divine justice or mercy in isolation; see [[Justice and Mercy]] and [[Repentance and Forgiveness]].

The qualification in verse 30 is also important: the Lord's approval is directed to the church collectively, not automatically to every individual. Institutional claims therefore should not be treated as declarations of individual infallibility or righteousness.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated word **voice** links the opening summons with the closing declaration about the Lord speaking through servants. The progression is deliberate: the Lord's voice addresses all people (vv. 1–2), becomes a warning through disciples (v. 4), must be heard in both divine and prophetic forms (v. 14), and is finally described as effective whether spoken directly or through servants (v. 38). In context, this supports commissioned representation. Verse 24's acknowledgment of weakness and human language cautions against expanding verse 38 into an unrestricted theory that every utterance by a religious leader is identical with God's speech.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
D&C 1 is densely scriptural in diction. The hidden deeds and housetops of verse 3 parallel [[Luke 12]]; reciprocal measuring in verse 10 parallels [[Matthew 7]]; the weak overcoming the mighty in verse 19 parallels [[1 Corinthians 1]]; and the enduring word of verse 38 parallels [[Matthew 24]]. The most distinctive relationship is with [[Isaiah 34]]: D&C 1 separates Isaiah's sword bathed in heaven and judgment upon Idumea between verses 13 and 36, while adding the interpretive gloss “or the world.” These relationships firmly establish biblical intertextuality, but they do not by themselves decide whether the language arose through conscious composition, revelatory reuse, or both.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should verse 24's language of weakness and accommodation shape expectations about prophetic wording and correction? Does verse 25 refer to errors inside the revelations, errors in the servants' broader conduct or understanding, or both? What limits does the immediate context place on the “same” voice claim in verse 38? How does the collective qualification in verse 30 affect applications of the claim that the church is true and living? What does the equation of Idumea with the world accomplish when compared with [[Isaiah 34]]?
