# D&C 76 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 76]] · My notes: [[D&C 76 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 76 presents itself as a joint vision received by Joseph Smith and Sidney Rigdon on February 16, 1832, while they were engaged in translation and reflecting on [[John 5]]. Its central testimony concerns the living Christ and his far-reaching redemptive work. Beginning with John’s contrast between the resurrection of those who did good and those who did evil, the vision gives a more differentiated account of resurrection, judgment, and celestial, terrestrial, and telestial glory, while retaining a severe exception for the sons of perdition.

The resulting picture combines broad [[Salvation]] with unequal glory, divine presence, and dominion. These are the document’s reported revelations. The text establishes what the witnesses claimed to see and hear; it does not independently establish the historical or metaphysical truth of the reported vision.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful outline is:

1. **Verses 1-10:** Praise of God and a promise that mysteries will be revealed by the Spirit.
2. **Verses 11-19:** Date, witnesses, translation setting, and meditation on John 5:29.
3. **Verses 20-24:** Testimony of the living and creating Christ.
4. **Verses 25-49:** Lucifer, the sons of perdition, and the limits of redemption.
5. **Verses 50-70:** The celestial community and its covenantal, priestly, and christological identity.
6. **Verses 71-80:** The terrestrial community.
7. **Verses 81-112:** The telestial community, comparison of the three glories, judgment, and Christ's completed victory.
8. **Verses 113-119:** Closure, including mysteries that could not be written and a final doxology.

The promises of spiritual knowledge in verses 5-10 correspond to the closing explanation in verses 114-118, framing the entire account as [[Revelation]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter gives [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] an unusually broad scope: everyone except the sons of perdition is brought forth through Christ's triumph, and even the telestial are called heirs of salvation. This breadth should be read together with the chapter's distinctions. The terrestrial lack the Father's fulness, and the telestial cannot enter where God and Christ dwell.

The celestial portrait integrates [[Faith]], [[Baptism]], [[Obedience]], cleansing, the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], endurance, and divine grace. Its highest promises remain explicitly dependent on Jesus, the mediator whose blood makes just persons perfect.

The sons of perdition should not be equated casually with ordinary sinners. The text applies specific and extreme descriptions to them and calls them the only persons subject to the second death.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The vision is densely intertextual. [[John 5]] supplies its stated point of departure. [[1 Corinthians 15]] supplies celestial and terrestrial terminology and the sun-moon-stars comparison, which D&C 76 develops into named glories and communities. [[Hebrews 12]] closely parallels the ordered language about Zion, the heavenly city, innumerable angels, the church of the Firstborn, perfected righteous persons, Jesus the mediator, and covenant blood. [[1 Corinthians 3]] parallels the declaration that all things belong to the saints, the saints to Christ, and Christ to God. The factional names in D&C 76:99-100 also reuse language from [[1 Corinthians 1]].

Two further parallels deserve attention. D&C 76:44 closely repeats the Mark 9 refrain concerning the undying worm and unquenched fire, while D&C 76:73 combines language associated with imprisoned spirits and preaching to the dead in [[1 Peter 3]] and [[1 Peter 4]].

These parallels strongly demonstrate biblical reuse. They do not determine whether that reuse reflects revelation expressed through familiar scripture, remembered biblical language, conscious literary composition, or some combination.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Doctrinal Language
> The repeated contrast between the sons of perdition and "all the rest" is strong textual evidence that the chapter envisions an exceptionally broad scope for Christ's redemptive victory. That breadth includes resurrection for all others and a form of salvation even for telestial heirs, while still preserving unequal glories and exclusion from the dwelling place of God and Christ.

> [!evidence] Textual Intertextuality
> The explicit use of John 5 and the clustered parallels with 1 Corinthians and Hebrews provide strong evidence that D&C 76 communicates its vision through sustained biblical intertextuality. These parallels illuminate how the chapter enlarges familiar biblical language into a distinctive account of post-resurrection glory.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How does this chapter use the word “salvation” when telestial persons are heirs of salvation but cannot enter where God and Christ dwell?

Are the terrestrial descriptions in verses 72-79 separate populations, overlapping descriptions, or examples rather than an exhaustive classification?

What does being “valiant in the testimony of Jesus” require, given that the chapter does not define the phrase in detail?

How should the statement that celestial inhabitants are made equal in power, might, and dominion be read alongside the statements that they belong to Christ and that Christ belongs to God?

How should D&C 76:73, which says the Son visited and preached to imprisoned spirits, be related to [[D&C 138]], which says Christ did not personally go among the wicked but commissioned messengers to preach to them?

What interpretive function is served by the chapter’s disclosure that additional mysteries were shown but not written?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the astronomical imagery in D&C 76:70-98 with [[1 Corinthians 15]], noting what D&C 76 adds to Paul’s discussion of resurrected bodies. Compare D&C 76:66-69 phrase by phrase with [[Hebrews 12]], and D&C 76:59-61 and 99-101 with [[1 Corinthians 3]] and [[1 Corinthians 1]]. Compare D&C 76:44 with [[Mark 9]], and D&C 76:73 with [[1 Peter 3]] and [[1 Peter 4]]. Finally, read D&C 76:72-74 alongside [[D&C 138]], attending both to their shared spirit-world doctrine and to their different descriptions of who personally preached to the imprisoned spirits.
