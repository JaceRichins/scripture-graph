# D&C 138 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 138]] · My notes: [[D&C 138 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 138 presents a first-person vision arising from reflection on the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] and close reading of [[1 Peter 3]] and [[1 Peter 4]]. Its central interpretive question is how Christ could perform extensive ministry among the dead during the short interval between crucifixion and resurrection. The chapter answers by distinguishing Christ’s personal appearance among the faithful from the wider preaching performed by authorized messengers whom he organized.

The resulting account connects [[Death and the Spirit World]], [[Missionary Work]], [[Repentance]], [[Baptism for the Dead]], [[Resurrection]], and [[Temples]]. This is a coherent description of the chapter’s theology. Treating it as an objectively revealed description of postmortal reality additionally depends on accepting the narrator’s testimony; textual coherence and intertextual richness do not independently prove supernatural causation.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Scriptural setting (vv. 1–10):** Reflection on Christ’s sacrifice leads to reading Peter’s statements about preaching to the dead.
2. **Christ among the faithful (vv. 11–24):** The righteous await resurrection and rejoice in Christ’s presence, while the rebellious remain in darkness.
3. **The timing question (vv. 25–28):** The narrator wonders how the necessary ministry could occur during so short an interval.
4. **Delegated preaching (vv. 29–37):** Christ organizes authorized messengers to carry the gospel among the dead.
5. **The assembled faithful (vv. 38–52):** Biblical figures, Nephite prophets, and other faithful spirits await deliverance and future labor.
6. **Latter-day service and redemption (vv. 53–59):** Premortal preparation, temple work, continued preaching, repentance, ordinances, and judgment are joined together.
7. **Concluding testimony (v. 60):** The narrator bears witness to the vision.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents redemption as wrought through Christ’s sacrifice and received under stated conditions that include faith, [[Repentance]], reception of the gospel, and obedience to authorized [[Ordinances]]. It therefore neither portrays ordinances as saving independently of Christ nor teaches automatic salvation for everyone who hears the postmortal message.

Its account of [[Resurrection]] is strongly embodied: the body is restored, spirit and body are permanently reunited, and that reunion permits a fulness of joy. Separation from the body is described as bondage.

Verses 55–57 connect [[Premortal Life]], mortal vocation, and postmortal service. Verses 58–59 retain the language of penalty, cleansing, works, and differentiated reward. The chapter names these elements but does not fully systematize their relationship or define the ultimate limits of postmortal repentance.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly pairs darkness with light, captivity with liberty, bondage with deliverance, bodily separation with reunion, and rebellion with repentance. These images express moral and spiritual conditions, but they should not automatically be treated as a technical map of postmortal geography.

Liberty language is explicitly associated with [[Isaiah 61]]. “Bondage” also has more than one related application: spirits are captive in darkness and sin, while verse 50 describes prolonged separation of spirit and body as bondage. Redemption consequently includes release from sin and death, restoration of the body, and entrance into promised blessing.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The narrative follows a repeated inquiry pattern: reading and pondering precede opened understanding in verses 1–11, while wondering about Peter’s language precedes the delegated-ministry explanation in verses 25–37. This gives the account a question-and-resolution movement and portrays revelation as arising through engaged scriptural reflection.

The catalogue in verses 38–49 broadens the scriptural setting. Isaiah contributes liberty imagery, Ezekiel bodily restoration, Daniel the latter-day kingdom, and Malachi the Elijah promise later associated with temple sealing. These are explicit literary and theological connections, but they serve different functions and do not independently corroborate the objective presence of the people named or the divine origin of the vision.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Scriptural Intertextuality
> The verbatim biblical quotations and explicit prophetic allusions provide strong evidence that the chapter is deliberately constructed as an interpretation and expansion of earlier scripture.

> [!evidence] Intertextual Synthesis
> The chapter integrates language and themes attributed to Peter, Isaiah, Ezekiel, Daniel, Malachi, Nephite prophets, and latter-day temple teaching into one redemption narrative. This strongly supports the limited conclusion that the text is deliberately intertextual and theologically synthetic.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted literary observation · [[Intertextual Synthesis in D&C 138|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Literary And Theological Coherence
> The distinction between Christ's personal preaching and the preaching of commissioned messengers supplies a coherent internal answer to the narrator's stated timing problem and reconciles the chapter's claim that Christ did not personally enter the wicked assembly with Peter's broader statement that the gospel was preached to the dead.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · accepted literary finding; disputed larger interpretation · [[Internal Literary And Theological Coherence in D&C 138|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How does the distinction between Christ’s personal ministry and delegated preaching address the timing question in verses 25–37?
2. What differences, if any, does verse 32 imply between those who lacked knowledge and those who had rejected prophets?
3. Why does the chapter use bondage imagery for both sin and separation from the body?
4. How do Christ’s grace, repentance, ordinances, payment of penalties, cleansing, works, and reward relate in verses 14, 19, and 58–59?
5. What does the inclusion of Eve and faithful daughters establish, and what questions about their postmortal roles remain unanswered?
6. Should terms such as place, prison, darkness, and assembly be understood spatially, symbolically, morally, or through some combination of these senses?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the quoted passages in [[1 Peter 3]] and [[1 Peter 4]] with D&C 138:25–37, while noting that Christian interpretations differ over the identity of the spirits, the agent of the preaching, and when it occurred. Trace liberty imagery through [[Isaiah 61]], bodily restoration through [[Ezekiel 37]], and the Elijah promise through [[Malachi 4]], [[D&C 2]], and [[D&C 110]]. Compare [[D&C 128]] for additional Latter-day Saint teaching about proxy ordinances, sealing, and relationships between the living and the dead.
