# D&C 87 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 87]] · My notes: [[D&C 87 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 87 is a prophecy of political conflict, social upheaval, calamity, and divine judgment. It begins with “the rebellion of South Carolina,” describes division between Southern and Northern States and Southern appeals to Great Britain and other nations, and extends its horizon to war upon all nations and the day of the Lord. Its final emphasis is practical: “stand ye in holy places, and be not moved” (D&C 87:8).

A document identified by the Joseph Smith Papers dates the revelation to December 25, 1832, before the American Civil War but during the Nullification Crisis. The later Civil War supplies notable correspondences, especially South Carolina, sectional conflict, extensive suffering, and Confederate appeals abroad. Those correspondences support serious study but do not establish supernatural foreknowledge or fulfillment of every clause. Contemporary political tensions made some elements foreseeable, while the global and eschatological portions remain disputed or open.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter presents a chain of overlapping predictions rather than a strict concentric design:

1. **Political and international conflict (D&C 87:1–3):** South Carolina, sectional division, foreign appeals, and war upon all nations.
2. **Social and territorial upheaval (D&C 87:4–5):** slaves rise against masters, and unidentified remnants vex the Gentiles.
3. **Judgment and suffering (D&C 87:6–7):** violence, famine, plague, earthquake, heavenly phenomena, and the appeal arising from the saints and their blood.
4. **Concluding imperative (D&C 87:8):** stand in holy places and remain unmoved until the day of the Lord.

The expressions “shortly,” “the time will come,” and “after many days” indicate temporal staging, but they do not by themselves yield an uncontested chronology. In particular, “shortly” remains in tension with readings that defer much of the passage across generations.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The text itself interprets the calamities of verse 6 as divine wrath, indignation, and chastening. Verse 7 portrays God as hearing the cry of the saints and of their blood, but the chapter does not direct disciples to pursue private vengeance. Its explicit instruction to the audience is instead to “stand ye in holy places, and be not moved” (D&C 87:8). This joins [[Prophecy]], [[War and Peace]], and [[Endurance]] while keeping the theological interpretation of calamity distinct from the observable occurrence of disaster.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several expressions place D&C 87 within biblical prophetic language. “The consumption decreed” (D&C 87:6) closely echoes [[Isaiah 10]]. James 5:4 provides a close verbal parallel to cries entering the ears of the “Lord of Sabaoth,” while [[Revelation 6]] provides a thematic parallel involving slain saints appealing for vengeance. “Sabaoth” conventionally represents the Hebrew term rendered “hosts”; in this chapter the title plausibly contributes to the judgment register, although its meaning is not limited to literal armies. D&C 87:8 also closely parallels [[D&C 45]] in joining holy places, steadfastness, and the day of the Lord. These relationships establish intertextuality more securely than they establish fulfillment or divine origin.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter accumulates political, international, social, natural, and eschatological threats, though it does not expand in a perfectly linear sequence: “all nations” appears in verse 2 before verse 3 returns to sectional particulars. The clearest large-scale movement is from prediction to exhortation. After seven verses describing forces largely beyond the audience’s control, verse 8 identifies the response within their control: stand in holy places and remain unmoved. That shift gives the closing imperative particular rhetorical weight.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical Correspondence
> Several particulars in D&C 87:1–3 correspond notably with the later American Civil War: South Carolina led secession, Northern and Southern states fought one another, the war caused immense death and misery, and the Confederacy sought recognition and assistance from Great Britain and other European powers. The correspondence is meaningful, but the wording about war upon all nations extends beyond the Civil War and should not be treated as fully exhausted by it.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.9 · historical correspondences accepted; significance disputed · [[Historical Correspondence in D&C 87|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Documentary Chronology
> The Joseph Smith Papers identifies this revelation with the date 25 December 1832, well before the American Civil War. That documentary chronology is essential when assessing the passage's predictive character.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · documentary chronology accepted · [[Documentary Chronology in D&C 87|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Alternative Explanation / Contemporary Context *(tentative)*
> Nat Turner's slave rebellion in Southampton County, Virginia (August 1831) was a major, widely reported event roughly sixteen months before this revelation, and it intensified fears throughout the South of organized slave violence. This provides a plausible naturalistic backdrop for D&C 87:4's imagery, weakening (though not eliminating) a reading of that verse as unprompted foreknowledge.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.8 · plausible contemporary context; documentation needed

> [!evidence] Historical Correlation *(tentative)*
> The revelation's date (per its canonical heading, December 25, 1832) places it about a month after South Carolina's Nullification Ordinance (November 24, 1832), a live, nationally reported crisis in which South Carolina threatened armed resistance to federal tariff enforcement. This is important context for weighing verse 1's specificity.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.8 · historical context accepted; sourcing incomplete

> [!evidence] Historical Correlation *(tentative)*
> During the Civil War the Confederacy did seek British support (attempts at diplomatic recognition, purchase of British-built commerce raiders, reliance on cotton exports), which resembles D&C 87:3's "call on... Great Britain... in order to defend themselves." However, Britain never entered the war on the Confederacy's behalf, so the clause is only partially matched.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.7 · partial historical correspondence

> [!evidence] Historical Correlation *(tentative)*
> The American Civil War is commonly cited by Latter-day Saint commentators as beginning the predicted wars, since the first shots were fired at Fort Sumter, South Carolina, in April 1861 and the war was substantially a North-South conflict, matching D&C 87:1-3's opening frame.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · notable correspondence; interpretation disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does “beginning at this place” in D&C 87:2 designate: South Carolina, the United States, or the location of the named rebellion?
- In D&C 87:4, does “who shall be marshaled and disciplined for war” modify the masters, the slaves, or some broader party to the conflict?
- Who are “the remnants who are left of the land” in D&C 87:5? What evidence beyond this verse supports proposed identifications?
- Should war upon “all nations” describe a single conflict, an extended spread of warfare, apocalyptic hyperbole, or a still-future event?
- How should “shortly” be weighed alongside “the time will come” and “after many days”?
- What may qualify as “holy places,” and what does the close wording of [[D&C 45]] add without predetermining the answer?
- How does the retrospective statement in [[D&C 130]] illuminate the prophecy without proving its fulfillment?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
For documentary chronology, examine the December 25, 1832 revelation and historical introduction in the Joseph Smith Papers, distinguishing original composition, surviving copies, circulation, publication, and canonization. For historical comparison, study the Nullification Crisis, South Carolina’s secession, the opening of the Civil War, Confederate diplomacy, and the background of slave-revolt fears. Compare [[D&C 130]] for a later canonical retrospective.

For literary study, compare D&C 87:6–8 with [[Isaiah 10]], [[Revelation 6]], Matthew 24, James 5, and [[D&C 45]]. Keep three questions separate throughout: what the 1832 text demonstrably says, which later events resemble it, and what evidentiary or theological conclusion the resemblance warrants.
