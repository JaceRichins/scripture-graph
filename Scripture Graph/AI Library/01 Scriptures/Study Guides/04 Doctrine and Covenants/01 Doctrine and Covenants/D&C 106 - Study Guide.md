# D&C 106 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 106]] · My notes: [[D&C 106 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 106 is a short, occasional revelation. It first calls Warren A. Cowdery to preside as a high priest over the Church in the land of Freedom and to preach in the adjoining counties, promising that his needs will be met as he devotes his whole time to the work (vv. 1–3). It then turns outward to a general warning: the Second Coming will overtake the unprepared world 'as a thief in the night,' and disciples are called to ready themselves as 'children of light' (vv. 4–5). The final verses return to Warren personally — heaven rejoiced at his earlier submission, yet the Lord also names 'the vanity of his heart' directly, promises to lift him up as he humbles himself, and conditions a crown in the Father's mansions on his continuing to be 'a faithful witness and a light unto the church' (vv. 6–8). Historical sources commonly identify Warren as Oliver Cowdery's elder brother, but that connection comes from Church-history record, not from the text of this chapter. See [[Priesthood]], [[Missionary Work]], and [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The eight verses move through three compact units, joined at both seams by the identical formula 'And again, verily I say unto you' (vv. 4, 6), which marks each pivot from one subject to the next:

1. **Calling and support (vv. 1–3):** appointment as presiding high priest, a regional preaching charge, and the promise that necessary things will be added as Warren devotes his whole time to the work.
2. **Eschatological readiness (vv. 4–5):** the Lord's coming compared to a thief in the night, and the command to become children of light so that day will not overtake the unprepared.
3. **Personal assessment and conditional promise (vv. 6–8):** joy in heaven over Warren's earlier submission, a candid naming of 'the vanity of his heart,' and a crown promised only if he continues faithful.

This personal–general–personal shape is a useful heuristic for the text's movement, but there isn't enough here to establish it as a deliberately composed literary inclusio.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The revelation binds a local, seemingly minor administrative appointment to substantial doctrine. [[Priesthood]] leadership here is inseparable from [[Missionary Work]]: presiding over a place and preaching beyond it are given in the same breath (vv. 1–2). Full-time consecrated service is expected (v. 3, 'devote his whole time'), and material provision is promised in that context — the text does not specify a mechanism or guarantee unrestricted prosperity. Readiness for the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]] is expressed as a matter of identity and conduct ('children of light'), not as a basis for calculating a date (vv. 4–5). Warren's case holds [[Grace]], [[Humility]], and [[Endurance]] together without flattening them: a specific flaw is named ('the vanity of his heart'), mercy and enabling grace are promised, and the final blessing — a crown 'in the mansions of my Father' — still depends on his continuing faithful (vv. 7–8).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter uses stock revelatory diction found throughout the Doctrine and Covenants: 'verily I say unto you' as a transition marker (vv. 4, 6) and 'gird up your loins' as watchfulness idiom (v. 5). Verse 3's language for seeking the kingdom and verses 4–5's warning both draw on distinctive New Testament phrase clusters (see Literary, below); this is useful for interpretation, but the text alone cannot settle whether the resemblance reflects deliberate quotation, remembered biblical idiom, or revelatory reuse of earlier divine language.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
D&C 106 draws on a cluster of New Testament texts of varying closeness. The strongest, most distinctive parallels are Matthew 6:33 ('seek...the kingdom...and his righteousness...all these things shall be added,' echoed in v. 3), the 'laborer is worthy of his hire' formula shared with Luke 10:7 and D&C 84:79 (v. 3), and above all 1 Thessalonians 5:2–5, whose thief-in-the-night, overtaking, and children-of-light language appears together in vv. 4–5 — a dense enough cluster to make some relationship to that passage likely, though 2 Peter 3:10 shows 'thief in the night' alone is not unique to Paul. 'Mansions of my Father' (v. 8) closely echoes John 14:2's 'many mansions.' Weaker, more thematic echoes include Luke 12:35's girded-loins watchfulness image, the Luke 15 parables' 'joy in heaven' over a person's turning to God, and Revelation 2:10's conditional crown — none share the same distinctive combination of words found in the stronger parallels above, so read them as resonance rather than demonstrated quotation. Within the chapter itself, light gives structural cohesion: the congregation is called to be 'children of light' anticipating the Lord's coming (v. 5), while Warren personally must remain 'a light unto the church' (v. 8), moving from a communal identity to one leader's responsibility to embody it.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean that the Lord names Warren's 'vanity of heart' directly rather than omitting it, and how does that shape the way grace and calling are held together in this chapter?
- Why might a warning about the Second Coming (vv. 4–5) be placed inside a revelation about a single local appointment rather than delivered as a stand-alone message?
- Does 'there was joy in heaven when my servant Warren bowed to my scepter' (v. 6) describe an earlier conversion, or joy over this present calling itself?
- What would it mean for a presiding officer today to be both a faithful witness and 'a light unto the church' (v. 8)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
