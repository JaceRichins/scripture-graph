# D&C 117 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 117]] · My notes: [[D&C 117 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> 1–9, The Lord’s servants should not covet temporal things, for “what is property unto the Lord?”; 10–16, They are to forsake littleness of soul, and their sacrifices will be sacred unto the Lord.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves through five units:

1. Verses 1–3: an urgent command to settle business and depart from Kirtland.
2. Verses 4–9: repentance from covetousness, followed by an argument from divine ownership, providence, and abundance.
3. Verses 10–11: specific leadership assignments for William Marks and Newel K. Whitney.
4. Verses 12–15: commendation and commissioning of Oliver Granger, with sacrifice placed above increase.
5. Verse 16: a final communal charge to remember God and preserve his house.

Repeated movement commands—settle, journey, arise, go, come up, and not tarry—make obedience a matter of leaving attachment behind and acting promptly.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Repentance]] to economic desire: covetousness can obstruct obedience just as directly as other sins. Its doctrine of stewardship is carefully qualified. Property is to answer legitimate debts, but a remainder may be retained; Granger's future merchant work is intended for the people's benefit. Thus the chapter supports disciplined stewardship and [[Sacrifice]], not a simplistic claim that all ownership or increase is evil.

Leadership is likewise measured by action. Whitney must be a bishop "not in name but in deed," while Marks's prospective enlargement of responsibility depends upon faithfulness in smaller responsibilities. Granger's treatment adds an important corrective to outcome-centered judgment: sacrifice may be sacred even when visible increase is modest.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verses 4–8 use a sequence of divine questions: What is property to the Lord? Does he not possess the creatures, make the earth, and govern nations? Is there not room in the promised destination? The questions shift the frame from scarcity and possession to divine sovereignty and abundance.

"Olaha Shinehah" is followed by the appositional explanation "the land where Adam dwelt." The chapter supplies that association but gives no etymology, so proposed translations of the expression require evidence outside this text. Likewise, "Nicolaitane band" clearly evokes [[Revelation 2]], but its local referent is not defined.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
D&C 117 contains several close biblical echoes. The promise that a solitary place will blossom recalls [[Isaiah 35]]; “more weighty matters” recalls [[Matthew 23]]; faithfulness over a few leading to rule over many closely recalls [[Matthew 25]]; “Nicolaitane” evokes [[Revelation 2]]; and the Lord’s house and moneychangers recall the temple-cleansing narratives in [[Matthew 21]] and [[John 2]]. The Matthew 25, Isaiah 35, and temple-cleansing correspondences are especially strong. Together these parallels establish substantial use of King James biblical diction, although they do not prove that every phrase is a consciously constructed allusion.

The chapter also contrasts rebuked delay and covetousness with Granger’s honored sacrifice. This juxtaposition favors faithful response over attachment to possessions or exclusive reliance on visible success.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Historical Background (Extra-Textual) *(tentative)*
> The chapter's emphasis on debts, property, and urgency to leave before winter is commonly read against the historical collapse of the Kirtland Safety Society (1837) and the resulting financial crisis and leadership dissent in Kirtland, though this specific causal background is not stated in the text itself, which offers only a theological rationale (divine ownership of all property).

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What specific conduct did "Nicolaitane band" designate in this setting, beyond its biblical association? Does Granger's promised rising after he falls describe recovery from failure, vindication after discouragement, resurrection, or deliberately multivalent assurance? What obligations are included in "the redemption of the First Presidency"? Who are the moneychangers of verse 16, and is the word literal, typological, or both? The chapter alone does not settle these historical and semantic questions.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the chapter’s language of divine ownership with [[Psalm 50]], while recognizing that this is a broad biblical motif rather than a certain direct source. Compare the blossoming solitary place with [[Isaiah 35]], “weighty matters” with [[Matthew 23]], and Marks’s commission with [[Matthew 25]]. Read the Adam-ondi-Ahman references beside [[D&C 116]], and compare the moneychanger language with [[Matthew 21]] and [[John 2]]. Reliable historical documents concerning Kirtland property, outstanding obligations, the First Presidency’s circumstances, and Oliver Granger’s work are necessary before assigning precise economic meanings to “redemption,” “increase,” or the chapter’s financial background.
