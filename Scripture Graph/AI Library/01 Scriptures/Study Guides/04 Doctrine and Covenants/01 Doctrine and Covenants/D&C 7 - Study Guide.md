## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Invitation and request (vv. 1–2):** The Lord asks John's desire, and John chooses continued life for the sake of ministry.

2. **Promise (v. 3):** John will tarry until Christ comes and prophesy among many peoples.

3. **Comparison with Peter (vv. 4–5):** The Lord explains the saying preserved in [[John 21]] by contrasting two good desires.

4. **Continuing ministry and keys (vv. 6–7):** John's work is portrayed with angelic imagery, and Peter, James, and John receive power and keys connected with the ministry.

5. **Conclusion (v. 8):** Both disciples will receive according to the desires in which they rejoice.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter treats desire as morally significant but does not reduce faithful discipleship to one approved life pattern. Peter's desire to enter Christ's kingdom is explicitly good, while John's desire for prolonged [[Service]] is described as a greater work among people. The qualification "among men" matters: it explains the greater extent of John's labor without requiring the conclusion that John is more righteous than Peter.

John's commission is oriented toward others—bringing souls to Christ, prophesying among peoples, and ministering to heirs of [[Salvation]]. His promised power over death is consequently presented as an instrument of ministry rather than an end in itself.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The noun and verb forms of **desire** recur throughout the chapter, linking the opening question to the final promise. Another refrain is Christ's coming: John will tarry "until I come," the saying to Peter uses the same horizon, and the three apostles retain their commission "until I come." These repetitions bind personal desire to an eschatological assignment.

Verse 6 combines "flaming fire," "ministering angel," and "heirs of salvation," closely recalling Hebrews 1:7,14. This is a strong textual connection, although it does not decide whether the relationship should be understood as direct literary reuse, shared scriptural idiom, or revealed restatement.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter functions as an interpretive expansion of [[John 21]]. The Gospel records Jesus's conditional statement about the beloved disciple and notes that it generated a report that the disciple would not die. D&C 7 supplies a narrative explanation: John requested continued life for missionary service, and the Lord granted that desire. This relationship is textually clear; whether the expansion represents historical disclosure, revelatory interpretation, or nineteenth-century biblical elaboration is a separate and disputed larger question.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- In what sense is John's anticipated work “more, or a greater work,” and what exactly is being compared?

- How does John's stated motive in verse 2 shape the meaning of the extraordinary promises that follow?

- What physical or transformed condition is implied by “power over death” and tarrying until Christ comes? How does the account compare—without assuming identical conditions—with [[3 Nephi 28]]?

- What is encompassed by “this power and the keys of this ministry” in verse 7, and what institutional claims remain unstated?

- Since the apparent addressee is Peter, how should “thy brother James” be understood: biological kinship, apostolic fraternity, or another grammatical relation?

- How should the strong echoes of [[John 21]] and Hebrews 1 inform literary interpretation without being made to prove the revelation's historical or supernatural origin?
