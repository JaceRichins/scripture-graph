# D&C 128 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 128]] · My notes: [[D&C 128 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 128 joins administrative precision to expansive theology. Its immediate concern is the reliable witnessing and recording of [[Baptism for the Dead]], but it locates those records within a larger account of [[Priesthood]], authorized [[Ordinances]], [[Resurrection]], reciprocal perfection between living and dead, restored keys, and [[Temples]]. The movement from minutes, signatures, and certificates to restoration voices and cosmic praise gives the chapter unusual rhetorical range. These observations describe the chapter's argument; they do not independently establish heavenly ratification or the historicity of its reported manifestations.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A restrained outline follows the chapter's major transitions:

1. **Verses 1–5 — Authenticated records.** Local recorders document proceedings, witnesses support their reports, and certified records pass to a general church recorder.
2. **Verses 6–11 — Records, binding, and authority.** [[Revelation 20]] and [[Matthew 16]] are interpreted through earthly-heavenly recording and priesthood keys.
3. **Verses 12–18 — Likeness and interdependence.** Baptism signifies grave and [[Resurrection]]; Pauline language and [[Malachi 4]] are applied to mutual perfection and a welding link.
4. **Verses 19–23 — Restoration voices and praise.** A catalogue of claimed revelations expands into an exhortation for the living, the dead, and creation to rejoice.
5. **Verses 24–25 — Temple record and close.** Malachi's refining and righteous-offering imagery frames the directive to present a worthy record of the dead in the temple.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Within the chapter's argument, [[Baptism for the Dead]] belongs to an authorized order combining [[Priesthood]], faithful performance, witnesses, and a proper record. Paperwork is therefore integrated into the ordinance rather than presented as independently salvific. Immersion and emergence signify grave and [[Resurrection]]. Salvation is portrayed relationally: the living and the dead cannot be made perfect in isolation, and the proposed welding ultimately encompasses dispensations, keys, powers, and glories.

The chapter treats authorized earthly action as capable of heavenly recognition. That is a theological assertion of the text. Its detailed procedures show how record keeping functions within this theology, but administrative precision alone cannot verify the asserted heavenly correspondence.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The opening repeatedly uses documentary terms: seeing, hearing, certifying, signatures, witnesses, dates, names, books, and records. In verse 8, "taking a different view of the translation" signals an interpretive restatement of binding in terms of recording; it does not demonstrate that "bind" and "record" are lexical equivalents. "Propria persona" distinguishes personal participation from participation through agents. "Summum bonum" means the chief or highest good and redirects attention to the powers of the Holy Priesthood. "Welding" depicts an intergenerational and dispensational connection. Verse 3's requirement that witnesses allow "that in the mouth of two or three witnesses every word may be established" echoes [[Matthew 18]]:16, though the same testimony formula appears more broadly in scripture, so it reflects a shared legal idiom rather than one unique source. Verse 17 cites the Elijah prophecy as "Malachi, last chapter, verses 5th and 6th," a citation format that follows the KJV's four-chapter division of Malachi; the Masoretic/Hebrew numbering treats the same text as Malachi 3:23-24 within a three-chapter book. In verses 19-23, repeated references to a "voice" and repeated commands to rejoice transform exposition into proclamation and praise.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Records provide a recurring organizing motif: the chapter begins with eyewitnesses, minutes, signatures, and a general church book; interprets judgment and priesthood binding through records; and concludes by calling for a worthy temple book of the dead. This recurrence may reflect deliberate literary design or simply the letter staying on its stated topic across administrative and theological sections; either reading supports seeing the chapter as coherent without proving elaborate preplanning. The rhetoric also widens—from local recorders to the church, from the living to the dead, from families to dispensations, and finally to all creation. Verse 24's description of the Lord as a "refiner's fire" who will "purify the sons of Levi" echoes Malachi 3:2-3 (as quoted by the resurrected Christ in [[3 Nephi 24]]) without the explicit citation marker the chapter uses for [[Revelation 20]], [[Matthew 16]], [[1 Corinthians 15]], and Malachi 4 — though verses 19-23 also contain unmarked biblical diction, so this is not a unique lapse. None of this independently verifies the chapter's supernatural claims.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Scriptural Intertext And Historical Inference
> D&C 128 appeals to 1 Corinthians 15:29 as biblical precedent for baptism for the dead. The Pauline wording is strong evidence that an activity described as being 'baptized for the dead' was intelligible to its original audience, but the isolated verse does not by itself determine who practiced it, its exact form, or whether Paul endorsed it.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 1.0 · limited historical inference; broader meaning disputed · [[Scriptural Intertext And Historical Inference in D&C 128|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Literary Coherence

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does [[Revelation 20]] identify its books as earthly ordinance records, or is that identification supplied by D&C 128?
- How much of the equation between binding and recording comes from [[Matthew 16]], and how much comes from this chapter's interpretive restatement?
- Does the chapter make a record sufficient, or does it require authority, faithful performance, witnesses, and record together?
- What can [[1 Corinthians 15]] establish about an ancient activity called baptism for the dead, and what remains uncertain about its form, practitioners, and Paul's stance?
- Why does the chapter identify [[Baptism for the Dead]] as the welding link when [[Malachi 4]] itself speaks only of turning hearts?
- Does “their own agents” imply anything about the agency or acceptance of the dead, or is that question left unanswered here?
- Is the temple book the whole “offering in righteousness,” or its immediate concrete expression?
- How does the shift from administrative precision to cosmic praise strengthen the rhetoric without independently proving its claims?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[D&C 127]] for the immediate epistolary setting. Compare the chapter's use of books with [[Revelation 20]], binding language with [[Matthew 16]] (and its witness-formula echo in [[Matthew 18]]), baptismal and resurrection arguments with [[1 Corinthians 15]], and heart-turning language with [[Malachi 4]] and [[3 Nephi 25]]. Verse 15's language about the fathers being unable to be made perfect without us draws on Hebrews 11:40. [[D&C 110]] provides a close restoration-context comparison concerning Elijah and keys. [[D&C 138]] offers a later Latter-day Saint development concerning the dead. These comparisons should distinguish direct quotation, adaptation, later theological development, and dependent restoration narratives from independent historical corroboration.
