# D&C 128 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 128]] · My notes: [[D&C 128 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 128 joins administrative precision to expansive theology. Its immediate concern is how [[Baptism for the Dead]] can be witnessed and recorded reliably, but it places those records within a larger account of [[Priesthood]], authorized [[Ordinances]], [[Resurrection]], intergenerational perfection, restored keys, and [[Temples]]. The chapter moves from minutes, signatures, and certificates to restoration voices and cosmic praise. These connections accurately describe the chapter's argument; they do not independently demonstrate heavenly ratification or the historicity of each reported manifestation.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful outline is:

1. **Verses 1–5 — Authenticated records.** Ward recorders document proceedings, and certified records pass to a general church recorder.
2. **Verses 6–11 — Records, binding, and authority.** [[Revelation 20]] and [[Matthew 16]] are interpreted through earthly-heavenly recording and priesthood keys.
3. **Verses 12–18 — Likeness and interdependence.** Baptism signifies grave and [[Resurrection]]; Pauline language and [[Malachi 4]] are applied to mutual perfection and a welding link.
4. **Verses 19–23 — Restoration voices and praise.** A catalogue of claimed revelations expands into an exhortation for the living, dead, and creation to rejoice.
5. **Verses 24–25 — Temple record and close.** Malachi's refining and righteous-offering imagery frames the call to present a worthy record of the dead in the temple.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Within the chapter's argument, [[Baptism for the Dead]] belongs to an authorized order combining [[Priesthood]], faithful performance, witnesses, and a proper record; paperwork is not presented as independently salvific. Immersion and emergence signify the grave and [[Resurrection]]. Salvation is portrayed relationally: the living and the dead cannot be made perfect in isolation. The resulting welding extends beyond immediate ancestry to dispensations, keys, powers, and glories. These are the chapter's theological assertions. Administrative detail establishes how the text integrates records into its ordinance theology, but it does not independently verify divine authorization or heavenly recognition.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The opening repeatedly uses documentary terms: seeing, hearing, certifying, signatures, witnesses, dates, names, books, and records. In verse 8, “taking a different view of the translation” signals an interpretive restatement of binding in terms of recording; it does not demonstrate that “bind” and “record” are lexical equivalents. “Propria persona” distinguishes personal participation from participation through agents. “Summum bonum” means the chief or highest good and redirects attention to the powers of the Holy Priesthood. “Welding” depicts an intergenerational and dispensational connection. In verses 19–23, repeated references to a “voice” and repeated commands to rejoice transform exposition into proclamation and praise.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Records provide a recurring organizing motif: the chapter begins with eyewitnesses, minutes, signatures, and a general church book; interprets judgment and priesthood binding through records; and concludes by calling for a worthy temple book of the dead. The rhetoric also widens—from local recorders to the church, from the living to the dead, from families to dispensations, and finally to all creation. This coherence supports a literary reading of administrative and theological unity, although it neither proves elaborate preplanning nor verifies the chapter's supernatural claims.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does [[Revelation 20]] itself identify its books as earthly ordinance records, or is that identification supplied by D&C 128?
- How much of the equation between binding and recording comes from [[Matthew 16]], and how much from this chapter's interpretive reformulation?
- Does the chapter make a record sufficient, or require authority, faithful performance, witnesses, and record together?
- What can [[1 Corinthians 15]] establish about an ancient activity called baptism for the dead, and what remains uncertain about its form, practitioners, and Paul's stance?
- Why does the chapter identify [[Baptism for the Dead]] as the welding link when [[Malachi 4]] itself speaks only of turning hearts?
- Is the temple book the whole “offering in righteousness,” or its immediate concrete expression?
- How does the shift from administrative precision to cosmic praise shape the chapter's argument without independently proving it?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[D&C 127]] for the immediate epistolary setting. Compare the chapter's use of books with [[Revelation 20]], binding language with [[Matthew 16]], baptismal and resurrection arguments with [[1 Corinthians 15]], and heart-turning language with [[Malachi 4]] and [[3 Nephi 25]]. [[D&C 110]] provides a close restoration-context comparison concerning Elijah and keys. [[D&C 138]] offers a later Latter-day Saint development concerning the dead. These comparisons should distinguish direct quotation, adaptation, later theological development, and dependent restoration narratives from independent historical corroboration.
