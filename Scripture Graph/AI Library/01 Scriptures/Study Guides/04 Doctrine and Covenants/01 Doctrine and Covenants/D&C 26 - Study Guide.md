## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has two movements:

1. **Present duties and later direction (v. 1):** four coordinated activities continue until a journey and conference, followed by further instruction.
2. **Common consent, prayer, and faith (v. 2):** the focus broadens from the recipients’ assignments to how things are to be done “in the church.”

This specific-to-corporate movement is a useful description of the chapter’s arrangement, though the text does not prove that it was designed as a formal literary pattern or define verse 2’s complete institutional scope.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Common consent.** D&C 26:2 says that “all things shall be done by common consent in the church, by much prayer and faith.” Its broad wording establishes the principle but does not define voting procedures, eligibility, majority rule, authority to veto revelation, or the relationship between consent and presiding authority. It is closely related to [[Sustaining Church Leaders]], while extending beyond anything the verse specifically says about leaders.

**Present duty and later guidance.** Verse 1 gives concrete responsibilities before disclosing the next assignment. This can illustrate acting faithfully on present direction without knowing every later step, but the circumstance-specific instruction should not be turned into a comprehensive doctrine of [[Revelation]].

**Material work.** Labor on the land is commanded alongside study, preaching, and congregational work. The verse neither opposes material and religious duties nor ranks one category below the other.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase “confirming the church at Colesville” is not defined here. Because “the church” is the direct object, strengthening or establishing the congregation is the most immediate reading. A connection with the ordinance of confirmation is possible but is not established by these verses and would require evidence from contemporary usage or historical records.

Verse 2 repeats “all things”: things in the church are to be done by common consent, while all things are to be received by faith. The repetition gives the sentence breadth, but the two clauses concern different actions and need not have identical scope.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 1 uses a coordinated series—studying, preaching, confirming, and laboring—before the temporal turn “and then,” which introduces promised future direction. Verse 2 joins common consent with prayer and faith, then repeats “all things” and “faith” in its closing rationale. These are observable verbal patterns; they do not by themselves establish an elaborate literary design.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Who are the unnamed recipients addressed as “you,” and what historical evidence identifies them?
- What destination is meant by “the west,” and why was further direction connected with the next conference?
- Does “confirming the church at Colesville” mean strengthening the congregation, performing ordinances, or some combination of activities?
- What did common consent require in early practice, and which procedural details come from sources beyond this chapter?
- How should “much prayer and faith” shape participation without erasing the administrative dimensions of common consent?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare the wording and setting of common-consent passages in [[D&C 20]] and [[D&C 28]], verifying the text directly before drawing conclusions about scope or procedure.
- Compare [[D&C 30]] with D&C 26:1 to test whether there is a distinctive time-allocation pattern rather than only a shared genre of personal instruction.
- Examine early records and contemporary uses of “confirm” and “confirmation” to determine whether “confirming the church at Colesville” referred primarily to strengthening a congregation, administering ordinances, or both.
- Investigate the historical identities of the recipients, the location described as “the west,” and the work required on the land; keep those externally established conclusions distinct from what the two verses state.
