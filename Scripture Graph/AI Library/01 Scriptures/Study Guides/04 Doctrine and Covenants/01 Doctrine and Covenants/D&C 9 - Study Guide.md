# D&C 9 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 9]] · My notes: [[D&C 9 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 9 redirects its addressee from attempting translation back to writing (scribing) for Joseph Smith Jr., explaining that the earlier opportunity to translate was expedient when he began but lapsed because he "did not continue as you commenced" and, in his own fear, let the time pass (vv.1–6, 10–11). Verse 7 diagnoses the deeper problem: he expected an answer simply to be handed over, having "took no thought save it was to ask." Verses 8–9 give the corrective method that has become one of the most quoted passages in the Doctrine and Covenants: study a matter out in the mind, then ask whether it is right; a right answer brings a burning in the bosom, a wrong one brings no such feeling but a "stupor of thought." The chapter closes by distinguishing this correction from condemnation — "neither of you have I condemned" (v.12) — and exhorting continued faithfulness, with a promise of being "lifted up at the last day" (vv.13–14).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter falls into three movements:

1. **Reassignment and future possibility (vv. 1–6):** continue writing for Joseph; assistance with other records remains a future possibility, but translation is not presently expedient.
2. **Explanation and instruction (vv. 7–12):** the failed attempt involved insufficient prior thought and fear; study must precede asking, and the lost opportunity is distinguished from condemnation.
3. **Exhortation and promise (vv. 13–14):** obey, resist temptation, and stand fast in the present calling.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter links [[Education and Learning]], [[Prayer]], and [[Revelation]] without treating them as substitutes for one another. Mental effort is expressly required, but sacred writing is still said to depend upon divine bestowal. The teaching is clearest when applied to the translation episode itself. Using the burning bosom and stupor of thought as an automatic rule for every life decision goes beyond what this chapter explicitly says.

The chapter also separates discipline from final rejection. A privilege is withdrawn and an assignment changes, yet neither participant is condemned. That distinction may help readers avoid treating every correction or lost opportunity as proof of divine abandonment.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"Study it out in your mind" (v.8) pairs an intellectual verb with a devotional purpose. "Bosom shall burn within you" (v.8) and "stupor of thought" (v.9) form a positive/negative pair describing confirmation and its absence, though the text does not specify their physiological intensity, duration, or exact phenomenology — readers should be cautious about over-defining what these experiences involve. The repeated address "my son" (vv.1, 3, 6) keeps the register personal and pastoral. The promise that "a hair of your head shall not be lost" (v.14) is likely a conventional idiom of divine preservation; whether it is meant literally, temporally, or as final vindication is not specified by the text.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 8–9 are built as a matched antithesis: right answer / wrong answer, burning bosom / stupor of thought, feeling it is right / forgetting the thing that is wrong. This deliberate positive-negative pairing is the chapter's clearest literary feature. D&C 9 also shows verified text-overlap with [[Alma 38]] (3 verses) and [[Alma 36]] (2 verses) — both scenes of a father figure counseling a son — though the specific overlapping language has not yet been identified, and it would be a stretch to characterize Shiblon or Helaman as "struggling" the way this chapter's addressee is; any relationship is better treated as an open question than an established parallel.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Textual Teaching
> Within this translation instruction, D&C 9 strongly supports a model in which disciplined mental effort and petitionary prayer work together: merely asking is criticized, while studying and then asking are commanded. Its immediate scope is sacred translation, so extending it into a universal test for every personal decision requires additional argument.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the immediate translation setting limit or shape the application of verses 8–9 to ordinary decisions?
- What is the difference in this chapter between being corrected, losing a privilege, and being condemned?
- Why does the chapter pair a future possibility of assisting with other records with a firm present reassignment?
- What safeguards are needed before identifying an emotional or cognitive experience as revelation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
