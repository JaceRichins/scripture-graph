# D&C 24 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 24]] · My notes: [[D&C 24 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 24 combines correction, reassurance, and practical commissioning. The unnamed singular addressee is called and delivered but remains accountable for transgression (vv. 1-2). He is assigned writing, scriptural exposition, ordinances, and service to the churches while relying on church support for necessities. Oliver Cowdery is then discussed by name as a witness who must seek glory in Christ rather than himself (vv. 10-12).

The chapter is especially relevant to [[Missionary Work]]. Verses 13-18 use a recognizable New Testament mission pattern involving healing, exorcism, limited travel provisions, dependence on others, and dust as testimony against rejection. Verse 19 describes the commission through final vineyard-pruning imagery closely comparable to [[Jacob 5]]. These intertexts illuminate the chapter's scriptural language but do not by themselves determine whether the reuse is literary, revelatory, or both.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful reading follows the chapter's grammar and subject changes:

1. Verses 1-2: calling, deliverance, rebuke, and the command to sin no more.
2. Verses 3-9: duties of the singular addressee—church visits, writing, [[Scripture Study]], endurance, and ordinances.
3. Verses 10-12: the same addressee is told about Oliver Cowdery's public witness and Christ-centered strength; Oliver is discussed in the third person rather than directly addressed.
4. Verses 13-16: plural instructions concerning [[Miracles]], reception, rejection, and violent opposition.
5. Verses 17-19: singular address resumes with litigation, material provision, and the vineyard commission, which is extended to those ordained.

This organization reflects observable pronoun and subject changes without requiring an elaborate literary design.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Calling does not remove moral accountability. Verses 1-2 place divine deliverance and vocation beside unexcused transgression and the command to sin no more. This supports an interpretation of restoration to continued service without moral exemption, although the chapter does not use the word grace.

Divine presence does not mean immunity from [[Adversity]]. Verse 8 predicts many afflictions and gives the Lord's presence as a reason for [[Endurance]].

Writing, scriptural exposition, ordinances, proclamation, and [[Miracles]] are presented as governed responsibilities. Verses 13-14 plainly restrict requiring miracles, although the grammatical relationship among divine command, the listed signs, the desire of recipients, and fulfillment of scripture is not completely clear.

Verses 3, 9, and 18 connect ordinary labor, specialized calling, and church support. Their immediate setting is a particular commission; the text does not establish a universal rule against temporal labor or settle whether the promised lack of strength is literal, situational, or vocational.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated blessing/curse contrast gives reception of the messengers covenant-like seriousness, while 'as a testimony' in verse 15 clarifies the symbolic function of removing dust. Because 'cursing' recurs in several different settings, its precise force should be studied rather than assumed to denote identical consequences each time.

The phrase 'in mine own due time' is an important restraint within the severe language of verse 16. The missionaries pronounce or request judgment, but the Lord claims control of its execution and timing.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The clearest intertextual feature is the cluster of New Testament mission instructions in verses 13-18. Healing, casting out devils, traveling without ordinary provisions, receiving support, and shaking off dust occur across [[Matthew 10]], [[Mark 6]], [[Luke 9]], and [[Luke 10]]. The wording resembles a combination of accounts rather than one account in every detail: notably, [[Mark 6]] permits a staff, whereas D&C 24:18 agrees with the prohibitory form in [[Matthew 10]] and [[Luke 9]]. This supports purposeful engagement with the biblical mission-discourse tradition, but textual comparison alone cannot decide among direct literary reuse, revealed restoration of an apostolic pattern, or a combination.

The signs involving devils, serpents, deadly substances, and healing also resemble [[Mark 16]]. Readers should be told that the longer ending of Mark containing these verses has disputed manuscript status; this affects claims about the passage's earliest textual history but not its availability in the King James Bible.

Verse 19 shifts to vineyard imagery. Its combination of pruning, shared labor, might, and the last time closely resembles the final-labor sequence in [[Jacob 5]]. The connection is strong and study-worthy, while broader biblical vineyard traditions remain an important alternative source.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How do verses 1-2 qualify any assumption that being chosen excuses a leader's failures?

- What balance between ordinary labor, specialized calling, and community support emerges from verses 3, 9, and 18?

- Does 'require not miracles' chiefly forbid demanding signs from God, performing signs for display, or both?

- Which earlier scriptures are intended by 'that the scriptures might be fulfilled' in verse 14?

- How should the judgment language of verses 4, 6, and 15-17 be interpreted alongside the restraint of 'in mine own due time'?

- Why does the equipment list resemble several Gospel accounts while differing from [[Mark 6]] about carrying a staff?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare D&C 24:13-18 line by line with [[Matthew 10]], [[Mark 6]], [[Luke 9]], and [[Luke 10]], recording both verbal agreements and differences. Compare the signs in verses 13-14 with [[Mark 16]], while noting that the longer ending of Mark has disputed manuscript status. Then compare D&C 24:19 with the final-labor sequence in [[Jacob 5]], especially verses 61-62 and 70-71. These exercises clarify the chapter's scriptural vocabulary but cannot by themselves resolve questions of authorship or revelation.
