# D&C 121 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 121]] · My notes: [[D&C 121 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 121 moves from anguished [[Prayer]] to reassurance, judgment upon oppressors, promised [[Revelation]], and a searching treatment of religious power. The response does not explain why suffering was permitted or promise immediate release. It describes [[Adversity]] as brief within a larger horizon, calls for [[Endurance]], and affirms that God sees the works of persecutors. The closing verses then warn that conferred religious authority does not guarantee heavenly power: unrighteous control grieves the Spirit, while legitimate influence relies on persuasion, patience, gentleness, sincere love, kindness, and pure knowledge.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
One useful analytical outline is:

1. Verses 1–6: lament and plea for intervention.
2. Verses 7–10: peace, endurance, loyal friends, and a qualified comparison with Job.
3. Verses 11–25: warnings and judgment directed toward accusers and persecutors.
4. Verses 26–33: promised knowledge through the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], culminating in the Missouri River image.
5. Verses 34–46: why few are chosen and how [[Priesthood]] influence must operate.

The repetition of “many are called, but few are chosen” in verses 34 and 40 frames the diagnosis in verses 35–39; the positive instruction then continues through verse 46. These divisions are a study aid rather than boundaries explicitly supplied by the text.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verses 36–37 distinguish conferred priesthood rights from the heavenly power associated with their righteous exercise. Attempts to conceal sin, gratify [[Pride]] or vain ambition, or impose unrighteous control cause the heavens and the Spirit to withdraw—“Amen” to the priesthood or authority of that person. The passage does not specify the administrative status of ordination or office after that withdrawal.

Verses 41–46 present the positive alternative: persuasion, long-suffering, gentleness, meekness, sincere love, kindness, pure knowledge, [[Charity]], and virtuous thought. Reproof is narrowly bounded by timing, prompting from the Holy Ghost, and a subsequent increase of love. The closing promise describes righteous dominion as flowing “without compulsory means.”

Verses 28 and 32 also contain unresolved language about “one God or many gods” and a council before the world. Their wording is clear, but the chapter does not provide a complete theological explanation.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter uses substantial biblical diction and imagery. It explicitly invokes Job, repeats the called-and-chosen saying found in [[Matthew 22]], parallels the millstone warning concerning little ones and the “generation of vipers” denunciation, and uses “kick against the pricks.” These parallels strongly establish biblical intertextuality and scriptural register, but they do not determine whether each instance is conscious quotation, remembered idiom, or editorial phrasing—and they do not independently establish divine origin or antiquity.

Verse 4 also uses the transliterated Hebrew term “Sheol.” That lexical fact is noteworthy but, without textual-history evidence, proves nothing about Hebrew competence or authorship. In verse 43, the precise nuance of “sharpness” is less certain than its explicit safeguards: reproof must be timely, prompted by the Holy Ghost, and followed by increased love.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The opening lament asks where God is and repeatedly asks how long suffering will continue. This resembles the broad language of biblical lament, though no specific dependence on a particular psalm is established. Verse 10 invokes Job but limits the comparison by emphasizing the addressee's loyal friends. Verse 33 makes the promise of unstoppable heavenly knowledge concrete through the image of trying to stop or reverse the Missouri River.

A plausible thematic contrast runs from unlawful oppression near the beginning to the rejection of unrighteous dominion and compulsory means near the end. This can illuminate the chapter ethically, but the text does not explicitly identify the later priesthood teaching as a designed inversion of the earlier persecution.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Internal Geographical Specificity *(tentative)*
> The Missouri River comparison gives the promise of revelation a concrete geographical image: obstructing knowledge from heaven is compared to trying to stop or reverse that river. This is modest evidence of situational specificity, not proof of when, where, or by whom the passage was composed.

> [!evidence] Ethical And Literary Inference *(tentative)*
> The transition from condemning persecutors to warning priesthood holders against domination is strong textual evidence that the chapter's moral critique is at least partly self-implicating: suffering injustice does not authorize the sufferer to exercise unjust power.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation

> [!evidence] Internal Literary Coherence *(tentative)*
> The recurrence of knowledge and heavenly bestowal across verses 26–33 and 42–46 is meaningful internal evidence that the revelation and priesthood-influence passages can be read as thematically connected: legitimate heavenly power enlightens and distills rather than coerces.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation

> [!evidence] Internal Normative Teaching *(tentative)*
> As an internal normative standard, verses 36–46 strongly reject the proposition that priesthood office by itself legitimizes coercion. They make righteous conduct, noncompulsory influence, and the presence of the Spirit tests of legitimate priesthood exercise.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted

> [!evidence] Literary-Thematic Coherence *(tentative)*
> The chapter has strong thematic coherence around abusive power: it begins with victims of unlawful oppression, condemns those who drive, murder, and testify against them, and concludes by rejecting unrighteous control and compulsory means among priesthood holders. This supports reading verses 34–46 as an ethical answer to the problem of domination raised earlier in the chapter.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How does “peace be unto thy soul” respond to the lament without resolving every question it raises? Why does the text invoke Job and then immediately qualify the comparison? What distinction does the chapter draw between authority being conferred and heavenly power being available? How do verses 41–46 constrain the permission to reprove in verse 43? Why are “almost all men” and “as they suppose” important qualifications in verse 39? What readings of the language about “one God or many gods” and a premortal council remain possible when later theological developments are not assumed?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
