# D&C 131 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 131]] · My notes: [[D&C 131 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 131 gathers several compact teachings, likely recorded on more than one occasion around May 1843. Verses 1-4 distinguish three internal degrees within celestial glory and make the highest of them conditional on entering 'this order of the priesthood [meaning the new and everlasting covenant of marriage]' — without it, a person 'may enter into the other' degree but 'cannot have an increase.' Verse 5 (marked with its own embedded date, a sign the section compiles more than one saying) defines 'the more sure word of prophecy' as a person's own revealed knowledge that he is sealed up unto eternal life. Verse 6 states tersely that no one can be saved in ignorance. Verses 7-8 teach that there is no such thing as immaterial matter — spirit is real, finer matter, presently beyond ordinary sight but visible once our bodies are purified. Several key terms ('the other,' 'increase') are left undefined within the chapter itself; readers should distinguish what D&C 131 actually states from later clarification found in [[D&C 132]], which more fully develops the marriage covenant just weeks later in the same Nauvoo period.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The section falls into four compact units rather than one continuous argument:
1. **Verses 1-4** — degrees of celestial glory and the marriage condition.
2. **Verse 5** — a definition of the more sure word of prophecy, opening with its own parenthetical date, '(May 17th, 1843.)' — a visible seam suggesting this verse (and what follows) was recorded separately from vv.1-4.
3. **Verse 6** — a terse, free-standing maxim on salvation and ignorance.
4. **Verses 7-8** — the materiality of spirit.

The abrupt transitions between units caution against reading the section as a single unfolding sermon.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Marriage and celestial degrees** (vv.1-4): entering the marriage covenant is necessary to obtain the highest of the three celestial degrees. The text does not say that failing to do so excludes a person from celestial glory altogether — v.4 allows entry into 'the other' degree, only denying 'increase.' Neither 'the other' nor 'increase' is defined within this chapter; fuller claims about eternal posterity or a specific lower kingdom depend on reading [[D&C 132]] and other sources back into this text, and should be labeled as such.

**Revealed assurance and salvation** (vv.5-6): the 'more sure word of prophecy' is defined as a person's own knowledge — gained by [[Revelation]] and the spirit of [[Prophecy]] through priesthood power — that he is sealed up unto eternal life. Verse 6 then states that salvation is impossible in ignorance; whether this continues the specific theme of v.5 or stands as an independent maxim about saving knowledge generally is not settled by the text.

**The materiality of spirit** (vv.7-8): spirit is affirmed to be real matter, only 'more fine or pure' than what unaided eyes can see, becoming visible once the observer's own body is purified. This teaches the substantiality of spirit; it does not by itself specify a complete metaphysics, nor does it explicitly address the embodiment of God.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two expressions require restraint. First, "the other" in verse 4 is singular even though verse 1 names three degrees; the passage does not identify precisely which lower degree is intended or whether the wording compresses both lower possibilities. Second, "increase" is left undefined. Later doctrinal usage may clarify it, but that later meaning should be distinguished from what this chapter explicitly says.

Similarly, "matter" in verses 7–8 should not automatically be assigned every implication of modern scientific materialism. The text's own modifiers—"more fine or pure" and discernible by "purer eyes"—show that it posits a form of matter unlike what ordinary sight presently detects.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The section is aphoristic rather than narrative or argumentative: each unit states a compact maxim (e.g., vv.2-3's if/then structure: 'must enter... if he does not, he cannot obtain it') rather than developing an extended argument. Verses 7-8 turn on present versus future sight ('we cannot see it... we shall see that it is all matter'), a simple contrast rather than a developed literary structure.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Historical/Compositional *(tentative)*

> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · unverified historical claim

> [!evidence] Comparative Theology *(tentative)*
> D&C 131:7's claim that 'there is no such thing as immaterial matter' and that 'all spirit is matter' stands in explicit contrast to classical Christian creedal descriptions of God and spirit as incorporeal — for example, the Westminster Confession of Faith (1646) describes God as a being 'without body, parts, or passions' — marking a clear doctrinal divergence between this teaching and mainstream historic Christian metaphysics.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What does 'the other' degree in v.4 refer to — a lower celestial degree, or something outside celestial glory entirely? The chapter does not say. What does 'increase' mean here, and how does that meaning sharpen (or shift) when read alongside [[D&C 132]]? Does v.6's warning against being 'saved in ignorance' continue v.5's specific theme of revealed assurance, or stand as an independent maxim about saving knowledge generally? What is gained or lost by calling vv.7-8 'materialist,' given the text's own qualifiers ('more fine or pure,' 'purer eyes')?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
