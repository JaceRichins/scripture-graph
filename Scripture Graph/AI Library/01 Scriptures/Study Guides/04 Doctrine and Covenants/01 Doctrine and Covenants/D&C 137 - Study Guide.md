# D&C 137 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 137]] · My notes: [[D&C 137 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 137 records a vision Joseph Smith received of the [[Kingdoms of Glory|celestial kingdom]], including its gate, the blazing throne where the Father and the Son are seated, and its golden streets (vv. 1-4). He sees Adam, Abraham, his parents, and his deceased brother Alvin, and marvels that Alvin obtained a place there despite dying before the Lord's latter-day gathering of Israel and without baptism for the remission of sins (vv. 5-6). The voice of the Lord answers by declaring that those who die without gospel knowledge — whether already dead or yet to die — can become heirs of the celestial kingdom if they would have received the gospel with all their hearts, and that judgment weighs both works and the desires of the heart (vv. 7-9). The chapter closes with an unconditional statement that children who die before the years of accountability are saved in the celestial kingdom (v. 10). The chapter bears directly on [[Kingdoms of Glory]], [[Final Judgment]], and [[Justice and Mercy]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Vision and setting (verses 1-5):** The opened heavens, celestial imagery, the Father and the Son, and recognized persons.

2. **The problem posed by Alvin (verse 6):** His celestial inheritance appears surprising because he died without baptism and before the stated latter-day gathering.

3. **The Lord's answer (verses 7-9):** Gospel opportunity, hypothetical willingness, works, and desires all enter the explanation.

4. **Children before accountability (verse 10):** The text returns to the language of seeing and gives a categorical statement about their salvation.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter teaches that absence of gospel knowledge during mortal life is not presented as automatic exclusion from celestial inheritance. Its condition is not mere ignorance: verses 7-8 specify that the person would have received the gospel, while verse 9 joins works with the desires of the heart. This offers a compact account of judgment sensitive to both conduct and opportunity.

Verse 10 states that children who die before accountability are saved in the celestial kingdom. It does not specify a numerical age, describe the developmental meaning of accountability, or explain how this teaching relates procedurally to [[Baptism]].

Although Alvin's lack of baptism prompts the revelation, the chapter itself does not explain [[Baptism for the Dead]]. That doctrine should not be inserted into verses 7-9 as though it were explicitly stated there.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The wording carefully marks limits and comparisons. In verse 1 the narrator cannot tell whether the experience is bodily or out of the body. The gate is "like unto" flames, and the streets have "the appearance" of gold (verses 2, 4). These expressions firmly establish what was perceived while leaving open whether the imagery should be read as literal celestial material, visionary symbolism, or accommodated description.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Joseph Smith's disclaimer in verse 1 — that he cannot tell whether he was 'in the body or out' — echoes the cautious, first-person epistemic language Paul uses of his own visionary experience in 2 Corinthians 12:2-3. The description of the kingdom's gate as 'circling flames of fire' and its streets as 'paved with gold' places the account within a recognizable biblical genre of apocalyptic heavenly-city imagery; the golden-street image in particular recalls Revelation 21:21, though the text does not otherwise quote Revelation directly, and the parallel may reflect a shared visionary vocabulary rather than deliberate literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What relationship does verse 9 establish between observable works and unfulfilled but sincere desires?
- Does 'would have received it' describe divine foreknowledge of a counterfactual choice, or a concise way of describing someone's settled disposition of heart?
- Why does the Lord's answer promise an inheritance without explaining how Alvin's lack of baptism is resolved — is this vision a genuine forerunner of the later doctrine of [[Baptism for the Dead]], or a separate principle of mercy that the ordinance doctrine builds upon rather than grows out of?
- Does seeing a person in the celestial kingdom describe present location, an assured future inheritance, or symbolic assurance? The text does not explicitly settle the vision's temporal mode.
- Does 'the Lord had set his hand to gather Israel the second time' (v. 6) refer to a specific, datable event, or a general latter-day process?
- How does the fuller account in [[D&C 138]] develop questions about the dead that remain unanswered here?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
