# D&C 136 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 136]] · My notes: [[D&C 136 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
[[D&C 136]] joins revelation, migration planning, and community ethics. The westward movement is organized as a covenant enterprise: leaders are appointed, material responsibilities are distributed, advance parties prepare crops, and companies care for households least able to travel independently. The latter half turns from organization to the character required for the journey—honesty, peaceful speech, stewardship, worship, prayer, humility, and endurance.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful outline is:

1. Verses 1–4: the Camp of Israel and its covenant organization.
2. Verses 5–16: supplies, advance work, proportional care, and named company leaders.
3. Verses 17–19: reassurance concerning enemies, Zion, and self-promoting leadership.
4. Verses 20–29: a practical covenant ethic for property, speech, debt, stewardship, joy, and sorrow.
5. Verses 30–33: trial, chastisement, humility, and spiritual enlightenment.
6. Verses 34–40: persecution, judgment, Joseph Smith, and the continuity of sacred history.
7. Verses 41–42: final charge to the church and its elders.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
D&C 136 presents covenant fidelity as both ritual and practical. The companies covenant to keep God's commandments and walk in his ordinances (vv. 2–4), while the same revelation requires preparation, shared responsibility for vulnerable households, honest handling of debts and lost property, and careful stewardship (vv. 5–10, 20–27). Verse 27 describes possessions as gifts of God entrusted to human stewards.

Joy is directed toward [[Gratitude]] through praise and thanksgiving, while sorrow is brought to God through [[Prayer]] (vv. 28–29). Verses 31–33 connect trial and chastisement with preparation, and teach that wisdom and spiritual enlightenment are sought through [[Humility]] and prayer. Verses 37–39 place Joseph Smith within the revelation's sequence of sacred history and interpret his death as sealing his [[Testimony]] with blood; that is the text's theological interpretation of his death, not independent proof of his claims.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Camp of Israel,” “the God of Abraham and of Isaac and of Jacob,” deliverance from Egypt, and the outstretched divine arm give the chapter a strongly biblical register (vv. 1, 21–22). Together they invite an [[Exodus and Deliverance]] reading of the westward journey and connect the displaced community with Israel's sacred story. These correspondences do not prove that every migration detail reenacts Exodus or that the language could not arise from conventional nineteenth-century biblical usage.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter moves from large-scale administration to ordinary interpersonal conduct. Captains and presidents alone cannot create a trustworthy community if participants break pledges, covet, contend, speak harmfully, conceal unpaid debts, or mishandle entrusted goods (vv. 20–27). This placement makes communal trust part of preparation for the journey.

The leaders over hundreds, fifties, and tens in D&C 136:3, 15 closely parallel the graded organization in [[Exodus 18]], which also includes rulers over thousands. The correspondence is clear; whether D&C 136 consciously adopts that specific biblical model remains uncertain because decimal organization also has practical administrative value. Verse 36's blood that “crieth from the ground” likewise echoes the biblical idiom associated with Abel in Genesis 4:10, without proving direct quotation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Why does the chapter place care for the poor, widows, fatherless children, and military families inside the migration plan rather than outside it as private charity?
2. How does the warning against building oneself up in verse 19 qualify the hierarchy established in verses 3 and 15?
3. What would verses 23–27 require from a community trying to preserve trust under scarcity and displacement?
4. Does the promise of deliverance in verses 17 and 30 mean avoidance of all suffering, or divine preservation of the work despite real losses?
5. How should the collective judgment language of verses 34–36 be read without assigning guilt indiscriminately to every member of a nation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare D&C 136:3,15 with [[Exodus 18]]; D&C 136:21–22 with [[Exodus 3]]; and D&C 136:38–39 with [[D&C 135]]. For the stewardship principle in verse 27, compare [[D&C 104]]. These comparisons distinguish three related features of the chapter: a biblical organizational model, an Exodus identity, and a Restoration interpretation of Joseph Smith's death.
