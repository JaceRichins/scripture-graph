# D&C 135 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 135]] · My notes: [[D&C 135 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 135 is a memorial announcement of the deaths of Joseph and Hyrum Smith at Carthage Jail, Illinois, on June 27, 1844. It combines eyewitness-derived narration (vv.1-2), eulogy (v.3), farewell material connected to [[Ether 12]] (vv.4-5), and a closing argument built on their innocent blood as a [[Testimony]] to the world (vv.6-7). The chapter does not name its author; tradition attributes it chiefly to John Taylor, one of the two Apostles present in the jail (v.2), based on his eyewitness-level detail — but this is a later historical judgment, not a claim the text makes about itself. Readers should distinguish the chapter's concrete historical reporting from its eulogistic and theological judgments, which are the testimony of grieving associates rather than independently demonstrated conclusions.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The attack (vv. 1–2):** date, place, assailants, deaths, wound, and escape.
2. **Joseph's works (v. 3):** a concentrated eulogy centered on scripture, revelation, gathering, and city-building.
3. **Farewell texts (vv. 4–5):** Joseph's anticipated death, Hyrum's reading from [[Ether 12]], and the testament metaphor.
4. **Martyr interpretation (vv. 6–7):** glory, innocent blood, failed civil protection, and anticipated divine judgment.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents sacred witness as something that may carry mortal cost. Its strongest doctrinal emphasis is not that death logically proves a message, but that the authors interpret faithful endurance through death as sealing [[Testimony]]. The quotation from [[Ether 12]] links faithfulness and acknowledged weakness with [[Grace]], clean garments, heavenly rest, and [[Final Judgment]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Legal and documentary terms bind the chapter together: testimony, seal, testators, testament, law, court, state, pledged faith, witness, and magna charta. These terms move the discussion from a jail and a disputed earthly legal order toward divine judgment. The recurring phrase “their innocent blood” then broadens the event from a physical stain at Carthage into a religious and political symbol.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The prose leans on parallelism and antithesis for eulogistic effect — "He lived great, and he died great"; "They lived for glory; they died for glory"; "In life they were not divided, and in death they were not separated!" (v.3, closely echoing the wording used for Saul and Jonathan in 2 Samuel 1:23). Verse 7 builds cumulatively, piling clause upon clause ("their innocent blood on the floor... on the escutcheon... on the banner...") before resolving into an explicit citation of Revelation 6:9-10's martyrs under the altar. Other verses layer in scriptural language without naming a source: Joseph's "lamb to the slaughter" (v.4) recalls Isaiah 53:7's suffering servant, though the same idiom appears elsewhere in the Old Testament (e.g., Jeremiah 11:19) and need not be an exclusive allusion; "a conscience void of offense towards God, and towards all men" (v.4) closely matches Paul's distinctive wording in Acts 24:16; and "if the fire can scathe a green tree... how easy it will burn up the dry trees" (v.6) adapts Jesus's own words en route to his crucifixion in Luke 23:31. These correspondences show deliberate scriptural framing; they do not by themselves establish that every parallel was intended as a precise equation with Christ.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical Specificity
> The names, date, approximate time, location, sequence of deaths, and reported wounds make the account historically specific and testable, but specificity within one memorial account is not independent corroboration of every detail.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.9 · sound epistemic hygiene, well-calibrated · [[Historical Specificity in D&C 135|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Martyrdom And Testimonial Commitment
> Being killed in connection with a religious mission can support the conclusion that the victims bore severe costs associated with that mission, but their deaths do not by themselves demonstrate that all of their religious claims were true.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.9 · well-calibrated evidentiary-significance claim · [[Martyrdom And Testimonial Commitment in D&C 135|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Martyrdom-As-Evidence Argument *(tentative)*
> The chapter argues that the innocent blood of Joseph and Hyrum is 'a broad seal affixed to "Mormonism" that cannot be rejected by any court on earth' and 'a witness to the truth of the everlasting gospel that all the world cannot impeach' (D&C 135:7). Evaluated as an evidentiary claim, martyrdom demonstrates the sincerity and depth of conviction of the martyrs, but it does not by itself establish the truth of the doctrines they professed.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.8 · disputed; overstates what is "established"

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- The chapter shifts from eyewitness narrative (vv.1-2) to eulogy (v.3) to legal-civic argument (v.7). Whom is each register trying to persuade, and what does each contribute?
- What might have drawn Hyrum to the passage he read from [[Ether 12]] that morning — its language of acknowledged weakness, clean garments, and farewell to the Gentiles — without assuming we can know his reasons for certain?
- The chapter repeatedly borrows language associated with Christ's own suffering (Isaiah 53, Luke 23) for Joseph and Hyrum. What is gained, and what risks of overstatement does that borrowing carry?
- D&C 135:7 claims that innocent blood "cannot be rejected by any court on earth" as a witness to truth. What can martyrdom actually demonstrate — sincerity and cost of conviction — and what can it not demonstrate about the truth of the beliefs themselves?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
