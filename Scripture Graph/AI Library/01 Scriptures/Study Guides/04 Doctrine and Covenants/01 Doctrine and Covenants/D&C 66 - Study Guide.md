# D&C 66 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 66]] · My notes: [[D&C 66 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 66 is a personally addressed commission that moves from affirmation to correction and then to service. William E. McLellin has turned from iniquity and received truth, yet he still needs [[Repentance]] (vv. 1–3). The Lord then specifies an eastward mission with Samuel H. Smith (vv. 4–9), gives further moral warnings (v. 10), and closes with promises tied to faithful endurance (vv. 11–13). The chapter therefore joins [[Covenants]], personal holiness, and [[Missionary Work]] rather than treating them as separate concerns.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful reading divides the chapter into four movements:

1. **Reception and spiritual diagnosis (vv. 1–3):** blessing, covenant, and unfinished repentance.
2. **Disclosure of the Lord's will (vv. 4–9):** travel, proclamation, companionship, healing, patience, and prayer.
3. **Moral and vocational fidelity (vv. 10–11):** freedom from encumbrance and unrighteousness, a specific warning about adultery, and the magnifying of an office.
4. **Endurance and divine identification (vv. 12–13):** [[Eternal Life]] is promised for continuing to the end, and Jesus Christ identifies himself as the speaker.

This organization is interpretive, but it follows the chapter's changes in subject and repeated transitional language.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The phrase “clean, but not all” holds affirmation and correction together: William's earlier turning is acknowledged, while further [[Repentance]] remains necessary (D&C 66:1, 3). The text presents this as William's particular situation; broader conclusions about sanctification are interpretations rather than explicit definitions.

The missionary commission combines proclamation, [[Testimony]], reasoning, companionship, laying hands on the sick, [[Prayer]], and patience in [[Adversity]] (D&C 66:5–9). Verse 9 states a healing promise but reports no completed healing and does not explain exceptions; [[D&C 42]] offers relevant broader canonical context.

Verse 10 distinguishes temptation from transgression. It says that adultery was a temptation with which William had been troubled, not that he had committed adultery. The warning is therefore relevant to [[Chastity]] and [[Satan and Temptation]] without supporting a stronger biographical accusation.

The concluding promise connects continued faithfulness with [[Eternal Life]] (D&C 66:12), while the chapter's repeated conditions should not be turned into a claim that every adverse outcome proves unfaithfulness.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Titles of Jesus Christ frame the revelation: “Lord,” “Redeemer,” and “Savior” appear near the beginning, while the closing identifies the speaker as “the Lord your God, your Redeemer, even Jesus Christ” (D&C 66:1, 13).

The ask-and-knock wording in verse 9 belongs to recognizable New Testament prayer language. Its “knock” clause closely parallels [[Matthew 7]], while “Ask, and ye shall receive” also resembles Johannine wording; it is therefore safer to describe the line as shared Gospel or KJV diction than as a quotation from one passage.

More distinctively, “songs of everlasting joy upon their heads” closely echoes the return-to-Zion formula in [[Isaiah 35]] and [[Isaiah 51]]. The correspondence strongly supports biblical intertextuality in the English wording and gives the movement toward [[Zion]] the imagery of joyful return. It does not establish whether either passage was consciously cited or how the wording entered the revelation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does the combination of “clean” and “not all” suggest about the relationship between conversion and continuing sanctification?
- Why does the commission combine testimony and reasoning rather than presenting them as competing approaches?
- How should the healing promise in verse 9 be related to the immediate command to be patient in affliction and to fuller canonical treatments of illness?
- Does “songs of everlasting joy” deliberately invoke both Isaiah passages, or does it reflect scriptural language absorbed into the revelation's general diction?
- What exactly does “push many people to Zion” mean: conversion, migration, spiritual preparation, or some combination?
