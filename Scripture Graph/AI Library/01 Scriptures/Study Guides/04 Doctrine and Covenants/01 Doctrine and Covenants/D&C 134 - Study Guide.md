# D&C 134 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 134]] · My notes: [[D&C 134 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 134 is a collective declaration about civil and religious authority. Its repeated “We believe” formula presents communal principles rather than narrative or direct divine speech, without by itself deciding whether the declaration was considered inspired. Government is said to exist for human benefit and social safety; its laws should protect conscience, property, and life. Civil officers, citizens, and religious societies are each assigned powers and limits, making bounded jurisdiction a central theme. This connects especially with [[Government and Laws]] and [[Freedom and Liberty]].

Verse 12 creates a serious unresolved moral tension. It defers preaching and baptism among bond-servants to masters’ wishes and condemns influence that might make them dissatisfied, partly in the name of public peace. The provision therefore accommodates a slaveholding legal order and restricts enslaved persons’ religious choice. It does not by itself declare slavery morally good, but a possible safety-based explanation does not remove the restriction. The tension should be examined alongside D&C 101:79.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful thematic outline is:

1. **Purposes of civil order (vv. 1–3):** government serves society; law protects conscience, property, and life; just officers enforce law.
2. **Conscience and reciprocal obligation (vv. 4–7):** government may restrain harmful conduct but not dictate worship; citizens support governments that protect their rights; civil and divine law occupy distinguishable domains.
3. **Coercion and jurisdiction (vv. 8–10):** civil law punishes crime, government must not privilege one religion, and churches may impose only fellowship-based discipline.
4. **Remedy, defense, and the troubling servitude provision (vv. 11–12):** legal redress is primary, emergency defense is permitted, and missionary action among bond-servants is restricted.

This arrangement highlights the chapter’s recurring pattern: it first recognizes an authority or duty and then limits its scope.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter supports several carefully bounded propositions. Civil order is morally accountable and should serve the governed (vv. 1–3). Conscience and worship have a protected sphere, although religious claims do not authorize injury, sedition, or conspiracy (vv. 4, 7). Civic loyalty is linked to governmental protection of rights (v. 5). Religious discipline is legitimate only within the domain of fellowship and standing, not property, bodily punishment, or life (v. 10). Legal process is the ordinary response to injury, with self-defense reserved for urgent situations in which legal relief is unavailable (v. 11).

These propositions connect naturally with [[Government and Laws]], [[Freedom and Liberty]], and [[War and Peace]]. They should not be expanded into claims that every government act is divinely approved or that religious conduct is wholly exempt from neutral laws protecting others.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The most consequential words are often the qualifiers: “unless” in verse 4, “while” in verse 5, “so long as” in verse 7, “provided” in verse 10, and “where” in verse 11. Removing them would transform limited principles into absolutes. Close reading therefore resists both unlimited state power and unlimited claims of conscience, institutional religion, civic obedience, or private defense.

The repeated “We believe” formula presents collective convictions, while antithetical clauses—restrain crime but never control conscience; punish guilt but never suppress freedom—make institutional boundaries memorable.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter’s coherence comes from balanced oppositions: government and citizen, civil law and divine law, crime and conscience, public punishment and ecclesiastical discipline, legal appeal and emergency defense. These are not perfect binaries, because conduct motivated by religion can cross into civil harm. The literary pattern nevertheless directs the reader to ask which institution has jurisdiction and what limits accompany its power.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Ethical And Intra-Textual Tension
> Verse 12 is strong textual evidence that the declaration accommodated governments and masters who permitted human servitude: it prohibited preaching to or baptizing bond-servants contrary to their masters’ wishes and justified the restriction partly by appeal to public peace. This creates a serious moral tension with the chapter’s language of inherent rights.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Direct textual accommodation; moral significance candidly stated · [[Ethical And Intra-Textual Tension in D&C 134|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Context *(tentative)*
> Verse 12's refusal to proselytize bond-servants against their masters' will, and its warning that such interference is 'dangerous to the peace of every government allowing human beings to be held in servitude,' is generally read by historians against the backdrop of the Church's 1833 expulsion from Jackson County, Missouri, where Mormons had been accused by local settlers of abolitionist sympathies; the 1835 declaration is understood as partly aimed at reducing this specific source of communal conflict.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · Historically plausible context; causal motive unverified

> [!evidence] Historical/Authorship *(tentative)*
> Historians commonly attribute primary authorship of D&C 134 to Oliver Cowdery rather than to dictation by Joseph Smith; this is consistent with the section's declarative 'We believe' form (see genre-declaration-not-revelation) and its adoption by vote of the assembled Church rather than presentation as personal revelation.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Does “instituted of God” in verse 1 describe the need for government, or does it authorize particular governments and policies?
2. How does the conditional word “while” in verse 5 affect the scope of civic obedience?
3. Where should law draw the boundary between protected belief and conduct that infringes another person’s rights?
4. What safeguards keep the emergency defense permitted in verse 11 from becoming private vengeance?
5. How should verse 12 be evaluated beside the declaration’s own language of inherent rights and D&C 101:79’s rejection of bondage?
6. What is lost if a safety-based historical explanation of verse 12 is mistaken for a moral justification of its restriction?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare D&C 134:3 with [[Mosiah 29]], especially Mosiah 29:25–29, on judges, justice, and the voice of the people. Compare D&C 134:5 with D&C 98:4–7 on support for laws that preserve rights and freedom. Read D&C 134:11 beside Alma 43:45–47 for another treatment of defensive force. Most importantly, place D&C 134:12 beside D&C 101:77–80, especially verse 79, and examine rather than conceal the resulting tension over bondage, liberty, and religious agency.
