# D&C 3 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful three-part reading is:

1. **Divine constancy and human fallibility (vv. 1–4):** God's purposes do not fail, though human work can fail through pride and self-direction.
2. **Rebuke, consequence, and renewed calling (vv. 5–15):** Joseph's fear of people and mishandling of what was sacred lead to temporary loss, but repentance remains possible.
3. **The enduring purpose of the record (vv. 16–20):** “Nevertheless” turns from individual failure to the continuing work and its Christ-centered purpose.

The return of the opening theme in verse 16 binds the rebuke to the concluding explanation rather than leaving it as an isolated reprimand.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds accountability and [[Justice and Mercy]] together. Joseph's conduct has serious consequences, and refusal to repent could cost him his gift; nevertheless, divine mercy leaves renewed service possible. Calling is therefore joined to accountability rather than immunity from it.

Verse 20 presents an intended salvation sequence: recipients are to learn the promises, believe the gospel, rely upon the merits of Jesus Christ, exercise [[Faith]] in his name, repent, and thereby be saved. Reliance on Christ's merits stands in thematic contrast to the boasting in personal strength condemned in verse 4. This is a literary and doctrinal reading of the chapter, not independent evidence for the historicity of its named peoples or predictions.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several repeated contrasts organize the chapter: God's work versus the work of men; straight divine paths versus crookedness; God's counsel versus personal judgment; fear of God versus fear of people; personal strength versus the merits of Jesus Christ; temporary human failure versus an enduring divine purpose. The doubled imperative “Remember, remember” in verse 3 anticipates the repeated command to remember in verses 5 and 10, making remembrance a response to fear, pride, and discouragement.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Structure
> The recurrence of the claim that God's work continues—first in the opening thesis and again after the rebuke—strongly supports reading the chapter as a unified movement from divine constancy, through human failure, to continued divine purpose.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 1.0 · Structure accepted; compositional implications limited · [[Literary Structure in D&C 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Textual Evidence
> Verses 17–20 are strong internal evidence for what the revelation claims about the plates' intended beneficiaries and purpose, but they are not independent external evidence that every named people existed historically or that any present population can be identified with them.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Epistemic distinction unanimously affirmed · [[Internal Textual Evidence in D&C 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Extra-Textual Historical Identification *(tentative)*
> LDS historical tradition (Joseph Smith's own history and this section's traditional heading) identifies the 'wicked man' to whom sacred, translated material was delivered up (vv12-13) as Martin Harris, who lost the 116-page manuscript of the translated 'Book of Lehi' in June 1828. The text of D&C 3 itself, however, never names Joseph Smith's surname, Martin Harris, or the 116 pages — this identification is entirely extra-textual.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · Traditional identification accepted; sourcing incomplete

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the distinction between God's work and human work preserve both confidence in God and accountability for human choices?
- What do 'still chosen,' 'again called,' and the warning of losing the gift imply about the conditional nature of a calling?
- Does 'the gift' mean specifically the power to translate, or something broader?
- What were the 'strict commandments' mentioned in verse 5, and can they be identified without using sources outside this chapter?
- How does reliance on Christ's merits in verse 20 relate to the warning against boasting in personal strength in verse 4?
- Why does the chapter move from Joseph's individual failure to the multigenerational purpose attributed to the plates?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[D&C 10]] for a fuller treatment of how the translation project could continue despite human interference. Compare [[Alma 37]] for another account of sacred records entrusted to a steward and preserved for a wise purpose. Proverbs 29:25 sharpens the warning about fearing people, while Isaiah 55:10–11 provides a biblical parallel to the assurance that divine purposes will be accomplished. Finally, 2 Nephi 2:8 helps clarify the Book of Mormon's broader use of “merits” language in relation to [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]].
