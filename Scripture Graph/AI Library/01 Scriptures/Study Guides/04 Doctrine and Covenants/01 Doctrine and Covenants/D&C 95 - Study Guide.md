# D&C 95 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 95]] · My notes: [[D&C 95 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
[[D&C 95]] is a corrective revelation centered on a neglected command to build a sacred house. Its rebuke is severe, but the stated purpose of chastening includes forgiveness and deliverance from temptation. The chapter renews the building command and describes the house as a place for worship, instruction, apostolic preparation, and endowment with power.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves through four overlapping stages:

1. **Loving rebuke (vv. 1–3):** chastening is explained, and neglect of the house command is named as grievous sin.
2. **Purposes and further failures (vv. 4–10):** apostolic preparation, solemn assembly, promised power, spiritual darkness, and contention are addressed.
3. **Conditional recommissioning (vv. 11–12):** obedience is associated with power to build; disobedience with loss and darkness.
4. **Building instructions (vv. 13–17):** the house is to follow a divinely shown pattern, receive specified dimensions, and provide space for worship and apostolic schooling.

The phrase “a very grievous sin” recurs in verses 3, 6, and 10. This is a clear threefold rhetorical recurrence, although the audiences may overlap.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents chastening as capable of serving loving and restorative purposes, linking it with [[Repentance and Forgiveness]] and deliverance from temptation. It also distinguishes being ordained or called from being chosen, though it does not fully explain how chosen status is obtained or changed.

Obedience is directly connected with power to build, while disobedience is connected with walking in darkness. The sacred house brings together [[Temples]], [[Sacrament]], [[Fasting]], [[Prayer]], [[Education and Learning]], and preparation for service in the vineyard. These activities portray worship, instruction, and outward labor as related purposes.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Darkness at noon-day” is a pointed paradox in which abundant outward light coincides with spiritual failure. Verse 12 returns to the broader image of walking in darkness.

Verse 7 supplies the text's own unusual interpretation of “Lord of Sabaoth” as “the creator of the first day, the beginning and the end.” This internal gloss should be distinguished from questions about the term's historical Hebrew meaning, which require external philological evidence. Verse 17 similarly presents “Son Ahman,” “Alphus,” and “Omegus” before explicitly identifying the speaker as Jesus Christ. The origins and etymologies of these forms cannot be settled from this chapter alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The revelation uses several recognizable scriptural expressions. The called-and-chosen wording parallels [[Matthew 22]]; the “strange act” parallels [[Isaiah 28]]; final vineyard labor parallels [[Jacob 5]]; and the Father's promise, tarrying at Jerusalem, and power from on high closely parallel [[Luke 24]]. Together these establish a strongly scriptural idiom. They may reflect deliberate literary modeling, but familiar scriptural diction and readers' thematic synthesis remain important alternative explanations. The parallels do not establish any larger theory of revelation or authorship.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should the assured love of verse 1 be related to the warning about the Father's love in verse 12?
- Does “chosen” in verses 5 and 8 describe a fixed status, a role conditioned on faithfulness, or both?
- Which features of the house are rejected as “the manner of the world”: architectural style, social purpose, conduct within the building, or some combination?
- Why does the revelation specify three appointed designers without naming them in the chapter?
- How do solemn assembly, fasting, mourning, sacrament, preaching, prayer, and schooling collectively define the intended sacred community?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the earlier house command in [[D&C 88]], especially D&C 88:119, with the rebuke and renewed instructions here. Compare D&C 95:8–9 with [[Luke 24]], especially Luke 24:49, to examine the Jerusalem pattern of tarrying and receiving power. The phrase-level parallels in [[Matthew 22]], [[Isaiah 28]], and [[Jacob 5]] are also useful, provided they are treated as non-exclusive literary connections rather than proof of a larger theory of authorship.
