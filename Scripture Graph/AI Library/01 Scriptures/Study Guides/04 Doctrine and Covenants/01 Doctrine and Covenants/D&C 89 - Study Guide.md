# D&C 89 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 89]] · My notes: [[D&C 89 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 89, popularly known as the [[Word of Wisdom]], was given through Joseph Smith in Kirtland, Ohio (traditionally dated 27 February 1833) to the council of high priests and the Church. It presents itself as "a principle with promise" concerning "the temporal salvation of all saints," sent "not by commandment or constraint, but by revelation and the word of wisdom" (vv. 1-3). The chapter combines prohibitions, limited or qualified uses, affirmative provisions, and governing virtues: wine and strong drink are judged unfit for ordinary drinking, with a sacramental exception for homemade grape wine (vv. 5-6); tobacco is rejected as food but allowed a limited medicinal or veterinary use (v. 8); "hot drinks" are likewise rejected, though the text never names coffee or tea (v. 9); and wholesome herbs, fruit, meat, and grain are each ordained for use but qualified by season, restraint, prudence, and thanksgiving (vv. 10-17). It closes with promises of health, hidden wisdom, endurance, and protection likened to Israel's deliverance from "the destroying angel" (vv. 18-21). The chapter is thus broader than a simple list of banned substances: it ties bodily practice to [[Gratitude]] and [[Obedience]] as parts of a single "temporal salvation."
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. **Audience, genre, and purpose (vv. 1–3):** a word of wisdom for temporal salvation, described as a principle with promise.
2. **Reason for warning (v. 4):** present and future harmful designs.
3. **Things restricted or assigned limited uses (vv. 5–9):** wine, strong drink, tobacco, and hot drinks.
4. **Foods ordained for use (vv. 10–17):** herbs, fruit, flesh, and grain, governed by thanksgiving, prudence, season, and restraint.
5. **Conditional promises (vv. 18–21):** health, knowledge, endurance, and Passover-like protection.

The movement is from purpose, to warning, to concrete practices, to promised consequences.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's theology is strongly embodied: divine instruction addresses food, drink, health, knowledge, and protection together. “Temporal salvation” in verse 2 therefore should not be reduced either to physical wellness alone or to an exclusively inward spirituality. Verses 18–21 also connect promised blessings with remembering, doing, and walking in [[Obedience]]. That conditional language does not itself justify treating illness as proof of disobedience; the passage does not explain every cause of sickness or promise that faithful people will never suffer.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several textual specifics deserve attention because later tradition sometimes glosses over them. The chapter bans "wine or strong drink" as a beverage (v. 5) but requires wine "of your own make" for the sacrament (v. 6), and treats strong drink as fit for external "washing of your bodies" rather than for drinking (v. 7). It names only "hot drinks" (v. 9) - never coffee or tea - and permits barley "for mild drinks" (v. 17) without defining how "mild" differs from the banned "strong drink" (v. 5). The "only" clauses in verses 13 and 15, together with the pronoun "these" in verse 15, also raise a genuine syntactic question: context favors reading them as an unusually restrictive rule limiting meat chiefly to times of winter, cold, or famine, but the antecedent is not airtight. These gaps matter because later interpretive tradition - identifying "hot drinks" with coffee and tea, and reading the chapter as requiring total abstinence from alcohol - fills in specifics the 1833 text does not spell out on its own.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The dietary instructions in verses 7-10 (strong drink, tobacco, hot drinks, herbs) are bound together by the repeated formula "And again," giving this portion of the revelation a catalogue-like rhythm; the instructions on meat (v. 12, "Yea") and grain (v. 14, "All grain") that follow drop the formula and shift tone. The conclusion draws on two recognizable biblical patterns: D&C 89:20 closely parallels [[Isaiah 40]], where renewed strength includes running without weariness and walking without fainting, and D&C 89:21 explicitly invokes the children of Israel and the destroying angel, linking the promise to [[Exodus 12]] and [[Passover]]. These connections illuminate the rhetoric of endurance and deliverance; they do not require every detail of the chapter to map onto Isaiah or the Exodus narrative.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Scriptural Intertextuality
> The destroying-angel promise is explicit textual evidence of a deliberate Passover allusion: the revelation itself compares the promised deliverance to that of the children of Israel.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · best-supported version of the Passover connection · [[Explicit Scriptural Intertextuality in D&C 89|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Reminiscence *(tentative)*
> A widely repeated Latter-day Saint historical account (a reminiscence given by Brigham Young in 1868, decades after the fact) attributes the occasion of the revelation to Joseph Smith's reaction to tobacco use and spitting by men attending School of the Prophets meetings in Kirtland, reportedly after a complaint from Emma Smith about cleaning up the meeting room.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, single late reminiscence

> [!evidence] Historical/Institutional Development *(tentative)*
> The common identification of 'hot drinks' with coffee and tea, and the modern total-abstinence application of the Word of Wisdom (including its use as a temple-recommend and membership standard), developed through Church teaching and practice over subsequent decades rather than being stated explicitly in the 1833 text.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · believing scholarship, needs dates/sources

> [!evidence] Historical Scholarship *(tentative)*
> Historians of Mormonism (notably Thomas G. Alexander's study of the Word of Wisdom) document that strict, total-abstinence enforcement of D&C 89 - including its use as a worthiness standard - was formalized institutionally in the early twentieth century, well after 1833, and that practice among nineteenth-century Latter-day Saints (including some leaders) was more variable than later practice.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · believing scholarship, imprecise as stated

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should the chapter's own disclaimer ("not by commandment or constraint," v. 2) be related to its later formal use as a temple-recommend and membership standard, and to the language of "the commandments" in v. 18?
- What is the antecedent of "these" in verse 15, and how do the "only" clauses in verses 13 and 15 qualify the general permission for meat in verse 12?
- Does "mild drinks" from barley (v. 17) refer to a low-alcohol or a non-alcoholic beverage, and how does that relate to the ban on "strong drink" (v. 5)?
- What information beyond this chapter is needed to identify "hot drinks," and how did the later identification with coffee and tea develop?
- What can plausibly be inferred about the "conspiring men" of verse 4, given the concerns of the early nineteenth-century American temperance movement?
- Should the concluding promises (vv. 18-21) be read as ordinary consequences, covenantal assurances, poetic biblical idiom, or some combination?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare this chapter's sacramental wine instructions with [[D&C 27]], its endurance promise with [[Isaiah 40]], and its destroying-angel imagery with [[Exodus 12]] and [[Passover]] - each a well-grounded textual parallel. Thematic (not verified textual) comparisons are also worth exploring: [[D&C 49]] and [[D&C 59]] similarly affirm created things, including food, as gifts requiring restraint rather than excess, and [[Daniel 1]] connects dietary faithfulness with health and wisdom, though its specific diet and narrative setting differ substantially from D&C 89.
