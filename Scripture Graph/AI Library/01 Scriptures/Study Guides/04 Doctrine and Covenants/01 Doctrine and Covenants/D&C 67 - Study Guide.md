# D&C 67 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[D&C 67]] · My notes: [[D&C 67 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
D&C 67 responds to elders assembled to consider revelations compiled for the Book of Commandments. The Lord acknowledges their prayers and desires (vv.1-2) but names fear, not disbelief, as what kept them from a blessing they sought (v.3). Verses 4-9 offer a [[Testimony]] of the commandments' truth: the elders knew Joseph Smith's language and imperfections and wondered whether they could improve on his wording, so the wisest among them is invited to try producing something like the least of the commandments (vv.5-8) — success would justify continued doubt, failure would obligate them to bear record the commandments are true. The commandments' claimed authority rests on their being free of unrighteousness and coming 'from the Father of lights' (v.9), echoing James 1:17. The section then turns from the text to the elders themselves: a vision of God is promised once they strip away jealousy and fear and grow sufficiently humble (v.10), but they cannot yet abide God's presence or angelic ministry and are told to continue in patience until perfected (vv.11-13), with the promise deferred to 'mine own due time' (v.14).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. Vv.1-4: address to the assembled elders, diagnosis of fear as the obstacle to blessing, and announcement of a testimony. 2. Vv.5-9: acknowledgment of Joseph Smith's known imperfections, a conditional challenge to produce a commandment comparable to 'the least' in the collection, and a claim that the commandments' authority rests on their righteous origin. 3. Vv.10-14: the concern shifts from the text to the elders' own spiritual readiness — a conditional promise to see God, an explanation of why they cannot yet abide his presence, and a closing exhortation with a deferred promise. The challenge in vv.6-8 uses a compact conditional structure (succeed and doubt is excused; fail and testimony is owed) but supplies no criteria for judging 'the least' commandment, who is 'most wise,' or what counts as 'like unto it.'
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Two threads run through the chapter. First, the elders' doubt centered on Joseph Smith's language, which the text concedes was imperfect (v.5), yet the commandments' claimed authority is tied instead to their being free of unrighteousness and 'from the Father of lights' (v.9) — the text juxtaposes these ideas without fully explaining how far human imperfection may extend into revealed wording. Second, vv.10-13 teach that no 'natural man,' 'after the carnal mind,' can abide the presence of God; even ordained elders are 'not able to abide the presence of God now, neither the ministering of angels,' and must 'continue in patience' toward a future, Spirit-quickened capacity rather than an immediate one.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verse 5 names Joseph Smith's 'language' and 'imperfections' plainly rather than presenting the revelations as flawless dictation, which frames the challenge of vv.6-8 as plausibly a test of content rather than polish — though the text does not say explicitly that only content, not expression, is at issue. Verses 10-12 build a deliberate contrast among 'carnal,' 'natural,' and 'spiritual' to describe the kind of perception required to see God.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 9's 'cometh down from above, from the Father of lights' closely echoes James 1:17, and verse 11's 'no man has seen God at any time in the flesh, except quickened' echoes the cadence of John 1:18, both repurposing familiar biblical idiom. Verse 12's 'natural man' language belongs to the same tradition as 1 Corinthians 2:14 and Mosiah 3:19 ('the natural man is an enemy to God'), and the promise of spiritual sight in vv.10-12 parallels Moses 1:11, where Moses says his natural eyes could not have beheld God without being quickened by the Spirit. As a literary device, the imitation challenge in vv.6-8 is rhetorically forceful but methodologically limited: the chapter supplies no stated standard of comparison, names no outcome, and inability to imitate a text would not, on its own, establish divine origin.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Internal Textual Argument *(tentative)*
> D&C 67:5 and 67:9, read together, ground the revelations' claimed authority in their moral/spiritual origin ('no unrighteousness in them... cometh down from above') rather than in the polish or precision of Joseph Smith's own language, whose 'imperfections' are conceded outright. This textual pairing supports (but does not by itself establish) a reading of Latter-day Saint revelation as compatible with prophetic linguistic fallibility rather than requiring verbal inerrancy.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What was 'the blessing which was offered' in v.3? What makes a commandment 'the least' in v.6, and what would make a new composition sufficiently 'like unto it'? Does 'they are true' in v.8 mean divine origin, moral righteousness, doctrinal content, or all three? What specifically was 'conferred upon you by the hands of my servant Joseph Smith' in v.14 — priesthood ordination is a plausible contextual inference from v.10's 'ordained unto this ministry,' but the text does not say so directly.
