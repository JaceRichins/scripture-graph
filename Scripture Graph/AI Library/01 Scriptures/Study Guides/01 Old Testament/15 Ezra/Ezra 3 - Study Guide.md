## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Gathering and altar (vv.1-3)** — the people assemble at Jerusalem, rebuild the altar, and offer sacrifices despite fear of surrounding peoples.
2. **Ordered worship (vv.4-6)** — the Feast of Tabernacles and recurring/voluntary offerings resume; the narrator notes the temple foundation was 'not yet laid' (v.6), so worship precedes structure.
3. **Preparation and labor (vv.7-9)** — materials, skilled workers, and Levitical supervision (age twenty and up) are organized under Cyrus's grant.
4. **Foundation ceremony (vv.10-11)** — priests and Levites praise and give thanks 'after the ordinance of David king of Israel' when the foundation is laid.
5. **Joy and lament (vv.12-13)** — shouting and weeping become indistinguishable in the assembled crowd; the text does not state why the elders wept.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Worship precedes infrastructure.** The returnees rebuild the altar and resume [[Sacrifice]] under the [[Law of Moses]] before the temple foundation is even laid (Ezra 3:2-6), suggesting covenant life need not wait for ideal conditions.
- **Continuity of pattern and priesthood.** Offerings follow what 'is written,' and the dedication liturgy follows 'the ordinance of David king of Israel' (v.10) — the narrative presents renewed worship as restoration of inherited practice, not improvisation.
- **Corporate, not purely private, response.** The people gather, offer, and celebrate 'as one man' and 'together,' reflecting [[Gathering of Israel]] as communal covenant renewal — though vv.12-13 show that unity did not mean uniform emotion.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase “as it is written” anchors the renewed practices in an authoritative written tradition. “As one man” depicts collective action, but should not be pressed to mean that every participant felt identically: verses 12–13 explicitly record sharply different emotional responses. The final contrast between “the shout of joy” and “the weeping” gives equal narrative audibility to celebration and loss.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 13's description of indistinguishable weeping and shouting is the chapter's most distinctive moment: rather than resolving which emotion is 'correct,' the narrator reports the sound as it was heard, preserving the community's genuine ambivalence at a long-awaited but visibly incomplete restoration. The text also uses repetition to anchor new worship in old precedent: 'as it is written' (vv.2, 4) and the refrain 'because he is good, for his mercy endureth for ever' (v.11, echoing [[1 Chronicles 16]]) both root the returnees' practice in prior sacred language rather than novel composition. Why the elders wept is not stated in the text itself; readers should hold multiple explanations (loss, memory, relief, comparison to the first temple) as open rather than settled.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the text emphasize that offerings resumed *before* the temple foundation existed (v.6)? What might this suggest about priorities in rebuilding a religious community?
- Does the 'fear... because of the people of those countries' (v.3) explain why the altar was built, describe an obstacle overcome despite it, or both? The wording permits more than one reading.
- Why did 'the ancient men, that had seen the first house' weep (v.12)? The text names their memory but not their reason — grief over scale, loss of the former temple/monarchy, or emotional overwhelm are all plausible.
- What does the joint appeal to Moses (vv.2,4) and David (v.10) communicate about how the returned community understood continuity after captivity?
