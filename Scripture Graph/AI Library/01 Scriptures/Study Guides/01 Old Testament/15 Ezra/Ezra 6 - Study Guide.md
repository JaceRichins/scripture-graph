# Ezra 6 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Darius renews the decree of Cyrus to build the temple—It is finished and dedicated, and sacrifices and feasts commence again.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter falls into four movements:

1. **The record recovered (vv. 1-5)** — the archival search and the text of Cyrus's original decree: dimensions, funding, and restoration of the temple vessels.
2. **Darius's decree enforced (vv. 6-12)** — noninterference, full funding from royal tribute, supplies for daily offerings, prayer for the king, and a curse formula against violators.
3. **Compliance and completion (vv. 13-18)** — Tatnai's officials obey "speedily"; the temple is finished on a specific date (3 Adar, Darius's 6th year), with credit given jointly to royal decree and the prophesying of Haggai and Zechariah ([[Prophets]]); priests and Levites are organized for service "as it is written in the book of Moses" ([[Law of Moses]]).
4. **Restored worship (vv. 19-22)** — purification, the returned community's [[Passover]], and the theological framing of royal favor as the LORD's own doing.

The movement from imperial paperwork to temple liturgy mirrors the book of Ezra's larger argument: political permission secures and funds covenant worship, without itself constituting it.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Divine sovereignty over foreign rulers.** The chapter's final line credits the LORD with turning "the heart of the king" toward the temple project (v.22), echoing Ezra 1:1's claim that the LORD stirred Cyrus to issue the original decree. Ezra 6:14 credits both "the commandment of the God of Israel" and the commands of Cyrus, Darius, and Artaxerxes side by side, without collapsing one into the other — restoration is located in divine providence working through, not around, imperial politics ([[Government and Laws]]).
- **Continuity of covenant identity.** The sin offering of twelve he-goats "according to the number of the tribes of Israel" (v.17) is the text's own explanation that the offering represented all Israel — a statement of symbolic and covenantal continuity with the whole nation. The chapter does not claim members of every tribe were physically present ([[Gathering of Israel]], [[Abrahamic Covenant]]).
- **Authority of revealed law.** Priestly and Levitical organization (v.18) is anchored explicitly to "the book of Moses," distinguishing civil permission (Persian) from religious authority (Mosaic) as two separate, cooperating sources ([[Law of Moses]]). The Passover that follows (vv.19-20) keeps the same Mosaic timing without the text repeating the "book of Moses" citation there.
- **Worship and imperial reciprocity.** The decree supplies sacrificial materials so priests may "pray for the life of the king, and of his sons" (v.10) — Persian patronage of the Jerusalem cult is explicitly bound up with a request for intercession ([[Prayer]]).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter continues the Aramaic royal-correspondence section running from Ezra 4:8 through 6:18 (the surrounding narrative is Hebrew) — a genuine bilingual seam reflecting the book's use of official documents as source material. Within that material, "God of heaven" is the term used in the royal instructions about offerings and prayer (vv.9-10), while "house of God" and "God of Israel" dominate elsewhere in the chapter; the text does not explain the variation, but it is consistent with Persian chancery idiom sitting alongside Israelite religious language. Recurring formulaic phrases — "without fail," "let it be done with speed" — read as standard Achaemenid administrative diction. Verse 22's designation of the ruling king as "the king of Assyria" is a genuine anomaly worth flagging as an open question: the chapter elsewhere calls him Darius and links him, with Cyrus and Artaxerxes, to Persia (v.14), so the shift to "Assyria" is unexplained by the text itself.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 3-5 present the text of Cyrus's decree as recovered from the archives, followed by Darius's own reissued and strengthened decree — administrative writing that authorizes construction, paralleling how sacred writing ("the book of Moses," v.18) later authorizes worship. The sequence of archival search, recovered record, royal directive, and official compliance gives the narrative's legitimation of the rebuilding a documentary character, though this pattern alone cannot establish the historical provenance or exact wording of either document. The curse formula closing Darius's decree (vv.11-12 — timber pulled from the offender's house and used against him, his house turned to a dunghill) is consistent with severe sanctions attested in other ancient royal and treaty texts, though no specific parallel document is cited here. Note also the puzzling reference to "the king of Assyria" in v.22 (see Uncertainties) — worth flagging as an open textual question rather than resolving artificially.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary Evidence
> The sequence of archival search, recovered memorandum, royal directive, and official compliance provides strong evidence that the narrative is constructed to legitimate the rebuilding through recognized administrative authority. This literary function does not by itself authenticate every reported decree as a preserved historical document.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · well-hedged literary evidence claim, matches real scholarly debate · [[Internal Literary Evidence in Ezra 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Geography / Administrative Background *(tentative)*
> Achmetha is the biblical Aramaic name for Ecbatana, the Median city that served as the Achaemenid Persian kings' summer residence; that the decree-copy turns up there rather than at Babylon (where the search began) fits the known Persian practice of royal courts and archives moving seasonally between capitals (Babylon, Susa, Ecbatana, Persepolis).
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · Achmetha=Ecbatana standard; seasonal-archive claim unsourced

> [!evidence] Extrabiblical Corroboration Of General Royal Policy *(tentative)*
> The general policy Cyrus is credited with here — funding the restoration of a subject people's central sanctuary and returning cultic vessels taken by a prior conqueror — matches the ideology of religious restoration attested in the Cyrus Cylinder, an authentic sixth-century Babylonian inscription describing Cyrus returning displaced gods/cult objects to their sanctuaries and repatriating peoples.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.8 · real source, overstated match to Ezra's specifics

> [!evidence] Literary/Legal Genre Parallel *(tentative)*
> The decree closes with a curse formula against anyone who alters it (destruction of house, impalement/hanging on its own timber, and reduction of the offender's house to a dunghill), a rhetorical pattern paralleled in other ancient Near Eastern royal decrees and treaty curses that threaten violators with reversal of their own household and property.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.8 · curse clause real, no specific ANE parallel cited

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does v.14 name Artaxerxes alongside Cyrus and Darius as commanding the temple's completion, when Artaxerxes' reign (per the book's later chapters) began after the building itself was finished under Darius (v.15)? Is the verse summarizing the whole restoration project rather than the building alone?
- What accounts for the difference between the 60-cubit height and breadth decreed in v.3 and the smaller dimensions recorded for Solomon's original temple (1 Kings 6:2 gives 60 x 20 x 30)? Possibilities include a maximum authorized allowance rather than a required size, a different unit convention, or a transmission error — the text does not decide among them.
- Why is the Persian king called "the king of Assyria" in v.22? Is this an inherited imperial title, a rhetorical reference to the ruling Mesopotamian empire generally, an editorial trace of an older source, or a copying error?
- Who are those who "separated themselves... from the filthiness of the heathen of the land" in v.21 — Israelites who had remained in the land, converts, or some other group? The verse does not specify.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
For the Cyrus Cylinder as background to Persian religious-restoration policy generally (not specific to Jerusalem), see the British Museum's published translation and commentary on the cylinder (object ME 90920) — note that the Cylinder never names Jerusalem, Judah, or the God of Israel, so it corroborates a general pattern of policy rather than this specific decree's wording. Compare the Passover of vv.19-22 with its Mosaic basis in [[Numbers 9]] and with the two earlier reform-era Passovers it resembles, [[2 Chronicles 30]] (Hezekiah) and [[2 Chronicles 35]] (Josiah), both of which pair national purification with renewed Passover observance. Compare the official inquiry in [[Ezra 5]] with the archival answer and enforcement here in [[Ezra 6]].
