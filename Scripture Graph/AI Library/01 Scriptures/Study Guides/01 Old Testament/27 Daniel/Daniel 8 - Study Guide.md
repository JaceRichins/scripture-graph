# Daniel 8 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Daniel 8]] · My notes: [[Daniel 8 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Daniel sees in vision a ram (Media and Persia), a goat (Greece), four other kings, and then, in the last days, a fierce king who will destroy the holy people—This king will be broken when he stands up against the Prince of Princes.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Setting and vision of imperial conflict (8:1-8):** Daniel sees the ram's expansion, the goat's violent victory, and the breaking of the goat's great horn.
2. **The little horn and the sanctuary crisis (8:9-14):** The horn's ambition rises from territorial expansion to hostility against heaven, sacrifice, sanctuary, and truth. A heavenly dialogue sets a limit on the desecration.
3. **Gabriel's interpretation (8:15-26):** The animals and horns are identified with kings and kingdoms; the final hostile ruler is characterized and his overthrow announced.
4. **Daniel's response (8:27):** The vision leaves Daniel ill and astonished, yet he eventually resumes the king's business.

The movement is therefore not merely from one empire to another. It narrows from international conflict to the fate of worship and the holy people.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Daniel 8 contributes to [[Prophecy]] and [[Revelation]] by showing that receiving a vision and understanding it are distinct events. Daniel actively seeks meaning, and interpretation comes through Gabriel. The chapter also joins [[Temples]] and [[Sacrifice]] to the issue of divine sovereignty: suppression of worship is portrayed as an assault on heaven, not merely a change in public policy. The ruler's self-magnification illustrates [[Pride]], while his eventual overthrow limits every earthly government's claim to ultimate power.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated verb of becoming or making oneself 'great' traces the moral escalation of power. The ram becomes great, the goat becomes very great, and the little horn becomes exceedingly great before magnifying itself against the heavenly prince. Greatness is progressively detached from legitimate limits and converted into sacrilege.

The temporal expression translated 'two thousand and three hundred days' is paired with the later description of the vision as one of 'the evening and the morning.' Interpreters therefore debate whether it denotes 2,300 full days or 2,300 evening-and-morning offerings, approximately 1,150 days. The ambiguity should be retained rather than concealed.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The vision repeatedly reverses claims of invincibility. No one can deliver from the ram; then no one can deliver the ram from the goat. The goat reaches maximum strength and its great horn breaks. The final king prospers and destroys, yet is himself broken without human hand. This pattern turns imperial success into dramatic irony: apparent autonomy operates only within a limit the vision says is appointed.

The chapter also moves from visible animals to interpreted political realities. That internal decoding provides unusually firm anchors for reading the symbolism, even though the identities of the first Greek king, the four kingdoms, and the final fierce king are not explicitly named.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical-Correlation
> The little horn's described actions—suppressing the daily sacrifice, desecrating the sanctuary, warring against 'the mighty and the holy people,' and being destroyed by no human hand—closely match the historically documented actions of the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes, who in 167 BCE banned Jewish sacrifice and desecrated the Second Temple, an identification most scholars extend from the parallel, more detailed account in Daniel 11.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · Mainstream historical identification; not textually named · [[Historical-Correlation in Daniel 8|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Identification
> The sequence from Greece through divided successor kingdoms to a ruler who persecutes the holy people and profanes the sanctuary strongly supports Antiochus IV Epiphanes as the principal historical referent of the little-horn figure. This identification is historically strong but remains an interpretation because Daniel 8 does not name Antiochus.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the 'time of the end' mean the end of the specific persecution described, the final period of history, or a historical event that also functions as a pattern of the final conflict?

- Should the 2,300 evenings and mornings be counted as complete days or individual daily offerings?

- Does the little horn arise from one of the four horns or from one of the four winds? The wording has generated grammatical and interpretive debate.

- Who is the 'Prince of princes'? The title clearly denotes a supreme heavenly opponent of the king, but the chapter does not explicitly define the figure further.

- Why does Daniel remain astonished after Gabriel has supplied an interpretation? The ending may distinguish receiving an explanation from fully comprehending a revelation's implications.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Daniel 7]] for another arrogant horn that attacks the holy people, [[Daniel 11]] for the closest detailed treatment of sanctuary profanation and interrupted sacrifice, and [[Daniel 12]] for sealed revelation, measured periods, and the removal of the daily sacrifice. These are thematic connections; the verified index finds no verse-level textual overlap between Daniel 8 and Daniel 11 or 12. [[Luke 1]] provides a later canonical appearance of Gabriel as a named revelatory messenger.

For the Antiochus IV setting, compare 1 Maccabees 1 and 4, which describe the suppression of Jewish worship and the temple's subsequent rededication. Study the traditional sixth-century authorship position alongside the critical view that Daniel's final form arose during the second-century BCE crisis. The historical correspondence does not by itself determine whether the passage is predictive prophecy, history written in prophetic form, or a historical pattern receiving later typological application.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
