## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful outline is:

1. **Trouble, deliverance, resurrection, and glory** (12:1–3)
2. **Command to seal the book** (12:4)
3. **First question: “How long?” and the oath-bound answer** (12:5–7)
4. **Daniel's non-understanding and second question** (12:8–10)
5. **The 1,290 and 1,335 days** (12:11–12)
6. **Daniel's rest and final vindication** (12:13)

The movement from broad cosmic events to a promise addressed personally to Daniel gives the ending pastoral force.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Daniel 12:2 is among the Hebrew Bible's clearest expressions of awakening from death to differentiated destinies. The language strongly supports individual resurrection and judgment rather than merely national restoration, but it does not by itself settle whether “many” means everyone, whether the resurrection is limited to particular righteous and wicked persons, or whether the two groups rise at different times. Verse 13 applies the hope personally to Daniel: his promised rest and later standing in his lot naturally evoke death followed by resurrection or vindication.

Verse 10 juxtaposes purification, trial, continuing wickedness, wisdom, and understanding. This gives understanding an ethical dimension, but the verse does not fully explain whether purification causes understanding or instead characterizes the group that will understand.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV statement that “knowledge shall be increased” (12:4) is often applied to modern scientific or technological growth. That application is possible as a later theological reading, but it is not explicit in the verse. The immediate literary setting concerns sealed words that the wise will eventually understand (12:4, 9–10), making increased understanding of the revelation a strong contextual reading.

The chapter also uses “book” in two settings. The book of verse 1 functions as a registry associated with deliverance, whereas the book sealed in verse 4 contains Daniel's words. Their shared imagery is meaningful, but the chapter does not say they are one object.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The dialogue deliberately preserves Daniel's limitation. He hears the solemn answer yet says, “I heard, but I understood not” (12:8). Rather than supplying a simple decoding key, the response returns to sealing, purification, wickedness, wisdom, and patient waiting. This makes the chapter resistant to interpretations that treat successful date calculation as its sole concern.

Its closing image balances uncertainty with hope. Daniel is dismissed from the attempt to master every detail, but he is not dismissed without assurance: he will rest and stand in his lot (12:13).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical-Context Alignment
> Within the historical-critical reading of Daniel, the removal of the daily sacrifice and installation of the desolating abomination most plausibly refer in their initial historical horizon to Antiochus IV Epiphanes and the Jerusalem temple crisis of the second century BC. The repeated language within Daniel strengthens this identification, although Daniel 12 does not name Antiochus.

> [!evidence] Textual-Doctrinal Evidence
> The imagery of people sleeping in the dust and then awakening, followed by differentiated destinies, is strong textual evidence that Daniel 12 presents an individual postmortem resurrection and judgment rather than merely national restoration.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Broadly accepted; scope remains debated · [[Textual-Doctrinal Evidence in Daniel 12|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Vs. Traditional Interpretation *(tentative)*
> The referent of the 1,290- and 1,335-day figures and the "abomination that maketh desolate" in Daniel 12:11-12 is disputed: many biblical scholars read them as pointing primarily to the historical desecration of the Jerusalem temple under the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes (c. 167 BC), while other readers, including many Latter-day Saint commentators, apply them (in whole or in part) to a still-future "time of the end."
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · Fairly framed but attribution needs sources

> [!evidence] Textual/Interpretive *(tentative)*
> Daniel 12:4's phrase "many shall run to and fro, and knowledge shall be increased" is popularly applied in modern Latter-day Saint teaching to an increase in travel and secular/scientific knowledge as a latter-day sign, but this is one reading among several, and the text itself does not specify the content of the knowledge or the nature of the movement.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does “many” in verse 2 designate only part of humanity, or can it function as an inclusive multitude?
- Does verse 2 describe two temporally separate resurrections, or one awakening with two outcomes?
- How should the three time expressions in verses 7, 11, and 12 be related when the chapter itself does not state the conversion unit?
- What does the chapter gain by ending with Daniel's promised lot rather than with an explanation of every number?
- How do [[Daniel 8]] and [[Daniel 11]] constrain the initial meaning of the daily sacrifice and desolating abomination, and how do [[Matthew 24]] and later scripture reuse that language?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
