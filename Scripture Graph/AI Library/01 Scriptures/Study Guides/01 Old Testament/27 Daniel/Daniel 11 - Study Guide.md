## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Daniel 11:1:** A bridge from the angelic revelation of the preceding chapter.
2. **Daniel 11:2–4:** Persia, a mighty Greek ruler, and the fragmentation of his dominion.
3. **Daniel 11:5–20:** Alternating northern and southern rulers, failed alliances, invasions, and successions.
4. **Daniel 11:21–35:** A contemptible ruler gains power through intrigue, campaigns against the south, attacks the covenant and sanctuary, and persecutes those who remain faithful.
5. **Daniel 11:36–45:** The ruler’s self-exaltation and final campaigns. Whether these verses continue Antiochus IV, idealize his final defeat, or shift toward an eschatological antagonist is the chapter’s major interpretive dispute.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter’s theology is not that faithful people always escape suffering. Those who know God act and instruct others, yet some fall by sword, flame, captivity, and spoil (Daniel 11:32–35). Their endurance is joined to refinement: adversity can “try,” “purge,” and “make them white” without making the persecutor righteous. This supports a sober account of [[Endurance]], not a promise of immediate deliverance.

Royal power is repeatedly self-directed, deceitful, and temporary. The kings act according to their will, but the end remains appointed and determined (Daniel 11:24, 11:27, 11:35–36). The final ruler receives no help (Daniel 11:45). The passage therefore places [[Pride]], [[Government and Laws]], and [[War and Peace]] beneath a larger claim about divine limits on political power.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV preserves several expressions whose meanings are less transparent in English than their historical setting: “Chittim” in Daniel 11:30 commonly denotes a western maritime power; “arms” often refers to military force; and “the glorious land” is conventionally understood as Judea. “The desire of women” in Daniel 11:37 is especially ambiguous and should not be made the basis of a confident doctrine or biographical claim.

Repeated vocabulary carries the argument: rulers “do according to” their will (Daniel 11:3, 11:16, 11:36); deception appears through flatteries, devices, and lies (Daniel 11:21–27, 11:32, 11:34); and the “time appointed” limits apparent success (Daniel 11:27, 11:29, 11:35).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter moves through repeated cycles of royal ascent, conflict, alliance, deceit, and reversal rather than steady political progress. Dynastic agreements fail in Daniel 11:6 and 11:17, a league is followed by deceit in Daniel 11:23, and kings sharing one table speak lies in Daniel 11:27.

A repeated phrase links otherwise changing rulers: they “do according to” their will in Daniel 11:3, 11:16, and 11:36. Another verbal contrast spans the chapter: in Daniel 11:16 none can stand before a conqueror, while in Daniel 11:45 the final ruler comes to his end with none to help him. This is a thematic echo across the chapter, not evidence that both verses necessarily describe the same ruler.

The movement from imperial rivalry to covenant crisis supplies the moral center. In Daniel 11:30–35, the decisive contrast is no longer merely north against south, but covenant-forsakers and flatterers against “the people that do know their God.”
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Event Correspondence
> The ships of Chittim that force the northern ruler to withdraw are commonly connected with Roman intervention against Antiochus IV near Alexandria in 168 BCE. The correspondence explains why the ruler returns frustrated and redirects hostility toward the covenant community.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · Standard event identification with lexical caveat · [[Event Correspondence in Daniel 11|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Identification
> The “vile person” of verses 21–35 is strongly identified with Antiochus IV Epiphanes. His irregular accession, political deceit, campaigns against Egypt, hostility to the Jewish covenant, suppression of sacrifice, sanctuary profanation, and persecution align closely with the chapter’s progression.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Broad scholarly consensus on immediate referent · [[Historical Identification in Daniel 11|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Cumulative Historical Correspondence
> The sequence in verses 5–20 is widely identified with Ptolemaic-Seleucid conflicts: the failed marriage of Berenice and Antiochus II, Ptolemy III’s retaliatory invasion, later campaigns of Antiochus III, the marriage involving Cleopatra I, and the short reign of Seleucus IV. The cumulative sequence is substantially more specific than a generic prediction of war.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · Broad scholarly consensus; individual mappings vary · [[Cumulative Historical Correspondence in Daniel 11|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Correspondence *(tentative)*
> The mighty Greek ruler whose dominion is rapidly broken, divided toward four directions, and not inherited by his posterity fits Alexander III and the fragmentation of his empire among successor powers with high specificity.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · Broad identification accepted; specificity overstated

> [!evidence] Historical Correlation *(tentative)*
> The pattern in vv.21-31 (a ruler gaining power through intrigue rather than legitimate succession, entering Egypt, then desecrating the Jerusalem sanctuary and halting the daily sacrifice) is the passage most often cited as matching the actions of the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes around 175-164 BC recorded in extra-biblical sources such as 1 Maccabees, and this is the majority scholarly identification for the "vile person" of v.21.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Majority identification; documentation incomplete in A

> [!evidence] Historical Correlation *(tentative)*
> Many interpreters correlate vv.2-4 with the sequence of Achaemenid Persian kings culminating in the invasion of Greece, followed by Alexander the Great, whose empire (323 BC) fragmented among his generals rather than passing to his own heirs.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · Broad historical identification; details disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Where does Daniel 11 itself identify a ruler, and where does a reader supply a historical name?
- Does Daniel 11:36 continue the description of Antiochus IV, broaden it typologically, or introduce an eschatological ruler? Does Daniel 11:40 mark a clearer transition?
- How should “the time of the end” in Daniel 11:35 and 11:40 relate to the second-century BCE crisis, later scriptural uses of Daniel, and claims about final fulfillment?
- Why are the faithful portrayed as both strong and vulnerable to death in Daniel 11:32–35? What kind of strength does the passage describe?
- How do failed marriages, deceptive leagues, and lies at one table shape the chapter's view of political peace?
- Why does the verified index identify “Ammon (son of Mosiah)” here? Daniel 11:41 refers to the people or territory of Ammon, not the Book of Mormon missionary; that index match should not be treated as a genuine connection.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Daniel 8]] for the Greek kingdom, successor powers, attack on the sanctuary, and ruler broken without human hand. Compare [[Daniel 9]] and [[Daniel 12]] for the removal of sacrifice, desolation, appointed periods, and the time of the end. Matthew 24:15 and Mark 13:14 show the desolating-abomination language being reused for a later crisis, which provides a textual basis for typological application while leaving open how far that application extends.

For historical context, compare the chapter’s sequence with ancient accounts of Antiochus IV and the Judean persecution, while remembering that historical correlation cannot by itself decide between predictive revelation, retrospective composition, or a combination of historical reference and later typological fulfillment.
