## Overview

> Daniel sees four beasts representing the kingdoms of men—He sees the ancient of days (Adam) to whom the Son of Man (Christ) will come—The kingdom will be given to the Saints forever.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Vision setting and four beasts (7:1–8):** Daniel records the dream; four dissimilar beasts arise from the sea, and a speaking horn emerges among the fourth beast's horns.
2. **Heavenly court and transfer of dominion (7:9–14):** Judgment is convened, the beast is destroyed, and one like the Son of man receives everlasting dominion.
3. **Initial interpretation (7:15–18):** An attendant identifies the beasts as four kings and announces that the saints will possess the kingdom.
4. **Clarification of the fourth beast (7:19–27):** Daniel focuses on the final beast and horn; persecution lasts only until judgment and the saints' reception of the kingdom.
5. **Daniel's response (7:28):** The revelation ends with continued distress and guarded reflection.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter contrasts two figures in the heavenly court: the fiery-throned 'Ancient of days' (Daniel 7:9) and 'one like the Son of man,' who is brought before him and given 'dominion, and glory, and a kingdom' (Daniel 7:13-14) — a scene the New Testament applies to Jesus (Matthew 26:64). The official chapter heading identifies the Ancient of days as Adam and the Son of Man as Christ; [[D&C 116]] explicitly ties the Ancient of Days title to 'Daniel the prophet,' while Daniel 7's own verses supply only the titles. The judgment scene, where 'the judgment was set, and the books were opened' (Daniel 7:10), contributes directly to [[Final Judgment]] imagery reused throughout scripture, portraying judgment not merely as punishment but as the termination of oppressive dominion and vindication of the saints. The saints' eventual possession of the everlasting [[Kingdom of God]] (7:18,22,27) supports hope and [[Endurance]], though the text does not promise they escape suffering — the horn first makes war against them and prevails for a time before their vindication.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The vision characterizes worldly rule as predation: the beasts devour, crush, and trample (7:4-7,19), while the Son of man figure receives dominion without any comparable act of conquest (7:13-14) — a moral contrast between coercive and granted rule. The four beasts also escalate in the vividness of their menace, culminating in a fourth beast the text declines to compare to any known animal, calling it merely 'dreadful and terrible, and strong exceedingly' (7:7). This invites comparison with [[Daniel 2]]'s four-part statue vision, which similarly narrates a succession of kingdoms displaced by one 'which shall not be destroyed' (Daniel 2:44) — though the two visions use entirely different imagery, and the parallel is thematic rather than a shared wording (the two chapters share no overlapping verse text). Later apocalyptic writers drew directly on Daniel 7's imagery: Revelation's sea-beast fuses the lion, bear, and leopard into one composite creature bearing Daniel's ten horns (Revelation 13:1-2), and elsewhere interprets ten horns as kings much as Daniel 7:24 does (Revelation 17:12).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Interpretive Gloss
> Because the chapter itself explains the beasts and horns as political rulers or kingdoms, readings that treat these symbols as political powers have strong internal textual support; the internal explanation does not identify the specific historical powers.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · uncontested, textually grounded · [[Internal Interpretive Gloss in Daniel 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Text-Critical/Interpretive Ambiguity
> Whether 'one like the Son of man' in Daniel 7:13 denotes a distinct individual heavenly figure or is a symbolic personification of 'the saints of the most High' collectively (paralleling how the four beasts symbolize kingdoms rather than literal animals) is a genuinely disputed question, since the chapter's own interpretation glosses the beasts and horns but never explicitly glosses the son-of-man figure the same way.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · disputed identification, honestly presented · [[Text-Critical/Interpretive Ambiguity in Daniel 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary And Thematic Correspondence
> The parallel grants of everlasting dominion to the one like the Son of man and to the saints strongly support a deliberate relationship between that figure and the holy people, but the text does not explicitly define the relationship.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · disputed identification, honestly presented · [[Literary And Thematic Correspondence in Daniel 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Dating Argument *(tentative)*
> Mainstream historical-critical scholarship widely reads the little horn's described actions (persecuting the saints, attempting to 'change times and laws,' and being restrained after a period equal to about three and a half years) as a close match to Antiochus IV Epiphanes' persecution of Judea and desecration of the Jerusalem temple (c. 167-164 BC), and takes this correspondence as evidence that Daniel 7 (or at least its little-horn material) was composed near those events rather than genuinely predicted centuries earlier under Belshazzar as the chapter's own opening date claims.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.5 · genuinely disputed between traditional and historical-critical readings

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Which elements receive an explicit interpretation in verses 17–27, and which remain unexplained?
- How should the kingdom given to the Son-of-man figure in verses 13–14 relate to the kingdom given to the saints in verses 18, 22, and 27?
- Why does Daniel remain deeply troubled after hearing that the saints will ultimately possess the kingdom?
- Does the phrase “time and times and the dividing of time” specify a calculable duration, or chiefly emphasize that persecution is limited?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Daniel 2]] for the earlier four-kingdom pattern, [[Revelation 13]] and Revelation 17 for later reuse of the beasts and horns, and Revelation 20 for the judgment scene with opened books. Matthew 26:64 records Jesus applying Daniel's cloud-coming Son of man language to himself. [[D&C 116]] explicitly ties the Ancient of Days title to 'Daniel the prophet' at Adam-ondi-Ahman. Notably, this chapter's strongest mechanically verified textual overlap is with D&C 82 (three shared verses) — a stronger match than any of the thematic parallels above — but neither this study nor either research pass has yet identified which verses correspond or why; that comparison is worth pursuing directly.
