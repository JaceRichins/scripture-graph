# Daniel 2 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Daniel 2]] · My notes: [[Daniel 2 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Daniel 2 is a court narrative built around an apparently impossible royal demand: Nebuchadnezzar requires his advisers to disclose not just the meaning of his dream but the dream itself, a test the Chaldeans admit 'there is not a man upon the earth' who can meet (2:10). The KJV phrase 'the thing is gone from me' (2:5, 8) is ambiguous — it may mean the king has forgotten the dream, or simply that his demand is fixed and non-negotiable — and the story's point does not depend on resolving which. When the wise men fail, Daniel and his three companions [[Prayer|seek mercy]] from God, and the secret comes to Daniel 'in a night vision' (2:19). Daniel's own doxology (2:20-23) reframes the episode theologically: wisdom and might, and control over rulers and kingdoms, belong to God, who 'revealeth the deep and secret things.' The dream itself — a composite image of gold, silver, brass, and iron-and-clay, destroyed by a stone 'cut out of the mountain without hands' that grows to fill the whole earth — becomes one of the Old Testament's most influential pictures of an enduring [[Kingdom of God]] outlasting merely human empires. The chapter itself names only the first kingdom (Nebuchadnezzar's own); later kingdoms are described but never identified, leaving their historical referents to interpretive tradition. The official chapter heading identifies the stone with 'the latter-day kingdom of God,' an application consistent with [[D&C 65]], though it goes beyond what Daniel 2 states outright.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The royal demand and death decree (vv. 1–13):** The king demands both the dream and its interpretation, threatening death for failure.
2. **Petition, revelation, and praise (vv. 14–23):** Daniel gains time, turns to prayer with his companions, and receives the vision; the doxology (vv. 20–23) appears before Daniel ever speaks to the king, so the reader receives the theological framework before its narrative payoff.
3. **Daniel before the king (vv. 24–30):** He asks that the wise men be spared and attributes the disclosure to God rather than his own wisdom.
4. **The dream reported (vv. 31–35):** A many-metalled image is destroyed by a stone that becomes a mountain.
5. **The interpretation (vv. 36–45):** The materials represent successive kingdoms, followed by an indestructible kingdom set up by God.
6. **Recognition and promotion (vv. 46–49):** Nebuchadnezzar honors Daniel's God and elevates Daniel and his companions.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter grounds [[Revelation]] and [[Prophecy]] in God's sovereignty over history rather than in human technique: 'he removeth kings, and setteth up kings' (2:21), and 'the great God hath made known to the king what shall come to pass hereafter' (2:45). God discloses what human specialists cannot discover (2:22, 27–28). Daniel's companions join him in seeking God's mercies concerning the secret (2:17–18) — the text describes a desire for mercy rather than a recorded prayer, but revelation follows the appeal. Daniel's insistence that the secret came 'not... for any wisdom that I have more than any living' (2:30) models [[Humility]] as a response to a spiritual gift rather than personal achievement. The vision's climax — an everlasting [[Kingdom of God]] that 'shall break in pieces and consume all these kingdoms' (2:44) — is read within Latter-day Saint tradition (see [[D&C 65]]) as pointing toward the [[Restoration of the Gospel]], though the chapter itself leaves the timing and identity of that kingdom's arrival unspecified beyond 'in the days of these kings.'
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Daniel 2:4 marks a language shift, noting the Chaldeans answered the king 'in Syriack' — the KJV's term for Aramaic. In the underlying Hebrew Bible, the text from 2:4b through the end of Daniel 7 is actually written in biblical Aramaic rather than Hebrew, one of the longest continuous Aramaic sections in the Old Testament; annotated study editions typically flag this shift even though ordinary English translations do not. Separately, the recurring phrase 'the thing is gone from me' (2:5, 8) is ambiguous in English — it may mean the king has forgotten the dream or simply that his demand is fixed and non-negotiable — and the narrative's point, that the interpreters cannot verify their claims without independent knowledge of the dream, does not depend on resolving which.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The story repeatedly reverses apparent power. The king can issue a death decree but cannot obtain the knowledge he wants; the court experts possess status but confess inability; Daniel is a threatened captive yet becomes the bearer of the secret; and the enormous, terrifying image is reduced to windblown chaff by an initially separate stone. The doxology in verses 20–23 supplies the theological key to those reversals: wisdom, hidden knowledge, and changes of kings belong to God.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does Nebuchadnezzar demand the dream itself and not only its interpretation (2:5–9) — is this a narrative device meant to rule out a lucky guess? Which conclusions in the interpretation are explicit, and which depend on a later interpretive scheme? The head of gold is directly identified with Nebuchadnezzar's rule (2:38), but the remaining kingdoms are never named, and the chapter never gives the feet a specific number of toes — readers should resist converting them into a precise chronology the text does not supply. Does 'latter days' (2:28) mean simply 'the future' from the king's vantage point, a final eschatological period, or both? What should we make of Nebuchadnezzar's response in 2:46–47 — he prostrates himself before Daniel and orders an offering, but then directs his praise to Daniel's God — conversion, political flattery, or ordinary homage toward someone who mediated divine power?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
