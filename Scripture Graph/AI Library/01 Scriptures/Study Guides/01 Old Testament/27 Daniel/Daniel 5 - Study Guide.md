# Daniel 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Daniel 5]] · My notes: [[Daniel 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Belshazzar and his revelers drink from the vessels of the temple—A hand writes upon the wall, telling of Belshazzar’s downfall—Daniel interprets the words and reproves the king for pride and idolatry—That night Babylon is conquered.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Feast and profanation (5:1-4):** the temple vessels are used while material gods are praised.
2. **Sign and failed expertise (5:5-9):** the hand writes, the king panics, and the wise men fail.
3. **Daniel recalled (5:10-16):** the queen recommends Daniel from his earlier reputation.
4. **Indictment (5:17-24):** Daniel dismisses the offered reward, recalls [[Daniel 4]], and rebukes Belshazzar for pride, idolatry, and failure to learn.
5. **Sentence interpreted (5:25-28):** the kingdom is numbered, the king weighed, and the realm divided.
6. **Investiture and collapse (5:29-31):** Daniel receives the promised honors, Belshazzar dies that night, and Darius takes the kingdom.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents human political power as granted and removable by God: the Most High gave Nebuchadnezzar his kingdom and rules “in the kingdom of men” (5:18-21). Belshazzar's accountability is intensified by knowledge—he did not humble himself although he knew Nebuchadnezzar's history (5:22). His [[Pride]] appears in refusing dependence upon God, profaning vessels from God's house, and honoring idols that cannot see, hear, or know. The chapter therefore brings [[Humility]], [[Temples]], and [[Government and Laws]] into a single theological argument about divine judgment over rulers.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Daniel's interpretation is terse and cumulative: **numbered**, **weighed**, **divided** (5:26-28). The images move from the allotted duration of a kingdom, to appraisal of its ruler, to transfer of the realm. The inscription has UPHARSIN in 5:25 and the interpretation PERES in 5:28. These forms are commonly explained through related Aramaic forms and wordplay, including a sound association with Persia, but the English text does not display the morphology or the inscription's original vocalization. The familiar expression “writing on the wall” draws its force from a celebration continuing after judgment has already been announced.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several reversals shape the chapter. The king who commands a thousand nobles loses control of his own body when the hand appears (5:6). Babylon's experts cannot read the palace inscription, but an exile from Judah can. The ruler offers Daniel status, yet Daniel speaks as the one authorized to pronounce judgment upon the ruler. Daniel then receives the promised office immediately before the granting regime collapses. The chapter also uses [[Daniel 4]] as an explicit foil: Nebuchadnezzar eventually acknowledged divine sovereignty, while Belshazzar knowingly refused the lesson.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical Difficulty
> The chapter's historical setting also contains unresolved difficulties: extant evidence identifies Nabonidus, not Nebuchadnezzar, as Belshazzar's father, and no proposed identification of Darius the Median has achieved broad scholarly acceptance. These are material limitations on claims of straightforward historical precision.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Historical difficulties broadly recognized; resolutions disputed · [[Historical Difficulty in Daniel 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Corroboration
> Cuneiform evidence independently establishes Belshazzar as a real Babylonian royal figure, the son of Nabonidus, who exercised substantial authority during his father's prolonged absence. This supports the conclusion that Daniel 5 preserves authentic knowledge of a once-obscure late Babylonian figure, but the degree to which it confirms the chapter's detailed narrative is limited.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · Historical observation accepted; larger implications limited · [[Historical Corroboration in Daniel 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical Discrepancy And Proposed Resolution *(tentative)*
> The chapter repeatedly calls Belshazzar the 'son' of Nebuchadnezzar and Nebuchadnezzar his 'father,' but extrabiblical records identify Belshazzar as son of Nabonidus, who was not a known blood descendant of Nebuchadnezzar; this is usually explained as Hebrew/Aramaic idiom in which 'father'/'son' can denote 'predecessor'/'successor' or a more distant ancestor, though a specific (unattested) maternal descent from Nebuchadnezzar has also been proposed.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · Historical tension accepted; resolutions disputed

> [!evidence] Historical/Archaeological Corroboration *(tentative)*
> Belshazzar's existence and role as co-regent in Babylon, once doubted because ancient king lists named Nabonidus as Babylon's last king and did not mention a 'Belshazzar,' were later corroborated by cuneiform texts (notably the Nabonidus Cylinder from Ur and related administrative texts) identifying Belshazzar as Nabonidus's son who exercised royal authority in Babylon while his father resided at Tayma; this also plausibly explains why Belshazzar could only offer the 'third' highest rank in the kingdom, since he himself occupied the second, under his father.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · Existence and authority accepted; co-regency inference disputed

> [!evidence] Historical Identification Problem *(tentative)*
> Darius the Median, said to take the kingdom after Belshazzar's death, is not clearly identified by that name in independent contemporary records; Babylon's fall in 539 BC is usually attributed to Cyrus's forces (commanded by a general, Ugbaru/Gubaru, in the Nabonidus Chronicle). Proposed identifications of Darius the Mede include Gubaru as governor of Babylon, an otherwise unattested Median king, or a throne-name/title, but none is broadly accepted.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · Underlying historical problem accepted; details need correction
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Daniel dismiss the offered reward in 5:17 but allow the honors to be bestowed in 5:29? What motives does the text support, and which remain speculative?
- Does the queen's separate entrance and knowledge of Nebuchadnezzar's court support identifying her as a queen mother, or can the scene be explained otherwise?
- How should the repeated “father” and “son” language for Nebuchadnezzar and Belshazzar be understood in light of evidence that Belshazzar was the son of Nabonidus?
- What does the unresolved identity of Darius the Median mean for historical readings of 5:31?
- How does Belshazzar's prior knowledge affect the chapter's presentation of accountability?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Daniel 4]] beside Daniel 5:18-23 and identify what Belshazzar knew but failed to learn. Trace the temple vessels from Daniel 1:2 through Daniel 5 and Ezra 1:7-11, noting removal, profanation, and return. Compare Daniel 5:23 with Psalms 115:4-8, Psalms 135:15-18, and Job 12:10 as examples of broader biblical language about powerless idols and life held in God's hand. For thematic study, consult [[Pride]], [[Humility]], [[Temples]], [[Prophecy]], and [[Revelation]].

Historical study should keep two findings together: cuneiform evidence securely supports Belshazzar's existence and substantial delegated authority, but it does not verify the banquet, the supernatural writing, or Daniel's presence. Belshazzar's relationship to Nebuchadnezzar and the identity of Darius the Median remain disputed and should not be harmonized as settled facts.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
