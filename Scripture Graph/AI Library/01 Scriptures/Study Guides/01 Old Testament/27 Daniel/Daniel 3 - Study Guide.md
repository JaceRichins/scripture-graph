# Daniel 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Daniel 3]] · My notes: [[Daniel 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Nebuchadnezzar creates a golden image and commands all men to worship it—Shadrach, Meshach, and Abed-nego refuse and are cast into the fiery furnace—They are preserved and come out unharmed.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Image, assembly, and decree (vv. 1–7):** Officials are gathered, music signals worship, and the furnace enforces compliance.
2. **Accusation and refusal (vv. 8–18):** The three Jewish officials are denounced, questioned, and given another opportunity to comply.
3. **Punishment (vv. 19–23):** The enraged king intensifies the fire and has the men bound and cast in.
4. **Deliverance and inspection (vv. 24–27):** Four figures are seen walking loose; the three prisoners emerge entirely unharmed.
5. **Royal response (vv. 28–30):** Nebuchadnezzar praises their God, issues another coercive decree, and promotes the men.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The clearest treatment of [[Faith]] occurs in verses 17–18. The men trust God's power without claiming that faith guarantees their preferred outcome. Their response also raises the problem explored under [[Government and Laws]]: obedience to political authority reaches a boundary when the state demands worship contrary to allegiance to God. The rescue is an instance of [[Exodus and Deliverance]], but the declaration “But if not” prevents the chapter's message from being reduced to a promise that faithful people will always escape suffering.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The Aramaic phrase describing the fourth figure, rendered in the KJV as "like the Son of God" (Daniel 3:25), is more literally "a son of the gods" (or "a son of the divine beings")—language that fits naturally in the mouth of a polytheistic king rather than a technical theological title. Nebuchadnezzar's own later description, that God "sent his angel" (Daniel 3:28), points toward an angelic reading within the story itself. Many Christian and Latter-day Saint readers additionally identify this figure with the premortal [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)|Jesus Christ]]; that identification is a longstanding interpretive tradition rather than something the chapter states explicitly, and should be held as disputed rather than certain.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The story repeatedly recites official titles, instruments, and the decree's wording. This repetition makes the machinery of imperial conformity feel comprehensive and public. Against it stand the short, decisive answers of the three men. The plot then reverses the king's intentions: those sent to execute are killed, those bound are seen loose, those condemned are vindicated, and those brought down into the furnace are raised to office. A related court-deliverance pattern appears in [[Daniel 6]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does “But if not” change how faith should be understood when a desired intervention does not occur?
- What does the chapter explicitly say about the fourth figure, and what identifications go beyond its explicit wording?
- Does Nebuchadnezzar's praise demonstrate conversion, political pragmatism, astonishment at divine power, or some combination?
- How does the second violent decree complicate an uncomplicated reading of the ending as religious reform?
- Why might the narrator repeat the lists of officials and instruments so extensively?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare with [[Daniel 2]] for the three men's governmental position and with [[Daniel 6]] for another conflict between royal command and devotion to God. Exodus 20:3-5 clarifies the commandment background of their refusal to worship an image. Isaiah 43:2 offers a related prophetic image of preservation through fire, and Hebrews 11:34 ("quenched the violence of fire") may echo traditions of fiery deliverance from Second Temple Judaism, though it does not name Shadrach, Meshach, and Abed-nego and may reflect a general topos rather than a direct callback to this chapter.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
