# Daniel 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Daniel 6]] · My notes: [[Daniel 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Darius makes Daniel the first of his presidents—Daniel worships the Lord in defiance of a decree of Darius—He is cast into the den of lions—His faith saves him, and Darius decrees that all people are to revere the God of Daniel.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Administrative elevation (6:1-3):** Daniel is placed over the kingdom's officials.
2. **Conspiracy and decree (6:4-9):** rivals exploit Daniel's religious allegiance, and Darius signs the prohibition.
3. **Prayer, accusation, and legal pressure (6:10-15):** Daniel continues his prior practice; the officials expose the violation and block Darius's attempted rescue.
4. **The sealed den and anxious night (6:16-20):** Daniel is confined while Darius fasts, loses sleep, and returns at dawn.
5. **Deliverance and vindication (6:21-23):** Daniel credits God and an angel, and no injury is found.
6. **Retributive reversal (6:24):** the accusers and their families receive the punishment intended for Daniel.
7. **Royal proclamation and epilogue (6:25-28):** Darius praises God's enduring rule, and Daniel prospers under Darius and Cyrus.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Daniel's allegiance is expressed through durable practices: honest public service, regular [[Prayer]], gratitude, and continued worship when compliance becomes costly. Darius twice describes his service to God as continual; the crisis reveals an established commitment rather than creating it. The narrator explicitly associates Daniel's preservation with [[Faith]], while the account does not promise that faith always prevents suffering or death.

The story also contrasts political power with ultimate sovereignty. The law presented as unchangeable cannot accomplish the king's later desire, whereas God is proclaimed living, steadfast, able to deliver, and ruler of an indestructible [[Kingdom of God]]. This invites reflection on [[Government and Laws]] without teaching general contempt for civil authority: Daniel violates the worship restriction but remains free of administrative fault.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter develops its themes through repetition, contrast, and reversal. The officials find no fault in Daniel; Daniel later asserts innocence before God and harmlessness toward the king; and the final inspection finds no injury. This is a coherent progression in the KJV, though it has not been shown to involve repeated source-language roots.

The supposedly unchangeable decree exposes the king's limitations, while God is proclaimed steadfast and ruler of a kingdom that cannot be destroyed. The conspirators who arrange Daniel's descent undergo the same punishment themselves.

[[Daniel 3]] is the closest literary companion: both are court-deliverance narratives moving from imperial compulsion and accusation to threatened death, divine rescue, inspection, and royal acknowledgment. Shared genre may explain part of the correspondence, and the parallel is literary evidence rather than independent historical corroboration.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary-Thematic
> The repeated assertion that the king's law cannot be changed, followed by the proclamation that God's kingdom cannot be destroyed, strongly supports an intended contrast between rigid but limited imperial authority and God's enduring dominion.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strong literary reading; intentionality debated · [[Literary-Thematic in Daniel 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Comparative-Literary-Structure *(tentative)*
> The extensive correspondence between Daniel 3 and Daniel 6 is strong literary evidence that the stories have been deliberately arranged or shaped to interpret faithful life under imperial power. It is not independent corroboration that the narrated events occurred.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · literary relationship accepted; deliberate shaping disputed

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical *(tentative)*
> The historical identity of 'Darius the Mede' is not independently attested in extra-biblical Persian or Babylonian records that name a ruler of Babylon by that name between Belshazzar and Cyrus, making his identification uncertain.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.8 · historically disputed and insufficiently sourced
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrator emphasize that Daniel's prayer included thanksgiving during danger?
- Does the claim that all the presidents approved the decree indicate deception, conventional rhetoric, or a non-exhaustive use of "all"?
- How does Darius's inability to revise his decree qualify the appearance of absolute royal power?
- What ethical distinction should readers make between God's rescue of Daniel and Darius's punishment of the accusers' wives and children?
- How can Daniel's rescue nurture [[Faith]] without becoming a promise that faithful people will always avoid suffering?
- What can responsibly be concluded—and what cannot—from the absence of a secure historical identification of Darius the Mede?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Daniel 6:10 with 1 Kings 8:46-50 and Psalm 55:17 for the direction and regularity of prayer; these are contextual parallels, not demonstrated sources. Read [[Daniel 3]] alongside this chapter and trace imperial command, accusation, execution, deliverance, inspection, and royal response, while noting their differences. Compare Daniel 6:25-27 with [[Daniel 4]] and [[Daniel 7]] for recurring proclamation formulas and language about universal, enduring dominion. Hebrews 11:33 reflects later scriptural remembrance of faith that stopped lions' mouths. Finally, compare Daniel 6:24 with Deuteronomy 24:16 to distinguish narrated imperial retaliation from Israelite teaching about individual responsibility.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
