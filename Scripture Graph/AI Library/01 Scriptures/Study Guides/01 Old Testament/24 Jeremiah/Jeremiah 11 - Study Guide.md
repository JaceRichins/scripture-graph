## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The covenant recalled (vv. 1-5):** obedience, covenant identity, and the ancestral land oath.
2. **The covenant proclaimed and violated (vv. 6-10):** repeated commands to hear and do are answered by persistent refusal and idolatry.
3. **Judgment and failed appeals (vv. 11-17):** neither cries to the Lord nor appeals to other gods will deliver; the planted olive tree faces fire and broken branches.
4. **The plot against Jeremiah (vv. 18-23):** [[Revelation]] exposes the threat, Jeremiah submits his cause to the righteous judge, and judgment is pronounced on the men of Anathoth.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents covenant identity as relational but morally demanding: “ye shall be my people, and I will be your God” accompanies the command to hear and do. An inherited covenant does not make persistent rebellion inconsequential. The prohibition against intercession in verse 14 should be read within this particular judicial setting; it does not overturn the broader scriptural practice of [[Prayer]]. The passage also portrays divine judgment as following repeated warning rather than an unexplained impulse, while leaving difficult questions about the scope and severity of the announced punishment.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The vocabulary of curse, obedience, Egypt as an “iron furnace,” covenant identity, and a land “flowing with milk and honey” places the chapter within the language of Israel's foundational covenant traditions. Compare [[Deuteronomy 4]], [[Deuteronomy 6]], and [[Deuteronomy 27]]. These correspondences strongly establish shared covenant discourse, but they do not by themselves settle questions of literary dependence, editing, or date.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 16-17 portray the covenant people as a fair and fruitful olive tree planted by the Lord, then judged by fire and broken branches. Verse 19 soon uses another tree image for the conspirators' intended erasure of Jeremiah: “Let us destroy the tree with the fruit thereof.” Their proximity permits a suggestive literary comparison, but the images have different referents and may simply reuse conventional tree imagery rather than form an intentional pair.

Jeremiah's comparison of himself to an unsuspecting animal brought to slaughter also resembles Isaiah 53:7. In Jeremiah 11, however, Jeremiah is the immediate threatened victim. A later Christian typological comparison may be discussed as interpretation, but the chapter does not explicitly present the line as messianic prophecy or establish literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the chapter repeat both “hear” and “do,” and what distinction between receiving covenant words and practicing them does that repetition imply?
- How do the failed cries of verses 11-14 expose the inadequacy of worship undertaken only when disaster arrives?
- What does opposition from Anathoth, Jeremiah's own community according to [[Jeremiah 1]], add to the cost of his prophetic calling?
- How should Jeremiah's request for vengeance be understood alongside his decision to entrust his cause to the Lord's judgment?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
