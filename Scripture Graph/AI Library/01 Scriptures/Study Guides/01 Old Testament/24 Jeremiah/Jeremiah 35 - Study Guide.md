# Jeremiah 35 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 35]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 35 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Jeremiah 35 turns the fidelity of the Rechabites into a concrete indictment of Judah. Their refusal of wine is significant because it demonstrates sustained obedience to Jonadab's inherited instructions. God then contrasts that response with Judah's refusal to hear repeated prophetic calls to [[Repentance]]. The chapter ends with corresponding outcomes: judgment for those who would not hear and a favorable promise for the obedient house of Jonadab.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Commission and enacted scene (vv. 1–5):** The word of the Lord directs Jeremiah to assemble the Rechabites in a carefully identified chamber of the house of the Lord and offer them wine.

2. **Refusal and explanation (vv. 6–11):** They recount Jonadab's commands and explain that fear of Chaldean and Syrian armies brought them to Jerusalem.

3. **Prophetic application (vv. 12–17):** A second word of the Lord interprets their obedience as a contrast with Judah's refusal to hear God and his prophets.

4. **Promise to Jonadab's house (vv. 18–19):** Their fidelity is acknowledged, and Jonadab's line receives a continuing favorable promise.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The main subject is [[Obedience]] expressed as responsive hearing. The comparison is deliberately unequal: the Rechabites obey one ancestor's instruction, while Judah rejects God's repeated appeals through [[Prophets]]. Verse 15 gives that appeal moral and religious content: turn from evil, amend conduct, and abandon other gods.

The passage does not independently formulate the [[Word of Wisdom]]. It attributes the wine rule to Jonadab and places it alongside prohibitions on houses, fields, sowing, and vineyards. Its direct doctrinal force concerns faithful response to received commands. This qualification does not prevent readers from finding an analogy to later abstinence commitments; it prevents the analogy from being mistaken for the chapter's explicit law.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The vocabulary of response drives the oracle. The Rechabites “obeyed,” “done according to,” “performed,” and “kept” what they received. Judah does not “hearken,” “incline” the ear, “hear,” or “answer.” Hearing is therefore treated as enacted compliance rather than passive reception.

The repeated phrase “rising early” depicts God's speaking and sending of prophets as persistent. [[Jeremiah 7]] and [[Jeremiah 25]] confirm that this language belongs to a wider recurring indictment within the book.

“Stand before me” in verse 19 promises favorable continuity, but the chapter does not define a specific office. Accepted standing, service, and continuing descendants are plausible aspects of the expression; a securely priestly interpretation exceeds the immediate evidence.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two announcements of the word of the Lord shape the chapter. The first initiates the acted scene; the second supplies its interpretation. Wine is placed before the Rechabites, their refusal elicits their history, and that history becomes evidence in God's case against Judah.

The rhetoric is lesser-to-greater. If descendants preserve a human ancestor's demanding rule, Judah has less excuse for ignoring God, who spoke repeatedly and sent many servants. The comparison depends on the difference in authority; it does not make Jonadab's rules equivalent to divine law.

The ending balances judgment and promise. Judah receives the evil previously pronounced because it did not hear or answer, while Jonadab's house receives continuity because it obeyed and kept its charge. [[Jeremiah 34]] offers a useful contrast between a revoked commitment and sustained fidelity, although adjacency does not prove that the two episodes occurred consecutively.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Contextual And Literary Evidence
> The chapter strongly supports reading the Rechabite wine rule as a particular ancestral command used to demonstrate obedience, rather than as a universal command imposed on Judah or all readers. The text consistently attributes the rule to Jonadab, joins it to several other Rechabite practices, and identifies Judah's required response as repentance from evil and idolatry.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · strong contextual conclusion · [[Contextual And Literary Evidence in Jeremiah 35|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Chronological Cross-Reference *(tentative)*
> 2 Kings 10:15-23 features 'Jehonadab the son of Rechab' (an English spelling variant of Jonadab) allying with Jehu against Baal worship, roughly two centuries before Jehoiakim's reign. If this is the ancestor named repeatedly as 'our father' in Jeremiah 35 (vv.6,8,10,14,16,18), the term denotes a remote lineage founder rather than an immediate parent.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · probable traditional identification; not explicit

> [!evidence] Historical Cross-Reference *(tentative)*
> Verse 11's pairing of 'the army of the Chaldeans' and 'the army of the Syrians' as the twin threats driving the Rechabites into Jerusalem closely resembles 2 Kings 24:2's description of 'bands of the Chaldees, and bands of the Syrians' sent against Judah during Jehoiakim's reign, suggesting the episode is set amid raids preceding Nebuchadnezzar's 597 BC capture of Jerusalem.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · plausible historical correlation; exact event uncertain

> [!evidence] Intertextual Echo *(tentative)*
> The rationale in verse 7, 'that ye may live many days in the land where ye be strangers,' resembles in structure (though not in exact wording) the Decalogue's land-tenure formula in Exodus 20:12, 'that thy days may be long upon the land which the LORD thy God giveth thee,' but substitutes sojourner/stranger status for covenant possession of the land.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why is the demonstration staged in a chamber of the house of the Lord, and who besides the Rechabites may have witnessed it?

- Does the concluding commendation approve every feature of Rechabite life, or does it primarily use their fidelity as the lesson for Judah?

- How does their wartime move to Jerusalem qualify descriptions of the Rechabites as strictly or permanently nomadic?

- What range of meanings does “stand before me” carry elsewhere in scripture, and which meanings can Jeremiah 35 itself support?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Jeremiah 7]] and [[Jeremiah 25]] for recurring language about persistently sent prophets, refusal to hearken, turning from evil, and abandoning other gods. These parallels place Jeremiah 35 within the book's sustained explanation of Judah's judgment.

Read [[Jeremiah 34]] alongside this chapter as a canonical contrast between a commitment reversed and a tradition maintained; adjacency does not prove chronology or deliberate editorial pairing. [[2 Kings 10]] supplies the principal earlier biblical account of Jehonadab son of Rechab, although identifying him with the ancestor in Jeremiah 35 remains an inference. [[2 Kings 24]] may illuminate the Chaldean and Syrian threat in verse 11, but it cannot establish the precise incursion or date.

[[Numbers 6]] offers a useful contrast with the Nazirite vow. Both practices involve wine avoidance, but Numbers 6 describes personally undertaken separation with additional requirements, while Jeremiah 35 reports an inherited household rule governing wine, housing, and agriculture. The two should not be treated as the same institution.
