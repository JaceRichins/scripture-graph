# Jeremiah 4 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Israel and Judah are called to repentance—Jeremiah laments for the miseries of Judah.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Return and inward reform (4:1-4):** Israel and Judah are summoned to return, break up fallow ground, and circumcise the heart.
2. **Alarm, invasion, and a renewed appeal (4:5-18):** Trumpets, standards, fortified cities, a northern destroyer, and encircling watchers portray approaching war. Within the warning, Jerusalem is still told to wash its heart so that it may be saved (4:14).
3. **Anguish and moral diagnosis (4:19-22):** The speaker experiences the alarm bodily; the people are described as skilled in evil but ignorant of good.
4. **Cosmic desolation with a stated limit (4:23-28):** Earth, heavens, mountains, creatures, cultivated land, and cities collapse toward darkness and emptiness. The imagery resembles de-creation without forming an exact reversal of Genesis 1. Nevertheless, the Lord will not make a full end.
5. **Flight and Zion's travail (4:29-31):** Cities empty, attempted beautification proves futile, and the daughter of [[Zion]] cries out like a woman in labor.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Repentance]] as a return to the Lord involving conduct and inward disposition. Agricultural work, circumcision, and washing all describe change beneath the surface (4:1-4, 14). It also joins moral agency to consequence: 'Thy way and thy doings have procured these things unto thee' (4:18). Yet judgment is not depicted as unrestricted annihilation, because the Lord declares, 'yet will I not make a full end' (4:27). This qualification invites study alongside [[Justice and Mercy]] without minimizing the severity of the devastation.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated word **heart** carries several senses. It is the object of repentance (4:4, 14), the seat of failed courage among rulers (4:9), the inward destination of sin's bitterness (4:18), and the location of prophetic pain (4:19). This repetition connects personal morality, civic leadership, deserved consequence, and lament.

Verse 22 uses bitter irony: the people are 'wise to do evil' but have no knowledge 'to do good.' Wisdom has not disappeared; it has been redirected toward destructive ends.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Jeremiah 4:23-26 strongly evokes de-creation. The phrase "without form, and void" closely parallels [[Genesis 1]], while darkness, absent humanity and birds, wilderness, and ruined settlements depict an ordered and populated land collapsing into desolation. The correspondence is thematic rather than a precise reverse sequence: the trembling mountains may also draw on broader divine-presence imagery, and the ruined cities have no direct Genesis 1 counterpart.

The chapter repeatedly gives catastrophe an embodied voice. The speaker in verses 19-21 feels the alarm in the heart, though these verses do not explicitly name him as Jeremiah. The daughter of [[Zion]] then cries like a woman in first-labor anguish, spreads her hands, and declares herself exhausted before murderers (4:31).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How do the commands to cultivate, circumcise, and wash clarify what genuine [[Repentance]] entails?
2. Who is understood to have announced peace in verse 10, and why does Jeremiah describe the Lord as having deceived the people? How do possible answers preserve the difficulty of the verse rather than explaining it away?
3. What is gained by portraying invasion as the undoing of [[Creation]] in verses 23-26?
4. How does 'yet will I not make a full end' (4:27) qualify, without canceling, the surrounding judgment?
5. Does the emotional voice in verses 19-21 belong solely to Jeremiah, or does it also voice the suffering of the land or people?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the heart-circumcision command with [[Deuteronomy 10]] and [[Deuteronomy 30]], and the fallow-ground image with [[Hosea 10]]. Compare the northern alarm with [[Jeremiah 6]]. For the de-creation imagery, read Jeremiah 4:23-26 beside [[Genesis 1]], observing both the verbal correspondences and the differences.
