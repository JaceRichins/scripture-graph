# Jeremiah 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 10]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Learn not the way of other nations—Their gods are idols and molten images—The Lord is the true and living God.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Warning and idol satire (vv. 1–5):** Israel is warned against the nations' way and fear of heavenly signs; manufactured objects cannot speak, move, harm, or help.
2. **The incomparable living Creator (vv. 6–16):** The LORD's kingship, life, and creative power are contrasted with breathless images made by artisans.
3. **Expulsion and lament (vv. 17–22):** Inhabitants prepare their goods, expulsion is announced, tent imagery depicts loss, failed shepherds are associated with scattered flocks, and devastation approaches from the north.
4. **Prayer under judgment (vv. 23–25):** The speaker confesses human dependence, requests correction without destruction, and asks for fury upon Jacob's devastators.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter distinguishes the LORD from idols chiefly through action and [[Creation]]. Human artisans make, fasten, clothe, and carry the images; the LORD makes and governs the world (vv. 3–5, 9, 11–13). Verse 16 adds reciprocal belonging: the LORD is Jacob's portion, while Israel is described as the rod of his inheritance. Reading this as covenant theology is plausible, but the KJV word "rod" should not bear an overly precise claim without lexical study.

Verses 23–24 place dependence beside accountability. The speaker acknowledges an inability to direct his own steps yet asks to be corrected, so the passage is relevant to [[Agency]] without by itself establishing either determinism or autonomous self-direction. The appeal for correction without destruction also bears on [[Justice and Mercy]]. The threatened displacement and scattered flocks provide a thematic connection to [[Scattering of Israel]].

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Jeremiah 10:11 stands out because the Masoretic textual tradition switches from Hebrew to Aramaic. Since the verse begins with an instruction to say its sentence "unto them," the shift may suit a response intended for people who understood Aramaic. That function is plausible rather than certain; rhetorical code-switching, international usage, or a secondary gloss are also proposed explanations.

In the KJV, **bruit** in verse 22 means a report or tidings. It introduces the report of a great commotion approaching from the north.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's strongest literary device is antithesis. Idols cannot speak, walk, do good, do evil, or breathe; the living LORD speaks, and the created world responds. Their makers must fashion, fasten, clothe, and carry them, whereas the LORD establishes the world itself.

The word "brutish" recurs across several targets (vv. 8, 14, 21), extending the criticism from physical images to failures of human understanding and leadership. The tent lament in verses 19–20 converts national catastrophe into intimate images of broken cords, absent children, and a shelter no one remains to raise. The exact speaker may be Jeremiah, a personified city or people, a representative sufferer, or a blended voice.

Jeremiah 10:12–16 is extensively paralleled in [[Jeremiah 51]]. [[Psalm 135]] combines related weather language with an idol polemic, and [[Psalm 79]] closely parallels the closing imprecation. These relationships support shared literary or traditional material but do not independently establish borrowing direction, date, or authorship.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What practices might be included in "the way of the heathen," and what does the chapter actually specify about the "signs of heaven"?
- Why does the chapter answer idolatry principally through the contrast between the Creator and manufactured objects?
- How does the movement from cosmic praise to a ruined tent change the force of that contrast?
- Who is speaking in verses 19–20: Jeremiah, a personified city or people, a representative sufferer, or a deliberately blended voice?
- Does verse 23 deny meaningful human direction, or acknowledge limited control amid catastrophe? How does the request for correction in verse 24 constrain the reading?
- How should readers evaluate the appeal for measured correction of Jacob beside the request for fury upon other peoples in verse 25? What prevents a descriptive imprecation from becoming a universal command?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Jeremiah 10:12–16 line by line with [[Jeremiah 51]] 51:15–19, recording repetitions and differences without assuming which passage is earlier. Compare Jeremiah 10:13 with [[Psalm 135]] 135:7, then compare the idol polemic with Psalm 135:15–18, [[Psalm 115]], [[Isaiah 44]], and [[Isaiah 46]]. Read the closing imprecation beside [[Psalm 79]] 79:6–7 and the confession about human steps beside [[Proverbs 16]], treating the latter as a thematic analogy rather than proven literary dependence.

Within Jeremiah 10, trace "vanity" (vv. 3, 8, 15), "brutish" (vv. 8, 14, 21), and the language of making or workmanship (vv. 3, 9, 11–12, 15–16). These repetitions sharpen the contrast between manufactured images and the Creator.
