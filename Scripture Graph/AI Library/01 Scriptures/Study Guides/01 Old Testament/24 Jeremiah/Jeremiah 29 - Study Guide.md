# Jeremiah 29 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 29]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 29 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jeremiah tells the Jews in Babylon to prepare for seventy years of captivity—Those remaining in Jerusalem will yet be scattered—Shemaiah prophesies falsely and is cursed.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1-3 — Epistolary setting:** Jeremiah's letter travels from Jerusalem to the exiles through named couriers.

2. **Verses 4-7 — Life during exile:** The people are to build, plant, form families, increase, seek the city's peace, and pray for it.

3. **Verses 8-14 — False immediacy and durable hope:** Jeremiah rejects deceptive dreams, sets a seventy-year horizon, and promises that wholehearted seekers will be heard and gathered.

4. **Verses 15-23 — Judgment and rival prophets:** Those remaining in Jerusalem face disaster, while Ahab and Zedekiah are condemned for false prophecy and adultery.

5. **Verses 24-32 — Shemaiah's counterletter:** Shemaiah asks Jerusalem's priests to restrain Jeremiah; the letter is read to Jeremiah, who sends a judgment back to the exiles.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Endurance]] with ordinary responsibility. Faithfulness in adversity includes homes, work, [[Family]], civic concern, and [[Prayer]]. Verse 7 gives a striking account of life under foreign rule: believers can seek the peace of a city responsible for their displacement because their neighbors' welfare affects their own.

The restoration promise supports [[Hope]], but its seventy-year setting guards against treating hope as a guarantee of immediate relief. Verses 12-14 place [[Prayer]] and wholehearted seeking at the center of the promised renewal. The movement from dispersion to return also connects [[Scattering of Israel]] with [[Gathering of Israel]].

The disputes with rival speakers contribute to the chapter's teaching about [[Prophets]], [[Prophecy]], and [[Truth]]. Claiming God's name does not authenticate a message; the chapter repeatedly condemns those whom God did not send and who cause people to trust a lie.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The word **peace** binds present duty to future promise. The exiles must seek the peace of Babylon because their peace depends upon it (verse 7), and God describes his own thoughts toward them as thoughts of peace (verse 11). The repetition allows present civic faithfulness and future divine restoration to belong to one vision without collapsing the long interval between them.

A second repeated formula concerns sending. God says that he caused or sent the people into exile, sent true prophetic words, and did not send the deceptive prophets. Within the chapter's theology, history and prophetic authority are both interpreted through divine commission. The text does not resolve every philosophical question raised by that claim.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is organized around letters and reported speech. Jeremiah's message travels from Jerusalem to Babylon; Shemaiah sends correspondence from Babylon to Jerusalem; Zephaniah reads it to Jeremiah; and Jeremiah sends a response to the exiles. The repeated movement of messages makes correspondence the narrative vehicle for the dispute over prophetic authority.

The chapter also contrasts the prospects of two communities. The deportees receive commands directed toward survival, growth, and eventual return, while those remaining in Jerusalem are compared to inedible figs and threatened with further judgment. This imagery forms a strong literary parallel with [[Jeremiah 24]]. The controversy over prolonged captivity also gains context from [[Jeremiah 28]], where a rapid return is explicitly predicted by a rival prophet. These parallels do not by themselves determine authorship or editorial history.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Context
> The immediate literary evidence strongly favors reading Jeremiah 29:11 first as a promise to the exiled community: its audience is the Babylonian captives, its horizon is the seventy-year period, and its expected outcome is gathering and return.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · strongly supported contextual reading · [[Literary Context in Jeremiah 29|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Chronology *(tentative)*
> The 'seventy years' of Jeremiah 29:10 does not correspond cleanly to a single, uncontested historical span; proposed reconstructions differ (e.g., first deportation c. 605 BC to Cyrus's decree c. 538 BC is roughly 67 years; the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC to the decree is closer to 48 years; 586 BC to the second temple's dedication in 516 BC is closer to 70 years).
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · chronological ambiguity broadly recognized; dates need sourcing

> [!evidence] Intertextual Citation *(tentative)*
> Daniel 9:2 records Daniel understanding from 'the word of the LORD... to Jeremiah the prophet' that Jerusalem's desolations would last seventy years, showing that at least one later biblical writer treated Jeremiah's seventy-year figure (found here and in Jeremiah 25:11-12) as a specific, checkable prophetic datum rather than mere rhetoric.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · Jeremiah citation accepted; identification and significance disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How does the seventy-year setting change the way verse 11 should be applied to individual circumstances?

What practices allow a displaced religious community to seek a host city's peace without surrendering its identity or endorsing every action of its rulers?

Why might promises of immediate deliverance have been more persuasive than Jeremiah's command to build and plant?

What criteria within this chapter distinguish true prophecy from false prophecy, and which questions remain unanswered until events unfold?

How should readers understand the chapter's repeated claim that God caused or drove the people into exile alongside Babylon's human agency?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
