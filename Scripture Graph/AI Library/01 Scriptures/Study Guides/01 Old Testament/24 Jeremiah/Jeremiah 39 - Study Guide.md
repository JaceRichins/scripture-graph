## Overview

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Siege and breach (vv. 1-3):** Exact regnal and calendar notices frame the Babylonian entry.
2. **The fall of Zedekiah (vv. 4-7):** Flight gives way to capture, dynastic destruction, blindness, and exile.
3. **The fate of city and people (vv. 8-10):** Jerusalem is burned and breached; many are deported, while some poor inhabitants receive land.
4. **Jeremiah's release (vv. 11-14):** Imperial orders reverse the prophet's imprisonment.
5. **Oracle to Ebed-melech (vv. 15-18):** A chronological flashback ends the chapter with promised personal deliverance.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter places judgment and mercy together without making them interchangeable. Jerusalem's disaster is announced and accomplished, but Ebed-melech is promised life within that disaster. His deliverance is explicitly connected to trust in the Lord, making the passage pertinent to [[Faith]] and [[Justice and Mercy]]. The account does not promise that faith always prevents suffering; Ebed-melech is already afraid and exposed to danger. It instead portrays trust as meaningful amid a crisis that is not removed.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The officials' list in verse 3 is textually difficult: “Nergal-sharezer” occurs twice, and the KJV punctuation does not securely establish how every personal name and court title should be divided. This should be treated as a genuine textual and philological question, not proof either of eyewitness precision or of scribal corruption.

In verse 16, “evil” and “good” contrast calamity with well-being in the announced fate of the city; the wording does not declare every Babylonian act morally good. In verse 18, the promise that Ebed-melech's life will be “for a prey” portrays survival carried away from catastrophe like recovered spoil.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter creates a striking reversal of status: Zedekiah cannot save himself, while an imprisoned prophet is protected and an Ethiopian servant receives a promise of life. This is a persuasive literary pattern, though the text does not formally announce it as the chapter's structure.

The ending is retrospective. After Jeremiah's release is narrated, the text returns to a revelation received during his confinement, giving Ebed-melech and his trust the chapter's final emphasis.

The close parallels in [[Jeremiah 52]] and [[2 Kings 25]] establish literary relationship, not automatically independent corroboration. Shared sources, direct dependence, or editorial reuse remain plausible. The shorter Greek textual tradition also lacks much of the material corresponding to KJV/MT verses 4-13, so the chapter's historical specificity and its tension with [[Jeremiah 40]] must be evaluated with textual history in view.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the chapter place the earlier oracle to Ebed-melech after the account of Jerusalem's fall?
- What does the contrast between Zedekiah and Ebed-melech suggest about the kinds of security the narrative considers dependable?
- Was giving fields to the poor primarily relief, Babylonian administration, social restructuring, or some combination?
- How should Jeremiah 39:14 and [[Jeremiah 40]] be related when they describe Jeremiah's release differently?
- Does the repeated “Nergal-sharezer” in verse 3 represent two officials, difficult name-title parsing, or textual duplication?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Jeremiah 39:4-10 with [[Jeremiah 52]] and [[2 Kings 25]], noting both shared sequence and omitted material. Then read [[Jeremiah 38]] for the warning to Zedekiah and Ebed-melech's rescue of Jeremiah. [[Jeremiah 40]] continues the story of Jeremiah and Gedaliah but also raises a textual question about where and when Jeremiah was finally released.
