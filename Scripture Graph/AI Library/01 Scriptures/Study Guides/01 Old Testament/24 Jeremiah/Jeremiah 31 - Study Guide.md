# Jeremiah 31 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 31]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 31 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Jeremiah 31 belongs with chapters 30 and 33 to a block of restoration oracles often called the "Book of Consolation" — a sustained turn toward hope within a book otherwise dominated by judgment. The chapter moves through several promises addressed to both the northern tribes (Ephraim) and the house of Judah: a joyful ingathering of scattered Israel, including those normally left behind such as the blind, the lame, and pregnant women (vv.1-14); Rachel's lament for her exiled children answered by hope of their return, and Ephraim's own voice of repentance met with divine compassion (vv.15-22); judgment reversed into rebuilding (vv.23-30); the promise of a [[Covenants|new covenant]] written on the heart rather than only externally taught (vv.31-34); and a closing double assurance — Israel's standing before God is tied to the fixed order of creation (vv.35-37), and Jerusalem itself will be rebuilt and made holy (vv.38-40). The chapter pairs God's prior love and initiative (v.3) with real moral accountability (vv.29-30, 18-19) without fully resolving how the two relate.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter's second half is marked by a triple refrain, "Behold, the days come, saith the LORD" (vv.27, 31, 38), organizing three restoration oracles: repopulation of the land (27-30), the new covenant (31-37, since the cosmic-assurance verses continue the covenant unit), and the rebuilt city (38-40). The first half (1-26) is looser poetry organized by scene: gathering and celebration (1-14), Rachel's lament and Ephraim's repentance answered by mercy (15-22), and a closing report of a dream or vision (v.26). Verse 28's promise "to build, and to plant" echoes the positive half of the LORD's commissioning verbs to Jeremiah in [[Jeremiah 1]]:10 — a real verbal echo, though whether it functions as a deliberate structural inclusio for the whole book is an interpretive judgment rather than a settled fact.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**New covenant (vv.31-34).** Contrasted with the covenant made at the Exodus, which Israel broke (v.32), the new covenant is marked by several features together: law written "in their inward parts" and hearts rather than only externally taught, renewed covenant belonging ("I will be their God, and they shall be my people"), knowledge of the LORD reaching "from the least...unto the greatest," and forgiveness with sin "remembered no more" (v.34). Internalization is a major stated feature, not necessarily the only difference. This is among the most extensively quoted Old Testament passages in the New Testament, cited at length in [[Hebrews 8]]:8-12 and again in [[Hebrews 10]]:16-17 as part of an argument about Christ's mediation of a better covenant — a reception that need not exhaust Jeremiah's own horizon of hope for Israel and Judah returning from exile.

**Individual accountability (vv.29-30).** The chapter retires a proverb of inherited guilt ("the fathers have eaten a sour grape...") in favor of personal responsibility. The same proverb is addressed in [[Ezekiel 18]], suggesting the exiles were wrestling with this question from more than one prophetic voice. The point is personal accountability, not a claim that one person's choices never affect others.

**Ephraim's status (vv.9, 18-20).** "Ephraim is my firstborn" asserts covenant/birthright favor, not literal birth order — Ephraim was not Jacob's eldest grandson but was elevated in Jacob's blessing (Genesis 48). Ephraim's own words of repentance and shame (vv.18-19) are directly quoted by the LORD, who responds with paternal compassion (v.20).

**Prior love and repentance (v.3, vv.18-20).** The oracle opens with God's "everlasting love" preceding any account of repentance, and later depicts turning as both something Ephraim voices and something requested from God ("turn thou me, and I shall be turned"). The text does not fully resolve how prior divine love, discipline, and human turning relate to one another.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Hebrew wordplay largely disappears in translation. The root behind "turn"/"return" (shuv) recurs throughout: God "gathers" those he "scattered" (v.10), Ephraim asks to be "turned" and reports he "was turned" (vv.18-19), and Israel is told twice in one verse to "turn again" (v.21) — the same word covers both geographic return from exile and moral repentance, so the chapter may be trading on that double sense rather than treating them as identical processes. Verse 28 answers a string of destruction verbs (pluck up, break down, throw down, destroy, afflict) with just two restoration verbs (build, plant), echoing [[Jeremiah 1]]'s commissioning language. "Rahel" (v.15) is simply the Hebrew form of "Rachel." Family vocabulary — father, firstborn son, mother, virgin daughter — carries much of the chapter's emotional and theological weight without necessarily being literal genealogical claims.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Rachel functions as a personified matriarchal figure mourning for the nation's exiled "children"; centuries later Matthew 2:17-18 reapplies her lament to the killing of children near Bethlehem, joining two scenes of bereaved motherhood rather than claiming Herod's massacre was Jeremiah's sole original referent. The chapter overall favors reversal: mourning into joy (v.13), a "backsliding daughter" called to turn again (vv.21-22), destruction verbs answered by building and planting (v.28). Verse 26's report of waking from a "sweet" sleep may frame some preceding material as a dream or vision, though the text doesn't specify how much. Verse 22's notoriously obscure line, "a woman shall compass a man," has drawn many proposed readings — reversed vulnerability, a courtship idiom, later messianic readings — without settled consensus; its difficulty is genuine and worth naming rather than resolving.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- The new covenant (vv.31-34) is described as law written on the heart rather than only externally taught — what does that change, and what stays the same, compared to the earlier covenant?
- Verse 3's "everlasting love" precedes any account of Israel's repentance in the chapter. What does that ordering suggest about the relationship between divine love and repentance?
- Why might the LORD quote Ephraim's own words of repentance (vv.18-19) directly rather than simply describing his contrition?
- How do vv.29-30's rejection of the sour-grape proverb relate to other texts that speak of consequences passing to children (e.g., Exodus 20:5) — correction, clarification, or an answer to a specific complaint among the exiles?
- How should later uses of this chapter in [[Matthew 2]] and [[Hebrews 8]] shape how we read Jeremiah's own historical setting, without either context erasing the other?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
