# Jeremiah 28 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 28]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 28 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Hananiah prophesies falsely that the Babylonian yoke will be broken.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Hananiah's restoration oracle (vv. 1–4):** Babylon's yoke will be broken within two years.
2. **Jeremiah's qualified response (vv. 5–9):** he wishes the promise were true but invokes fulfillment as the test of a peace oracle.
3. **Hananiah's enacted sign (vv. 10–11):** he breaks Jeremiah's yoke before the people.
4. **The divine counterword (vv. 12–14):** broken wooden yokes are replaced by iron yokes.
5. **Judgment and reported outcome (vv. 15–17):** Jeremiah declares Hananiah unsent, predicts his death, and the narrator reports that death in the seventh month.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter treats reassuring religious speech as potentially dangerous when it lacks divine authorization: its concern is not that hopeful messages are inherently false, but that Hananiah's specific promise is unsent and induces the people to trust in a lie (Jeremiah 28:15). For [[Prophecy]] and [[Prophets]], the emphasis falls on accountability and testing over time rather than on immediate certainty. The chapter also treats a nation's political subjection (the yoke) as bound up with prophetic interpretation of the nation's circumstances, connecting [[Government and Laws]] to that reading, and makes [[Truth]] a matter of communal consequence rather than only private conviction.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated image of the **yoke** links political submission with prophetic symbolism. Hananiah breaks wood to dramatize release; the response substitutes iron, intensifying rather than merely restoring the original image. The phrase “prophet which prophesieth of peace” in verse 9 does not condemn peace itself. It makes fulfillment especially important when a favorable prediction departs from the earlier pattern of warnings described in verse 8.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The yoke is the chapter's controlling image: Hananiah breaks wood to dramatize release, and the reply intensifies rather than reverses it -- wood becomes iron (vv.10-14). The narrative also sets claimed speech against narrated revelation: Hananiah repeatedly opens with "Thus saith the LORD" (vv.2,11), while the narrator instead reports that "the word of the LORD came unto Jeremiah" only after Jeremiah has left the confrontation (v.12) -- a contrast built into the narrator's own point of view, not neutral external proof of who was truly sent. Notably, the narrator keeps calling Hananiah "the prophet" at several points even after Jeremiah's verdict against him (vv.12,17), though the title drops at vv.11 and 13; whether this reflects retained office, ordinary usage, or narrative habit is left open. The tight correspondence between "this year thou shalt die" (v.16) and "died... in the seventh month" (v.17) gives the chapter a pointed ending, though it remains internal narrative evidence rather than independent corroboration.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Fulfillment
> Within the chapter's own narrative, Hananiah's death in the seventh month directly and promptly corresponds to Jeremiah's prediction that Hananiah would die that year. This strongly supports the chapter's literary presentation of Jeremiah, rather than Hananiah, as the prophet whose warning is vindicated.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation, self-hedged · [[Internal Narrative Fulfillment in Jeremiah 28|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Fulfillment *(tentative)*
> The narrative presents Hananiah's death as vindicating Jeremiah by satisfying Jeremiah's own stated test (v.9): the word 'came to pass.' Within the text's own framework this reads as compelling confirmation, but as evidence usable outside that framework it is weak: the account survives only in the very source that records Jeremiah's counter-prophecy, so there is no independent corroboration of the timing or cause of Hananiah's death, and a death occurring roughly two months after a dated prediction cannot, on the information given, be distinguished from a natural death that the narrative frames as fulfillment after the fact.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · source-critical caution valid; internal logic confused
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Jeremiah answers Hananiah with "Amen, the LORD do so" (v.6) before challenging him. What does it mean that Jeremiah can want a prophecy to be true and still judge that it was not truly sent?
- Verse 9's test -- that a prophet of peace is known once his word "comes to pass" -- would apply just as much to Jeremiah's own prophecies of future restoration. Is this a general principle Jeremiah is willing to be judged by, or a claim specific to this dispute?
- What persuasive force would Hananiah's public breaking of the yoke have had, apart from the truth of the interpretation attached to it?
- Why does the narrative keep calling Hananiah "the prophet" (vv.12,17) even after reporting that the LORD did not send him?
- How do [[Jeremiah 27]], [[Jeremiah 28]], and [[Jeremiah 29]] together frame the tension between submission to Babylon and hope for rapid restoration?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
