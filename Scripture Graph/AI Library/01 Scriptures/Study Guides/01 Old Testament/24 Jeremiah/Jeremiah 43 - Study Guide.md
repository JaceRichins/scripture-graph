# Jeremiah 43 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 43]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 43 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Jeremiah finishes delivering the divine message, but Johanan, Azariah, and the men called proud reject it as false and blame Baruch for influencing him. The captains take the surviving Judean community—including Jeremiah and Baruch—into Egypt. At Tahpanhes, Jeremiah receives a new command: he is to hide stones publicly near Pharaoh's house and announce that Nebuchadrezzar will establish his authority there. The place chosen for safety is thus presented as exposed to the same Babylonian power the migrants feared.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Rejection of the message (43:1–3):** Jeremiah is accused of falsehood and Baruch of conspiracy.
2. **Migration into Egypt (43:4–7):** the narrator twice identifies the decision as disobedience.
3. **Public sign at Tahpanhes (43:8–10):** hidden stones mark the announced location of Nebuchadrezzar's throne.
4. **Oracle against Egypt (43:11–13):** death, captivity, and the sword are followed by judgment on Egyptian temples and images.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's clearest theological theme is [[Obedience]]. The issue is not merely whether the group hears Jeremiah, but whether it accepts an unwelcome message as the voice of the LORD. Its treatment of [[Prophecy]] and [[Revelation]] is also sobering: a majority's confident accusation does not determine the truth of a prophetic claim within the narrative. Nebuchadrezzar's designation as God's servant portrays imperial power as an instrument of judgment, without necessarily offering moral endorsement of the ruler.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"Nebuchadrezzar" (v.10) is a variant spelling of the king's name that appears alongside the more common "Nebuchadnezzar" used elsewhere in Jeremiah and in Kings/Daniel/Esther; the variation is generally explained as reflecting different transliterations of the underlying Akkadian name, though this is a linguistic note rather than a claim bearing doctrinal weight. "Beth-shemesh" (v.13), literally "house of the sun," is widely identified by scholars with the sun-temple center the Greeks called Heliopolis (biblical "On") — a plausible and long-held identification, but one resting on etymology and tradition rather than an explicit statement in the passage itself.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter turns on an explicit reversal: the leaders enter Egypt specifically to escape the Chaldeans (v.3), yet the oracle that follows announces that Babylonian power will reach them there too (vv.10-11) — the refuge they chose offers no escape from the danger they feared. The buried-stones sign-act (vv.8-9) belongs to the same enacted-prophecy tradition as the broken flask of [[Jeremiah 19]] — a witnessed, physical act meant to make an oracle concrete rather than merely spoken. The shepherd-and-garment image in v.12 ("he shall array himself with the land of Egypt, as a shepherd putteth on his garment") suggests an easy, total dominion, though the precise force of the metaphor is debated among translators. Some readers see the forced descent into Egypt (v.7) as an intentional inversion of the Exodus — a self-willed retreat into the land Israel was once delivered from — but the text itself supplies no explicit Exodus vocabulary, so this remains an interesting, unproven literary suggestion rather than an established authorial device.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the statement that the captains “took” Jeremiah and Baruch imply coercion, or merely inclusion in the migration? The chapter does not settle the question.
- Why do the leaders blame Baruch rather than only rejecting Jeremiah's authority?
- How does the public setting of the hidden-stones sign affect its function as a witness to the later oracle?
- Does calling Nebuchadrezzar God's servant describe divine authorization, divine use of an otherwise independent ruler, or both?
- How does [[Jeremiah 43]] alter the reader's assessment of the request for guidance and warning recorded in [[Jeremiah 42]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Jeremiah 42]], [[Jeremiah 43]], and [[Jeremiah 44]] as a continuous sequence: request for guidance, rejection and migration, then prophetic confrontation in Egypt. Compare Jeremiah 43:10 with Jeremiah 25:9 for the recurring designation of Nebuchadrezzar as God's servant. Any claim that the Tahpanhes sign was historically fulfilled should be evaluated separately using securely dated historical and archaeological evidence rather than inferred from the narrative alone.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
