## Overview

> [!info] Chapter heading (official)

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The sign and its basis (27:1–7):** Jeremiah receives the command to wear bonds and yokes. The Lord's authority over nations is grounded in [[Creation]], and Babylon's rule is announced as real but temporary.
2. **Message to the nations (27:8–11):** Resistance brings sword, famine, pestilence, and removal; submission permits continued life in the land.
3. **Message to Zedekiah (27:12–15):** The same choice is applied directly to Judah's king, with a warning against prophets who deny the need to serve Babylon.
4. **Message to priests and people (27:16–22):** Jeremiah disputes predictions that the temple vessels will return shortly and instead predicts the removal and eventual restoration of those still in Jerusalem.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter depicts the Lord as sovereign over lands and kingdoms, including a foreign empire. Nebuchadnezzar can therefore serve a divine purpose without the text declaring every feature of Babylonian power righteous. [[Obedience]] here takes an unexpected political form: accepting a humiliating yoke becomes the path to life during a specific historical judgment.

The chapter also offers a restrained form of hope. Restoration is promised for the temple vessels, but only after removal and an unspecified period in Babylon. Hope does not erase the announced judgment or justify promises of immediate relief.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The yoke unifies the chapter's action and language. Jeremiah first places it on his own neck; nations are then told to place their necks under Babylon's yoke. The repeated image turns a diplomatic and military reality into a bodily choice between submission and destructive resistance.

The message moves inward: from foreign kings, to Judah's king, to the priests and people, and finally to the objects remaining in Jerusalem and the Lord's house. Repetition across these audiences emphasizes that neither international alliances, royal policy, prophetic assurances, nor sacred vessels exempt Jerusalem from the announced judgment. [[Jeremiah 28]] continues the symbolic and prophetic contest over this yoke.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What evidence does the chapter itself offer for distinguishing Jeremiah from the prophets he condemns? Divine commission, intercession, concrete predictions, and eventual outcomes all appear relevant, but the chapter does not provide a simple test available before events unfold.
- Why is submission called the way to live in this setting? How does the specific Babylonian crisis limit attempts to turn this command into a general political rule?
- What does the delayed restoration of the vessels suggest about the difference between durable hope and promises of immediate relief?
- How should verse 1's reference to Jehoiakim be assessed alongside verse 3's setting under Zedekiah and verse 20's reference to Jeconiah's completed deportation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Jeremiah 28]] as the immediate continuation of the dispute: its conflict over the yoke and the temple vessels provides a narrative test of the rival claims introduced here. [[Jeremiah 29]] extends the counsel to accept a prolonged Babylonian setting rather than trust predictions of quick deliverance.
