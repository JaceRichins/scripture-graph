# Jeremiah 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 15]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The people of Judah will suffer death, the sword, famine, and captivity—They will be scattered into all the kingdoms of the earth—Jerusalem will be destroyed.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Judgment beyond intercession (15:1–4):** even Moses and Samuel would not avert the announced sentence; death, sword, famine, and captivity summarize its reach.
2. **Jerusalem's abandonment and bereavement (15:5–9):** rhetorical questions and family imagery make the disaster personal.
3. **The prophet amid the crisis (15:10–14):** Jeremiah laments his contested existence, while the judgment speech continues with spoil and exile.
4. **Jeremiah's prayer and accusation (15:15–18):** delight in God's word gives way to isolation, pain, and the image of failing waters.
5. **Correction and renewed commission (15:19–21):** Jeremiah must return, distinguish the precious from the vile, remain independent of his opponents, and trust the renewed promise of divine protection.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter places both the people and the prophet under demands expressed through the language of return. The people do not return from their ways (15:7), while Jeremiah hears, “If thou return” (15:19). For Jeremiah, this may concern repentance, renewed prophetic fidelity, disciplined speech, or some combination of these; the compressed wording does not settle every detail.

The opening raises a difficult question about [[Justice and Mercy]]. The announced judgment is associated with Manasseh's earlier conduct, but the chapter also accuses the present people of forsaking the LORD and refusing to return (15:6–7). The text therefore joins corporate historical consequence with present culpability without explaining fully how responsibility relates to each person who suffers.

The closing promise belongs with [[Adversity]] and [[Endurance]]. Jeremiah's enemies will continue to fight him, but the LORD promises presence and deliverance (15:20–21). Protection is presented as preservation within conflict, not exemption from pain.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Thy words were found, and I did eat them” (15:16) compresses reception, internalization, and delight into one bodily metaphor. The parallel in [[Ezekiel 3]] shows that eating a divine message can symbolize prophetic commissioning, but Jeremiah's immediate sequence is distinctive: joy becomes solitude and then accusation.

Return language shapes the divine answer in 15:19. Jeremiah must “return”; God will “bring” him again; the people must return to him; he must not return to them. The repetition turns a common repentance term into a precise boundary for prophetic fidelity.

“Northern iron” in 15:12 is suggestive but not self-interpreting. It may evoke an unusually strong northern power, plausibly the invading threat elsewhere associated with the north, but the verse itself never names Babylon.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's most consequential literary turn occurs at verse 10, where the national catastrophe is refracted through the prophet's personal lament. Public strife has made Jeremiah himself “a man of strife.” Maternal imagery also crosses the sections: bereaved mothers embody Jerusalem's disaster in verses 7–9, and Jeremiah addresses his own mother when lamenting his birth in verse 10.

The ending recalls Jeremiah's call in [[Jeremiah 1]]. Both passages make him a fortified wall, anticipate conflict, deny his enemies final victory, and ground survival in the LORD's presence and deliverance. This strong verbal recurrence supports reading 15:19–21 as a renewed commission after crisis, though it cannot by itself settle questions of authorship or editorial formation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why are Moses and Samuel chosen as the imagined intercessors, and what do their earlier ministries contribute to the force of 15:1?
- Does “If thou return” in 15:19 rebuke Jeremiah's accusation in verse 18, recall him from despair, or encompass both?
- What might count as “the precious” and “the vile” in prophetic speech?
- How does the chapter distinguish promised deliverance from a promise of comfort or safety?
- How should readers acknowledge the moral severity of corporate judgment without either dismissing the text or minimizing the suffering it depicts?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the intercessory ministries of Moses in [[Exodus 32]] and Samuel in [[1 Samuel 7]] with Jeremiah 15:1. Read the Manasseh traditions in [[2 Kings 21]] and [[2 Kings 24]] alongside Jeremiah 15:4. For Jeremiah's vocation, compare the fortified-wall promises in [[Jeremiah 1]] and [[Jeremiah 15]], then set this lament beside [[Jeremiah 12]] and [[Jeremiah 20]]. The ingestion metaphor can be followed through [[Ezekiel 3]] and [[Revelation 10]], noting both the shared imagery and the different emotional effects.
