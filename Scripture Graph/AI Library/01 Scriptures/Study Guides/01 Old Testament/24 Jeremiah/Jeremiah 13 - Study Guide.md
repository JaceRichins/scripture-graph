## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv1-11 — The linen girdle sign-act.** Command and obedience (vv1-2, 4-5), retrieval and ruin (vv6-7), divine interpretation (vv8-11).
- **vv12-14 — The wine-bottle proverb.** A saying about full bottles (v12) reinterpreted as judgment on "kings...priests...prophets...inhabitants" (vv13-14).
- **vv15-21 — Appeal, lament, and captivity.** A call to give glory before darkness (vv15-16), the prophet's private weeping (v17), a word to "the king and...the queen" (v18), total captivity announced (v19), and invaders "from the north" (vv20-21).
- **vv22-27 — Diagnosis and judgment.** Habituated sin (vv22-23), scattering like stubble and its stated cause — forgetting the LORD and trusting falsehood (vv24-25), and a closing indictment of covenant infidelity ending in an open question (vv26-27).
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verse 11 frames Israel and Judah's relationship with God in covenantal terms: the LORD caused them to "cleave" to him so they might be his people, name, praise, and glory — but "they would not hear." Their refusal turns intended closeness into the ruined-girdle image, tying [[Covenants]] to the chapter's central warning. [[Pride]] is named directly as the target of the girdle sign (v9) and again at the emotional center of the poetic oracle (vv15-17), where its antidote is stated plainly: "[[Humility|Humble]] yourselves" (v18). The proverb of the Ethiopian and the leopard (v23) speaks to how entrenched habitual sin becomes — the verse's own comparison point is "accustomed to do evil," not a claim about any group's fixed or unchangeable nature. The chapter's judgment language is severe throughout, but the appeal to "give glory to the LORD" before darkness falls (vv15-16) keeps a note of address and urgency rather than presenting the outcome as beyond response.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The girdle (Hebrew ezor) was worn directly against the body, which sharpens the covenant simile of v11 as one of closeness rather than mere formality. Clothing and exposure imagery recur across the chapter: the girdle is put on, hidden, and found ruined (vv1-7), and near the close the same body-imagery returns as public shame — "thy skirts discovered, and thy heels made bare" (v22) and "I will discover thy skirts upon thy face" (v26) — describing personified Jerusalem's humiliation without softening the charge. A similar phrase appears in the judgment oracle against Nineveh in Nahum 3:5, though one additional occurrence is not enough on its own to call this a settled 'stock idiom' rather than coincidence or shared convention. Verse 27's "adulteries, and thy neighings" recalls the horse-imagery for illicit desire used earlier in the book (cf. [[Jeremiah 5|Jeremiah 5:8]]), though whether this is a deliberate echo or simply a shared conventional image cannot be established from the text alone. A proposed pun linking "bottle" (nebel) in v12 to "fool" (nabal) is intriguing but unsourced here and should be held loosely, not presented as established.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's persuasive strategy is to start from something ordinary — a girdle, a proverb about wine bottles, a proverb about animal markings — and push each image past its literal sense into a warning about Judah's fate. [[Pride]] is a recurring thread rather than a formal frame, named at the girdle's interpretation (v9) and again at the pivot of the poetic oracle (vv15-17). The chapter's final line — "wilt thou not be made clean? when shall it once be?" (v27) — ends an unrelenting judgment oracle on a question instead of a sentence; this can be read either as leaving rhetorical room for repentance or as an expression of exasperation at conduct that shows no sign of changing. Both readings are worth holding, since the text does not resolve which is meant.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary Evidence
> Because the text itself explains how the girdle corresponds to Judah's pride, disobedience, and failed relationship with God, the passage provides strong internal literary evidence that the episode functions as a symbolic prophetic act.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · sound observation, overprecise self-scoring · [[Internal Literary Evidence in Jeremiah 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Identification *(tentative)*
> Many commentators identify the paired 'king and queen' of verse 18 with young King Jehoiachin and his politically prominent queen mother (gebirah), who according to the historical record were deported together to Babylon shortly after Jehoiachin's brief reign — making this plausibly a datable oracle from just before the 597 BC deportation rather than a generic address.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · disputed historical identification, no citations

> [!evidence] Textual/Geographical *(tentative)*
> Scholars are divided on whether the 'Euphrates' of verses 4-7 (Hebrew Perath) is the great river roughly 700 km/400+ miles round-trip from Jerusalem, or a much nearer village such as Parah near Anathoth (Jeremiah's home territory, cf. Joshua 18:23), with the same Hebrew consonants possibly functioning as a deliberate pun regardless of which site is meant.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · genuinely disputed, but mileage/pun details unsourced

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Is the appeal in vv15-16 offered as something that could still avert the announced disaster, or mainly as a formal warning that establishes accountability regardless of outcome?
- Who are "the king and...the queen" of v18 — a specific historical pair, or a more general address to the Davidic throne and its leading woman?
- Was the girdle carried to the Euphrates River, or to a nearer namesake place — and does the answer change how the sign functions rhetorically?
- How should the proverb of v23, which stresses habituated conduct, be related to scripture's broader teaching that people can change through repentance and grace?
