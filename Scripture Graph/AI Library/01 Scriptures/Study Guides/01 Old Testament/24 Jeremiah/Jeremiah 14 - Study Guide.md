# Jeremiah 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 14]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Jeremiah 14 moves from a devastating drought (vv. 2-6) to two communal prayers of confession (vv. 7-9, 19-22), each appealing to the LORD's own name and covenant rather than to the people's merit. Between the prayers, the LORD flatly refuses the people's fasting and offerings, forbids Jeremiah from interceding on their behalf, and announces sword, famine, and pestilence (vv. 10-12) - then condemns prophets who have been promising 'assured peace' in his name without being sent (vv. 13-16). Jeremiah himself is commanded to weep over the coming devastation (vv. 17-18). The chapter closes with a plea that only the LORD, not the 'vanities of the Gentiles,' can send rain (v. 22) - and ends there, without any recorded answer.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Drought affecting land, city, and animals (vv. 1-6).**
2. **Confession and appeal to the Lord's name and presence (vv. 7-9).**
3. **Divine refusal and prohibition of intercession (vv. 10-12).**
4. **Dispute over prophets promising peace (vv. 13-16).**
5. **Commissioned lament over sword and famine (vv. 17-18).**
6. **Renewed confession and covenant appeal (vv. 19-22).**

This sequence alternates human appeal and divine response, but the final appeal receives no answer before the chapter ends.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Fasting]], prayer, and sacrifice as not mechanically effective: the people fast, cry out, and offer burnt offerings and oblations, yet the LORD states plainly that he will not hear or accept them (vv. 10-12) so long as the underlying pattern of 'wandering' and unrepented sin continues. This does not mean such practices are condemned in themselves - only that ritual observance offered without change in conduct is refused in this specific moment of judgment. The chapter also draws a firm line between true and false [[Prophecy]]: prophets who promise peace and safety 'in my name' without having been sent are declared false, and both they and those who believed them bear consequences (vv. 14-16), showing that deceptive leadership does not remove a community's own accountability. Both communal prayers appeal to the LORD's name and to his [[Covenants]] rather than to the people's own standing (vv. 7, 21), reflecting confession of [[Sin]] without any stated demonstration of changed conduct within the chapter itself.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV text preserves several archaic terms worth glossing for readers: 'chapt' (v. 4) means cracked or fissured; 'astonied' (v. 9) means astonished or stunned; a 'wayfaring man' (v. 8) is a traveler passing through rather than staying; and 'dragons' (v. 6, describing the wild asses' panting) reflects an older translation convention, plausibly for jackals or similarly wild creatures, rather than mythical dragons. Drought and devastation are conveyed through accumulated images of emptiness and failure - empty vessels, cracked ground, an abandoned calf, failing eyes, absent grass - before the prayers shift to relational language for God's apparent distance (a stranger, a wayfaring man, an astonished warrior) set against the insistence that he remains 'in the midst of us' (v. 9). The paired phrase 'sword...famine[...and pestilence]' recurs across the chapter (vv. 12, 15, 16, 18) almost as a fixed formula rather than a spontaneous image.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's power builds from sensory, embodied imagery - cracked ground, empty vessels, animals gasping for lack of grass - before shifting into direct address and divine dialogue. The alternation between the people's plural voice ('we,' 'us') and the LORD's first-person response creates a call-and-response rhythm, but the two exchanges are not symmetrical: the first prayer (vv. 7-9) receives an explicit divine refusal (v. 10), while the second prayer (vv. 19-22) receives no recorded answer at all within the chapter, leaving the drought and threatened judgment formally unresolved on the page. A further reversal appears in verses 13-15, where the false prophets' promise that 'sword and famine shall not be' is answered with the judgment that 'by sword and famine shall those prophets be consumed' - though this kind of exact turnabout also matches standard covenant-judgment phrasing found elsewhere in Jeremiah, not necessarily a device unique to this passage.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What distinguishes the confession in verses 7-9 and 19-22 from the kind of [[Repentance]] God elsewhere accepts - does the LORD's rejection in verse 10 turn on the words themselves, or on an unstated but implied absence of changed conduct?
- Why would God forbid his own prophet from interceding on the people's behalf (v. 11), yet in the same chapter command that same prophet to weep unceasingly over their suffering (v. 17)? What does that combination suggest about the relationship between prophetic prayer, lament, and human agency?
- The false prophets of verses 13-15 spoke comforting words 'in my name' - what does the passage's own stated test (whether the LORD 'sent' and 'commanded' them, v. 14) suggest about how a hearer could recognize authentic prophecy in the moment, without the benefit of later judgment?
- Why does the chapter end with a renewed plea (v. 22) rather than a divine answer?
