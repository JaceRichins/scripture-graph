# Jeremiah 44 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 44]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 44 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Jeremiah 44 presents a confrontation between Jeremiah and Judean refugees living throughout Egypt. Jeremiah reads Jerusalem's ruins as the consequence of idolatry and ignored prophetic warnings. The refugees read their experience in precisely the opposite way: they associate worship of the queen of heaven with prosperity and its cessation with disaster. Their explicit refusal to listen turns the chapter into a contest over whose interpretation—and whose word—will stand. Judgment is described in sweeping terms, although a small remnant will survive and return to Judah.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Past warning and present accusation (vv. 1–10):** Judah's ruins are invoked as evidence of the consequences of refusing prophetic correction.
2. **First judgment oracle (vv. 11–14):** sword, famine, and pestilence are announced against the remnant in Egypt.
3. **The people's counterclaim (vv. 15–19):** they refuse Jeremiah's word and associate former prosperity with worship of the queen of heaven.
4. **Jeremiah's rebuttal (vv. 20–23):** he attributes Judah's desolation to those same offerings and to disobedience.
5. **Renewed oracle and sign (vv. 24–30):** the people's vows are answered with judgment, a small-remnant qualification, and the sign concerning Pharaoh-hophra.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Obedience]] as more than passive religious identification: the decisive issue is whether the people will hear, turn, and walk in the revealed law. It also depicts [[Prophets]] as warning before judgment rather than merely explaining disaster afterward. The people's explicit refusal shows that continued [[Apostasy]] is portrayed as deliberate within the narrative, although the chapter's theological explanation should not be confused with an independently testable account of every political or economic cause of Judah's collapse.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter’s strongest literary feature is the clash of two causal narratives. The people associate offerings to the queen of heaven with remembered abundance and their cessation with deprivation (vv. 17–18); Jeremiah attributes the desolation to those offerings and to wider disobedience (vv. 21–23). The text presents the opposing argument forcefully, although it does not independently establish the historical truth of either supernatural explanation.

Words and deeds are also joined. The people declare vows and fulfill them “with your hand” (v. 25), while the conclusion asks whose words will “stand” (v. 28). The sign involving Pharaoh-hophra gives that contest a proposed test within the narrative, without by itself proving the prediction’s date or fulfillment.

Finally, the sweeping destruction language of verses 12 and 27 is qualified by the escape clauses in verses 14 and 28. The most straightforward reading is near-total destruction with an explicit surviving remnant, though the rhetoric retains some tension.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why do the refugees interpret earlier prosperity as proof that their worship was effective, and what evidence would have changed their conclusion? How does Jeremiah distinguish an event from a correct explanation of that event? Does verse 19 appeal to male participation, authorization, or both? Why is Pharaoh-hophra's fate an appropriate sign for Judeans who sought security in Egypt? How should the sweeping destruction language be read alongside the promised small remnant?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Jeremiah 42]] and [[Jeremiah 43]] as the immediate narrative background: warning against entering Egypt is followed by migration there and then by this oracle. Compare [[Jeremiah 7]], especially its description of household participation in offerings to the queen of heaven. Jeremiah 39:5–7 supplies the account of Zedekiah’s fate invoked in Jeremiah 44:30. Deuteronomy 28 offers broader covenant-judgment context for the language of sword, famine, pestilence, curse, reproach, and displacement, though direct literary dependence should not be assumed.
