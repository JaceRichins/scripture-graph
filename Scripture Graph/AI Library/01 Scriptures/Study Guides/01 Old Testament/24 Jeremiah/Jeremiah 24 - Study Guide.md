# Jeremiah 24 — Study Guide

## Overview

> Zedekiah and the people of Judah will be cursed and scattered—Some will be gathered back from Chaldea to serve the Lord.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The symbolic scene (vv. 1–3):** Two sharply contrasting baskets stand before the temple, and Jeremiah identifies what he sees.

2. **The good figs interpreted (vv. 4–7):** The deportees are promised divine regard, return, rebuilding, planting, inward renewal, and restored covenant relationship.

3. **The evil figs interpreted (vv. 8–10):** Zedekiah, his princes, Jerusalem's remnant, and the Judeans in Egypt are threatened with dispersion, disgrace, sword, famine, and pestilence.

The two interpretations are deliberately unequal in emphasis: the good-fig section culminates in knowing God and wholehearted return, while the evil-fig section culminates in removal from the land.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter links [[Gathering of Israel]] with inward covenant renewal. Return to the land is important, but the promise reaches its climax in a transformed heart, knowledge of the LORD, and the relationship expressed by “they shall be my people, and I will be their God” (Jeremiah 24:7). This gives [[Repentance]] both a divine and a human dimension: God gives the heart, and the people return wholeheartedly.

The chapter also complicates simplistic readings of [[Adversity]]. The deportees' suffering is not evidence that God has abandoned them, and the relative security of those remaining in Jerusalem is not evidence of divine approval. Nevertheless, the text does not teach that every adversity is automatically beneficial; its positive claim concerns this specifically interpreted exile and God's promised work through it.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated words “good” and “evil” bind the vision to its interpretation. “For their good” in verse 5 turns the figs' quality into a statement about God's intended action toward the deportees. In verse 6, “build” opposes “pull down,” and “plant” opposes “pluck up”; these reversals recall the vocabulary of [[Jeremiah 1]] and announce restoration rather than demolition. The phrase “whole heart” in verse 7 makes the promised return more than geographical movement: it also describes undivided return to God.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter uses a simple but forceful symbolic contrast rather than an extended allegory. The objects are introduced, Jeremiah verifies their visible qualities, and the divine speech assigns each basket to a historical community. Repetition intensifies the contrast—“very good” against “very evil”—while the outcomes mirror it: divine regard, return, building, and planting stand against removal, shame, sword, famine, and pestilence. The design is strong evidence of purposeful literary organization, although it cannot by itself determine authorship or date.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why might those remaining in Jerusalem have appeared more favored than those deported, and does the chapter itself attest that expectation?

- Does “good figs” describe the exiles' prior moral character, God's intended treatment of them, or some combination? What does the passage actually specify?

- How do God's gift of a heart to know him and the people's wholehearted return relate without forcing the verse into a later philosophical debate about agency?

- Does “them that dwell in the land of Egypt” refer to a community already there at the time represented by the vision, or could the wording reflect a later literary horizon connected with [[Jeremiah 42]] and [[Jeremiah 44]]?

- How does the building and planting language shared with [[Jeremiah 1]] affect the tone of Jeremiah's commission?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the exilic promises in [[Jeremiah 29]] and [[Jeremiah 32]], especially their language of return and renewed relationship with God. [[Ezekiel 11]] closely joins gathering with transformation of the heart. Read Jeremiah 24:9-10 alongside [[Deuteronomy 28]] for covenant-curse background, while also noticing how often Jeremiah uses similar judgment formulas elsewhere. Compare the reference to those dwelling in Egypt with [[Jeremiah 44]], without assuming in advance that both chapters address exactly the same historical group. These parallels establish useful thematic and historical relationships, not necessarily direct literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
