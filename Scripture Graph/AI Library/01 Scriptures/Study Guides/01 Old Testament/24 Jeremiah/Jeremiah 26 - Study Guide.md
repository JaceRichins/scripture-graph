# Jeremiah 26 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 26]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 26 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Jeremiah 26 retells, from a narrative rather than an oracular angle, the aftermath of a temple sermon delivered early in the reign of Jehoiakim (v. 1). Jeremiah is commanded to stand in the temple court and speak the LORD's full message without omission (v. 2): if Judah will hearken and turn from evil, the LORD may relent of the threatened calamity; if not, the temple will share the fate of Shiloh and the city will become a curse (vv. 3-6). Priests, prophets, and the people seize Jeremiah and demand his death (vv. 7-9). The princes of Judah convene a hearing at the temple's "new gate," where Jeremiah defends himself solely by reasserting his divine commission, renewing the call to repentance, submitting his life to the assembly, and warning that killing him would bring "innocent blood" on the city (vv. 10-15). The princes and people reject the death sentence (v. 16). Elders then recall that [[Prophets|Micah]] delivered an almost identical warning about Zion a century earlier under Hezekiah and was not put to death (vv. 17-19) — but the chapter immediately follows this with the darker case of Urijah, a prophet with the same message, who was hunted down in Egypt and executed by Jehoiakim (vv. 20-23). The chapter closes by crediting Jeremiah's ultimate safety not only to the assembly's verdict but to the personal protection of Ahikam son of Shaphan (v. 24).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Commission and conditional warning (vv. 1-6)** — Jeremiah must speak every commanded word; repentance may avert the threatened judgment.
2. **Seizure and accusation (vv. 7-11)** — Priests, prophets, and the people seize Jeremiah in the temple; the princes convene a hearing at the new gate, where priests and prophets press for his death.
3. **Jeremiah's defense (vv. 12-15)** — He reasserts his commission, renews the call to repentance, submits his life to the assembly, and warns against shedding innocent blood.
4. **Verdict (v. 16)** — The princes and people reject the death sentence.
5. **Two precedents (vv. 17-23)** — The elders recall Micah's similar prophecy under Hezekiah, who was spared; the account then turns to Urijah, who delivered a comparable message under Jehoiakim and was executed.
6. **Coda (v. 24)** — Jeremiah's survival is credited to Ahikam son of Shaphan's protection.

Placing the Urijah account immediately after the favorable Micah precedent and the verdict keeps Jeremiah's fate feeling genuinely uncertain even after his acquittal; whether this ordering reflects deliberate authorial contrast or is simply a second data point about Jehoiakim's character is not something the text itself settles.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter teaches that judgment oracles are conditional, not fixed decrees: the LORD's own instruction states the purpose is that the people might repent "that I may repent me of the evil" (v. 3), Jeremiah repeats this logic before the princes (v. 13), and the elders' example from Hezekiah's day shows it having actually happened (v. 19). This ties directly to [[Repentance]] and [[Obedience]] — the entire warning turns on whether Judah will "hearken." The chapter also refuses to treat the temple itself as an unconditional guarantee of safety, warning that the sanctuary could share Shiloh's fate — related to [[Temples]]. Jeremiah's warning that killing him would bring "innocent blood" on the city raises [[Justice and Mercy]] as a live, practical consideration in the assembly's deliberation, not merely an abstract principle.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated vocabulary of hearing organizes much of the chapter. Judah has not "hearkened" to the law or the prophets (26:4–5), yet the accusers appeal to what the authorities have heard "with your ears" (26:11), and Jeremiah says his words were spoken "in your ears" (26:15). Hearing is therefore more than exposure to sound; the opening warning distinguishes hearing that leads to obedience from hearing that produces hostility.

The KJV expression that the LORD will "repent" of evil (26:3, 13, 19) refers in context to withdrawing threatened calamity after human turning. The chapter itself supplies that sense through the repeated if-then relationship; it does not portray God as confessing sinful conduct.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The clearest literary feature is the elders' citation of Micah by name with wording that closely matches Micah 3:12 (v. 18), invoked less than a century later as evidence that a stern judgment prophecy need not be a capital offense (vv. 18-19). Whether this reflects a stable written text of Micah already in circulation, careful oral memory, or later editorial harmonization by Jeremiah's compilers cannot be determined from this chapter alone. The account is immediately followed by Urijah's execution for an almost identical message (vv. 20-23), which complicates rather than confirms the elders' argument — the same kind of prophecy that spared Micah cost Urijah his life, suggesting royal disposition mattered as much as any legal principle. The official chapter heading's language of arraignment, trial, and acquittal is a useful shorthand, but the narrative itself is less procedurally explicit: it describes an accusation, a hearing, a defense, and a favorable declaration, without naming a specific law or formal charge.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Jeremiah's defense rest entirely on his divine commission rather than any other argument — is this a strength or a vulnerability in the hearing?
- Why does the narrative pair the Micah precedent that saves a prophet with the Urijah account that condemns one for an almost identical message, right after Jeremiah's own acquittal?
- Does "all the people" describe a single stable group, or does the crowd's position shift between verse 8 (accusers) and verse 16 (acquitters)?
- How should we weigh the princes' and people's declaration (v. 16) against Ahikam's personal protection (v. 24) in explaining why Jeremiah actually survived?
- What does the elders' ability to quote Micah accurately after roughly a century suggest about how prophetic words were preserved in ancient Judah?
