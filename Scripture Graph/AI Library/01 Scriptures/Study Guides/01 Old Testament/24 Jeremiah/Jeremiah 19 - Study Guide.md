# Jeremiah 19 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 19]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 19 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Lord will bring evil upon Judah—They sacrifice their children to Baal—In the siege they will eat the flesh of their sons and daughters.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Commission and witnesses (vv. 1–2):** Jeremiah obtains a bottle, takes elders of the people and priests, and goes to the valley.
2. **Announcement and indictment (vv. 3–5):** Calamity is announced and grounded in foreign worship, innocent blood, and child-burning.
3. **Consequences (vv. 6–9):** The valley is renamed; defeat, desolation, exposed corpses, and extreme siege conditions are predicted.
4. **Enacted sign and interpretation (vv. 10–13):** Jeremiah breaks the bottle, interprets the action, and compares Jerusalem’s defilement with Tophet.
5. **Public conclusion (vv. 14–15):** He returns to the Lord’s-house court, addresses all the people, and identifies persistent refusal to hear.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Jeremiah 19:5 emphatically rejects the burning of children to Baal as divinely authorized [[Sacrifice]]: it was not commanded or spoken by the Lord. The expression “neither came it into my mind” functions here as forceful repudiation and should not be converted into a technical claim about divine foreknowledge.

The chapter portrays [[Apostasy]] as involving worship, violence against innocents, and practices that defile communal places. It presents the coming judgment as a response to persistent communal wrongdoing and refusal to hear. Its emphasis falls overwhelmingly on judgment; it contains no explicit invitation to repentance or promise of mercy, so a full treatment of [[Justice and Mercy]] must draw upon the wider book.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV's "evil" in verses 3 and 15 is best read contextually as threatened calamity or disaster. The expressions "ears shall tingle" and "hardened their necks" are vivid idioms for horrifying news and stubborn refusal to listen. The bottle metaphor depends on its being a finished earthen vessel: once shattered, it cannot be remade as moist clay can.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Place, object, and hearing language work together. A location associated in the accusation with innocent blood and child-burning is renamed for coming slaughter. Jeremiah then visibly breaks the bottle and explains that the people and city will likewise be broken in a way that “cannot be made whole again” (Jeremiah 19:11). The movement from Tophet to the court of the Lord’s house also carries the warning from the place named in the indictment to a broader public audience.

Hearing language appears near both ends of the oracle: the coming calamity will make ears tingle (Jeremiah 19:3), while the conclusion condemns the people for refusing to hear the Lord’s words (Jeremiah 19:15). This creates a persuasive contrast between rejected warning and encountered consequence, although repeated prophetic idiom is another possible explanation.

[[Jeremiah 18]] offers an illuminating but not demonstrably intentional contrast. There clay is reworked and announced outcomes are conditional upon a nation’s response; here a completed vessel is shattered beyond repair. Read together, the images suggest escalating finality concerning the announced communal disaster, but they do not establish that no individual could repent.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Why are elders from both the people and the priests chosen to witness the sign?
2. What rhetorical purpose is served by performing the sign at Tophet and then proclaiming judgment in the court of the Lord’s house?
3. How does the repeated material in [[Jeremiah 7]], [[Jeremiah 19]], and [[Jeremiah 32]] function within the book?
4. How should the irreparable bottle in [[Jeremiah 19]] be read beside the workable clay and conditional language of [[Jeremiah 18]]?
5. Does the movement from child-burning in verse 5 to siege cannibalism in verse 9 suggest retributive irony, general social collapse, covenant-curse rhetoric, or some combination?
6. What does the three-part denial in verse 5 establish, and what philosophical conclusions would go beyond its immediate purpose?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Jeremiah 7]] for the closest internal parallel concerning Tophet and the valley of slaughter. Compare [[Jeremiah 18]] for the earlier pottery sign and its conditional language. Deuteronomy 28:53-57 supplies covenant-curse background for the siege imagery, while Lamentations 4:10 provides a later biblical depiction of the same kind of catastrophe. These correspondences illuminate Jeremiah 19, but they do not by themselves resolve questions about composition, dating, or the precise historical realization of the oracle.
