# Jeremiah 52 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 52]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 52 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Jeremiah 52 recounts the collapse of Judah's kingdom: Jerusalem is besieged and breached, Zedekiah is captured and punished, the temple and city are destroyed, leaders are executed, and inhabitants are deported. The narrator explicitly frames the disaster in terms of royal evil and the LORD's anger while also recording its human and political mechanisms. The final scene gives Jehoiachin improved treatment in Babylon, but it does not report Judah's restoration or a return to Jerusalem.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **Verses 1-3:** Zedekiah's reign and the narrator's theological frame.
- **Verses 4-11:** Siege, breach, flight, capture, and punishment of Zedekiah.
- **Verses 12-16:** Burning of Jerusalem, demolition of its walls, and population displacement.
- **Verses 17-23:** Dismantling and inventory of temple objects.
- **Verses 24-27:** Removal and execution of religious, military, and civic leaders.
- **Verses 28-30:** Three deportation notices and their total.
- **Verses 31-34:** Jehoiachin's release and continuing support in Babylon.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's explicit theological interpretation is concentrated in verses 2-3: royal evil, the LORD's anger, Judah's expulsion, and Zedekiah's rebellion appear together. The text thus holds divine judgment and accountable human action together without explaining exactly how they relate. This framing should not be generalized into a rule that every defeat or instance of suffering is direct punishment for a particular sin.

For study of [[Scattering of Israel]], verse 27 is central: Judah is carried captive out of its land. The chapter portrays displacement through famine, death, forced migration, and dismantled institutions. Its relevance to [[Temples]] is equally concrete: the house of the LORD is burned, and its metal furnishings and vessels are broken up or removed.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several repeated expressions intensify the account. Comprehensive terms such as “all” and “round about” accompany the burning of houses, demolition of walls, and seizure of vessels, creating an impression of thorough devastation. At the close, repetition shifts toward continuity: Jehoiachin eats before the king “continually,” receives a portion “every day,” and is maintained “all the days of his life.” The same chapter thus uses sustained duration first for Zedekiah's imprisonment and finally for Jehoiachin's provision.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Jeremiah 52 closely parallels [[2 Kings 25]] in sequence and content, while differing in details such as dates, measurements, and counts. Together with the closing formula in Jeremiah 51:64, this makes it plausible to read chapter 52 as an editorial historical appendix. That inference does not identify its compiler, source, date, or direction of dependence on Kings.

The extended temple inventory foregrounds the material and sacred dimensions of Jerusalem's fall, though it may preserve administrative or archival material rather than—or as well as—deliberate symbolism. Its objects recall those described in [[1 Kings 7]]: what is constructed there is broken or removed here. The final juxtaposition contrasts Zedekiah's lifelong imprisonment with Jehoiachin's release and lifelong provision, but the text does not explicitly interpret Jehoiachin's treatment as a promise of national restoration.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying

- Why does the narrator devote seven verses to the temple objects and their measurements?
- What population is represented by the total of 4,600, and whom might that count exclude?
- How does the ending change if Jehoiachin's release is read as ordinary court history rather than as a sign of hope?
- How should the differences between [[Jeremiah 52]] and [[2 Kings 25]] be evaluated?
- How can readers distinguish the narrator's explicit theological interpretation from conclusions about the precise historical and literary process behind the account?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[2 Kings 25]] beside this chapter, noting both the extensive agreement and the differences in dates, measurements, personnel counts, and added material. Compare the destroyed objects in Jeremiah 52:17-23 with their construction in [[1 Kings 7]]. Read [[Jeremiah 39]] for another account of Jerusalem's fall and Jeremiah 34:2-5 for the warning concerning Zedekiah and the city. [[Ezekiel 12]] provides another relevant statement about Zedekiah's fate. These correspondences are significant for studying [[Prophecy]], but they do not by themselves settle composition dates, literary dependence, or supernatural prediction.
