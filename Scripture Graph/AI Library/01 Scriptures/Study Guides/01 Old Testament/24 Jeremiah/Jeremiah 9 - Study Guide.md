## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. **Verses 1–9:** grief, desire for withdrawal, and pervasive deceptive speech.
2. **Verses 10–16:** lament over the land, a question about its ruin, and the LORD's explanation and sentence.
3. **Verses 17–22:** communal wailing and images of death entering homes and public spaces.
4. **Verses 23–24:** false grounds for glory contrasted with knowing the LORD.
5. **Verses 25–26:** judgment on circumcised and uncircumcised peoples, climaxing in Israel's uncircumcision of heart.

The shifts at verses 23 and 25 are abrupt enough that this outline should not be treated as proof of a single, seamless literary design.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Knowing God is relational and ethical, not merely informational.** The chapter's low point is that the people "know not" the LORD (v. 3) and "refuse to know" him "through deceit" (v. 6); its resolution is the invitation to glory in "understand[ing] and know[ing]" him as the one who exercises lovingkindness, judgment, and righteousness in the earth — attributes echoed in the [[Justice and Mercy]] theme (v. 24). Verse 24 describes God's own character; it does not explicitly command believers to imitate it, though the contrast with the chapter's deceit and self-reliance makes an ethical reading reasonable.
- **[[Pride]] versus [[Humility]].** Verse 23 rejects wisdom, might, and riches as grounds for glorying, redirecting boasting toward knowing God.
- **[[Apostasy]] as a named cause of judgment.** Verses 13–14 give two specific causes: forsaking the law and disobeying the LORD's voice, then walking after "the imagination of their own heart, and after Baalim."
- **Outward covenant status does not exempt from judgment.** Verses 25–26 place Judah among nations under judgment and distinguish the nations' (physical) uncircumcision from "all the house of Israel" being "uncircumcised in the heart" — a warning against presuming an outward sign guarantees inward fidelity, not a rejection of circumcision or the covenant itself.
- **[[Scattering of Israel]].** Verse 16 explicitly forecasts exile "among the heathen, whom neither they nor their fathers have known."

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
- **Weaponized speech.** Verses 3 and 8 bracket the indictment of deceit with military imagery: tongues are bent "like their bow for lies" (v. 3) and become "an arrow shot out" that "speaketh deceit" (v. 8); the intervening verses (4–7) elaborate the social mechanics of that deceit — brother against brother, neighbor against neighbor — without extending the weapon metaphor itself.
- **The "know" refrain.** The people "know not" the LORD (v. 3), "refuse to know" him "through deceit" (v. 6), and are invited to glory in "understand[ing] and know[ing]" him (v. 24) — a verbal thread linking the chapter's indictment to its closing maxim across changing genres.
- **Refining imagery** (v. 7, "I will melt them, and try them") casts judgment as a metallurgical process; the chapter does not resolve whether the image points toward purification, mere testing, or destruction, especially given the later threat to "consume" the people (v. 16).
- **"Walked after the imagination of their own heart"** (v. 14) is an idiom Jeremiah also uses elsewhere (e.g., Jeremiah 7:24) to describe willful, self-directed disobedience; because this phrasing is common Deuteronomistic language, its recurrence may reflect shared covenantal idiom rather than a deliberate cross-reference between these two specific chapters.
- **Agricultural death imagery** (v. 22): corpses left "as dung upon the open field, and as the handful after the harvestman" inverts an ordinary harvest image into a picture of mass, unburied death.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Lament expands in scale. It begins with one speaker's desired fountain of tears (verse 1), moves to mourning over mountains and wilderness habitations (verse 10), and becomes organized communal wailing taught from women to daughters and neighbors (verses 17–20). Death is then personified as penetrating windows and palaces (verse 21), and the unburied dead are compared to refuse and abandoned harvest handfuls (verse 22). These images deny the possibility of keeping catastrophe at a safe distance.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the movement from “know not me” in verse 3 to “understandeth and knoweth me” in verse 24 clarify the chapter's definition of knowing God?
- What happens to a community when deceptive speech becomes habitual, as described in verses 3–8?
- Why are wisdom, might, and riches inadequate grounds for glory in verses 23–24?
- What distinction does verse 26 make between physical circumcision and the condition of the heart, and what conclusions would go beyond the verse?
- Does the repeated mourning language indicate a deliberate literary frame, or can it be adequately explained by the chapter's subject matter?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Jeremiah 9:25–26 with Deuteronomy 10:16 and Romans 2:28–29 for three uses of heart-circumcision language. Compare Jeremiah 9:13, 26 with Jeremiah 31:31–34 for the contrast between forsaken law, an unresponsive heart, and law written in the heart. Jeremiah 9:23–24 may also be studied alongside 1 Corinthians 1:31, while preserving the differences between Jeremiah's fuller formulation and Paul's compressed wording.
