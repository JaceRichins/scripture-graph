## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful reading outline is:

1. **The unsuccessful search for faithfulness (5:1-5):** neither the poor nor the great obey God's way.
2. **Transgression and deserved visitation (5:6-9):** predatory-animal images accompany charges of idolatry and adultery.
3. **Rejected warning and the fiery word (5:10-14):** the people deny sword and famine; God's word becomes fire.
4. **Foreign invasion, limited destruction, and exile (5:15-19):** a distant nation consumes the land, yet does not make a full end.
5. **Creation's boundary and Judah's rebellion (5:20-25):** the bounded sea is contrasted with an unbounded rebellious heart.
6. **Social and religious corruption (5:26-31):** deceit enriches the wicked, the vulnerable receive no justice, and prophets, priests, and people sustain falsehood.

The repeated question “Shall I not visit for these things?” in verses 9 and 29 helps frame the two large catalogs of wrongdoing.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter treats [[Repentance]] as more than suffering consequences: the people have been struck, but refuse correction and refuse to return (5:3). It also rejects the idea that correct religious phrases alone constitute faithfulness; “The LORD liveth” can be spoken falsely (5:2). Judgment responds not only to idolatry and sexual misconduct but also to deceit and failure to defend the fatherless and needy (5:26-29). Thus covenant loyalty includes [[Truth]], moral fidelity, and public justice.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several governing images intensify the accusation. A lion, wolf, and leopard depict the approaching danger (5:6). God's word becomes fire and the people wood (5:14). The invader's quiver is an open sepulchre (5:16). Wicked people trap human beings as hunters trap birds, while houses filled through deceit resemble cages full of captured birds (5:26-27). These are not decorative comparisons: each converts concealed moral danger into a visible threat.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Jeremiah 5 repeatedly uses contrast and reversal. The poor are assumed ignorant, but the knowledgeable great are equally rebellious (5:4-5). The turbulent sea obeys its boundary, while God's people possess a rebellious heart (5:22-23). Those who trap others become prosperous (5:26-28), reversing the expected relation between justice and success. The closing line then asks what such apparent success will become “in the end” (5:31). The distant nation of 5:15 also closely recalls [[Deuteronomy 28]], especially Deuteronomy 28:49, placing the invasion within covenant-curse language without by itself settling questions of literary direction or dating.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Intertextual Verbal And Thematic Parallel
> Jeremiah 5:15 closely parallels the covenant-curse language of Deuteronomy 28:49: both describe a nation brought from far away whose language Judah or Israel cannot understand. This strongly supports reading the announced invasion through a Deuteronomic covenant-curse framework.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong verbal parallel; direction of dependence unresolved · [[Intertextual Verbal And Thematic Parallel in Jeremiah 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Form Criticism / Genre Analysis *(tentative)*
> Jeremiah 5 exhibits features commonly associated by biblical scholars with the prophetic 'covenant lawsuit' (rib) form: direct address by the divine plaintiff, an indictment of covenant infidelity ('dealt very treacherously,' 5:11), a summons to witnesses/observers, and announced punishment — a recognized genre pattern rather than a claim unique to this chapter.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · Plausible form-critical reading; classification disputed

> [!evidence] Prophecy / Historical Correspondence *(tentative)*
> Jeremiah 5:19 frames coming exile as a measure-for-measure consequence: 'Like as ye have forsaken me, and served strange gods in your land, so shall ye serve strangers in a land that is not yours,' presenting foreign servitude as symmetrical retribution for served foreign gods, which later Israelite history (the Babylonian captivity) is traditionally read as fulfilling.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · Symmetrical judgment accepted; predictive significance disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the opening search join executing judgment with seeking truth (5:1)?
- What excuses for disobedience are tested and rejected by the movement from the poor to the great (5:4-5)?
- How do the two promises that there will not be a full end qualify the chapter's judgment language (5:10, 18)?
- Why does the text compare the people's heart with the sea's divinely fixed boundary (5:22-23)?
- How do prophets, priests, and the people each contribute to the crisis in 5:31?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Jeremiah 5:1 with [[Genesis 18]] and [[Ezekiel 22]] for three distinct treatments of righteous persons and threatened judgment. Compare Jeremiah 5:15 with [[Deuteronomy 28]] for the distant-nation covenant curse, and Jeremiah 5:21 with [[Deuteronomy 29]] for the imagery of unseeing eyes and unhearing ears. [[Jeremiah 23]] provides a fuller treatment of false prophets who assure hearers that evil will not come.
