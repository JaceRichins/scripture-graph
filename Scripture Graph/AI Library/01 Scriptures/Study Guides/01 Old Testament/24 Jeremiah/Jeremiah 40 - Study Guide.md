# Jeremiah 40 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 40]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 40 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The king of Babylon makes Gedaliah governor over the remnant left in Judah—Jeremiah is freed and dwells among them.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Jeremiah released (vv. 1–6):** Nebuzar-adan attributes Jerusalem's fall to Judah's sin, frees Jeremiah, and lets him choose where to live; Jeremiah joins Gedaliah at Mizpah.
2. **The remnant reorganized (vv. 7–12):** Gedaliah swears an oath of cooperation with Babylon, and refugees return from Moab, Ammon, and Edom to a resumed harvest.
3. **A warning rejected (vv. 13–16):** Johanan reports a plot against Gedaliah and offers to kill Ishmael pre-emptively; Gedaliah refuses both the killing and the accusation. Verse 16 is a hinge the narrative resolves in [[Jeremiah 41]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Nebuzar-adan's speech (vv. 2–3) frames Judah's fall as the fulfillment of the LORD's declared warning against disobedience — words placed in a foreign officer's mouth, best read as reported speech rather than a claim about his personal belief. Jeremiah's freedom to choose where to live (vv. 4–6) is a narrow, circumstantial version of [[Agency]] rather than a doctrinal treatise on it. The regional return of Jews to Judah (vv. 11–12) touches both [[Scattering of Israel]] and [[Gathering of Israel]], though it remains a small, precarious recovery rather than a completed restoration — a caution reinforced when the community collapses again in [[Jeremiah 41]].

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter opens with the classic 'word that came to Jeremiah' formula (v. 1), yet what follows is third-person narrative rather than a first-person oracle — a mismatch the text does not explain, though it may simply mark the start of a larger narrative unit (chs. 40–45). Verses 14–16 build dramatic irony for readers who already know from [[Jeremiah 41]] that Ishmael's plot was real: Gedaliah's confident dismissal ('thou speakest falsely of Ishmael,' v. 16) reads as tragic misjudgment in hindsight, though a first-time reader of this chapter alone has no way yet to judge whether the accusation was true.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does Nebuzar-adan's language in verses 2–3 reflect his own conviction, pragmatic recognition of Jeremiah's accuracy, or the narrator's theological shaping of the speech?
- Was Gedaliah's refusal to credit the warning principled restraint against extrajudicial killing, a fatal misjudgment, or both at once?
- What does the text actually establish about Baalis's role — is Ammonite sponsorship confirmed by Ishmael's later act, or does it remain an unverified accusation?
- What does Johanan's proposal to kill Ishmael secretly (v. 15) suggest about the political instability of the remnant community?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Jeremiah 40:5, 7–9 with [[2 Kings 25]], which preserves a closely parallel account of Gedaliah's appointment and policy. Read [[Jeremiah 41]] for the assassination and dispersal that follow, then [[Jeremiah 42]] and [[Jeremiah 43]] for the remnant's decisions after Gedaliah's death.
