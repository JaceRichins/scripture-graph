## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful thematic outline is:

1. **Remembered relationship (vv. 1–3):** youthful kindness, bridal love, wilderness following, holiness, and firstfruits.
2. **Summons and accusation (vv. 4–13):** deliverance is forgotten, leaders fail, and the people exchange their glory for what does not profit.
3. **Consequences and foreign reliance (vv. 14–19):** devastation is linked to forsaking the LORD and seeking the waters of Egypt and Assyria.
4. **Images of disordered allegiance (vv. 20–28):** harlotry, a degenerate vine, futile washing, uncontrolled animals, shame, and powerless idols portray persistent pursuit of substitutes.
5. **Refused correction and failed defenses (vv. 29–37):** correction is rejected, innocent blood is charged, innocence is claimed, and foreign confidences fail.

This outline describes thematic movement. It does not establish that the chapter originated as one uninterrupted speech or followed a rigid legal template.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Jeremiah 2 portrays [[Apostasy]] as a relational breach with practical consequences. Israel forsakes God and then constructs substitutes that cannot hold, save, or prosper (Jeremiah 2:13, 28, 37). Verse 19 presents [[Sin]] as bitter and partly self-correcting: wickedness and backsliding reprove the offender. This does not eliminate divine judgment; the LORD also pleads, exposes, and announces consequences.

Grace and deliverance precede the accusation. The LORD first brought Israel from Egypt, led the people through the wilderness, and gave them a fruitful land (Jeremiah 2:6–7). The indictment therefore concerns their response to prior gifts, creating strong connections with [[Exodus and Deliverance]] and [[Covenants]]. The failure is also systemic: priests, handlers of the law, pastors, prophets, kings, and princes are all implicated (Jeremiah 2:8, 26).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
A major verbal and thematic contrast is between **forsaking** the LORD and pursuing other sources of security. Israel forsakes God (Jeremiah 2:13, 17, 19) while following Baal, strangers, idols, and foreign confidences (Jeremiah 2:8, 23–28, 36–37).

The alternatives are repeatedly portrayed as ineffective. Verses 8 and 11 call them things that do not **profit**; verse 28 says the manufactured gods cannot save; and verse 37 says the rejected confidences will not prosper. These are related judgments, not repetitions of one identical word.

Water imagery sharpens the contrast. The LORD is the fountain of living waters, while Israel's manufactured cisterns are broken and cannot hold water (Jeremiah 2:13). Egypt's and Assyria's waters then represent rival sources of reliance (Jeremiah 2:18), though the exact diplomatic arrangements are not identified.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter accumulates varied images rather than relying on a single extended metaphor:

- **Youth and espousal** (vv. 2, 32): remembered early love contrasts with prolonged forgetting.
- **Fountain and broken cisterns** (v. 13): a living source is exchanged for failed human substitutes.
- **Distant nations** (vv. 10–11): even nations serving non-gods become an ironic foil for Israel's exchange.
- **Vine gone strange** (v. 21): careful planting gives way to distorted growth.
- **Uncontrolled animals** (vv. 23–24): compulsive pursuit is portrayed through mating imagery.
- **Stock and stone** (v. 27): idols receive parental language and are then challenged to save.
- **Bride and attire** (v. 32): ordinary bridal remembrance exposes Israel's extraordinary forgetting.

Together these images give the chapter substantial thematic coherence, but they do not prove single-stage composition or a formal symmetrical structure. The sexual-infidelity imagery is a severe prophetic analogy for broken allegiance; it should not automatically be treated as evidence of literal sexual ritual in every occurrence. [[Jeremiah 3]] continues the marriage and harlotry imagery.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Why does the accusation begin by recalling deliverance and early devotion rather than immediately listing sins (Jeremiah 2:2–7)?
2. How do the two actions in verse 13—forsaking and hewing—clarify the relationship between abandoned allegiance and substitute loyalties?
3. What institutions fail in verse 8, and how does their failure relate to the responsibility of the wider people?
4. In what sense can wickedness itself correct a person or society (verse 19), and how does that coexist with divine judgment?
5. Why do the people's claims of purity or innocence become part of the indictment rather than a successful defense (verses 23, 34–35)?
6. Which modern analogies preserve the difference between a dependable source and a broken cistern without reducing the passage to a vague warning about distractions?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare the deliverance-and-idolatry tension in [[Exodus 32]] with Jeremiah 2:6–11, without assuming direct borrowing.
- Read [[Jeremiah 3]] for the continuation of marriage, harlotry, and divorce imagery.
- Compare [[Jeremiah 17]], especially verse 13, where the LORD is again called the fountain of living waters.
- Compare [[Hosea 7]], especially verse 11, for another prophetic pairing of Egypt and Assyria as objects of misplaced reliance.
- Study Jeremiah 2:34 cautiously across translations and commentaries; the precise legal background of the bloodguilt accusation remains disputed.
