# Jeremiah 25 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 25]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 25 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Jeremiah 25 gathers the chapter's argument into a widening movement. Judah has ignored repeated prophetic calls to [[Repentance]] (vv. 1-8), Babylon is commissioned to impose a seventy-year period of desolation and service but will later be punished (vv. 9-14), and the cup of fury passes from Jerusalem to a broad catalogue of nations (vv. 15-29). The final oracle enlarges the horizon still further with imagery of judgment reaching the ends of the earth (vv. 30-38).

The chapter does not divide humanity into a righteous imperial power and guilty victims. Judah is accountable despite being associated with God's name, while Babylon's instrumental role does not shield Babylon from judgment for its own iniquity. That tension is central to the chapter's treatment of [[Justice and Mercy]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1-8 — Rejected warning:** A royal date introduces Jeremiah's review of twenty-three years of preaching. The message called for changed conduct and abandonment of other gods, but the people would not hear.

2. **Verses 9-14 — Babylonian domination and its limit:** Nebuchadrezzar is called God's servant; Judah and neighboring nations face desolation and seventy years of service to Babylon. Babylon is then promised punishment for its own iniquity and deeds.

3. **Verses 15-29 — The cup among the nations:** Jeremiah receives the cup of fury. Jerusalem heads an extensive list of peoples compelled to drink, and the king of Sheshach is named after them. Sheshach is commonly identified with Babylon, but that identification depends on Hebrew linguistic analysis.

4. **Verses 30-38 — Universalizing judgment and lament:** Roaring, shouting, a controversy with the nations, a whirlwind, unburied slain, and doomed shepherds intensify the conclusion. The language expands beyond the initial Judean setting without necessarily describing every modern nation exhaustively.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents prophetic warning as an act preceding judgment: the people are repeatedly told to turn, hear, and cease idolatry before destruction is announced. This makes [[Prophets]] and [[Repentance]] inseparable themes in the opening section.

Nebuchadrezzar's title "my servant" should be read functionally in this context. A ruler may serve a divine purpose without thereby being declared morally innocent; verses 12-14 explicitly subject Babylon to judgment. The passage consequently resists a simple equation between historical success and divine approval.

The city called by God's name is judged first rather than exempted. Within this chapter, belonging and accountability operate together. The same principle is then extended to rulers and nations, whose deeds are said to receive recompense.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated expression about "rising early" stresses persistence rather than a single neglected warning; compare [[Jeremiah 7]]. Repetition of "ye have not hearkened" makes refusal to listen the controlling accusation in verses 3-8.

The cup produces drunkenness, instability, vomiting, falling, and inability to rise. These are images of coerced participation in catastrophic judgment, explicitly interpreted by the chapter as the effect of the sword rather than ordinary drinking.

Verse 10's domestic soundscape is unusually concrete: celebration, weddings, food production, and household light all disappear. [[Revelation 18]] later uses a closely matching cluster in its portrayal of Babylon's fall.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly reverses apparent privilege and power. Jerusalem is called by God's name but is judged; Babylon is called God's servant but is later punished; shepherds and principal members of the flock possess status but cannot escape. These reversals support the chapter's insistence that no group can infer immunity from its present position.

Its geography also serves a literary function. The list proceeds from Jerusalem through named neighboring and distant peoples to comprehensive expressions such as all kingdoms and the ends of the earth. This creates an expanding rhetorical field. It should not automatically be treated as a modern geographical census, but it unmistakably enlarges the audience of the warning.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should the seventy years be counted: from Babylonian ascendancy, from Jerusalem's destruction, to Babylon's fall, to temple restoration, or as a conventional complete period? The chapter itself identifies service to Babylon and Babylon's punishment as the controlling endpoints but does not supply modern calendar dates.

Does "all the kingdoms of the world" describe the rhetorical horizon of Jeremiah's known geopolitical world, or an unrestricted global event? The universal language is real, but its intended geographical scope must be argued rather than assumed.

How does the divine commissioning of Babylon relate to Babylon's own agency and guilt? Verses 9, 12, and 14 should be kept together when considering that question.
<!-- SG:END questions -->
