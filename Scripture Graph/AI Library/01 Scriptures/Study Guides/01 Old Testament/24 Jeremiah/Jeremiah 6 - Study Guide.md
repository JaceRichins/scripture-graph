## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Jerusalem surrounded (vv. 1-8):** Signals announce danger, attackers encircle the daughter of Zion, and the LORD identifies oppression and violence within the city.
2. **Warning unheard (vv. 9-15):** The prophetic speaker cannot find a receptive audience; corruption reaches both ordinary people and religious leaders, who proclaim peace without addressing the wound.
3. **The good way refused (vv. 16-21):** The people reject both the old paths and the watchmen's trumpet. Their sacrifices are unacceptable because they have rejected the LORD's words and law.
4. **Invasion and lament (vv. 22-26):** A cruel northern nation advances, and the community responds with fear, travail, sackcloth, ashes, and mourning.
5. **The failed assay (vv. 27-30):** Metallurgical imagery portrays testing or refining that cannot remove the wicked; the people are consequently called rejected silver.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter links [[Obedience]] with genuine worship. Jeremiah 6:19-20 does not condemn [[Sacrifice]] merely as ritual; it rejects offerings from people who have refused the LORD's words and law. The prophetic critique therefore resists a division between worship and moral conduct.

The invitation to seek the good way shows that warning is itself an act of possible correction. Yet [[Repentance]] is not coerced: the people answer that they will neither walk nor hearken. The chapter holds together divine warning, human refusal, and judgment without suggesting that outward religious activity can substitute for covenant faithfulness.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The "uncircumcised" ear in verse 10 is a bodily metaphor for covenantal unresponsiveness: the following clause explains that the people cannot hearken and take no delight in the word of the LORD.

"Peace, peace" in verse 14 is memorable because repetition intensifies a confident assurance that the narrator immediately contradicts. In the setting of [[War and Peace]], the slogan functions as denial, not as a true promise.

In verses 1 and 19, "evil" contextually means approaching calamity or disaster. Verse 1 pairs it with destruction, while verse 19 calls it the fruit of the people's thoughts and the consequence of rejecting divine instruction.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly turns ordinary life into imagery of invasion and judgment. Shepherds and flocks become an encircling host; Jerusalem pours out wickedness as continuously as a fountain pours water; the threatened city becomes a woman experiencing travail and mourning an only son; and the final community is metal that cannot be successfully refined.

Two compact refusals organize the central appeal: "We will not walk therein" and "We will not hearken." Walking concerns conduct, while hearing concerns receptivity, together portraying comprehensive resistance.

Jeremiah 6:13-15 recurs closely in [[Jeremiah 8]]. This is strong evidence of literary reuse, but it does not by itself identify whether the prophet repeated an oracle or whether a compiler reused the passage.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Sustained Metaphor
> The closing verses form an extended metallurgical image involving brass, iron, bellows, lead, a founder, refining fire, and rejected silver. The concentration and sequence of these terms strongly support reading the conclusion as an assay or refining process that fails because the wicked are not removed.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong sustained-metaphor reading · [[Sustained Metaphor in Jeremiah 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Recurrence
> Jeremiah 6:13-15 and Jeremiah 8:10-12 contain a closely repeated sequence about universal covetousness, false prophets and priests, superficial healing, false peace, absence of shame, and an appointed fall. The extensive recurrence is strong evidence of a direct literary relationship between the passages.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Mechanically verified literary relationship · [[Textual Recurrence in Jeremiah 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical-Contextual Inference *(tentative)*
> Historically, the unnamed 'north country' invader in Jeremiah 6 is conventionally identified with Babylon under Nebuchadnezzar II, consistent with Jeremiah's documented ministry in the decades before Jerusalem's fall in 586 BC. This identification depends on the wider book of Jeremiah and external history, not on chapter 6 itself, which never names a nation.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does "the old paths" mean mere antiquity, or does the surrounding emphasis on the LORD's words and law identify the paths as established covenantal instruction?
- Who speaks in verses 4-5? Reading the lines as the besiegers' dialogue explains the commands to attack at noon and by night, but the rapid shift in voice is part of the passage's dramatic texture.
- In verse 27, is the person set among the people the prophet acting as an assayer? The refining context favors that reading, although the KJV wording "a tower and a fortress" leaves the precise metaphor less transparent.
- How does the promise of "rest for your souls" in Jeremiah 6:16 illuminate the similar expression in Matthew 11:29 without requiring the stronger claim of a formal quotation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

These comparisons clarify three major themes in Jeremiah 6: reused prophetic language, the recurring northern threat, and the inseparability of worship from justice and [[Obedience]].
