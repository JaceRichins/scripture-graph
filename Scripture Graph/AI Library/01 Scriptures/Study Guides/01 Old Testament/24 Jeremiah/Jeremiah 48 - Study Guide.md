## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. **Jeremiah 48:1-10:** the fall of Moabite cities and urgent calls to flee.
2. **Jeremiah 48:11-17:** Moab's long ease is depicted through wine settled on its lees, followed by forced emptying and humiliation.
3. **Jeremiah 48:18-25:** a geographical roll call presents judgment as reaching the whole land.
4. **Jeremiah 48:26-30:** drunkenness, derision, and concentrated accusations of pride.
5. **Jeremiah 48:31-39:** lament, agricultural loss, silenced worship, and public mourning.
6. **Jeremiah 48:40-46:** the eagle, captured strongholds, and the fear-pit-snare sequence bring the catastrophe to a climax.
7. **Jeremiah 48:47:** a brief restoration coda closes the oracle.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects judgment with false confidence and self-exaltation. Moab trusts in works and treasures (48:7), treats Israel with derision (48:27), and magnifies itself against the LORD (48:26, 42). The text supports [[Pride]] as a central rationale for judgment, though it is not the only charge — trust in wealth and mockery of Israel are named separately.

The chapter also contains one of its most ethically difficult verses: "Cursed be he that doeth the work of the LORD deceitfully, and cursed be he that keepeth back his sword from blood" (48:10). In its immediate setting this curse demands full execution of the judgment just announced against Moab, but it should not be read in isolation from the broader herem (holy-war) tradition found elsewhere in the Old Testament (compare Deuteronomy 20:16-18; 1 Samuel 15:3, 18-19), where withholding the sword in a divinely sanctioned war is itself condemned. Responsible study should name this violence honestly rather than generalizing it into a timeless principle or fully containing it as merely local rhetoric.

Verse 47 introduces hope after judgment, but only in outline — it specifies no timing, agent, or mechanism for Moab's promised restoration.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The wine metaphor in verses 11-12 is unusually sustained. Moab has remained settled on its lees, has not been poured from vessel to vessel, and has therefore retained its taste and scent. The announced agents will empty the vessels and break the containers. The image turns prolonged stability into an explanation for unchanged character and then depicts invasion as violent decanting.

The first-person laments in verses 31, 32, and 36 deserve careful attention. Because verse 30 explicitly says "saith the LORD," the ensuing voice can be read as divine lament; it can also be heard through the prophet's own lamenting voice. The grammar and discourse do not require readers to resolve that relationship too sharply.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Jeremiah 48 incorporates earlier scriptural language rather than presenting wholly isolated material. Verses 45-46 adapt Numbers 21:28-29. More extensively, the sequence in verses 29-35 closely parallels [[Isaiah 16]], while portions of the disaster geography also parallel [[Isaiah 15]]. These correspondences strongly indicate literary or traditional reuse. They do not, by themselves, determine whether Jeremiah directly adapted Isaiah, both drew from older Moab traditions, or the material underwent later editorial integration.

The chapter repeatedly reverses Moab's status: praise becomes shame, strength becomes a broken horn and arm, joy becomes lamentation, settled wine is emptied, and those who flee move from fear to pit to snare. The final restoration statement creates one more reversal, although it is not developed.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Thematic Repetition
> The explicit repetition of Moab's self-magnification against the LORD, reinforced by the concentrated pride vocabulary, strongly supports reading pride as a central rationale for the judgment.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted, textually dense · [[Internal Thematic Repetition in Jeremiah 48|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary Intertextuality
> The paired correspondences involving Moab's pride, mourning for Kir-heres, Sibmah and Jazer, the loss of harvest joy, and high-place worship constitute strong evidence of a literary or shared-traditional relationship between Jeremiah 48 and Isaiah 16.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · strong, well-attested intertextual relationship · [[Literary Intertextuality in Jeremiah 48|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical/Archaeological Corroboration *(tentative)*
> Several Moabite towns named in Jeremiah 48 (including Kiriathaim, Dibon, Nebo, and Aroer) also appear as Moabite cities in the 9th-century BCE Mesha Stele, a Moabite royal inscription independent of the biblical text. This overlap is evidence that the chapter's geography reflects real, specific knowledge of Moabite territory current in the biblical period, though it does not by itself establish the historicity of the particular invasion Jeremiah describes or date the composition of the chapter.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · plausible, external epigraphic claim not mechanically checkable here

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Who is speaking in the first person in verses 31-36: the LORD, Jeremiah, or a prophetic voice in which the two are deliberately difficult to separate?
2. How should verse 47 affect the reading of verse 42, which says that Moab will be destroyed from being a people?
3. What does the reuse of [[Isaiah 15]], [[Isaiah 16]], and Numbers 21 contribute to the authority and rhetorical force of the oracle?
4. How can verse 10 be interpreted within its ancient judgment-oracle setting without converting its violent language into a general religious principle?
5. Why does the text include mourning for the very nation whose destruction it announces?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Jeremiah 48:29-35 with [[Isaiah 16]], attending to sequence as well as vocabulary: pride, Kir-heres, Sibmah, Jazer, lost harvest joy, and high-place worship. Then compare Jeremiah 48:34 with [[Isaiah 15]] and Jeremiah 48:45-46 with Numbers 21:28-29. The central historical-literary question is not whether correspondence exists, but which model of reuse best explains it.
