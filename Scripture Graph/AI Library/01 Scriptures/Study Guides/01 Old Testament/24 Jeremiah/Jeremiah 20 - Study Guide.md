# Jeremiah 20 — Study Guide

## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Pashur's punishment of Jeremiah (vv. 1–2):** Jeremiah is struck and confined in stocks at a temple gate.
2. **The sign-name and judgment oracle (vv. 3–6):** Pashur becomes Magor-missabib; the announced judgment expands from him and his friends to Judah, the city's wealth, and his household.
3. **The unbearable prophetic word (vv. 7–10):** Jeremiah reports ridicule, resolves to stop speaking, and finds the word impossible to contain.
4. **Confidence, appeal, and praise (vv. 11–13):** He affirms that his persecutors will not prevail, submits his cause to God, and calls for praise.
5. **Curse upon birth (vv. 14–18):** The chapter closes with an unresolved lament over Jeremiah's existence and suffering.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Revelation]] as costly and compelling: Jeremiah does not depict the divine word as a path to social honor but as a fire he cannot contain (v. 9). His persistence therefore illuminates [[Endurance]], though the text does not portray endurance as emotional serenity.

Jeremiah's speech also broadens the scriptural language of [[Prayer]]. He brings accusation, fear, a plea for vengeance, confidence, praise, and despair before God. The presence of these words in scripture shows that anguish may be voiced in prayer; it does not make every curse or desire expressed in lament a universal moral command.

The prediction that Judah and its treasures will be carried to Babylon belongs to the book's larger treatment of [[Prophecy]] and the [[Scattering of Israel]] (vv. 4–6).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three repetitions help connect the received chapter. The designation Magor-missabib is followed by language of terror in verse 4 and by "fear on every side" in verse 10. The English verb "prevail" also changes hands: the LORD has prevailed over Jeremiah (v. 7), Jeremiah's enemies hope to prevail against him (v. 10), and Jeremiah declares that they will not prevail (v. 11). Finally, daily derision appears in both verses 7 and 8. These echoes connect outward persecution with Jeremiah's inward struggle. The patterns are verifiable in the supplied English text; claims that they reproduce identical Hebrew words or prove deliberate single-stage composition require additional philological evidence.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The sharpest juxtaposition occurs between verses 13 and 14: 'Sing unto the LORD' is followed immediately by 'Cursed be the day wherein I was born.' This should not automatically be harmonized into a smooth emotional sequence. It may represent abrupt movement within lament, the joining of distinct poetic units, or an intentional editorial arrangement that preserves both faith and despair.

The closing birth curse closely resembles [[Job 3]]. Both passages curse the day of birth and ask why death did not come from the womb. Meanwhile, [[Jeremiah 1]] declares that Jeremiah was known and appointed before birth. These connections intensify the tragedy: the womb that marks his divine calling also becomes the focus of his wish for nonexistence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary/Form-Critical
> The abrupt shift from praise (20:13) to a birth-curse (20:14-18) supports the view that this passage preserves raw, theologically unresolved emotional expression rather than a tidy devotional resolution. It does not establish a single coherent 'moral' for the chapter, nor that the curse language is meant as normative prayer instruction.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · supported reading with acknowledged alternatives · [[Literary/Form-Critical in Jeremiah 20|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Corroboration *(tentative)*
> The Babylonian conquest and exile of Judah broadly matches the historical events of 597-586 BC, corroborating the general captivity prophecy of 20:4-6. However, Pashur's specific personal fate (dying and being buried in Babylon) is not independently attested outside this text, so the prophecy's fulfillment is verifiable only at the national/collective level, not for the individual detail.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · broad historical correspondence; chronology implications disputed

> [!evidence] Literary Coherence *(tentative)*
> The recurrence of terror, surrounding hostility, and the verb prevail provides substantial evidence of deliberate thematic and verbal cohesion within the chapter. It supports reading the narrative and confession together as an exploration of prophetic opposition, but it does not by itself settle how or when the units were composed.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · repetitions verified; deliberate cohesion remains interpretive

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why might the chapter place Jeremiah's praise and birth curse directly beside one another rather than resolving the tension?
- What changes when 'prevail' is applied first to God's influence over Jeremiah, then to the plans of Jeremiah's enemies?
- How does Pashur's position in the house of the LORD complicate any assumption that religious office guarantees a true response to [[Prophecy]]?
- What does Jeremiah's failed attempt at silence suggest about his understanding of prophetic vocation?
- How can readers take Jeremiah's lament seriously without treating his curses as general ethical instructions?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the promise in [[Jeremiah 1]] that Jeremiah's opponents will not prevail with Jeremiah 20:11. Read [[Jeremiah 15]] as another confession involving opposition, birth, and the received word. Compare the fire imagery in Jeremiah 20:9 with [[Jeremiah 23]], and study the birth lament beside [[Job 3]]. Finally, compare the Babylon oracle in verses 4–6 with the later destruction and deportation narrative in [[Jeremiah 52]].
