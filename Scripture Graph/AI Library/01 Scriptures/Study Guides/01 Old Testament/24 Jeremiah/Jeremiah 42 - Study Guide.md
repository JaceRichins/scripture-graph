# Jeremiah 42 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 42]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 42 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jeremiah promises Johanan and the remnant of Judah peace and safety if they remain in Judah, but the sword, famine, and pestilence if they go to Egypt.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Petition and pledge (vv. 1-6):** The leaders and people ask for guidance and promise to obey the answer.
2. **Waiting and promise (vv. 7-12):** After ten days, Jeremiah announces building, planting, deliverance, and mercy if they remain.
3. **Egypt alternative and warning (vv. 13-18):** The apparent refuge from war and hunger is associated instead with sword, famine, pestilence, and disgrace.
4. **Admonition and exposure (vv. 19-22):** Jeremiah prohibits the journey, accuses the petitioners of dissembling, and restates the threatened consequences.

The repeated expression “from the least even unto the greatest” emphasizes that both the request and the answer address the whole assembly. The ten-day interval separates the petition from the answer, but the text does not explain whether the interval carries symbolic or theological significance.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Prayer]], [[Revelation]], and [[Obedience]] as connected but distinct. Asking for an answer does not itself demonstrate willingness to obey it. Jeremiah says the petitioners' promise concealed dissembling, although the text does not independently disclose when or how firmly their preference for Egypt became settled.

The oracle joins warning with mercy. Remaining carries promises of divine presence, deliverance, rebuilding, and compassion; entering Egypt carries threatened judgment. The hearers are presented with meaningful [[Agency]], but the passage concerns this particular claimed revelation and should not be generalized into a rule that migration or flight is inherently faithless.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The dialogue repeatedly varies the possessive language for God. The petitioners initially say “the LORD thy God,” later promise obedience to “the LORD our God,” and Jeremiah answers in the name of “the LORD your God” and “the God of Israel.” The changes are textually clear, but their rhetorical significance is uncertain; they may mark claimed relationship and responsibility, or they may simply be ordinary dialogue formulas.

The KJV wording “I repent me of the evil” in verse 10 should be read within its immediate contrast. Pulling down and plucking up are to give way to building and planting, so “evil” most naturally denotes experienced calamity or judgment here, not divine moral wrongdoing.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's clearest literary reversal concerns feared outcomes. Egypt is imagined as a place without war, trumpet, or hunger, yet the oracle says that sword and famine will pursue the migrants there. The rhetoric does not make their political fear unintelligible; within the oracle, it argues that they have misidentified the path to safety.

The paired verbs “build/pull down” and “plant/pluck up” connect Jeremiah 42:10 with recurring language in [[Jeremiah 1]], [[Jeremiah 18]], and [[Jeremiah 24]]. Here the destructive actions are negated and the constructive actions emphasized. This is a strong intrabook verbal connection, though it does not establish a particular author, editor, or deliberate allusion to one passage.

The narrative continues into [[Jeremiah 43]], where the leaders reject Jeremiah's word and enter Egypt. That continuation reinforces the accusation of unwillingness to obey within the book's narrative, but it does not independently prove the historicity of every speech or the petitioners' original private intentions.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Jeremiah say that the people “have not obeyed” immediately after delivering the command? Does this diagnose an inward refusal, anticipate their response, or reflect retrospective narration?
- Does the ten-day interval have recoverable significance, or is it simply a chronological notice?
- What fears made Egypt appear safer, and how does the oracle respond to those fears?
- How does [[Jeremiah 43]] affect the reader's assessment of the promise in Jeremiah 42:5-6 without independently proving the petitioners' earlier motives?
- What effect is created by the recurring verbs “build,” “pull down,” “plant,” and “pluck up”?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Jeremiah 42 with [[Jeremiah 43]] for the remnant's response and with [[Jeremiah 44]] for later oracles to Judeans in Egypt. Jeremiah 41:17-18 supplies the immediate narrative background for their fear of Babylon. Jeremiah 24:6 offers a close verbal parallel to the promise to build and plant, while Jeremiah 18:7-10 provides broader context for conditional national judgment. Numbers 14:1-4 may also be studied as a thematic comparison involving fear and attraction to Egypt, without assuming direct literary dependence.
