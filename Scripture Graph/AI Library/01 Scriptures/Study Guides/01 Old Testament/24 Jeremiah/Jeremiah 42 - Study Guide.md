# Jeremiah 42 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Jeremiah 42]] · My notes: [[Jeremiah 42 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jeremiah promises Johanan and the remnant of Judah peace and safety if they remain in Judah, but the sword, famine, and pestilence if they go to Egypt.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Petition and pledge (vv. 1-6):** The leaders and people, named "from the least even unto the greatest," ask for guidance and promise to obey the answer "whether it be good, or whether it be evil."
2. **Waiting and promise (vv. 7-12):** After ten days, Jeremiah again gathers everyone "from the least even to the greatest" and announces building, planting, deliverance, and mercy if they remain.
3. **Egypt alternative and warning (vv. 13-18):** Egypt is imagined as a refuge from war and hunger; the oracle answers that the very sword and famine they fear will pursue them there.
4. **Admonition and exposure (vv. 19-22):** Jeremiah prohibits the journey, accuses the petitioners of dissembling, and restates the threatened consequences, framed twice by "know certainly" (vv. 19, 22).

The repeated phrase "from the least even unto the greatest" (vv. 1, 8) marks the whole assembly as party to both the request and the answer, and the movement from the people's pledge to "obey the voice of the LORD our God" (v. 6) to Jeremiah's charge that they "have not obeyed the voice of the LORD" (v. 21) is a striking verbal reversal spanning most of the chapter. The ten-day interval separates the petition from the answer, but the text does not explain whether the interval carries symbolic or theological significance.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Prayer]], [[Revelation]], and [[Obedience]] as connected but distinct. Asking for an answer does not itself demonstrate willingness to obey it — the group pledges obedience "whether it be good, or whether it be evil" (v. 6) and is then charged with not obeying (v. 21). Jeremiah's charge that the petitioners "dissembled" (v. 20) is the narrative's own prophetic diagnosis; the chapter gives readers access to that inward posture only through Jeremiah's accusation and its confirmation in the following chapter's narrative, not through any independent window into the petitioners' private intentions in vv. 1-6.

The oracle joins warning with mercy. Remaining carries promises of divine presence, deliverance, rebuilding, and compassion (vv. 10-12); entering Egypt carries threatened judgment (vv. 15-18, 22). The hearers are presented with meaningful [[Agency]], but the passage concerns this particular claimed revelation to this particular remnant and should not be generalized into a rule that migration or flight is inherently faithless — the same book has Jeremiah instruct exiles already in Babylon to settle and build there ([[Jeremiah 29]]), suggesting the concern here is obedience to a specific, received directive rather than a blanket prohibition on leaving the land.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The dialogue repeatedly varies the possessive language for God. The petitioners initially say "the LORD thy God," later promise obedience to "the LORD our God," and Jeremiah answers in the name of "the LORD your God" (echoed in his closing charge, v. 21) and "the God of Israel." The changes are textually clear, but their rhetorical significance is uncertain; they may mark claimed relationship and responsibility, or they may simply be ordinary dialogue formulas.

The KJV wording "I repent me of the evil" in verse 10 should be read within its immediate contrast. Pulling down and plucking up are to give way to building and planting, so "evil" most naturally denotes experienced calamity or judgment here, not divine moral wrongdoing.

The triad "sword...famine...pestilence" appears twice in the chapter's close (vv. 17, 22), anticipated by "sword...famine" in verse 16. The same triad recurs elsewhere in Jeremiah as a recognizable phrase for covenant judgment, though its precise scope varies by context rather than being a rigidly fixed formula.

A minor detail worth flagging: the leader named beside Johanan in verse 1, "Jezaniah the son of Hoshaiah," is named "Azariah the son of Hoshaiah" when the same narrative role recurs in Jeremiah 43:2. The shared patronymic makes identification of the two as one person plausible, but the text itself does not explain the discrepancy, and scribal variation, dual naming, or two distinct sons of the same father all remain possible.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's clearest literary reversal concerns feared outcomes. Egypt is imagined as a place without war, trumpet, or hunger (v. 14), yet the oracle answers that the sword and famine the people fear will pursue them there (v. 16) and that none will "remain or escape" (v. 17). The rhetoric does not make their political fear unintelligible; within the oracle, it argues that they have misidentified the path to safety.

The paired verbs "build/pull down" and "plant/pluck up" connect Jeremiah 42:10 with recurring language in [[Jeremiah 1]], [[Jeremiah 18]], and [[Jeremiah 24]]. Here the destructive actions are negated and the constructive actions emphasized. This is a strong intrabook verbal connection — the same formula in Jeremiah 24:6 is applied to the Babylonian exiles rather than to those remaining in Judah, suggesting the promise functions as a reusable assurance of restoration rather than being tied to one specific audience. The parallel does not establish a particular author, editor, or deliberate literary allusion.

The narrative continues into [[Jeremiah 43]], where the leaders reject Jeremiah's word and enter Egypt, and into [[Jeremiah 44]], where a further oracle of sword, famine, and pestilence renews the warning against that same community after they arrive. That continuation reinforces the accusation of unwillingness to obey within the book's narrative, but it does not independently prove the historicity of every speech or the petitioners' original private intentions, and Jeremiah 44 mainly restates and extends the threat rather than narrating its fulfillment.

The proposed flight to Egypt for safety also resonates with the broader biblical pattern in which a voluntary return to Egypt is treated with suspicion (compare Deuteronomy 17:16, which restricts the king from leading the people back to Egypt, and Deuteronomy 28:68, which lists an imposed return to Egypt among covenant curses). The chapter never explicitly invokes this tradition, so the connection is best treated as illuminating background rather than a signaled allusion.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Jeremiah say that the people "have not obeyed" immediately after delivering the command, before any journey to Egypt is narrated? Does this diagnose an inward refusal, anticipate their response, or reflect narration shaped by the events that follow in [[Jeremiah 43]]?
- Does the ten-day interval have recoverable significance, or is it simply a chronological notice?
- How politically reasonable would Egypt have appeared as a refuge after Gedaliah's assassination, apart from accepting Jeremiah's prophetic authority?
- How does [[Jeremiah 43]] affect the reader's assessment of the promise in Jeremiah 42:5-6 without independently proving the petitioners' earlier motives?
- What effect is created by the recurring verbs "build," "pull down," "plant," and "pluck up," and why is the same promise given both to this remnant (42:10) and to the exiles already in Babylon (24:6)?
- Why does the leader named "Jezaniah the son of Hoshaiah" in 42:1 appear as "Azariah the son of Hoshaiah" in 43:2 — scribal variation, dual naming, or a different individual entirely?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Jeremiah 42 with [[Jeremiah 43]] for the remnant's response and with [[Jeremiah 44]] for later oracles to Judeans in Egypt. Jeremiah 41:17-18 supplies the immediate narrative background for their fear of Babylon following Gedaliah's assassination. Jeremiah 24:6 offers a close verbal parallel to the promise to build and plant (there applied to the Babylonian exiles), Jeremiah 29:5 shows Jeremiah giving comparable settle-and-build counsel to those already exiled, and Jeremiah 18:7-10 provides broader context for conditional national judgment. Jeremiah 35's account of Rechabite obedience to a human command offers a thematic contrast to this remnant's failure to obey a directly requested divine word, though the two episodes are not explicitly cross-referenced. Numbers 14:3-4 may be studied as a thematic comparison involving fear and attraction to Egypt, without assuming direct literary dependence or equating this remnant's response with the wilderness generation's open rebellion.
