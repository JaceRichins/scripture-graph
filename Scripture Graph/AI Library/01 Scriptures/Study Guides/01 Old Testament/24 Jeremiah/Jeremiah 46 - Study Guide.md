## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Headings and historical setting (vv. 1-2):** Jeremiah's words concerning the nations begin with Egypt and the defeat at Carchemish.
2. **Mobilization becomes rout (vv. 3-6):** Commands to arm horses and soldiers give way to panic beside the Euphrates.
3. **Egypt's flood-like boast and the LORD's day (vv. 7-12):** Egypt imagines an overwhelming advance, but the battle becomes divine judgment and an incurable wound.
4. **A second word against Egypt (vv. 13-26):** Warnings spread through Egyptian cities; soldiers retreat, Pharaoh is mocked, and images of cattle, serpent, forest, and woodcutters portray collapse. Judgment reaches rulers, gods, and those who trust them, yet Egypt is later to be inhabited again.
5. **Consolation and correction for Jacob (vv. 27-28):** Israel's return and rest are promised alongside measured punishment.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays the LORD as sovereign over armies and rulers while resisting a simple destruction-versus-favor scheme. Egypt is judged but is afterward to be inhabited again (v. 26). Jacob is promised preservation and return but is not exempted from measured correction (vv. 27-28). This makes the chapter relevant to [[Justice and Mercy]], though broader claims about the ultimate fate of every nation would exceed the passage.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The poetry repeatedly presents Egyptian strength and then reverses it. Mobilized troops become fugitives (vv. 3-6); a flood-like advance becomes the LORD's sacrifice beside the Euphrates (vv. 7-10); balm cannot cure Egypt's wound (v. 11); and the second oracle compares Egypt and its forces to a heifer, fatted bullocks, a retreating serpent, and a forest felled by innumerable attackers (vv. 20-23). Egypt is also personified as a daughter (vv. 11, 19, 24). The closing change of addressee replaces Egypt's battle imagery with Jacob's promised rest and freedom from fear. A specific Nile allusion in verses 7-8 is plausible but not explicit.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Extrabiblical Historical Corroboration
> The Battle of Carchemish (605 BC) described in Jeremiah 46:2-12 is independently attested outside the Bible in the Babylonian Chronicle series (the tablet often called the 'Nebuchadnezzar Chronicle,' BM 21946), which records Nebuchadnezzar (then crown prince) defeating an Egyptian army at Carchemish on the Euphrates.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Broadly accepted historical corroboration · [[Extrabiblical Historical Corroboration in Jeremiah 46|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Corroboration
> The historical framework in Jeremiah 46:2 accords with the Babylonian Chronicle's report that Nebuchadnezzar defeated the Egyptian army at Carchemish in 605 BCE. This is strong external corroboration for the chapter's Carchemish setting, while leaving the oracle's date of composition and predictive status unresolved.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · Broadly accepted historical corroboration · [[Historical Corroboration in Jeremiah 46|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Extrabiblical Historical Correlation (Fragmentary) *(tentative)*
> A possible, but disputed, historical correlate exists for the second Egypt oracle (Jeremiah 46:13-26): a fragmentary Babylonian Chronicle tablet (BM 33041) refers to a campaign in Nebuchadnezzar's 37th year (c. 568/567 BC) involving Amasis of Egypt, which some scholars have connected to this prophecy of Babylonian invasion of Egypt.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.4 · Possible historical correlate; disputed and fragmentary
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the sudden reversal between verses 3-4 and 5-6 affect the portrayal of military confidence?
- What objects of trust are exposed in verses 17, 21, and 25?
- How do Egypt's promised later habitation in verse 26 and Jacob's measured correction in verse 28 qualify an overly simple destruction-versus-favor reading?
- Why might the reassurance also found in [[Jeremiah 30]] have been placed at the end of an oracle against Egypt?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Jeremiah 46:27-28 with [[Jeremiah 30]] to examine the reuse of the Jacob consolation oracle. Compare the futile balm of Jeremiah 46:11 with [[Jeremiah 8]], and read [[Jeremiah 25]] and [[Jeremiah 27]] for the wider presentation of Babylon as an instrument of judgment. [[Jeremiah 50]] is an important counterbalance: the empire used against Egypt is itself later judged. [[Ezekiel 30]] provides a separate prophetic treatment of Egypt, Nebuchadnezzar, and the day of the LORD.
