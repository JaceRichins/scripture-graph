<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The standard for Davidic rule (vv. 1-5):** justice brings the prospect of continued kingship; refusal brings desolation.
2. **The fall of the royal house and city (vv. 6-9):** magnificent Lebanon-like strength becomes a burned wilderness because the covenant was forsaken.
3. **Shallum (vv. 10-12):** exile without return.
4. **Jehoiakim (vv. 13-19):** unpaid labor and cedar luxury are contrasted with Josiah's defense of the poor; dishonorable death is announced.
5. **Prosperity, false security, and coming anguish (vv. 20-23):** longstanding refusal to hear ends in captivity and shame.
6. **Coniah (vv. 24-30):** the royal signet is removed, the king is deported to Babylon, and his line is barred from ruling Judah.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's sharpest theological statement is Jeremiah 22:16: Josiah's defense of the poor and needy is identified with knowing the LORD. This does not reduce knowledge of God to social ethics, but it does make justice an indispensable test of that knowledge. The treatment of workers, strangers, orphans, widows, and victims of violence is presented as a covenant matter.

The chapter also qualifies appeals to divine promises about David's throne. The kings possess a genuine inherited office, yet Jeremiah does not treat that office as immunity from judgment. [[Obedience]] and righteous rule matter; even Coniah, imagined as a signet on God's right hand, can be removed.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated cedar imagery is both architectural and political. The royal house resembles Lebanon and its choice cedars (vv. 6-7); Jehoiakim panels his enlarged house with cedar (vv. 13-15); and the later addressee makes a nest in Lebanon's cedars (v. 23). Cedar conveys beauty, expense, height, and apparent security, while cutting, burning, and labor pains reverse that confidence.

Coniah's signet image works similarly. A signet normally suggests value, authority, and intimate possession, but the conditional wording intensifies the rejection: even such closeness would not prevent his removal.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly stages reversals of royal privilege. Palace gates become the setting of threatened desolation; cedar splendor becomes fuel for destroyers; a king who denies wages is denied lamentation and burial honor; the royal signet is plucked away; and a king with descendants is declared 'childless' with respect to a successful ruling succession. These reversals give the separate royal oracles a coherent moral logic, although they may have originated on different occasions.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- In what sense does defending the poor and needy constitute knowing God in verse 16?
- Does the chapter condemn wealth itself, or wealth constructed through injustice and trusted as proof of royal greatness?
- Who is directly addressed in verses 20-23: Jerusalem, the royal establishment, or another personified corporate addressee?
- How should 'childless' in verse 30 be read alongside Coniah's descendants in [[1 Chronicles 3]] and his place in [[Matthew 1]]?
- How does the conditional language of verses 4-5 relate to the promises concerning David's throne in [[2 Samuel 7]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Shallum's oracle with [[2 Kings 23]], Coniah's deportation with [[2 Kings 24]], and Jehoiakim's dishonor with [[Jeremiah 36]]. [[Jeremiah 7]] provides a particularly close thematic parallel to verse 3: security in the land is joined to justice for the stranger, fatherless, and widow and to the rejection of innocent bloodshed.
