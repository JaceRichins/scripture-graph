# 2 Kings 4 — Study Guide

## Overview

> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Elisha multiplies the widow’s oil—He promises a son to a Shunammite woman—The child dies and is raised to life by Elisha—He makes the poisonous food harmless—Bread and grain are multiplied for the people to eat.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter falls into four scenes marked by the text's own transitions:
1. **Widow's oil** (vv. 1-7) — scarcity turned to sufficiency, resolving debt-bondage.
2. **The Shunammite and her son** (vv. 8-37) — hospitality, a promised son, his death, and his revival; told in stages: death, concealment, urgent journey, a failed delegated remedy (Gehazi's staff), and Elisha's own successful intervention.
3. **Purified pottage** (vv. 38-41) — danger turned harmless.
4. **Multiplied loaves** (vv. 42-44) — scarcity turned to sufficiency again, echoing the opening episode.

The outer episodes (oil; loaves) both turn insufficient supply into enough with some left over, framing the central death-and-revival narrative. Episodes 1-2 also parallel [[1 Kings 17]]'s meal/oil and raised-son stories, though the accounts differ in detail (in 1 Kings 17:19 Elijah, not the mother, carries the child) and are not a point-for-point match.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Obedience precedes provision.** The widow's oil does not multiply until she acts — borrowing vessels, shutting the door, pouring (vv. 3-5); the miracle is bounded by her own preparation, stopping when no empty vessel remains (v. 6).

**Prophetic knowledge has limits.** Elisha admits the LORD had not told him why the Shunammite was distressed (v. 27) — the narrative does not present prophetic insight as automatic or unlimited.

**[[Prayer]] and personal presence.** Gehazi's attempt to revive the child using Elisha's staff produces no response; only after Elisha himself arrives, shuts the door, prays, and makes sustained physical contact does the child revive (vv. 29-35). The text contrasts a borrowed symbol of authority with the prophet's own prayerful ministry, though it does not explain why the staff failed.

**Provision paired with responsibility.** Elisha does not simply give the widow money; she sells the miraculously-provided oil herself to pay her debt (v. 7) — worth noting without overreading it as a general teaching on self-reliance.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV calls the Shunammite 'a great woman' (v. 8) — in context this more plausibly signals wealth or social standing (she can build a dedicated chamber and command a servant and an ass) than physical size or a moral judgment.

The woman's answers to her husband and to Gehazi are related but not identical: 'It shall be well' (v. 23, to her husband) and 'It is well' (v. 26, to Gehazi) — both spoken while her son is already dead. Whether this reflects composed faith, strategic reticence, or simply reserving her complaint for Elisha himself is not stated by the text.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
**Staff versus presence.** The narrative reports the staff's failure ('there was neither voice, nor hearing,' v. 31) before Elisha succeeds through prayer and bodily contact (vv. 33-35) — a deliberate contrast, though the text does not explain the mechanism.

**The Shunammite's oath.** Her vow, 'As the LORD liveth, and as thy soul liveth, I will not leave thee' (v. 30), is a striking, solemn form. The same continuation is also Elisha's own words to Elijah, repeated three times in [[2 Kings 2]] (vv. 2, 4, 6) — so rather than uniquely echoing David and Jonathan or David and Abigail, her oath may deliberately echo Elisha's own earlier refusal to leave his master.

**Numbers and repetition.** The child sneezes seven times (v. 35); twenty loaves feed a hundred men with food left over (vv. 42-44) — numeric details emphasizing completeness.

**Feeding-narrative pattern.** The insufficient-food-then-leftovers shape of vv. 42-44 (including a servant's doubt, v. 43) is a recognizable pattern also found in [[Luke 9]]'s feeding of the multitude, worth noting without claiming direct dependence.

**Geographic echo.** Mount Carmel (v. 25) links this chapter to Elisha's movements after Elijah's translation (2 Kings 2:25).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary And Narrative Parallel
> The combination of multiplying a widow's household provisions and restoring a woman's dead son closely parallels Elijah's ministry in 1 Kings 17. This supports the literary interpretation that 2 Kings presents Elisha as continuing Elijah's prophetic ministry.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Elisha's staff, sent ahead by Gehazi, fail to revive the child ('there was neither voice, nor hearing,' v. 31) — what changes when Elisha himself arrives and prays?
- Why does the Shunammite tell her husband and Gehazi only that 'it is/shall be well' before her son is revived — what might her timing suggest?
- What does Elisha's admission that 'the LORD hath hid it from me' (v. 27) contribute to how the chapter portrays prophetic knowledge?
- The boy's illness is described only as 'My head, my head' (v. 19) — what is gained by leaving the cause unspecified?
- How does the widow's role in selling the oil herself relate to the miracle that produced it?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
