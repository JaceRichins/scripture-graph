# 2 Kings 25 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Kings 25]] · My notes: [[2 Kings 25 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Nebuchadnezzar again besieges Jerusalem—Zedekiah is captured, Jerusalem and the temple are destroyed, and most of the people of Judah are carried into Babylon—Gedaliah, left to govern the remnant, is slain—The remnant flee to Egypt—Jehoiachin is shown favor in Babylon.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Siege, breach, and capture of Zedekiah (vv. 1–7).** Precise regnal and calendar notices frame the military collapse.

2. **Destruction and deportation (vv. 8–21).** Jerusalem's buildings and walls are destroyed, temple objects are removed, inhabitants are divided between deportees and agricultural laborers, and leading officials are executed.

3. **The failed remnant administration (vv. 22–26).** Gedaliah advocates submission to Babylon, but his assassination precipitates flight to Egypt.

4. **Jehoiachin in Babylon (vv. 27–30).** Years later, the imprisoned king receives status, food, clothing, and a continuing allowance.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter is directly relevant to [[Scattering of Israel]], [[Temples]], and [[Government and Laws]], but it supplies little explicit theological explanation within its own verses. It narrates judgment and displacement without directly stating here which sins caused them. Broader covenantal interpretations should therefore be grounded in the surrounding books rather than inserted into this chapter as though its narrator states them explicitly.

The distinction between deportees and those left to cultivate the land is also important. Biblical scattering can involve political subjugation, displacement, and the destruction of communal institutions without requiring the removal of every individual.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The summary, "So Judah was carried away out of their land" (v. 21), stands beside explicit references to poor people and a broader population remaining in Judah (vv. 12, 22–23). The phrase therefore functions as a national or political summary, not a precise population count.

The statement that Evil-merodach "did lift up the head of Jehoiachin" (v. 27) is clarified by the following actions: release from prison, kind speech, elevated status, changed garments, table fellowship, and a continual allowance (vv. 28–30).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
- **Temple bracketed by construction and destruction.** The pillars, sea, and bases destroyed here are explicitly the objects "which Solomon had made" (25:16), and their dismantling is described using the same architectural vocabulary (cubits, chapiters, pomegranates, wreathen work) used when [[1 Kings 7]] describes their construction — the temple's entire working life is framed by these two accounts.
- **A synchronized date.** The siege's start date (25:1) matches, to the day, the date Ezekiel records in Babylonian exile for an oracle marking "this same day" that the king of Babylon set himself against Jerusalem (Ezekiel 24:1-2) — though whether this reflects a contemporaneous revelation or later editorial alignment cannot be determined from the text alone.
- **A modest closing reversal.** Jehoiachin's change of status (25:27-30) is a real but limited reversal within an otherwise catastrophic chapter: Jerusalem remains ruined and Judah remains subject to Babylon, but one Davidic king is alive and treated favorably. Reading this as an opening toward hope is plausible; treating it as an explicit promise of restoration goes beyond what the text states.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Parallel
> The extensive agreement in sequence and detail between 2 Kings 25 and Jeremiah 52 is strong evidence of a literary relationship or shared source tradition.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation, disputed direction · [[Textual Parallel in 2 Kings 25|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Prophetic Coherence *(tentative)*
> Zedekiah's blinding at Riblah, immediately before being taken to Babylon, is read by many commentators as reconciling two prophecies that appear to conflict: Jeremiah's statement that Zedekiah would see the king of Babylon eye to eye and go to Babylon, and Ezekiel's statement that he would be brought to Babylon yet not see it — resolved if he saw Nebuchadnezzar at Riblah (fulfilling Jeremiah) but was blinded before ever seeing Babylon itself (fulfilling Ezekiel).
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed harmonization

> [!evidence] Internal Prophetic Synchronism *(tentative)*
> The siege's start date in 25:1 (ninth year, tenth month, tenth day) matches, to the day, the date Ezekiel records in Babylon for an oracle marking that 'the king of Babylon set himself against Jerusalem this same day,' linking the Jerusalem narrative and the exilic prophet's experience to a single shared date.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · accepted observation, disputed interpretation

> [!evidence] Extrabiblical/Archaeological Corroboration *(tentative)*
> Jehoiachin's release and provision in Babylon is widely connected by historians to Babylonian administrative ration tablets from Nebuchadnezzar's palace archive (published by Ernst Weidner in 1939) that list oil and food allotments for 'Yaukin, king of the land of Yahudu' and his sons, alongside other exiled royalty and craftsmen.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · real corroboration, narrower scope than claimed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the narrator devote more detail to the temple's metalwork than to the burning of the city? How should verse 21's sweeping statement about Judah's removal be read alongside the survivors in verses 12 and 22–23? Does Jehoiachin's elevation function as theological hope, as a court record, or as both? What can the close parallels with [[Jeremiah 52]] establish about shared tradition, and what questions of source direction remain unresolved?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the fuller, near-duplicate account in [[Jeremiah 52]], which overlaps this chapter almost verse-for-verse, and the related narratives in [[Jeremiah 39]] (siege and capture), [[Jeremiah 40]] (Gedaliah's administration), and [[Jeremiah 41]] (his assassination) — each adds detail beyond what 2 Kings 25 preserves. [[Jeremiah 43]] briefly overlaps with the remnant's flight to Egypt. For the temple furnishings' original construction, see [[1 Kings 7]]. For a Latter-day Saint canonical perspective, [[1 Nephi 1]] dates prophetic warnings of Jerusalem's destruction to the first year of the same King Zedekiah whose reign ends in this chapter.
