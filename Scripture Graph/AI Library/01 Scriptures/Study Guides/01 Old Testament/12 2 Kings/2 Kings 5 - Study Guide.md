# 2 Kings 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Kings 5]] · My notes: [[2 Kings 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
2 Kings 5 opens with the ironic notice that the LORD had given Syria's Naaman, a leprous Syrian general, his military success (5:1), then turns on the testimony of an unnamed Israelite captive girl who tells her mistress that 'the prophet that is in Samaria' could heal him (5:2-3). A letter to the king of Israel, misread as a political provocation, leaves the king in despair (5:6-7), but Elisha sends word that Naaman need only wash seven times in the Jordan (5:8-10). Naaman, expecting a dramatic personal ritual, reacts in anger and nearly refuses, until his servants persuade him to try the simple instruction (5:11-14). Once cleansed, he confesses that the LORD is God and offers Elisha a gift, which Elisha refuses (5:15-16). The chapter closes with Elisha's servant Gehazi secretly extracting payment from Naaman by deceit and being struck with the very leprosy Naaman was cleansed of (5:20-27).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The need and the witness (5:1-4)** — Naaman's success and affliction are introduced, and a captive Israelite girl points toward the prophet in Samaria.
2. **Kings and prophet (5:5-8)** — A diplomatic letter alarms the king of Israel; Elisha redirects the matter to himself, saying it will show 'there is a prophet in Israel' (5:8).
3. **Resistance and cleansing (5:9-14)** — Naaman's expectations collide with Elisha's plain instruction; his servants persuade him to obey, and he is healed.
4. **Confession and unresolved accommodation (5:15-19)** — Naaman confesses the LORD, requests Israelite earth, and asks pardon in advance for bowing in the house of Rimmon; Elisha's brief 'Go in peace' does not resolve the question.
5. **Gehazi's deception and reversal (5:20-27)** — Gehazi secretly obtains from Naaman what Elisha refused, and is struck with Naaman's leprosy.

Scenes 1 and 5 form a suggestive reversal — a foreign leper leaves clean, while an Israelite insider leaves leprous — though whether this reflects deliberate compositional design or simply narrative irony is not something the text itself states.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The narrative sequence strongly associates Naaman's cleansing with his eventual [[Obedience]], but it does not present the Jordan's water as independently powerful. The command derives its significance from the prophetic word. Naaman's changing posture also invites reflection on [[Humility]]: the obstacle is not inability to perform the act but anger that the prescribed act fails to match his expectations.

Elisha's refusal of payment, followed immediately by Gehazi's punished attempt to obtain it, supports a reading of the healing as an unpurchased gift related to [[Grace]]. That reading should remain qualified: the text directly condemns Gehazi's deception and acquisition, but it does not formulate a universal rule against all material support for ministry.

Naaman's confession and promised sacrifice are substantial signs of [[Conversion]], yet verses 17–19 resist a simplistic account. His request concerning Rimmon reflects the pressures of continued service to a pagan king, and Elisha's brief response leaves the precise moral evaluation unstated.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The narrative repeatedly gives decisive insight to socially subordinate people: a captive girl identifies the source of healing (5:2-3), and Naaman's own servants overcome his resistance (5:13). The ending compresses this pattern into a reversal: Naaman, introduced as a leper, departs clean, while Gehazi — an Israelite and the prophet's own servant — departs 'a leper as white as snow' (5:27), the same idiom used for Miriam's leprosy (Numbers 12:10). Whether this outsider/insider reversal reflects deliberate structural design or is simply a striking narrative irony is not something the text settles.

In the KJV, the 'little maid' of verse 2 and Naaman's flesh becoming like that of 'a little child' in verse 14 may form a verbal echo, but the underlying Hebrew adjective (qatan, 'small/young') is common throughout the Old Testament, so this resemblance should be treated as suggestive rather than a confirmed literary device. The unnamed captive girl, whose brief words set the whole account in motion, likewise stands in contrast to the named, trusted Gehazi, whose extended scheming brings judgment — though the narrator never draws this contrast explicitly.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the narrator note up front that the LORD had already given Syria deliverance through Naaman (5:1)? What assumptions about political power versus prophetic authority are exposed by the exchange between the two kings (5:6-8)? Is Naaman's anger directed mainly at the plainness of the command, Elisha's refusal to appear in person, or his preference for Syrian rivers (5:11-12)? Why does Elisha not comment on Naaman's stated plan to keep bowing in the house of Rimmon (5:18-19), and does 'Go in peace' constitute approval, accommodation, or simple dismissal? Why is the girl who sets the entire account in motion left unnamed while Gehazi, who acts destructively, is named at length? How should readers weigh greed, deception, and misuse of Elisha's name in assessing Gehazi's punishment (5:20-27)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Luke 4]], where Jesus explicitly cites Naaman's cleansing (Luke 4:27) alongside Elijah's aid to the widow of Zarephath (Luke 4:25-26) as evidence that a prophet's usefulness is not confined to his own people. See [[Numbers 12]] for the same 'leprous...as white as snow' idiom applied to Miriam. The healing of the centurion's servant in [[Luke 7]] offers a looser thematic parallel — a Gentile military figure seeking healing from a Jewish holy man without requiring his physical presence — though the two accounts differ in tone and outcome, and the resemblance may reflect a common storytelling pattern rather than direct dependence. The Mosaic rite for a cleansed leper in [[Leviticus 14]], which includes a sevenfold sprinkling (14:7), is sometimes compared to Naaman's sevenfold washing, but the ritual contexts differ enough (no priest, blood, or offering in Naaman's case) that this should be treated as a shared numeric motif rather than a confirmed allusion. For the wider Elisha narrative cycle, see [[2 Kings 4]], [[2 Kings 2]], and [[2 Kings 6]].
