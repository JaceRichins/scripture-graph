# 2 Kings 13 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Kings 13]] · My notes: [[2 Kings 13 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter combines two royal summaries with the closing episodes of Elisha's ministry. Israel's kings continue in [[Sin]], yet the LORD hears Jehoahaz, provides limited [[Exodus and Deliverance]], and preserves Israel because of the [[Abrahamic Covenant]]. Elisha's sign predicts three victories, which the final verse reports. The chapter therefore holds judgment and mercy together without depicting Israel's underlying apostasy as resolved.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has five movements:

1. **Jehoahaz's reign (verses 1–9):** wrongdoing, Syrian oppression, supplication, relief, continuing sin, and military devastation.
2. **Jehoash's regnal summary (verses 10–13):** another negative evaluation and a brief reference to the conflict expanded in [[2 Kings 14]].
3. **Elisha and Jehoash (verses 14–19):** the dying prophet enacts and interprets the arrow signs.
4. **Elisha's death and the revival (verses 20–21):** a dead man revives after touching Elisha's bones.
5. **Covenant compassion and recovery (verses 22–25):** oppression is set against divine mercy, and Jehoash wins the predicted three victories.

Jehoash's formal death and succession notice appears before the Elisha episode involving him. The presentation is therefore not an uninterrupted chronology, although the text does not disclose whether thematic arrangement, source organization, or both produced this order.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verse 23 is the chapter's clearest theological explanation: compassion toward a persistently disobedient Israel is attributed to God's covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. This supports study of [[Covenants]], [[Grace]], and [[Justice and Mercy]]. It does not teach that wrongdoing is inconsequential: oppression continues, the army is devastated, and the reprieve is qualified by "as yet."

Jehoahaz's appeal also belongs with [[Prayer]], but it should not automatically be labeled repentance. The text says that he besought the LORD and was heard; it separately says that Israel did not depart from Jeroboam's sins.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter uses both **Jehoash** and **Joash** for the son of Jehoahaz while also referring to a different Joash, king of Judah. Patronymics and kingdom titles prevent confusion.

The lower-case "saviour" in verse 5 is an unnamed deliverer in this translation; the chapter does not identify the agent.

Jehoash's words "the chariot of Israel, and the horsemen thereof" echo [[2 Kings 2]], where Elisha uses the same designation for Elijah. In this military context the title may associate Elisha with Israel's protection, but it could also function as a traditional honorific or lament. The wording does not establish that armies were unimportant.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The strongest internal correspondence is numerical: Joash strikes three times (v. 18), Elisha predicts three victories (v. 19), and the narrator reports three victories (v. 25). This clearly functions as prediction and fulfillment within the narrative. It should not be stretched into independent proof of supernatural foreknowledge, since literary or retrospective shaping remains possible.

The revival in verses 20–21 supplies a final, compact testimony to Elisha's extraordinary prophetic role. Because the narrative offers no command, explanation, or repeated practice involving his bones, it is a weak basis for constructing a broader doctrine about relics.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why is Jehoash rebuked for stopping after three strokes when the recorded command specifies no required number?

- Who is the unnamed "saviour" in verse 5? Can surrounding chapters narrow the possibilities without turning a proposal into a fact?

- Does Jehoahaz's supplication imply inward repentance, or should interpretation stop with the text's distinction between prayer and continuing practices?

- What does "as yet" contribute to the account of covenant preservation, and how does the broader history of Kings affect its force?

- Does the tomb episode chiefly magnify Elisha's prophetic role, suggest hope amid national weakness, or serve another purpose? Which conclusions are explicit, and which depend on literary placement?

- The revived man stands alive, but the text says nothing about his later life. Why should this event not automatically be equated with final, glorified [[Resurrection]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
