# 2 Kings 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Kings 14]] · My notes: [[2 Kings 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter surveys portions of three reigns but develops two especially clear contrasts. Amaziah receives qualified approval and observes a limit imposed by the [[Law of Moses]], yet his success against Edom is followed by an ill-advised confrontation with Israel and severe defeat. Jeroboam is condemned for persistent [[Sin]], yet the LORD uses his reign to relieve Israel's bitter affliction. Political success and moral approval therefore do not function as simple equivalents in this account.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Amaziah's accession and evaluation (vv. 1-4):** right conduct is qualified by comparison with David and by the survival of the high places.

2. **Justice and victory (vv. 5-7):** Amaziah punishes his father's assassins without killing their children, then defeats Edom.

3. **Challenge and reversal (vv. 8-14):** Jehoash's thistle-and-cedar parable warns Amaziah; the ignored warning culminates in defeat, capture, a breached wall, plunder, and hostages.

4. **Deaths and succession notices (vv. 15-22):** the records and deaths of Jehoash and Amaziah are reported, followed by Azariah's accession and restoration of Elath.

5. **Jeroboam's reign (vv. 23-29):** a negative religious verdict stands beside territorial restoration, fulfilled [[Prophecy]], and deliverance from national distress.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verse 6 connects royal [[Government and Laws]] with the written [[Law of Moses]]: punishment is imposed on the perpetrators rather than transferred to their children. Verses 24–27 place judgment and compassion side by side. Jeroboam's conduct is condemned, but Israel is saved because the LORD sees its affliction and lack of a helper. The passage therefore supports a distinction between divine mercy toward a suffering people and moral endorsement of their ruler.

The fulfilled word delivered through Jonah also makes [[Prophecy]] part of the explanation for Israel's restoration. The text attributes both the oracle and the deliverance to the LORD, while naming Jeroboam as the human instrument.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The word “father” has both immediate and dynastic force. Amaziah is Joash's son in verse 1, while David is called his father in verse 3; the second expression identifies an ancestor and dynastic standard.

Naming can also obscure the political sequence. The Israelite king called Joash in verse 1 is called Jehoash in verses 8 and 15-17, while Amaziah's own father was another Joash, king of Judah. Attending to kingdom and patronymic prevents the two rulers from being confused.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Jehoash answers Amaziah with a miniature fable: a thistle seeks a marriage alliance with a cedar but is crushed by a passing wild beast. Its imagery communicates disparity of power and the fragility of inflated ambition. The prose then supplies the outcome anticipated by the warning: Amaziah refuses to hear, Judah loses, and Jerusalem suffers. This sequence makes [[Pride]] an important reading, although the chapter does not explicitly state that divine punishment for pride caused the defeat.

For comparison, [[2 Chronicles 25]] supplies a fuller parallel account. Its additional explanations should be recognized as information from that chapter rather than silently imported into the wording of [[2 Kings 14]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the narrator preserve a positive but qualified evaluation of Amaziah before narrating his disastrous decision? Does the sequence from Edomite victory to an uplifted heart to defeat imply a theological judgment, or primarily report Jehoash's accurate political warning? What does the deliverance under a condemned king suggest about the relationship between providence, national suffering, and a ruler's character? How much can the repeated references to royal chronicles tell us when those cited works are no longer available?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

Compare 2 Kings 14:25 with [[Jonah 1]] for the shared identification of Jonah son of Amittai, while avoiding conclusions about composition or historicity that the name alone cannot establish.
