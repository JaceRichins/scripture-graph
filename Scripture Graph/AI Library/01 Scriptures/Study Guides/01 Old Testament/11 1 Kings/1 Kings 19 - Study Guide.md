# 1 Kings 19 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Kings 19]] · My notes: [[1 Kings 19 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jezebel seeks the life of Elijah—An angel sends him to Horeb—The Lord speaks to Elijah, not in the wind nor the earthquake nor the fire, but in a still, small voice—Elisha joins Elijah.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Threat and flight (vv.1-4)** — Jezebel's oath; Elijah flees to Beer-sheba, leaves his servant, and asks to die in the wilderness.
2. **Sustenance and journey (vv.5-8)** — an angel feeds him twice; strengthened, he travels forty days and nights to Horeb.
3. **Question and theophany (vv.9-14)** — 'What doest thou here, Elijah?' asked twice, framing wind, earthquake, fire, and the still small voice; Elijah's answer is repeated almost unchanged.
4. **Commission and remnant (vv.15-18)** — Hazael, Jehu, and Elisha are named; seven thousand faithful are disclosed.
5. **Call of Elisha (vv.19-21)** — the mantle, the sacrificed oxen, and Elisha's following.

The repeated question and answer bracket the theophany, inviting (without stating outright) the reading that the vision itself does not settle Elijah's complaint — the commission and remnant disclosure that follow do more of that work.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter is a key text on [[Revelation]]: divine communication is not confined to dramatic signs — the very kind of sign Elijah himself called down at Carmel ([[1 Kings 18]]) — but can come as 'a still small voice' (v.12). This should not be generalized into a rule that God never speaks through storm or fire elsewhere; the contrast is specific to this episode. The sequence of sleep, food, and water before any correction (vv.5-8) shows [[Adversity]] and physical depletion being addressed in their own right, though the text does not diagnose Elijah's state or frame what follows as deliberate therapy. The LORD's disclosure of seven thousand who have not bowed to Baal (v.18) counters Elijah's repeated claim to be alone; Paul later cites this same episode (Romans 11:2-5) as scriptural grounds for a doctrine of a remnant preserved by grace. The chapter also models prophetic succession: a symbolic call (the mantle), a costly break with a former life (the slaughtered oxen and burned equipment), and ongoing service (v.21) — though only Elisha's call is narrated here; the anointings of Hazael and Jehu commanded in vv.15-16 are carried out later in Kings, not within this chapter.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV's 'juniper tree' (vv.4-5) renders Hebrew *rothem*; many Hebrew lexicons associate this word with a desert broom shrub rather than a true juniper, though the precise botanical identification is not fully settled and does not affect the narrative. The 'still small voice' (v.12) renders a Hebrew phrase (*qol demamah daqqah*) whose translation is genuinely disputed among scholars — proposed renderings include 'a sound of thin silence' or 'a low murmuring sound' — so claims about the voice being especially 'gentle' should be held loosely; what the text clearly establishes is the contrast with the preceding wind, earthquake, and fire. 'Cast his mantle upon him' (v.19) uses a garment gesture elsewhere associated with prophetic identity and authority in the Elijah-Elisha narratives.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The central device is the doubled question 'What doest thou here, Elijah?' (vv.9,13) with Elijah's near-verbatim answer both times (vv.10,14) — an inclusio bracketing the theophany. Because his answer is unchanged, the vision alone does not appear to settle his complaint; what follows — commission and the remnant disclosure — does more of that work, though the text never states this explicitly. Within the theophany, the threefold refrain 'the LORD was not in' the wind, the earthquake, or the fire (vv.11-12) builds toward the unexpected final term, the still small voice. The setting — Horeb, 'the mount of God,' a cave, and the LORD passing by — evokes the Moses/Sinai traditions ([[Exodus 3]], [[Exodus 33]]); the shared place-name and passing-by motif are strong parallels, though the forty-day element is a looser echo, since Moses fasts forty days atop the mountain (Exodus 34:28) while Elijah is fed for forty days to journey toward it — same number, different content.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Narrative Contrast
> The explicit contrast between the spectacular phenomena and the subsequent voice is strong textual evidence that this episode emphasizes revelation through an unobtrusive voice rather than through the preceding displays of power. It should not be universalized into a claim that God never reveals himself through dramatic signs.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the LORD ask a question he already knows the answer to ('What doest thou here?') — twice?
- What does the theophany (vv.11-12) itself accomplish for Elijah, if his complaint is essentially unchanged afterward (compare vv.10 and 14)?
- Is the still small voice presented as revelation's normal mode, or as a corrective specific to Elijah's expectation of dramatic vindication after Carmel?
- Is 'seven thousand' (v.18) a literal count or a rhetorical figure for 'more than you think'?
- Why does Elisha destroy his own plowing equipment and oxen (v.21) as part of accepting the call?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the quiet theophany here with the dramatic fire-from-heaven sign at Carmel ([[1 Kings 18]]) — the same prophet who called down fire is now told the LORD is 'not in' the fire. Read Romans 11:2-5 directly against 1 Kings 19:10-18 to see how Paul reuses the remnant language. Compare Horeb, the cave, and the LORD passing by with [[Exodus 3]] and [[Exodus 33]] (noting the looser parallel with Exodus 34:28's forty days). The mantle cast on Elisha here (v.19) anticipates, but is distinct from, the later scene in [[2 Kings 2]] where Elijah's mantle falls to Elisha at his ascension.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
