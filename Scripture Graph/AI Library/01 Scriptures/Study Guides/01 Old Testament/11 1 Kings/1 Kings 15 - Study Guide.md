# 1 Kings 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Kings 15]] · My notes: [[1 Kings 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
First Kings 15 presents two kings of Judah followed by two kings of Israel, coordinating their reigns through accession synchronisms. Its principal contrast is evaluative: Abijam, Nadab, and Baasha continue condemned patterns, while Asa receives strong approval despite the survival of the high places. David serves as Judah's favorable royal standard, whereas Jeroboam becomes the recurring negative standard for Israel. Dynastic continuity, reform, conspiracy, war, and diplomacy are thus narrated within a theological assessment of rulers and their influence on their kingdoms.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Abijam of Judah (vv. 1–8):** accession, unfavorable evaluation, preservation of David's lamp, continuing war, death, and succession.

2. **Asa of Judah (vv. 9–24):** favorable evaluation, reforms, conflict with Baasha, alliance with Ben-hadad, death, and succession.

3. **Nadab of Israel (vv. 25–31):** unfavorable evaluation, assassination by Baasha, and destruction of Jeroboam's house.

4. **Baasha of Israel (vv. 32–34):** renewed war notice, accession at Tirzah, and continuation of Jeroboam's condemned pattern.

The repeated regnal formulas make theological evaluation prominent, although their standardized character should not be mistaken for proof of an unusually elaborate literary design.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter distinguishes wholehearted loyalty from flawless performance. Asa's heart is called perfect even though the high places remain (1 Kings 15:14), while David remains the positive standard despite the explicitly remembered matter of Uriah (1 Kings 15:5). In context, these judgments concern the enduring orientation of a king's loyalty; they should not be expanded into claims of sinlessness.

The account also portrays royal wrongdoing as socially consequential. Jeroboam is repeatedly condemned not only for sinning but for making Israel sin (1 Kings 15:26, 30, 34). This supports reflection on the responsibility of leaders for practices and institutions that influence communal conduct without eliminating the agency of the people they lead.

Baasha's execution of judgment against Jeroboam's house does not vindicate Baasha morally: he immediately receives the same unfavorable pattern of evaluation. Within the narrative, serving as an agent of judgment and receiving moral approval are distinct.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV phrase “heart was perfect” contrasts Abijam with David and describes Asa (1 Kings 15:3, 14). Verse 14 itself shows that the phrase does not mean flawless performance, since the high places remained; completeness, constancy, or wholehearted orientation is the stronger contextual reading.

The KJV term “sodomites” in verse 12 is an archaic rendering of an ancient cultic designation. It should not be mapped directly onto a modern sexual-orientation category. The precise role of the persons designated—including whether sexual activity is inherent in the term—remains disputed and requires current specialist lexical and historical study.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The reign summaries repeatedly combine chronology, family information, moral evaluation, conflict, references to other records, death, burial, and succession. The pattern presents a selective theological history rather than an exhaustive chronicle, but the lost royal records cannot now be used to verify events or reconstruct the compiler's exact sources.

David and Jeroboam function as contrasting royal precedents, while Abijam and Asa receive opposed heart evaluations. Verses 15 and 18 may also create a tension between bringing valuables into the house of the LORD and later using its remaining treasure to obtain Syrian intervention. Kings does not explicitly condemn this transaction or state that the same objects were removed, so deliberate irony remains possible rather than established. [[2 Chronicles 16]] supplies an explicit rebuke that must not be retrojected into Kings.

Verse 29 clearly presents Baasha's purge as fulfillment of the LORD's saying through Ahijah. This establishes the narrator's theological interpretation but is not independent proof of when the oracle originated or of supernatural prediction.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the narrator call Asa's heart perfect while immediately acknowledging that the high places remained? What distinction between wholehearted loyalty and completed reform does this suggest?

Does the successful outcome of Asa's alliance imply approval, or does Kings simply report its political effectiveness? How does the explicit criticism in [[2 Chronicles 16]] illuminate the episode without becoming wording attributed to 1 Kings 15?

How can Baasha fulfill judgment against Jeroboam's house and yet receive Jeroboam's own negative evaluation?

How does the repeated statement that Jeroboam “made Israel to sin” portray the relationship between royal power, communal practice, and personal agency?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
