# 1 Kings 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Kings 15]] · My notes: [[1 Kings 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
First Kings 15 presents four reigns through coordinated accession notices: Abijam and Asa in Judah, then Nadab and Baasha in Israel. Its principal contrast is evaluative. David supplies Judah's favorable standard, while Jeroboam supplies Israel's condemned pattern. Around that contrast, the chapter relates dynastic preservation, incomplete reform, prolonged war, international diplomacy, assassination, and prophetic fulfillment.

Judah's Davidic succession continues despite Abijam's sins, whereas Jeroboam's northern dynasty ends violently after only one succeeding generation. This contrast should not be reduced to a claim that every Davidic ruler is approved: Abijam is condemned, and continuity is attributed to the LORD's regard for David.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Abijam of Judah (vv. 1–8):** accession, negative evaluation, preservation of David's lamp, war, death, and succession.

2. **Asa of Judah (vv. 9–24):** positive evaluation, reforms and their limit, conflict with Baasha, alliance with Ben-hadad, death, and succession.

3. **Nadab of Israel (vv. 25–31):** negative evaluation, siege at Gibbethon, assassination, and destruction of Jeroboam's house.

4. **Baasha of Israel (vv. 32–34):** renewed war notice, accession at Tirzah, and continuation of Jeroboam's condemned pattern.

The standardized regnal formulas make moral comparison prominent. Their repetition is meaningful, but it does not by itself demonstrate an elaborate hidden literary structure.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter distinguishes wholehearted loyalty from flawless performance. Asa's heart is called perfect even though the high places remain (1 Kings 15:14), while David remains the favorable standard despite the explicitly remembered matter of Uriah (1 Kings 15:5). In context, these judgments concern the enduring orientation of a king's loyalty; they should not be expanded into claims of sinlessness.

Royal wrongdoing is also portrayed as socially consequential. Jeroboam is repeatedly condemned not only for sinning but for making Israel sin (1 Kings 15:26, 30, 34). This emphasizes a ruler's responsibility for shaping public worship and conduct without saying that the people lacked [[Agency]].

Baasha's destruction of Jeroboam's house fulfills the announced judgment, yet Baasha receives the same unfavorable pattern of evaluation. Within the narrative, acting as an instrument of judgment and receiving moral approval are distinct.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV expression “heart was perfect” contrasts Abijam with David and describes Asa (1 Kings 15:3, 14). Verse 14 immediately acknowledges an incomplete reform, so “perfect” is best read contextually as complete, steadfast, or wholehearted in allegiance—not free from every failure.

The KJV term “sodomites” in verse 12 is an archaic rendering of a Hebrew cultic designation. It should not be directly equated with a modern sexual-orientation category. The precise function of the persons designated, including the extent to which sexual activity is implied, remains lexically and historically disputed.

The “lamp” given for David in Jerusalem (1 Kings 15:4) is a dynastic image: its immediate explanation is the establishment of David's son after him, not a literal object.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly combines chronology, kinship, moral evaluation, conflict, archival notice, death, burial, and succession. This formulaic arrangement presents selective theological history rather than an exhaustive chronicle. References to royal chronicles (1 Kings 15:7, 23, 31) may indicate source traditions, but the unavailable works cannot now verify individual reports or reveal the compiler's exact method.

David and Jeroboam form opposed precedents. A related contrast appears in dynastic outcomes: David's line continues through morally unequal kings, while Jeroboam's house is eradicated in the coup that installs Baasha.

Verses 15 and 18 create a possible tension between bringing valuables into the house of the LORD and later using its remaining treasure to purchase Syrian intervention. Kings narrates the policy's immediate military success without explicit moral comment. [[2 Chronicles 16]] adds a prophetic rebuke, but that later parallel must not be turned into words attributed to Kings.

Verse 29 explicitly presents Baasha's purge as fulfillment of the LORD's saying through Ahijah. This securely establishes the narrator's fulfillment interpretation; standing alone, it does not independently determine the oracle's date or prove supernatural prediction.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How can Asa's heart be called perfect while the high places remain? What distinction does the narrator make between durable allegiance and completed reform?

Why is Abijam's dynasty preserved when Abijam himself is condemned? How does the stated reason, “for David's sake,” shape the chapter's view of covenantal continuity?

Does the military success of Asa's alliance indicate approval, or does Kings merely report political effectiveness? How does [[2 Chronicles 16]] illuminate the episode without being retrojected into Kings?

How can Baasha execute the announced judgment on Jeroboam's house and then receive Jeroboam's own negative evaluation?

What forms of responsibility are implied when a ruler is said to make a people sin?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the oracle against Jeroboam's house in [[1 Kings 14]] with its narrated fulfillment here, then follow the corresponding judgment against Baasha in [[1 Kings 16]]. Compare Asa's conflict with Baasha in [[2 Chronicles 16]], carefully distinguishing Chronicles' explicit criticism from Kings' silence. The expanded accounts in [[2 Chronicles 13]], [[2 Chronicles 14]], and [[2 Chronicles 15]] offer further comparisons for Abijah and Asa.

For the Davidic lamp and succession theme, compare [[1 Kings 11]] and the broader dynastic promise in [[2 Samuel 7]]. For the chapter's archival formulas, trace similar notices elsewhere in Kings while remembering that formulaic source citations do not make the cited chronicles recoverable.
