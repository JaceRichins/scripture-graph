## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Drought and Cherith (1–7):** Elijah announces the withholding of rain, obeys the command to hide, and is sustained by ravens until the brook dries up.

2. **Zarephath and daily food (8–16):** The Lord directs Elijah to a widow facing her final meal. Elijah promises continuing meal and oil, and the promise is fulfilled.

3. **The son and the confirming sign (17–24):** The son's condition overturns the household's temporary security. Elijah protests and petitions; the Lord hears him, the child revives, and the widow identifies Elijah as a man of God whose word is truth.

The movements intensify from sustaining one prophet, to sustaining a household, to restoring a life.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects [[Revelation]], [[Obedience]], [[Faith]], and [[Prayer]] without reducing them to a simple formula. Elijah obeys revealed directions, yet his brook still dries up. The widow acts on a promise, yet her son later suffers. Elijah's prayer includes a painful question before it becomes a petition. Providence is real within the narrative, but it does not mean an absence of continuing adversity.

The widow's accusation associates tragedy with remembered [[Sin]], but the narrator never confirms that her son's condition is punishment for her wrongdoing. That distinction cautions against automatically treating suffering as proof of personal guilt.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repetition gives the narrative its cohesion. “The word of the LORD came” introduces Elijah's movements, while promise-and-fulfillment language marks the food account. The ending supplies the interpretive climax: the widow declares that the word in Elijah's mouth is [[Truth]].

The chapter also prepares for [[1 Kings 18]]. The initial claim that Israel's God controls rain will become central in Elijah's confrontation with Ahab and the prophets of Baal. The move to Zarephath, which belongs to Zidon, places the Lord's provision outside Israel's territory before that confrontation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrative allow the brook to dry up after God has directed Elijah there?

- What does the widow's willingness to feed Elijah communicate, and what ethical discomfort remains in his request that she feed him first?

- Why does the widow say “Now” she knows Elijah is a man of God after already experiencing the sustained meal and oil?

- Does the narrator endorse the widow's belief that remembered sin caused her son's condition, or merely report her interpretation?

- What does Elijah's anguished prayer suggest about honest lament within [[Faith]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the use of this widow in [[Luke 4]], where Jesus emphasizes that Elijah was sent to a widow in Sidon. Compare Elijah's prayer tradition in [[James 5]]. The paired miracles in [[2 Kings 4]]—a widow's oil and the restoration of a woman's son—are especially important for examining how Elisha's ministry echoes and develops Elijah's.
