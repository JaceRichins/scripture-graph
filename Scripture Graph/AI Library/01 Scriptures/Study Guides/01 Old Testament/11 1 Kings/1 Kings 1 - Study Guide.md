# 1 Kings 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Kings 1]] · My notes: [[1 Kings 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
David's physical decline creates an urgent succession crisis. Adonijah stages a royal bid with elite supporters and a selective feast, but Nathan and Bath-sheba prompt David to reaffirm his oath concerning Solomon. David orders a public installation at Gihon, where Solomon rides the royal mule, is anointed, and receives popular acclamation. Adonijah's coalition collapses, and Solomon begins his reign with conditional mercy toward his rival. The chapter therefore concerns not merely who becomes king, but how the narrative distinguishes self-asserted power from publicly conferred authority.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **David's frailty (1:1–4):** Abishag ministers to the aged king.
2. **Adonijah's bid (1:5–10):** He exalts himself, gathers supporters, and excludes Solomon's coalition.
3. **The coordinated appeal (1:11–27):** Nathan and Bath-sheba separately approach David with mutually confirming reports.
4. **David's decision (1:28–37):** He renews his oath and specifies Solomon's immediate installation.
5. **Solomon's public accession (1:38–40):** The royal mule, anointing, trumpet, and public joy mark the transfer.
6. **Adonijah's collapse (1:41–53):** Jonathan's report disperses the feast; Adonijah seeks mercy at the altar and receives conditional pardon.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins divine providence with responsible human action. David blesses the Lord for allowing him to see a successor on his throne, while the outcome also depends on Nathan's counsel, Bath-sheba's courage, David's decision, and the obedience of Zadok and Benaiah (1:11–40, 48). This supports reflection on [[Agency]], [[Prophets]], and [[Government and Laws]] without requiring the conclusion that every political maneuver described was itself a direct revelation.

Solomon's first judgment combines [[Justice and Mercy]]. Adonijah is not executed merely for losing the contest, but his safety remains conditional on subsequent conduct (1:52–53). [[1 Kings 2]] shows that this settlement is provisional rather than a complete reconciliation.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The verb "exalted" in the description of Adonijah is evaluative: before anyone authorizes him, he elevates himself and says, "I will be king" (1:5). By contrast, David repeatedly says Solomon will reign "in my stead" and declares, "I have appointed him" (1:30, 35). The language establishes the chapter's contrast between seized status and conferred office.

The repeated formula "God save king" is spoken first within Adonijah's feast and then by the people after Solomon's anointing (1:25, 39). The same acclamation thus highlights rival claims while the surrounding circumstances distinguish their authority.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The clearest literary comparison reaches back to [[2 Samuel 15]]. Adonijah prepares chariots, horsemen, and fifty runners (1 Kings 1:5), while Absalom had prepared chariots, horses, and fifty runners (2 Samuel 15:1). The narrator then notes that Adonijah was born after Absalom (1 Kings 1:6). Together these details strongly invite comparison with an earlier son who challenged David, although the shared display could also reflect conventional royal self-presentation rather than direct verbal imitation alone.

Repetition also structures the chapter. Adonijah's feast is narrated and then reported by Bath-sheba and Nathan (1 Kings 1:9–10, 19, 25–26). Solomon's installation is narrated and then retold by Jonathan to Adonijah's company (1 Kings 1:38–40, 43–46). These reports move information between competing centers of power and allow readers to compare how the events are presented.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Evidence
> The chapter provides strong internal narrative evidence that Solomon's accession is distinguished from Adonijah's by Davidic authorization and public installation. This supports a literary claim about legitimacy within the narrative more strongly than it independently proves every historical detail of the succession.

> [!evidence] Literary Intertextual Parallel
> The unusually close correspondence between Adonijah's display and Absalom's earlier display is strong evidence that the Kings narrative invites comparison between the two rebellious sons, though the parallel alone cannot determine the author's date or full intention.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrator connect David's failure to correct Adonijah with the succession attempt (1:5–6)?
- Which features make Adonijah's claim appear royal, and which features distinguish Solomon's installation as authorized (1:5, 9, 25, 33–40)?
- What does Nathan's conduct suggest about the relationship between prophetic ministry and prudent political action (1:11–14, 22–27, 32–34)?
- Why does the narrative report the same events multiple times, and what changes when each speaker retells them?
- Does Adonijah grasp the altar's horns as an appeal for mercy, a legal claim to asylum, or both? What does the text explicitly say, and what must remain inferred?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
