# 1 Kings 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Kings 3]] · My notes: [[1 Kings 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter presents Solomon through three connected movements: a mixed opening portrait of royal advancement and qualified worship, a dream in which he asks for capacity to govern, and a difficult judgment that publicly demonstrates the promised wisdom. Solomon's service-directed request receives strong approval, but the portrait remains qualified. High-place worship is explained by the unfinished temple and also marked by the repeated word “only.” His marriage to Pharaoh's daughter gains negative significance when read retrospectively with [[1 Kings 11]], although 1 Kings 3 does not itself condemn the marriage.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Royal and worship setting (vv. 1–4):** Marriage, unfinished construction, high places, and sacrifice establish a favorable but qualified setting.
2. **Dream at Gibeon (vv. 5–15):** Solomon acknowledges inadequacy, requests an understanding heart, receives wisdom and additional gifts, and worships before the ark in Jerusalem. Lengthened life remains conditional on obedience.
3. **Wisdom demonstrated (vv. 16–27):** An unwitnessed dispute with incompatible testimony is resolved after the sword command elicits different priorities from the claimants.
4. **National recognition (v. 28):** Israel recognizes the wisdom of God in Solomon's judgment.

The request, promise, and lawsuit are connected both by placement and by verse 28's explicit attribution of the judgment to divine wisdom.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The narrative associates divine gifts with service and accountability. Solomon seeks wisdom because he must judge God's people, and God approves this purpose over longevity, riches, or an enemy's death (vv. 7–12). The additional gifts do not remove accountability: prolonged life is tied to continuing [[Obedience]] (v. 14).

The lawsuit connects [[Government and Laws]] with [[Justice and Mercy]]. Discernment identifies the mother within the story and preserves the child's life. The result should not turn the threatened killing of a child or a claimant's emotional response into a generally reliable legal procedure; the narrator guarantees the outcome in this particular account.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter clusters “heart,” “understanding,” “discern,” “judgment,” and “wisdom” (vv. 9, 11–12, 28). An “understanding heart” is therefore practical and moral capacity for government, especially hearing competing claims and distinguishing good from bad.

Solomon's description of himself as a “little child” (v. 7) most naturally expresses inadequacy or inexperience in context; the chapter does not state his age. The repeated “only” in verses 2–3 also matters. Verse 2 explains high-place sacrifice by the absence of a completed temple, while verse 3 qualifies an otherwise favorable description of Solomon. The wording creates tension but does not specify the legal status of every sacrifice made there.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The dream and lawsuit form a request-promise-demonstration sequence. Solomon requests discernment (v. 9), God grants it (vv. 11–12), and the next episode displays it (vv. 16–28). Because the testimony alone cannot resolve the dispute as presented, the sword command changes the evidentiary situation by eliciting the claimants' different responses.

The narrator calls the compassionate claimant the woman whose child was living before Solomon gives the verdict (v. 26). This assures readers that the judgment is correct within the story. The sequence is strong evidence of deliberate literary arrangement in the received chapter, but it is compatible with editorial combination of earlier traditions and cannot establish historicity or eyewitness composition.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does verse 2 temporarily excuse high-place sacrifice, or does the “only” of verse 3 retain a note of criticism?
- Why does Solomon respond to the Gibeon dream by worshipping before the ark in Jerusalem?
- What does the contrast with long life, riches, and an enemy's death reveal about the chapter's ideal of kingship?
- Would the maternity test be reliable outside this narrated case, where the narrator identifies the true mother?
- How should the favorable presentation be reassessed in light of [[1 Kings 9]] and [[1 Kings 11]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the dream account with [[2 Chronicles 1]] and the requirements for a king in [[Deuteronomy 17]]. Then trace the chapter's unresolved themes through [[1 Kings 8]], [[1 Kings 9]], and [[1 Kings 11]]: completion of the temple, renewed covenant warning, and Solomon's eventual religious failure. These comparisons clarify the larger narrative while remaining distinct from what 1 Kings 3 states directly.
