# 1 Kings 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Kings 3]] · My notes: [[1 Kings 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter presents Solomon through three connected movements: an opening portrait of royal advancement and qualified worship, a dream in which he asks for capacity to govern, and a difficult judgment that publicly demonstrates the promised wisdom. Solomon's devotion and service-oriented request receive strong emphasis, but the portrait is not uncomplicated. High-place worship is contextualized by the unfinished temple yet still qualifies the praise of Solomon. His marriage to Pharaoh's daughter gains negative significance when read retrospectively with [[1 Kings 11]], although 1 Kings 3 does not itself condemn that marriage.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Royal and worship setting (vv. 1–4):** Solomon's marriage, unfinished construction, high places, and sacrifice establish the chapter's setting.
2. **The dream at Gibeon (vv. 5–15):** Solomon acknowledges inadequacy, requests an understanding heart, receives wisdom and additional gifts, and then worships before the ark in Jerusalem. Long life remains conditional on obedience.
3. **Wisdom demonstrated (vv. 16–27):** A dispute with incompatible testimony is resolved when the proposed division of the child elicits sharply different responses.
4. **National recognition (v. 28):** Israel recognizes the wisdom of God in Solomon's judgment.

The final case is connected to the dream by both its placement and verse 28's explicit conclusion.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The narrative associates divine gifts with service and accountability. Solomon seeks wisdom because he must judge God's people, and God approves a request directed toward that responsibility rather than longevity, riches, or an enemy's death (vv. 7–12). The additional gifts do not remove moral accountability: prolonged life is tied to continuing [[Obedience]] (v. 14).

The lawsuit connects [[Government and Laws]] with [[Justice and Mercy]]. Within the story, discernment uncovers the true mother and preserves the vulnerable child's life. The dramatic test succeeds in this narrative, but emotional reactions under threat should not be treated as a universally sufficient legal method.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter clusters the ideas of heart, understanding, discernment, judgment, and wisdom (vv. 9, 11–12, 28). In context, an “understanding heart” is wisdom suited to judging competing claims and distinguishing good from bad, not merely abstract cleverness. Solomon's description of himself as a “little child” most likely expresses inadequacy or inexperience, but the chapter does not supply his age.

The repeated “only” in verses 2–3 also deserves attention. Verse 2 explains the people's high-place worship by the absence of a completed temple, while verse 3 uses the practice to qualify an otherwise favorable description of Solomon. The wording creates tension without specifying how severely every such sacrifice should be judged.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The dream and lawsuit form a request-promise-demonstration sequence: Solomon requests discernment (v. 9), God grants it (vv. 11–12), and the following case displays it (vv. 16–28). Ordinary testimony cannot resolve the dispute as presented, so the sword command changes the evidentiary situation by exposing the claimants' different priorities. The narrator identifies the compassionate claimant as the woman whose the living child was before Solomon announces his verdict, allowing readers to recognize that the test reaches the story's correct result.

This coherence is strong evidence of deliberate literary arrangement in the received chapter. It is compatible with an editor joining earlier traditions and does not independently establish the events' historicity, supernatural causation, compositional date, or eyewitness status.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the explanation for high-place sacrifice in verse 2 excuse the practice temporarily, or does “only” in verse 3 signal criticism despite that explanation?
- Why does Solomon respond to the Gibeon dream by worshipping before the ark in Jerusalem?
- What does Solomon's list of things he did not request reveal about the text's ideal of kingship?
- How reliable would the maternity test be outside this particular narrative, given that real people under threat do not always respond predictably?
- How should the chapter's favorable presentation be reread after Solomon's failures in [[1 Kings 11]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the dream with [[2 Chronicles 1]] and the royal requirements in [[Deuteronomy 17]]. Then trace the chapter's unresolved themes through [[1 Kings 8]], [[1 Kings 9]], and [[1 Kings 11]]: completion of the temple, renewed covenant warning, and Solomon's later religious failure. These comparisons illuminate the final form of the larger narrative, but they should remain distinct from meanings stated explicitly in 1 Kings 3.
