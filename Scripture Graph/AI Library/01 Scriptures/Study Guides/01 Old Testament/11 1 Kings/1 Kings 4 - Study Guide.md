# 1 Kings 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Kings 4]] · My notes: [[1 Kings 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter presents Solomon's kingdom through four connected portraits: central officials, twelve provisioning districts, dominion and material abundance, and divinely given wisdom with international fame. Its tone is strongly affirmative: the court lacks nothing, the realm has peace, and Judah and Israel dwell safely. At the same time, the immense quantities of food, fodder, horses, and monthly district obligations reveal the resources required to sustain the monarchy. The text supports a portrait of effective administration and prosperity, but it does not demonstrate that prosperity or cost was experienced equally by every subject. These tensions make the chapter especially relevant to [[Government and Laws]], [[Education and Learning]], and [[War and Peace]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Central administration (vv. 1–6):** priestly, scribal, military, household, and tribute offices establish the machinery of government.

2. **Twelve provisioning assignments (vv. 7–19):** officers supply the royal household in monthly rotation. The assignments mix tribal territories, cities, and wider regions rather than simply reproducing a twelve-tribe list.

3. **Population, dominion, abundance, and security (vv. 20–28):** expansive territorial language, daily provisions, horses, peace, and the vine-and-fig-tree image portray royal greatness and settled life.

4. **Wisdom and international reputation (vv. 29–34):** God gives Solomon wisdom expressed in sayings, songs, discourse about living things, comparison with other sages, and worldwide fame.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter explicitly identifies God as the giver of Solomon's wisdom. That wisdom is portrayed broadly through understanding, proverbial instruction, songs, learning about living things, and the ability to attract hearers. This supports study of [[Education and Learning]], though the text does not equate Solomon's discourse with modern scientific method or attribute every feature of his government directly to wisdom.

Peace is made concrete through the household image of each person beneath a vine and fig tree. Read beside [[Micah 4]], the image joins political peace with freedom from ordinary fear. Solomon's horse establishment should also be read beside [[Deuteronomy 17]]. The canonical comparison creates a genuine tension, even though this chapter voices no condemnation.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly uses comprehensive and expansive expressions: “all Israel,” “all kingdoms,” “all the days,” “all the region,” and “all people.” These expressions magnify the scope of Solomon's rule and reputation, but they need not be treated as modern statistical or cartographic claims. “From Dan even to Beer-sheba” depicts the land from north to south, while the vine-and-fig-tree expression translates national security into ordinary household life.

Sand imagery appears in verses 20 and 29. It first represents the multitude of the people and then the breadth of Solomon's understanding, verbally joining two forms of abundance. The first occurrence may also recall the [[Abrahamic Covenant]], although conventional abundance imagery is an adequate alternative explanation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Lists, numbers, geographic spans, and repeated totalizing terms turn Solomon's greatness into several forms of abundance: numerous subjects, an extensively supplied court, military resources, territorial dominion, wisdom, literary production, and fame. The progression moves from the organization surrounding the king to the wisdom located in him and finally to foreigners traveling toward him.

The portrait is idealizing without being simple. The monthly provisioning system and enormous daily ration can signify administrative success while also revealing royal consumption. Likewise, peace appears beside a large horse establishment. These details allow later chapters such as [[1 Kings 11]] and [[1 Kings 12]] to complicate the portrait without requiring readers to deny the positive emphasis of [[1 Kings 4]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Numerical Textual Discrepancy
> The forty thousand stalls reported in 1 Kings 4:26 differ from the four thousand stalls reported in 2 Chronicles 9:25, while both passages give twelve thousand horsemen. This is clear evidence of a numerical textual or source-tradition problem, but the comparison by itself does not identify the earlier figure.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted numerical discrepancy · [[Numerical Textual Discrepancy in 1 Kings 4|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual Numerical Discrepancy
> The KJV text of 1 Kings 4:26 reports forty thousand stalls of horses, whereas 2 Chronicles 9:25 reports four thousand stalls and the same total of twelve thousand horsemen. This is firm evidence of a numerical difference between the canonical passages, but it does not determine which number, if either, reflects the earliest recoverable wording or historical total.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · firm canonical numerical discrepancy · [[Intertextual Numerical Discrepancy in 1 Kings 4|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Parallel-Account Numerical Variation
> First Kings 4:26 reports forty thousand stalls of horses, whereas the parallel notice in 2 Chronicles 9:25 reports four thousand stalls; both report twelve thousand horsemen. The tenfold difference is direct evidence of a numerical textual or traditional problem that should be acknowledged rather than silently harmonized.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · firm canonical numerical discrepancy · [[Parallel-Account Numerical Variation in 1 Kings 4|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Numeric Variant Between Parallel Accounts
> 1 Kings 4:26 states Solomon had 'forty thousand stalls of horses for his chariots,' a figure ten times larger than the parallel notice of four thousand stalls given for the same king in the Chronicler's account (2 Chronicles 9:25), a well-known numeric discrepancy between the two administrative summaries of Solomon's reign.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted numerical discrepancy · [[Textual/Numeric Variant Between Parallel Accounts in 1 Kings 4|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism (Versional Evidence) *(tentative)*
> The cryptic close of 1 Kings 4:19, 'and he was the only officer which was in the land,' is difficult in itself (it is unclear which 'land' is meant, since Geber's district was Gilead); some interpreters and translations understand an underlying text that once referred more explicitly to a separate governor 'in the land of Judah,' which would make Judah an implied thirteenth administrative unit rather than a true omission.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.3 · textual difficulty accepted; proposed reconstruction unverified

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Did the provisioning districts deliberately cross tribal boundaries, or were they organized mainly for practical collection and transport?

- Why is the realm called “all Israel” while also being described as “Judah and Israel”?

- How should “they lacked nothing” be weighed against the amount each district supplied?

- Does the narrator expect the horse establishment to recall [[Deuteronomy 17]], or does that critical force emerge principally from reading the wider canon?

- How should the chapter's prosperity be held together with the later complaint about Solomon's heavy yoke in [[1 Kings 12]]?

- What textual history best explains forty thousand stalls in [[1 Kings 4]] and four thousand in [[2 Chronicles 9]]?

- How much historical weight can the detailed administrative roster bear without independent evidence?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the official list with [[2 Samuel 20]] and [[1 Chronicles 18]]. Read the notices about horses and dominion alongside [[Deuteronomy 17]], [[1 Kings 10]], and [[1 Kings 11]]. Then compare the provisioning system with the complaint preserved in [[1 Kings 12]], while avoiding the assumption that the later complaint proves every obligation in chapter 4 was oppressive. Compare the stall total directly with [[2 Chronicles 9]]; the numerical discrepancy is certain, but these two verses alone do not determine the earlier reading. For the vine-and-fig-tree image, compare [[Micah 4]] and [[Zechariah 3]].
