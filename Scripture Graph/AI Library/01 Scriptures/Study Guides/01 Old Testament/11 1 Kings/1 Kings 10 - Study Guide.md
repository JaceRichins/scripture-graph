## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- vv1-3: the visit and Solomon's answers
- vv4-9: the queen's reaction and her praise of the LORD
- vv10-13: gift exchange and departure
- vv14-22: annual income, gold armor, the ivory throne, the Tarshish fleet
- vv23-29: summary of unmatched wealth and the horse/chariot trade

The queen's firsthand testimony (vv1-13) reads as one worked example of the broader claim in vv23-25 that 'all the earth sought to Solomon' — a recurring fame motif rather than a strict verse-1/verse-24 bracket, since nine more verses of inventory follow v24.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The queen's own words in v9 credit Solomon's throne to the LORD's love for Israel and describe his kingship's purpose as 'judgment and justice' — a foreign monarch's diplomatic assessment, not a narratorial statement, though it is a notable outsider's acknowledgment of Israel's God, relevant to [[The Godhead]] and [[Government and Laws]]. Wisdom in this chapter is repeatedly treated as a gift God 'put in his heart' (v24) rather than a private achievement, and the servants' happiness is tied to continually hearing it (v8), connecting to [[Education and Learning]]. The text records the queen blessing the LORD but does not narrate a conversion or her entry into Israel's covenant.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
- 'almug trees' (vv11-12; 'algum' in the Chronicles parallel) — an unidentified wood, perhaps a form of sandalwood or juniper, used for temple pillars, the king's house, and instruments.
- 'there was no more spirit in her' (v5) is an idiom for being overwhelmed, not a claim about literal loss of spirit or a conversion experience.
- 'targets' (v16) and 'shields' (v17) are both gold armor plating of differing sizes; the exact distinction rests on the underlying Hebrew terms and should be treated cautiously rather than asserted with confidence.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The wealth catalog (vv14-29) uses court-annal rhetoric: specific weights (666 talents, 600 and 150 shekels), repeated superlatives ('no more such,' 'not the like made,' 'nothing accounted of'), and named trade partners (Hiram's navy, Tarshish, Egypt, the Hittites, Syria) present Solomon's reign in near-hyperbolic terms. Read against [[Deuteronomy 17]]'s specific prohibitions on a king multiplying horses, returning to Egypt for them, and multiplying silver and gold, the inventory's content is a real textual correspondence — whether it is intended as celebration, foreshadowing, or both is an open interpretive question the chapter itself does not resolve.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Literary-Theological Correspondence
> Solomon's extraordinary gold, multiplication of horses and chariots, and acquisition of horses from Egypt correspond closely to matters restricted for Israel's king in Deuteronomy 17:16-17. This creates a substantial canonical tension, although 1 Kings 10 itself does not explicitly pronounce these activities sinful.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation; interpretation labeled disputed · [[Canonical Literary-Theological Correspondence in 1 Kings 10|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Numerological Curiosity *(tentative)*
> Solomon's recorded annual gold income of 'six hundred threescore and six' talents numerically matches the 'number of the beast' in Revelation 13:18. This is almost certainly an unrelated coincidence given the centuries, languages, genres, and authorship separating the two texts, though the numeral overlap is sometimes noted in popular (non-scholarly) commentary.
> — strength 0.1 · confidence 0.3 · speculative, self-labeled coincidence

> [!evidence] Historical-Archaeological *(tentative)*
> The scale of wealth narrated here (666 talents of gold annually, 1,400 chariots, gold-plated shields and throne) is central to the broader scholarly 'United Monarchy' debate over whether 10th-century BCE Jerusalem's archaeological remains support a kingdom of this magnitude.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, uncited

> [!evidence] Comparative History/Tradition *(tentative)*
> 'Sheba,' the queen's homeland, is most often identified by scholars with the ancient South Arabian kingdom of Saba' (modern Yemen), attested in Sabaean inscriptions as a hub of the incense and spice trade referenced in this chapter's gift lists; a later Ethiopian tradition (the Kebra Nagast) instead identifies her as Ethiopian and makes her, through a son, ancestress of Ethiopia's royal line.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, uncited
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does verse 9 reflect the queen's own diplomatic praise, genuine conviction, or narratorial commentary — and does it matter which?
- Is the wealth inventory in vv14-29 meant purely to celebrate Solomon, or does its close correspondence to the king's law in [[Deuteronomy 17]] plant an intentional warning ahead of [[1 Kings 11]]?
- Why does the queen's acclamation of the LORD (vv6-9) sit directly beside an extended material inventory — two measures of the same success, or a quiet contrast?
- Who or where was 'Sheba,' and does it matter for interpreting the chapter whether she was Arabian or Ethiopian by later tradition?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- [[2 Chronicles 9]] is a near-verbatim parallel account of this chapter.
- [[1 Kings 11]] immediately follows with Solomon's foreign wives and apostasy — worth reading alongside this chapter's inventory of the horses and gold (and soon wives) that [[Deuteronomy 17]] warns a king against multiplying.
- Matthew 12:42 and Luke 11:31 have Jesus invoke 'the queen of the south' from this chapter as a witness against unbelief, declaring 'a greater than Solomon is here.'
