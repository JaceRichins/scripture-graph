# 1 Kings 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Kings 14]] · My notes: [[1 Kings 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter places both kingdoms formed after Solomon under theological evaluation. Jeroboam's disguised inquiry about his sick son becomes an oracle against his dynasty and a warning of national [[Scattering of Israel]]; the child's death, burial, and mourning are narrated as fulfillment of Ahijah's word. The account then turns to Judah, where Jerusalem's chosen status does not prevent apostasy or Shishak's seizure of temple and palace treasures. The two kingdoms are not treated as identical, but neither is presented as a righteous political alternative.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The disguised consultation (vv. 1–6):** Jeroboam sends his wife to the blind Ahijah, but divine disclosure exposes the disguise.

2. **Ahijah's oracle (vv. 7–16):** past favor is recalled, Jeroboam is indicted, judgment is announced against his house, the child's distinct fate is specified, and the oracle broadens to Israel's uprooting and scattering.

3. **Fulfillment and Jeroboam's regnal close (vv. 17–20):** the child dies, is buried and mourned, and Jeroboam's reign ends.

4. **Rehoboam and Judah (vv. 21–31):** Judah's offenses are listed, Shishak plunders Jerusalem, bronze shields replace Solomon's gold shields, and Rehoboam's reign closes.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Revelation]] as knowledge supplied by God rather than ordinary sight: Ahijah cannot see the visitor, yet knows her identity and purpose. It also depicts consequences at personal, dynastic, and national levels. Abijah's death despite the good found in him makes the passage morally difficult and resists a simplistic rule that goodness always brings immediate temporal safety. His burial and Israel's mourning distinguish him from the disgrace announced for the rest of the dynasty, but the text does not fully explain the justice of his death or explicitly call the distinction mercy. Jeroboam's influence and the people's participation together make the chapter relevant to both leadership and communal accountability.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several KJV expressions require care. The harsh idiom in verse 10 belongs to the oracle's language of eliminating Jeroboam's male line. The words rendered “groves” and “sodomites” in verses 23–24 are historically loaded English renderings and should not be used to build precise claims about ancient practices without consulting the underlying Hebrew and multiple modern translations. The chapter itself associates the practices with Judah's imitation of the nations but does not provide detailed definitions.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The opening scene is organized by concealment and perception: a disguised visitor approaches a physically blind prophet, yet the LORD has already disclosed her identity and purpose. The narrative then matches Ahijah's immediate prediction with the child's reported death, burial, and mourning, presenting the prophetic word as reliable within the story. This internal pattern does not independently establish supernatural foreknowledge or the account's historical sequence. The chapter also places the condemnations and regnal endings of the northern and southern kingdoms alongside one another without implying that their offenses were identical. Solomon's gold shields replaced by Rehoboam's bronze shields plausibly evoke diminished splendor, but symbolic intent is not stated.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What specific good was found in Abijah? The chapter affirms it but does not describe it.

- Why does Jeroboam seek Ahijah's knowledge while attempting to conceal the inquirer's identity?

- How should Abijah's death be understood alongside his favorable distinction without forcing a simple theory of individual reward and punishment?

- Does the placement of Shishak's invasion after Judah's sins imply divine judgment, even though this chapter gives no explicit causal formula?

- How does the condemnation of both kingdoms complicate attempts to identify political legitimacy with covenant faithfulness?

- What purpose, if any, is served by identifying Naamah as an Ammonitess at both ends of Rehoboam's notice?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[1 Kings 11]] for Ahijah's earlier conditional promise to Jeroboam, [[1 Kings 12]] for Jeroboam's rival worship system, and [[1 Kings 15]] for the destruction of his dynasty. Compare [[2 Chronicles 12]], which parallels Shishak's invasion and adds an explicit account of humbling and partial deliverance. Deuteronomy 12 and 28 provide covenantal background for the worship-location and dispersion language. For the longer canonical trajectory of the scattering warning, compare [[2 Kings 17]]. These literary connections should not automatically be treated as independent historical witnesses.
