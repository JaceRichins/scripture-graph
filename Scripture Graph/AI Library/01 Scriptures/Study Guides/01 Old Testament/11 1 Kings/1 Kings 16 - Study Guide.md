# 1 Kings 16 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Kings 16]] · My notes: [[1 Kings 16 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
First Kings 16 compresses several reigns into a narrative of dynastic instability: Baasha's house falls to Zimri, Zimri reigns seven days, Israel divides between Omri and Tibni, Omri founds Samaria, and Ahab receives the chapter's severest religious evaluation. The narrator interprets these events through [[Sin]], royal responsibility, and [[Prophecy]], while also naming human mechanisms such as conspiracy, military support, siege, and factional conflict. That theological interpretation is a feature of the narrative and should not be confused with independent proof of divine causation.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Jehu's word against Baasha (vv. 1–7):** Baasha is condemned for repeating Jeroboam's pattern.

2. **Elah's assassination and Baasha's dynastic collapse (vv. 8–14):** Zimri kills Elah and destroys Baasha's house.

3. **Zimri's seven-day reign (vv. 15–20):** The army makes Omri king, Tirzah is besieged, and Zimri dies in the burning royal house.

4. **Omri's contested rise and reign (vv. 21–28):** Omri's faction prevails over Tibni's, and Omri builds Samaria.

5. **Ahab's religious escalation (vv. 29–33):** Ahab serves Baal and builds a house and altar for Baal in Samaria.

6. **Hiel rebuilds Jericho (v. 34):** The notice explicitly connects the event with Joshua's earlier word.

Repeated references to royal chronicles underscore that the account is selective.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents two explicit word-and-fulfillment correspondences: the destruction of Baasha's house is linked to Jehu's oracle, and Hiel's rebuilding of Jericho is linked to Joshua's word. These are strong observations about the narrative's treatment of [[Prophecy]], but they do not by themselves establish when the predictions were recorded or how the reported events occurred.

The chapter also places theological judgment beside human action. Zimri conspires, the army chooses Omri, rival factions struggle, and Ahab chooses particular forms of worship. The narrator evaluates these events through loyalty to the LORD and repeatedly emphasizes rulers who cause Israel to sin. Zimri's purge fulfills the announced judgment, but the chapter does not explicitly say that the purge itself is the sin condemned in verse 19. Political success likewise does not function in the narrative as proof of divine approval.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The recurring expression about walking in the way or sins of Jeroboam functions as a compact royal indictment. A second pattern intensifies the assessment: Omri is worse than those before him, Ahab does evil above those before him, and Ahab provokes the LORD more than all earlier kings. The KJV's word "grove" in verse 33 is interpretively less specific for modern readers than the surrounding references to Baal's house and altar; identifying its precise cultic object requires attention to the underlying Hebrew and translation history.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repeated formulas unify the rapid succession of rulers: kings do evil in the LORD's sight, walk in Jeroboam's way, cause Israel to sin, die, and are followed by successors. References to royal chronicles indicate that additional acts lie outside this selective account. The narrator mentions Omri's might but devotes greater attention to religious evaluation.

Jehu's oracle and its stated fulfillment form a major correspondence within the chapter. The final notice about Hiel provides a second, shorter correspondence with [[Joshua 6]]. These links demonstrate deliberate literary and theological use of earlier words within the received narrative; without additional historical evidence, they do not settle when the predictions were recorded relative to the events.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the chapter relate narrated divine judgment to conspiracy, military action, siege, and factional conflict?

- What does the contrast between Omri's political achievements and the narrator's negative evaluation reveal about the standards used to judge rulers?

- Does the interval between Omri's acclamation and the stated beginning of his reign reflect the struggle with Tibni, or could another chronological convention explain it?

- Who are the most likely antecedents of "he" and "him" in 1 Kings 16:7, and how strongly does the surrounding narrative constrain the reading?

- What does the explicit reference to [[Joshua 6]] establish, and what questions about Hiel's sons and the event's mechanism remain unanswered?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
