## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves through four stages:

1. **Purpose (vv. 1–5):** Hiram contacts Solomon, who connects the proposed temple with rest from war and the promise concerning David's son.
2. **Negotiation (vv. 6–12):** Solomon requests cedar and skilled workers; Hiram proposes transport by sea and payment in provisions; peace and a league accompany the agreement.
3. **Labor mobilization (vv. 13–16):** Solomon organizes rotating labor from Israel and enumerates additional workers and supervisors.
4. **Material preparation (vv. 17–18):** The two kings' builders prepare dressed stone and timber.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Solomon describes the temple as a house built "unto the name" of the LORD (vv. 3, 5), language that associates the sanctuary with divine identity and honor without attempting here to explain how God is present there. The chapter also treats the promised succession from David to his son as relevant to temple building (v. 5; compare [[2 Samuel 7]] and [[1 Chronicles 22]]).

Verse 12 links the LORD's promised gift of wisdom with the political setting of peace and alliance. This permits a reading of wisdom as practically expressed in governance, but it should not be turned into blanket approval of every feature of Solomon's administration.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated expression "unto the name of the LORD" (vv. 3, 5) is more precise than saying simply that the structure is a residence built for God. The phrase deserves comparison with the fuller temple theology of [[1 Kings 8]].

The KJV term "levy" in verses 13–14 denotes conscripted labor in this context. Because the chapter gives no explicit moral verdict on that institution, readers should distinguish the narrator's report of efficient organization from an ethical endorsement of compulsory labor.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter turns on a rest/war contrast: David could not build 'for the wars which were about him on every side' (v.3), but Solomon has 'rest on every side' (v.4) — the same phrase marking the change in circumstance that makes the temple possible. This positions Solomon's identity as builder-king against David's identity as warrior-king. The chapter also moves from words to stone: it opens with messages exchanged between kings and closes with 'great stones, costly stones, and hewed stones' (v.17) being physically prepared, moving from spoken promise to material foundation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the tension between 'a levy out of all Israel' (5:13) and the later claim that Solomon made no Israelite 'bondmen' (1 Kings 9:21-22) reflect two distinct labor categories (rotating conscription vs. permanent servitude), or two competing traditions about Solomon's reign?
- What motivates Hiram's blessing of 'the LORD' in v.7 — genuine reverence, diplomatic courtesy, or the narrator's conventional framing of a foreign king's speech? The verse alone cannot decide.
- Does v.12 mean the Hiram treaty was itself an expression of Solomon's wisdom, or does 'as he promised him' simply recall the wisdom granted earlier at Gibeon ([[1 Kings 3]]), with the note of peace appended as a separate fact?
- How should the achievement of successful temple preparation be weighed against the compulsory labor described in vv.13-16, which this chapter itself does not morally evaluate?
- If Adoniram (5:14) is the same official later stoned as Adoram in [[1 Kings 12]], what would that continuity suggest about the long-term political cost of Solomon's building program? (The identification rests on similarity of name and office, not an explicit statement in either text.)
