# Leviticus 18 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 18]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 18 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Israel shall not live as the Egyptians and the Canaanites—Marriages to many close relatives and others are forbidden—Homosexual behavior and other sexual perversions are an abomination—The land expels those nations that practice sexual abominations.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Covenant frame and contrasting ways of life (vv. 1-5):** Israel is to follow the LORD's judgments rather than the practices of Egypt and Canaan.

2. **Kinship and marital boundaries (vv. 6-18):** A general prohibition concerning near kin is developed through particular family relationships.

3. **Additional boundaries (vv. 19-23):** Menstrual intercourse, adultery, Molech worship, male-male intercourse, and bestiality are prohibited.

4. **Defilement, land, and judgment (vv. 24-30):** The conclusion applies the warning to native and resident stranger, personifies the land as expelling defiled inhabitants, and returns to the declaration, "I am the LORD your God."
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Obedience]] as part of Israel's allegiance to God: those who keep the statutes shall "live in them" (v. 5). [[Ezekiel 20]] repeats this formula while recounting Israel's disobedience, and [[Romans 10]] and [[Galatians 3]] employ it in later arguments about law and faith. These passages make verse 5 important for studying [[Law of Moses]], [[Faith]], and [[Grace]], but verbal reuse alone does not settle larger theological debates or require "life" to have precisely the same nuance in every context.

Leviticus 18 also connects [[Sin]] with collective and territorial judgment. The land is depicted as defiled and as expelling its inhabitants (vv. 25, 28), and Israel is warned that it receives no automatic exemption from the judgment attributed to the preceding nations. This is covenantal personification and moral rhetoric; the chapter does not explain a literal causal mechanism.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated phrase "uncover the nakedness" is a sexual euphemism in this context. Its kinship formulations are relational: the nakedness of a father's wife is called the father's nakedness, and the nakedness of a brother's wife the brother's nakedness. This language presents sexual wrongdoing as a violation of family relationships as well as an individual act.

Verse 22 describes conduct, not a modern identity category. Responsible interpretation should neither erase the verse's direct prohibition of male-male intercourse nor claim that its wording supplies a complete account of modern sexual orientation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter moves from two foreign lands (Egypt and Canaan), to Israelite households, and finally back to the land as a whole. Repetition binds these levels together: "I am the LORD" punctuates the commands, "uncover nakedness" organizes the central list, and "defile" dominates the conclusion. The vivid image of the land vomiting out inhabitants turns territorial loss into a moral consequence and warns Israel that possession of Canaan is conditional rather than unconditional.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What kind of "life" is promised in verse 5, and how do [[Ezekiel 20]], [[Romans 10]], and [[Galatians 3]] reuse the formulation without necessarily giving it an identical meaning?

- Why is the Molech prohibition placed among laws otherwise dominated by sexual and family boundaries? What can be concluded from the placement itself, and what would require external historical or compositional evidence?

- Verse 18 resembles Jacob's concurrent marriages to Leah and Rachel in [[Genesis 29]]. How should readers compare a pre-Sinai patriarchal narrative with later Mosaic legislation, while remembering that narration does not necessarily endorse every reported practice?

- Which norms does the chapter portray as extending to prior nations and resident strangers, and what does it leave unstated about their application under later covenants or modern civil law?

- How does reading [[Leviticus 20]] alongside this chapter clarify the difference between a prohibited act, its rationale, and its stated penalty?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
