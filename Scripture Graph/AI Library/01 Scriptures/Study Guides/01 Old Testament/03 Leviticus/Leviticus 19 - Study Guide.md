# Leviticus 19 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 19]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 19 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
[[Leviticus 19]] addresses the whole congregation and grounds its opening command in the LORD's holiness. In the chapter's received form, worship and sacrifice stand beside family duties, agricultural provision, wages, truthful dealing, impartial judgment, neighbor-love, treatment of strangers, and commercial integrity. This strongly supports reading holiness here as embracing communal and social conduct as well as ritual observance. It does not, by itself, prove that every law originated in a single compositional layer. Repeated forms of “I am the LORD” continually return these varied commands to divine identity and authority.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious descriptive outline is:

1. Verses 1–4: the call to holiness, parents, Sabbaths, and idols.
2. Verses 5–10: peace offerings and harvesting provisions.
3. Verses 11–18: truth, wages, protection from abuse, judgment, hatred, reproof, vengeance, and neighbor-love.
4. Verses 19–31: mixtures, a difficult sexual and legal case, offerings, fruit trees, mourning customs, Sabbath, sanctuary, and prohibited spiritual practices.
5. Verses 32–36: honor for the aged, treatment of resident strangers, and honest measurement.
6. Verse 37: concluding obedience.

These divisions are approximate rather than explicit. The recurring declarations “I am the LORD” and “I am the LORD your God” provide firmer visible punctuation than any perfectly symmetrical outline.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter closely joins worship and ordinary social conduct. [[Sabbath Day]] observance and [[Sacrifice]] appear beside mandated gleaning, prompt wages, impartial judgment, protection of disabled persons, and [[Honesty]] in trade. This placement supports treating social ethics as part of the chapter's presentation of holy covenant life.

Verse 18 commands love for one's neighbor, and verse 34 applies the same “as thyself” language to the resident stranger. Israel's former strangerhood in Egypt is the stated reason for this command. The passage therefore connects remembered experience with [[Justice and Mercy]], while not resolving every modern question about immigration or citizenship.

Verses 21–22 connect a trespass offering, priestly atonement, and forgiveness in one specific legal case. This is important background for [[Sacrifice]] and [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], but repentance is not explicitly described and the passage is not a complete account of the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. Its setting also assumes slavery and unequal legal status; that moral difficulty should not be concealed or sanitized.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The recurring declaration “I am the LORD,” sometimes expanded to “I am the LORD your God,” punctuates many different kinds of commands. In the received chapter it repeatedly foregrounds the identity and authority of the lawgiver. The formula may also be conventional or editorial, so it does not establish single authorship, date, or compositional unity.

The repetition of “as thyself” in verses 18 and 34 connects love for the neighbor with commanded love for the resident stranger. This broadens the chapter's sphere of commanded love, though it does not conclusively define every use of “neighbour” or erase all legal distinctions elsewhere.

Verses 3–4 and 11–12 also have clear thematic affinities with the [[Ten Commandments]]. These parallels support a final-form reading centered on core covenant obligations, but shared Israelite legal tradition remains a sufficient alternative to direct literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How does the chapter's mixture of ritual, economic, judicial, and interpersonal commands affect its presentation of holiness?

Why is “fear thy God” attached to commands involving the deaf, blind, and aged? Does this emphasize divine accountability for abuse that may escape human detection, or is it a broader covenantal sanction?

How should “not suffer sin upon him” in verse 17 be understood in relation to rebuke, personal guilt, and the neighbor's wrongdoing?

How does verse 34 affect possible readings of “neighbour” in verse 18 without erasing the distinct wording and setting of either verse?

Why are the mixture prohibitions in verse 19 grouped together when the chapter supplies no explicit rationale?

How should readers confront the slavery, unequal legal status, disputed wording, and non-execution provision in verses 20–22 without concealing the difficulty or claiming more than the text establishes?

Which commands express broadly intelligible moral principles, and which depend especially on ancient Israel's ritual, agrarian, and legal institutions? What interpretive criteria justify the distinction?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
