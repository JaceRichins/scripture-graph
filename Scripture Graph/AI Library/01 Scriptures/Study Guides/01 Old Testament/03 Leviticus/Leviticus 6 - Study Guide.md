# Leviticus 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 6]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The people must first make restitution for sin, then offer a trespass offering, and thereby gain forgiveness through atonement made by the priests.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Property offenses, restitution, and trespass offering (vv. 1-7).** The offender restores the property, adds one fifth, and brings an unblemished ram.

2. **Law of the burnt offering (vv. 8-13).** The offering remains on the altar overnight; priests remove ashes and maintain the uninterrupted fire.

3. **Law of the grain offering (vv. 14-18).** A memorial portion is burned and the priestly remainder is eaten unleavened in the holy place.

4. **Offering of the anointed priest (vv. 19-23).** Flour and oil are offered morning and evening, and the whole offering is burned.

5. **Law of the sin offering (vv. 24-30).** The text regulates slaughter, priestly consumption, blood-spattered garments, cooking vessels, and the exceptional offering that must be burned.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Restitution as part of repentance.** Before the trespass offering is even brought, the guilty party must restore what was taken and add a fifth (vv. 4-5). This concrete sequence — repair the wrong to a neighbor, then present an offering to the LORD — is strong textual evidence that, at least for these property offenses, ritual atonement does not replace material repair to the person harmed. [[Repentance and Forgiveness]] here has both a human and a divine dimension.

**Mediated atonement.** After the offering is brought, "the priest shall make an atonement for him before the LORD: and it shall be forgiven him" (v. 7). The New Testament book of Hebrews later draws on the related rule that a sin offering whose blood enters the sanctuary must be burned outside the camp (compare Leviticus 6:30 with Leviticus 16:27) to interpret Jesus Christ's suffering "without the gate" (Hebrews 13:11-12) — a typological reading supplied by the New Testament author, not a claim this chapter makes about itself.

**Ordered holiness and priestly stewardship.** Priestly clothing, sacred locations, food portions, blood, cooking vessels, and fire are all carefully regulated (vv. 10-11, 16-18, 27-29). At minimum this shows that [[Aaronic Priesthood]] service required sustained, physical, material stewardship rather than isolated ceremonial acts. The garment change before ashes leave the sanctuary (vv. 10-11) and the statements that touching certain offerings makes a person or object "holy" (vv. 18, 27) are compatible with a broader theology of graded sacred space later associated with [[Temples]], though the chapter itself does not spell out a formal hierarchy of holiness zones.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
In the KJV, "meat offering" can mislead modern readers because "meat" once had the broader sense of food. The chapter identifies the general grain offering with flour, oil, and frankincense (v. 15), so "grain offering" is the clearer contemporary description — though note that the priest's own daily offering (vv. 20-21) specifies only flour and oil, without frankincense.

The phrase "trespass against the LORD, and lie unto his neighbour" (v. 2) is notable because its grammar binds religious and interpersonal accountability together. An alternative, more mundane explanation is worth naming alongside this: in the ancient Near East, oaths were commonly sworn in a deity's name, so false swearing was routinely treated as an offense against that deity by legal convention, not necessarily as a distinctively Israelite theological innovation. The text itself does not choose between these readings.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is organized by recurring formulas: "the LORD spake unto Moses" introduces units in verses 1, 8, 19, and 24, while "this is the law of" identifies sacrificial procedures in verses 9, 14, and 25. Repetition also emphasizes continuity in verses 9, 12, and 13, where the altar fire is repeatedly said to remain burning. This is clear textual emphasis; claims that the fire specifically symbolizes inner devotion go beyond what the chapter explicitly explains.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Textual Sequence
> The sequence of restitution, added compensation, sacrifice, priestly atonement, and forgiveness is strong textual evidence that, for these offenses, ritual atonement does not replace material repair to the person harmed.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · settled; well-hedged · [[Internal Textual Sequence in Leviticus 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Theological Crux *(tentative)*
> The rule that contact with the sin/grain offering's most-holy flesh transmits holiness (Leviticus 6:18, 27) sits in apparent tension with Haggai 2:12-13, where holy meat carried in a garment does NOT sanctify the food it subsequently touches, while uncleanness does transmit by contact — a recognized crux in priestly-law scholarship over whether and how holiness is understood to be "contagious."
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · genuine scholarly crux, disputed

> [!evidence] Comparative Ancient Law *(tentative)*
> The categories of trespass named in verse 2 — property "delivered him to keep" (bailment), goods held "in fellowship" (partnership), and property "taken away by violence" — correspond closely to standard categories found in other ancient Near Eastern legal collections (e.g., Old Babylonian laws on deposits and partnerships), suggesting Israel's civil-cultic law shared common legal categories with its wider cultural environment rather than being wholly sui generis.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed / uncited

> [!evidence] Intertextual Legal Comparison *(tentative)*
> The one-fifth addition required here for a self-confessed trespass is markedly lighter than the double, fourfold, or fivefold restitution required for theft detected or unconfessed in Exodus 22:1-9, which may indicate that voluntary acknowledgment of guilt was treated more leniently than being caught in the act.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · disputed, likely oversimplified

> [!evidence] Intertextual Duplication *(tentative)*
> The restitution-plus-one-fifth law given here is substantially duplicated in Numbers 5, indicating this specific remedy for wrongs against a neighbor was treated as an important, independently restated rule within the Torah's legal material rather than a single isolated ruling.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · duplication real, interpretive gloss overreaches

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the law call deception against a neighbor a trespass against the LORD (vv. 2-3)?

- What does the required restoration teach that sacrifice alone would not communicate (vv. 4-7)?

- Why might the priest change garments before carrying ashes outside the camp (vv. 10-11)? The text commands the change but does not explicitly explain its rationale.

- How do the different rules for priestly consumption in verses 16-18, 23, and 26-30 caution against treating all offerings as interchangeable?

- How does [[Leviticus 6]] develop or supplement the offering instructions in [[Leviticus 4]], [[Leviticus 5]], and [[Leviticus 7]]?
