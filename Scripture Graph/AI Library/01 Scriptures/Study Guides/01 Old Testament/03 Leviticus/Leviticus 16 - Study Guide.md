# Leviticus 16 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> How and when Aaron must enter the holy place is explained—Sacrifices are offered to reconcile Israel to God—The scapegoat carries away the sins of the people—The sins of all Israel are forgiven on the Day of Atonement.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Danger and preparation (verses 1–5):** access within the veil is restricted; Aaron washes, dresses in holy linen, and obtains the offerings.

2. **Offerings selected (verses 6–10):** Aaron's bullock and the congregation's two goats are assigned their roles.

3. **Blood rites (verses 11–19):** blood from the bullock and slain goat is brought within the veil and applied to the mercy seat and altar.

4. **Removal rite (verses 20–22):** Israel's confessed iniquities are placed upon the live goat, which is sent into the wilderness.

5. **Completion and washing (verses 23–28):** Aaron changes garments and offers burnt offerings; handlers wash before reentering the camp.

6. **Annual statute (verses 29–34):** the people rest and afflict their souls while the anointed priest performs the rite once each year.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays sin and uncleanness as affecting both people and the sacred place where God dwells among them; atonement therefore includes purification of the sanctuary as well as cleansing of worshippers (16:16-20, 33). It also holds together priestly mediation and communal participation: Aaron performs the rites — first securing atonement for himself and his household (16:6, 11) before acting for the people — while the congregation observes a sabbath-like cessation from work and afflicts its souls (16:29, 31).

The two goats depict two aspects of this atonement rather than one: the slain goat's blood addresses the sanctuary's defilement, while the live goat, confessed over and released, carries Israel's iniquities away from the camp (16:15-22). Later theological categories like "propitiation" and "expiation" can help describe this pairing, but Leviticus 16 itself does not name or systematize it that way.

For Christian study, [[Hebrews 9]] supplies the clearest canonical warrant for relating the chapter to the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. Hebrews emphasizes both similarity and contrast: Leviticus describes repeated annual ministry by a priest who first needs atonement for himself, whereas Hebrews presents Christ's offering as climactic and unrepeated (Hebrews 9:24-28).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV term "scapegoat" should be handled cautiously. The underlying Hebrew term often rendered or transliterated “Azazel” is disputed, and proposed meanings differ substantially. The secure textual observations are narrower: one lot belongs to the LORD, the second goat is presented alive, Israel's iniquities are confessed over it, and it is sent into the wilderness.

The command to “afflict your souls” is also not defined within this chapter. Later biblical and Jewish usage associates the observance with fasting, but a reader should not silently replace the chapter's wording with that later specification.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The word "atonement" (Hebrew *kaphar*) recurs insistently through the chapter — applied in turn to the priest, the holy place, the tabernacle, the altar, and the people — binding the passage's ritual actions together through repetition. Blood is sprinkled "seven times" both before the mercy seat and upon the altar (16:14, 19), a formulaic ritual number appearing elsewhere in Israelite cultic law.

The rite also has a pronounced spatial movement: Aaron passes inward through the veil with incense and blood; purification then extends outward to the tabernacle and altar; finally, confessed iniquities are carried outward again, beyond inhabited space, on the scapegoat (16:12-22). The garment change — linen donned for entry behind the veil, left there, then Aaron's ordinary garments resumed before he comes forth (16:4, 23-24) — marks the hidden, innermost portion of the rite as set apart from what precedes and follows it. Describing this movement as a deliberate, formal chiasm would go beyond what the text itself establishes.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why must the holy place and altar receive atonement because of Israel's uncleanness (16:16) — what does this suggest about how ancient Israel understood sin's effect on sacred space? How should the two goats be understood together: as one rite in two parts, or as expressing genuinely different ideas about dealing with sin (cleansing the sanctuary versus removing guilt from the community)? Why must Aaron secure atonement for himself and his household before acting for the congregation (16:6, 11)? What does "afflict your souls" (16:29, 31) require within the chapter itself, and what details — such as fasting — come only from later texts or tradition? Given that this atonement is repeated "once a year" "for ever" (16:34), what does that permanence suggest about how ancient Israel understood the sufficiency of the rite — a question later interpreters such as the author of [[Hebrews 9]] engage directly?
