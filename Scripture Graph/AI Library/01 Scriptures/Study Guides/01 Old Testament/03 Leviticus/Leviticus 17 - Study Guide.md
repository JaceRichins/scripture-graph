# Leviticus 17 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 17]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 17 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Leviticus 17 regulates sanctuary-centered sacrifice, prohibits eating blood, and prescribes cleansing after eating an animal that died by itself or was torn. Its central explanation is that the life of flesh is in the blood and that God has given blood upon the altar for atonement. These requirements repeatedly include resident strangers as well as Israelites. Unauthorized sacrifice and blood consumption lead to being “cut off,” while eating carrion brings uncleanness until evening if the prescribed washing is performed; refusing purification results in bearing iniquity. The chapter does not define the precise mechanism of being “cut off.”
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has four main movements:

1. Verses 1–7 regulate killing oxen, lambs, and goats and explain that sacrifices offered in the open field are to be brought to the tabernacle instead.
2. Verses 8–9 apply the sanctuary requirement for offerings to Israelites and resident strangers.
3. Verses 10–14 prohibit blood consumption, explain the connection between blood and life, and prescribe draining and covering the blood of hunted game.
4. Verses 15–16 prescribe purification after eating carrion.

This outline is clear, but the precise scope of verses 3–4—every slaughter of the listed animals or specifically slaughter associated with sacrifice—is less certain.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Leviticus 17:11 portrays altar blood as a provision assigned by God: “I have given it to you upon the altar to make an atonement for your souls.” The chapter associates blood with life and reserves it from consumption, but it does not give a complete theory of how atonement works.

Later Christian texts connect covenant and sacrificial blood with remission and the work of Christ, making this chapter relevant to [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. That is a later canonical interpretation: Leviticus 17 itself neither names Jesus nor presents verse 11 as an explicit standalone messianic prediction.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several expressions require caution. The KJV's “devils” in verse 7 identifies the recipients of illicit sacrifice but does not explain their nature within this chapter. Likewise, “cut off from among his people” states a serious sanction without specifying here whether it means death, divine judgment, expulsion, loss of covenant standing, or some combination. Conclusions about either expression require evidence beyond the English text of this chapter.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 10-14 form a compact argument: prohibition (v. 10), theological rationale (v. 11), restated prohibition (v. 12), practical rule for hunted animals (v. 13), and expanded restatement of the life rationale and sanction (v. 14). Repetition makes the reason for the command unusually explicit. The recurring inclusion of "the strangers that sojourn among you" also binds together the sections on offerings, blood, hunting, and impurity.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Do verses 3–4 regulate every killing of an ox, lamb, or goat during the wilderness period, or only slaughter intended as sacrifice? Verse 3 sounds broad, while verse 5 explicitly frames the purpose in terms of sacrifices.
- Why does eating blood lead to being cut off, while eating an animal that died naturally produces temporary uncleanness resolved by washing?
- How should [[Deuteronomy 12]]—which permits meat slaughter away from the sanctuary but retains the blood prohibition—be related to this chapter?
- Does the application to resident strangers help explain the inclusion of abstention from blood in [[Acts 15]], or are the social and theological settings too different for a direct derivation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the blood prohibition in [[Genesis 9]], [[Leviticus 7]], and [[Deuteronomy 12]]. Then examine its reception in [[Acts 15]] and the treatment of blood and remission in [[Hebrews 9]]. A careful study should keep three questions separate: what Leviticus 17 explicitly commands, how other biblical writers reuse related language, and what later theological systems infer from those connections.
