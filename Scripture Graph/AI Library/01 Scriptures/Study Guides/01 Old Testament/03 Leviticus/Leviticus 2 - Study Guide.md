## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Fine flour offered uncooked (vv. 1–3):** Oil and frankincense are added; a memorial portion is burned, and Aaron and his sons receive the remainder.
2. **Cooked forms (vv. 4–10):** Oven, pan, and frying-pan preparations follow the same general altar-and-priest division.
3. **Rules concerning ingredients (vv. 11–13):** Leaven and honey may not be burned; firstfruits may be presented without being burned for a sweet savour; salt is required with every offering.
4. **Offering from new grain (vv. 14–16):** Dried and beaten ears are presented with oil and frankincense, and a memorial portion is burned.

Verse 12 and verses 14–16 may concern different kinds of firstfruits; the chapter does not fully explain their relationship.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents worship through regulated gifts rather than indiscriminate destruction: one portion is burned before the Lord, while the remainder is assigned to Aaron and his sons as most holy. This directly connects [[Sacrifice]] with priestly service under the [[Law of Moses]]. Verse 13 also places salt within the language of [[Covenants]], but the passage does not explain whether durability, preservation, shared food, or another association is primary. Symbolic explanations of oil, frankincense, leaven, honey, salt, or the memorial portion should therefore be identified as interpretations rather than textual facts.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
In this chapter, the KJV expression "meat offering" refers contextually to an offering of flour or grain, not animal flesh. Likewise, "corn" in verses 14 and 16 denotes cereal grain in ears and should not automatically be restricted to modern American usage referring specifically to maize. The repeated term "memorial" names the portion burned, but the chapter does not define precisely what or whom it memorializes.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 2–3 and 9–10 form a strong procedural parallel: the priest removes a memorial portion, burns it as a sweet savour, and the remainder belongs to Aaron and his sons as most holy. This repetition helps readers see that the uncooked and cooked variations share one ritual logic. It is good evidence of careful legal organization, though not by itself evidence for a particular author or date.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> The phrase 'salt of the covenant' (v. 13) uses covenant language to describe a ritual ingredient; a close phraseological parallel appears in Numbers 18:19 ('a covenant of salt for ever'), suggesting salt functioned as a recognized symbol of covenant permanence in priestly legal material beyond Leviticus 2 alone.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why is all the frankincense burned while only part of the flour or grain and oil is burned?
- What aspect of covenant does salt signify in verse 13, and how does Numbers 18:19 inform that question?
- How does the permission to present firstfruits in verse 12 qualify the prohibition of verse 11?
- What does calling the priests' remainder "most holy" imply for its handling beyond what this chapter states?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
