# Leviticus 26 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 26]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 26 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Leviticus 26 concludes the main covenant legislation with conditional blessings for obedience (vv. 3–13), escalating judgments for persistent rebellion (vv. 14–39), and a final possibility of confession and covenant remembrance (vv. 40–45). The chapter is therefore not simply a contrast between prosperity and disaster. It moves from [[Obedience]], through discipline and exile, toward [[Repentance]] and continuing covenant mercy.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. Foundational duties: rejection of idols, Sabbath keeping, and sanctuary reverence (vv. 1–2).
2. Blessings for covenant obedience: rain, food, peace, victory, fruitfulness, and divine presence (vv. 3–13).
3. Escalating judgments for refusal to hearken (vv. 14–39). New stages are introduced in vv. 18, 21, 23–24, and 27–28.
4. Confession, humility, and remembrance of covenant and land (vv. 40–45).
5. Concluding attribution of the laws to the Lord through Moses at Sinai (v. 46).

The recurring escalation formulas are clearer evidence of deliberate organization than attempts to force every judgment into an exact numerical scheme.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Covenants]] as a relationship involving commands, communal consequences, divine presence, and divine fidelity. Obedience is associated with life and security in the land, but the text addresses Israel corporately; it should not be converted without qualification into a promise that every obedient individual will always prosper.

Judgment is severe, but it is not the chapter's final word. Confession and humility precede the Lord's remembrance of covenant (vv. 40–42), and even in enemies' lands he promises not to destroy Israel utterly or break the covenant (vv. 44–45). This ending provides a significant scriptural instance of [[Justice and Mercy]] held together rather than treated as mutually exclusive.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The expression "walk contrary" recurs throughout the judgment sequence (vv. 21, 23–24, 27–28, 40–41). In the KJV it describes sustained opposition rather than an isolated mistake. The reciprocal wording—Israel walks contrary to the Lord, and the Lord walks contrary to Israel—intensifies the covenantal character of the conflict.

"Seven times" also recurs (vv. 18, 21, 24, 28). It clearly signals intensification, but the chapter does not require the phrase to denote a mathematically measurable sevenfold increase in every calamity.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The sanctions frequently undo the blessings. Rain and fruitfulness give way to an iron heaven and unproductive earth; peace and victory give way to terror and enemy rule; plentiful bread gives way to rationing without satisfaction; residence in the land gives way to scattering and desolation. These reversals make the curse section a structured negation of the promised covenant order.

The land is portrayed almost as an interested participant: while desolate, it rests and enjoys its Sabbaths (vv. 34–35, 43). This closely joins the chapter to [[Leviticus 25]] and later receives a notable historical-theological echo in [[2 Chronicles 36]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying

- Does remembrance of covenant in vv. 42–45 imply restoration to the land, or does the passage deliberately stop short of stating the form and timing of restoration?
- How does the land's Sabbath connect ecological rest, worship, economic practice, and covenant accountability?
