# Leviticus 23 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 23]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 23 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Leviticus 23 orders Israel's recurring sacred time around the Lord. It begins with the weekly [[Sabbath Day]] and proceeds through observances connected with the first month, a fifty-day harvest count, and the seventh month. These occasions join holy assembly, rest, [[Sacrifice]], agricultural produce, provision for the poor and stranger, atonement, rejoicing, and remembrance of [[Exodus and Deliverance]].

The chapter records obligations under the [[Law of Moses]]. It does not itself describe later Jewish practice or explicitly identify its festivals as predictions of Jesus Christ; those applications require later scriptural interpretation.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. Weekly Sabbath and heading for appointed observances (vv. 1-4)
2. [[Passover]] and Unleavened Bread (vv. 5-8)
3. First harvested sheaf and eating restriction (vv. 9-14)
4. Fifty-day count, loaves, offerings, convocation, and gleaning (vv. 15-22)
5. Seventh-month trumpet memorial (vv. 23-25)
6. Day of Atonement (vv. 26-32; compare [[Leviticus 16]])
7. Tabernacles and the calendar summary (vv. 33-38)
8. Harvest rejoicing, branches, booths, and Exodus remembrance (vv. 39-43)
9. Moses declares the appointed observances to Israel (v. 44)

Verses 37-38 sound like a summary, but verses 39-43 return to Tabernacles and add its agricultural, joyful, and commemorative dimensions.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays sacred time as belonging to the Lord: Israel proclaims the assemblies, but the text calls them the Lord's appointed feasts. Work, food, offerings, communal gathering, penitence, joy, and memory are all brought under covenantal obligation.

The Day of Atonement particularly joins atonement, self-affliction, and complete rest. Other festival days commonly prohibit “servile work,” though the precise legal significance of the differing formulas should not be settled from English wording alone.

Verse 22 places harvest worship beside a requirement to leave field edges and gleanings for the poor and stranger. This strongly supports reading devotion and social provision together in the chapter's harvest setting, although the text does not state why the command was placed here.

Later Christian scripture connects Passover with Christ in [[1 Corinthians 5]] and calls the risen Christ firstfruits in [[1 Corinthians 15]]. The Passover connection is explicit; the firstfruits connection is a strong analogy but is not an explicit identification of the wave-sheaf procedure.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated expression "holy convocation" emphasizes summoned communal assembly. The prohibition varies between "no servile work" on many festival days and the unqualified "no work" or "no manner of work" for the weekly Sabbath and Day of Atonement.

KJV readers should also notice that "meat offering" does not mean an animal offering here. Verse 13 defines its substance as fine flour mingled with oil, and verses 16-17 describe a new offering made with two loaves. Animal sacrifices are listed separately.

The instruction to "afflict your souls" is central to the Day of Atonement, but this chapter does not itself define every practice included in that expression.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repeated speech formulas divide the chapter into units, while recurring dates, counts, holy convocations, work restrictions, offerings, and enduring statutes give it a shared legal and liturgical rhythm. The material is organized primarily by recurring time rather than by type of sacrifice. This strongly establishes the final chapter's function as a ritual calendar, but does not prove that all its materials originated together.

Tabernacles receives the fullest sensory and pedagogical treatment: gathered produce, branches, rejoicing, and temporary booths. The text explicitly assigns intergenerational remembrance to dwelling in booths; it does not expressly give the branches the same mnemonic function.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the weekly Sabbath belong within the appointed-time list, or does it frame the annual observances?
- Which Sabbath is intended by “the morrow after the sabbath” in verses 11 and 15, and how does that affect the fifty-day count?
- What does the placement of the gleaning law contribute to the harvest observance?
- Why do verses 37-38 summarize the feasts before verses 39-43 return to Tabernacles?
- Does the difference between “no servile work” and “no manner of work” mark distinct legal degrees of rest, or reflect varying formulas?
- What does “afflict your souls” require, and which details must be supplied from [[Leviticus 16]] or later practice?
- Which Christian applications are explicit in later scripture, and which are broader analogies?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Exodus 12]] for Passover and Unleavened Bread; [[Leviticus 16]] for the Day of Atonement ritual; and [[Numbers 28]], [[Numbers 29]], and [[Deuteronomy 16]] for complementary festival calendars. Compare the gleaning law with [[Leviticus 19]], [[Deuteronomy 24]], and its narrative realization in [[Ruth 2]]. [[Nehemiah 8]] records a later observance of Tabernacles.

For Christian reception, compare Passover imagery in [[1 Corinthians 5]], firstfruits imagery in [[1 Corinthians 15]], Pentecost in [[Acts 2]], Tabernacles in [[John 7]], and atonement interpretation in [[Hebrews 9]]. These later texts should illuminate reception without being treated as statements explicitly made by Leviticus 23.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
