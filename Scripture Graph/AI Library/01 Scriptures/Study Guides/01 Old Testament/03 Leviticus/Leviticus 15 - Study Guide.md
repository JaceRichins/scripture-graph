# Leviticus 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 15]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Laws, rites, and sacrifices are revealed for cleansing those who have a discharge and other types of uncleanness.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Ongoing male discharge (vv. 1–15):** transferred uncleanness, cleansing after recovery, and eighth-day offerings.
2. **Seminal emission and intercourse (vv. 16–18):** washing or bathing and uncleanness until evening.
3. **Menstruation (vv. 19–24):** seven-day impurity and rules for contact, beds, and seats.
4. **Prolonged female blood discharge (vv. 25–30):** continuing impurity, cleansing after recovery, and eighth-day offerings.
5. **Purpose and summary (vv. 31–33):** protection from death and defilement of the tabernacle.

The prolonged male and female cases have a strong procedural correspondence: both conclude with seven counted days, two birds on the eighth day, a sin offering, a burnt offering, and priestly atonement. They are not identical in every detail; verse 13 expressly prescribes laundering and bathing in running water for the man, while verse 28 does not repeat that formula for the woman.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter does not use “unclean” as a simple synonym for personally sinful. Semen emission, intercourse, and menstruation produce ritual uncleanness without an accusation of wrongdoing or command to repent. Nevertheless, uncleanness is not treated as trivial: verse 31 associates unmanaged impurity with defilement of the tabernacle and danger to Israel.

The sin offerings and priestly atonement after prolonged discharges show that involuntary impurity still has theological significance within the sanctuary system. These terms do not by themselves prove that the bodily condition was a voluntary moral offense, nor does this chapter establish an absolute separation between ritual and moral concerns everywhere in the [[Law of Moses]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV vocabulary—especially "unclean," "sin offering," "atonement," and "flowers"—can sound moralizing or obscure to modern readers. Within the chapter, however, "unclean" functions as a regulated condition that can spread through contact and end through time, washing, recovery, and sacrifice. "Flowers" in verses 24 and 33 refers to menstrual blood in the KJV's idiom. Modern medical diagnoses should not be imposed on the broad phrase "running issue" without additional evidence.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter displays a strong male–female procedural correspondence. The prolonged male discharge in verses 2–15 and prolonged female blood discharge in verses 25–30 both conclude with seven counted days, two birds on the eighth day, a sin offering, a burnt offering, and priestly atonement. Shorter rules for semen emission, intercourse, and menstruation stand between these extended cases.

This correspondence is observable, but its purpose is interpretive. It may reflect deliberate literary organization, practical legal classification, reuse of a standard purification formula, or some combination of these. It does not establish identical rules, equal social burdens, or modern gender equality.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Textual Classification

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why are a sin offering and atonement prescribed after some involuntary bodily issues if ritual uncleanness is not identical with deliberate sin?
- What practical difference does the chapter make between the affected person's status and the shorter uncleanness acquired by touching a person or object?
- How does the sanctuary rationale in verse 31 clarify the purpose of rules that might otherwise be read chiefly as hygiene?
- Where are the procedures genuinely parallel for men and women, and where do their wording or practical effects differ?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Leviticus 11]] and [[Numbers 19]] for contact-transmitted impurity, washing, and time-limited uncleanness. Compare [[Leviticus 12]] and [[Leviticus 14]] for purification periods, priestly presentation, bird offerings, sacrifice, and atonement. [[Mark 5]] describes a woman with a long-term issue of blood and therefore offers relevant later narrative context. The resemblance is useful, but the Gospel does not explicitly quote [[Leviticus 15]] or establish direct literary dependence.
