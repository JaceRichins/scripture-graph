# Leviticus 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 5]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The people are to confess and make amends for their sins—Forgiveness comes through a trespass offering—Priests thereby make an atonement for sin.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Specified cases (vv. 1-4):** withheld testimony, contact impurity, and an oath.
2. **Recognition and confession (v. 5):** the person confesses the particular offense.
3. **Offerings according to ability (vv. 6-13):** livestock, birds, or flour, followed by priestly atonement and forgiveness.
4. **Trespass in holy things (vv. 14-16):** an unblemished ram, compensation, and an added fifth.
5. **Unknown violation of a commandment (vv. 17-19):** ignorance does not erase liability, but atonement and forgiveness remain available.

The renewed formula in verse 14, “And the LORD spake unto Moses,” marks a clear internal transition. The final subject also continues into [[Leviticus 6]], so the modern chapter boundary should not be assumed to define the entire literary unit.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Confession precedes the offering.** Verse 5 explicitly requires the guilty person to confess before the offering procedures are described — for the cases identified in verses 1-4. The chapter does not state this as a rule for every later case (vv14-19), so it should not be generalized beyond its stated scope.

**Guilt can exist apart from full awareness, but the remedy still requires acknowledgment.** Verses 2-4 and 17 describe liability tied to a fact that is hidden or unrecognized; once it is known, the person must confess and bring an offering. This holds together with [[Justice and Mercy]]: liability is not erased by ignorance, yet a remedy is always available. This is cultic/ritual liability language, not a fully worked-out statement about subjective moral guilt.

**Silence can be actionable.** Verse 1 treats a witness's failure to disclose what was 'seen or known' as itself culpable — an omission, not only a wrongful act — tying to [[Honesty]] as a positive duty to speak, not merely to avoid falsehood.

**Restitution accompanies, rather than replaces, atonement.** When holy things are misused, even through ignorance (v15), verse 16 requires repayment plus a fifth in addition to the ram sacrifice.

**The offering is scaled to means.** The threefold sliding scale (vv6-11) keeps the remedy accessible across economic circumstances; this is an observed textual pattern with a plausible but unstated rationale, not itself a declared equity doctrine.

Christian readers may relate these rites typologically to the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], though the chapter itself describes priests, animals, flour, atonement, and forgiveness without naming Jesus Christ.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
In the KJV, “soul” ordinarily denotes the person in these laws, not merely a separable spirit. “Wist it not” in verse 17 means that the person did not know. “Meat offering” in verse 13 is older English terminology for a grain or meal offering; the offering in verses 11-13 is explicitly fine flour. The “fifth part” added in verse 16 is one fifth of the assessed harm.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two forms of repetition mark the chapter's rhetoric, and they are worth keeping distinct. The phrase 'the priest shall make an atonement' recurs five times, closing every offering case (vv6, 10, 13, 16, 18). The explicit promise 'and it shall be forgiven him' recurs four times (vv10, 13, 16, 18) — notably absent from the base lamb/kid case in verse 6, which ends with the atonement clause alone. Mapped correctly, the forgiveness refrain closes the bird offering (v10), the flour offering (v13), the holy-things trespass offering (v16), and the general trespass offering (v18).

The wording 'shall bear his iniquity' appears near the chapter's opening (v1) and again in v17. Whether this is a deliberate literary inclusio framing the whole catalogue, or simply recurring legal diction describing shared liability across cases, cannot be settled from the text alone — v17 is not itself the chapter's end, since the case continues through v19.

A smaller textual detail: the poor person's flour offering (v11) is denied oil and frankincense, ingredients otherwise expected on a standard grain offering (compare [[Leviticus 2|Leviticus 2:1]]). This distinguishes the penitential flour offering procedurally from a freewill grain offering, though the text does not state the reason for the omission.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What legal situation is envisioned by “the voice of swearing” in verse 1, and what obligation did it impose on a witness?
- Does “guilty” in verses 2-3 primarily describe ritual liability, moral blame, or an overlap of the two?
- Why does the bird alternative combine a sin offering with a burnt offering, while the flour alternative uses a single offering?
- Is confession required only for the cases in verses 1-4, or does verse 5 exemplify a broader principle?
- How does restitution in verses 15-16 clarify the relationship between forgiveness and repairing measurable harm?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
