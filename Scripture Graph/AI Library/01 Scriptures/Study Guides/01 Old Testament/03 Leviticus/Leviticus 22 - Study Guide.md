# Leviticus 22 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 22]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 22 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Those of the priests and their families who may eat of the holy things are described—Sacrificial animals are to be perfect and without blemish.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Priests and holy food (22:1–16).** The text addresses temporary uncleanness (vv. 3–9), household eligibility (vv. 10–13), and inadvertent consumption with restitution (vv. 14–16).

2. **Acceptable offerings (22:17–25).** Burnt and peace offerings are generally required to be without blemish. Verse 23 preserves a narrow distinction between freewill and vow offerings.

3. **Timing and treatment of animals and thanksgiving offerings (22:26–30).** A newborn animal becomes eligible from its eighth day; a mother and its young may not both be killed on one day; a thanksgiving sacrifice must be consumed that day.

4. **Theological conclusion (22:31–33).** Commandment keeping, the holiness of the divine name, divine sanctification, and deliverance from Egypt are brought together.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter roots its regulations in God's holiness rather than in human merit: the LORD repeatedly identifies himself as the one who sanctifies the priests and hallows Israel (22:9, 16, 32), and human observance functions as a response bounded by that reality — though the text itself does not state a causal or grace-versus-merit relationship explicitly, and that framing should be read as interpretation, not as the chapter's own stated logic. The law also distinguishes degrees of obligation: a bullock or lamb with something superfluous or lacking may be used for a freewill offering but "for a vow it shall not be accepted" (22:23), suggesting a vow is held to a stricter standard than a voluntary gift. In later Christian and Latter-day Saint reading, the demand for an unblemished, "perfect" offering (22:19-21) is seen as typifying [[Atonement of Jesus Christ|Christ]] as the perfect sacrifice (compare 1 Peter 1:19's "lamb without blemish and without spot"); this is a retrospective theological reading, not a claim the Leviticus text makes about itself, and should be labeled as such.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several English terms in this chapter require caution. "Unclean" (vv3-7) governs temporary access to holy food — resolved by washing and sunset — and should not be read as a judgment of personal wickedness. "Stranger" does double duty: in verses 10 and 25 it denotes an outsider to the priest's household barred from holy food or from supplying an offering, while in verse 18 "strangers in Israel" denotes resident non-Israelites permitted to bring vow and freewill offerings under the same blemish standard as native Israelites (22:25) — the same English word covers two different legal categories, and readers should not assume it refers to one status throughout. Verse 11's household-eligibility rule ("if the priest buy any soul with his money, he shall eat of it, and he that is born in his house") plainly concerns a priest's purchased slave and children born into that household's slavery — an uncomfortable feature of the text's social world that should be named directly rather than folded silently into "household member." The phrase "at your own will" (vv19, 29) should also be checked against other translations and, where possible, the Hebrew before building doctrine about individual agency on the KJV wording alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is bound by two devices: an inclusio on "profane...my holy name" (22:2, 32) and a refrain, "I am the LORD" (with the variant "I the LORD do sanctify them"), that recurs at the close of several legal subunits (22:2,3,8,9,16,30,31,32,33) — though this refrain is common throughout the wider Holiness Code (Leviticus 17-26) and may reflect a conventional formula rather than a frame built specifically for this chapter. The blemish law moves from a general principle (22:20) to an itemized list of defects (22:22) to one narrow exception for freewill offerings limited to bullocks and lambs (22:23), with the prohibition against bruised, crushed, broken, or cut offerings renewed rather than exempted in 22:24-25. The KJV's single word "stranger" does double duty for two different situations — an outsider barred from priestly food (22:10) and a non-Israelite resident permitted to sacrifice (22:18) — a reminder that translation can flatten distinctions present in the underlying legal vocabulary.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Typological/Intertextual Parallel *(tentative)*
> The chapter's repeated insistence that sacrificial animals be free of blemish and 'perfect to be accepted' (22:21) is read in later Christian and Latter-day Saint tradition as typifying Jesus Christ as the unblemished sacrifice, language echoed in 1 Peter 1:19's description of Christ 'as of a lamb without blemish and without spot.'
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · later theological typology, not textual claim

> [!evidence] Internal Literary And Thematic Evidence *(tentative)*
> The repeated concern with profaning or hallowing the divine name, together with repeated declarations that the LORD sanctifies the people, is strong internal evidence that holiness unifies the chapter's otherwise varied food and sacrifice regulations.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · evidence strength overstated per critique

> [!evidence] Lexical/Philological *(tentative)*
> The two different English uses of 'stranger' noted above (22:10 vs. 22:18) likely render two different underlying Hebrew terms common in Levitical cultic law — a non-priest 'outsider' (zar) barred from priestly food, versus a 'resident alien' (ger) permitted to worship alongside native Israelites — a standard distinction in Torah priestly vocabulary, though it cannot be verified against Hebrew from the KJV text alone.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · plausible but unverifiable from KJV alone

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the law demand more of a vow-offering than of a freewill offering (22:23), and what does that imply about how a vow is understood relative to a voluntary gift?
- What is the relationship between Israel hallowing the LORD and the LORD hallowing Israel (22:32)?
- How should the provision restoring a childless widowed or divorced priest's daughter to her father's table (22:13) be understood in light of [[Family]] and household belonging?
- Does the prohibition against killing a mother animal and its young on the same day (22:28) express compassion, symbolic order, protection of herds, or some combination? The text states the rule but not its rationale.
- Does the eighth-day timing for newborn sacrificial animals (22:27) intentionally echo the eighth-day circumcision requirement (Leviticus 12:3), or is this a coincidental shared pattern of a seven-day waiting period?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
