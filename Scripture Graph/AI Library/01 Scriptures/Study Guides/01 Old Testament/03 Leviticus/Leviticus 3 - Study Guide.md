# Leviticus 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 3]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Leviticus 3 regulates the peace offering, using parallel instructions for cattle, sheep, and goats. The worshiper presents and kills the animal; Aaron's sons manipulate the blood and burn the designated portions. Male or female animals are permitted, but herd and flock offerings must be without blemish. The chapter concludes by reserving fat to the LORD and prohibiting the consumption of fat and blood. [[Leviticus 7]] should be read alongside this chapter because it explains the meal, the offering's several occasions, and the portions assigned to priests.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has a clear three-part arrangement:

1. Herd offering (verses 1–5)
2. Lamb offering (verses 6–11)
3. Goat offering (verses 12–16)

Verse 17 concludes with a general statute. The repeated framework highlights both continuity and difference: all three cases share presentation, hand-laying, slaughter, blood application, and burning of internal fat, but the lamb additionally requires the whole rump. This is characteristic of procedural instruction, where repetition prevents ambiguity.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The offering organizes participation in worship rather than assigning every act to priests. The offerer approaches, lays a hand on the animal, and kills it; the priests administer the blood and altar portions. The division illustrates ordered access to sacred worship under the [[Law of Moses]] and the specialized service of the [[Priesthood]].

The declaration that the fat belongs to the LORD marks selected portions as unavailable for ordinary consumption. The blood prohibition is stated but not explained here; [[Leviticus 17]] connects blood with life and altar atonement. That later explanation supplies important context, though it should not be treated as proof that the prohibition had only one purpose.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Peace offering” translates the Hebrew designation often rendered “sacrifice of well-being.” The alternative rendering helps prevent the modern assumption that the rite concerned only the ending of interpersonal conflict; [[Leviticus 7]] associates it with thanksgiving, vows, and voluntary offerings. The wording “food of the offering” concerns what is placed upon the altar and should not be taken, without further evidence, to mean that God requires physical nourishment.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter uses formulaic repetition to present three animal cases. Each case restates a common ritual sequence, while the lamb case adds the whole-rump provision. This repetition makes the procedure easy to follow and preserves species-specific instructions. It is consistent with deliberate procedural shaping, but it is also ordinary legal drafting and could reflect editorial standardization; by itself it establishes neither authorship nor date.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does laying a hand on the animal signify? The chapter requires it three times but does not explain it; identification or designation is safer than assuming an explicit transfer of guilt.
- Why may a peace offering be male or female when the burnt offering in [[Leviticus 1]] specifies a male? The text clearly makes the distinction but does not state its rationale.
- How broad is the prohibition on fat? The immediate context emphasizes designated sacrificial fats; [[Leviticus 7]] is necessary for a fuller answer.
- What became of the meat not burned on the altar? [[Leviticus 7]] supplies the meal and priestly-distribution rules omitted here.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Leviticus 1]] to compare the peace offering with the burnt offering, especially the permitted animals and what is burned. Read [[Leviticus 7]] for the peace offering's thanksgiving, vow, and voluntary forms, its eating deadlines, and priestly portions. Read [[Leviticus 17]] with Deuteronomy 12:23 for the canonical association between blood and life.
