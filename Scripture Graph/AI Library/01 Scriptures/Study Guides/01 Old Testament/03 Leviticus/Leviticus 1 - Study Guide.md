# Leviticus 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Leviticus 1]] · My notes: [[Leviticus 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Leviticus 1 opens with the LORD addressing Moses from the tabernacle and sets out the burnt-offering procedure. Its three cases concern offerings from the herd (vv. 3–9), the flock (vv. 10–13), and birds (vv. 14–17). Each case culminates in burning the offering on the altar as a “sweet savour unto the LORD.” The chapter is central to [[Sacrifice]], the [[Law of Moses]], and the ritual work later associated with the [[Aaronic Priesthood]].

Verse 4 explicitly says that the herd offering makes atonement for the worshipper. Care is nevertheless needed with the official heading's phrase “atonement for sins”: the verse itself does not name a sin, and [[Leviticus 4]] separately introduces explicit sin-offering legislation. Leviticus 1 therefore supports a connection between burnt offering and atonement but does not, by itself, define every function of that atonement.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Divine instruction and permitted animals (vv. 1–2):** Moses receives the law at the tabernacle.
2. **Offering from the herd (vv. 3–9):** presentation, hand placement, slaughter, blood application, division, washing, arrangement, and burning.
3. **Offering from the flock (vv. 10–13):** a closely parallel procedure, with the added direction that slaughter occurs north of the altar.
4. **Offering from birds (vv. 14–17):** the priest adapts the procedure for a turtledove or young pigeon; the crop and feathers are discarded at the ash place.

The repeated conclusions in verses 9, 13, and 17 make the three cases visibly parallel while preserving differences appropriate to each animal.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents atonement as connected to an authorized sacrificial rite rather than to an animal's death in isolation. The offering must be presented in the appointed place, and the blood and altar work belong to the priests. This makes the chapter relevant to [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] in Christian typology, but that later reading should remain distinct from the direct observation that Leviticus 1 itself speaks only of the worshipper, the animal, the priests, the altar, and the LORD.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repetition is the chapter's main literary feature. The herd, flock, and bird sections follow comparable movements and close with nearly the same formula. This repetition emphasizes that the offerings differ in material and handling while sharing one ritual classification and destination.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Textual Pattern
> The availability of herd, flock, and bird offerings is strong internal evidence that the burnt-offering rite could be performed with more than one class of animal. It provides only limited evidence for the further claim that the alternatives constitute a deliberate economic accommodation, because this chapter never states why the options are offered.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation, properly hedged · [[Internal Textual Pattern in Leviticus 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Terminological Distinction
> Leviticus 1 explicitly connects the burnt offering with atonement, but it does not mention a particular sin or call the sacrifice a sin offering. Accordingly, the chapter alone does not establish the stronger summary that these animals are specifically sacrificed as atonement for sins.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted — important corrective to the official chapter heading · [[Terminological Distinction in Leviticus 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Comparative *(tentative)*
> Whether hand-laying signifies transfer of sin/guilt or merely designates legal ownership of the offering is disputed among biblical scholars; Leviticus 1 itself never mentions confession of sin over the animal (contrast the Day of Atonement scapegoat in Leviticus 16, where confession is explicit), which is cited by some scholars (e.g., Jacob Milgrom) as evidence favoring an ownership/designation reading over a sin-transference reading for the burnt offering specifically.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.5 · disputed; scholarly attribution unverified
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What precise function does “atonement” have in verse 4 when no particular sin is named?
- What does hand placement signify here, and which proposed meanings go beyond the chapter's own explanation?
- Are the three animal categories intended as economic accommodation, ritual taxonomy, or both?
- How does the burnt offering differ from the peace offering in [[Leviticus 3]] and the sin offering in [[Leviticus 4]]?
- What theological meaning, if any, should be assigned to the repeated phrase “sweet savour” beyond the observation that it marks the offering as pleasing to the LORD?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
