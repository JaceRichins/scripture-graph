# Malachi 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Malachi 3]] · My notes: [[Malachi 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Lord’s messenger will prepare the way for the Second Coming—The Lord will sit in judgment—The people of Israel are commanded to pay tithes and offerings—They keep a book of remembrance.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The coming Lord and purified worship (vv. 1-5):** A messenger prepares the way; the Lord comes to his temple; Levi is refined; judgment reaches both religious and social wrongdoing.
2. **Return expressed through covenant practice (vv. 6-12):** The Lord's constancy prevents Jacob's destruction. The command to return is answered concretely through tithes and offerings, followed by agrarian and national blessings.
3. **Is serving God worthwhile? (vv. 13-18):** Cynics point to the prosperity of the wicked. Those who fear the Lord respond by speaking together; they are remembered as his possession, and final discernment reverses the apparent moral confusion.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter binds worship to ethics. Purified offerings cannot be separated from judgment against adultery, false witness, withheld wages, and injustice toward the widow, fatherless, and stranger. This makes Malachi 3 significant for [[Tithing]], [[Temples]], [[Law of Moses]], and [[Justice and Mercy]] without reducing covenant faithfulness to any one practice.

The tithing promise should be read in its stated setting. It addresses a nation, a storehouse supplying God's house, crops, vines, and a land visible to other nations. The passage supports trust in divine blessing but does not itself promise that every individual tithe payer will receive immediate financial prosperity.

Verse 6 places divine constancy beside Jacob's continued survival. Here, "I change not" functions particularly as reassurance that judgment has not erased the covenant people. Verse 7 then shows that preservation is an invitation to [[Repentance]], not an endorsement of continued disobedience.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several repeated terms carry the argument. "Return" expresses restored relationship in verse 7 and reappears before the promised discernment in verse 18. "Serve" frames the final dispute: service is called vain in verse 14, honored in the father-son comparison of verse 17, and made the criterion of contrast in verse 18. Speech also divides the groups: words spoken against the Lord in verses 13-15 contrast with the God-fearing conversation heard in verse 16.

The paired images of refiner's fire and fullers' soap communicate severe cleansing through two different crafts: metal purification and cloth cleaning. The Lord is not merely compared to the cleansing agents; verse 3 depicts him sitting as the refiner who supervises the process.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly uses a disputation pattern: the Lord states an accusation or summons, the people answer with a skeptical question, and the Lord specifies the issue. "Wherein shall we return?" leads into tithes and offerings; "Wherein have we robbed thee?" receives the same answer; and "What have we spoken so much against thee?" introduces the complaint that service is unprofitable.

The final movement does not deny the observed success of some wicked people. Instead, it delays the resolution: a book of remembrance is written, the faithful are claimed as the Lord's own, and discernment between righteous and wicked becomes possible "then." This resembles the moral crisis and eventual reorientation in [[Psalm 73]].

[[3 Nephi 24]] gives the chapter a direct Book of Mormon reception history. Doctrine and Covenants 128:24 gives the Levitical offering another Latter-day Saint application by joining it to [[Temples]], [[Baptism for the Dead]], and [[Records and Sacred Writings]]. That later application should be distinguished from Malachi's immediate discussion of purified Levitical worship.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Prophetic And Canonical Interpretation
> Malachi 3:1-5 supplies substantial imagery used in Christian readings of the coming of Christ—arrival of the Lord, testing, purification, temple, and judgment—but the passage itself does not use the phrase 'Second Coming.' Its exclusive identification with that event is therefore an interpretation rather than a textual observation.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · textual imagery accepted; exclusive fulfillment disputed · [[Prophetic And Canonical Interpretation in Malachi 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual Textual Correspondence
> The text of 3 Nephi 24 substantially reproduces Malachi 3 and explicitly presents the material as words given by the Father to Malachi. This is strong evidence of deliberate literary reuse within the Book of Mormon text, but textual correspondence by itself cannot determine the historical mechanism or direction of dependence.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · textual reuse verified; historical mechanism disputed · [[Intertextual Textual Correspondence in Malachi 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Legal-Historical *(tentative)*
> The chapter's ethical indictment list in verse 5 (sorcery, adultery, false swearing, oppression of hired laborers, widows, orphans, and strangers) parallels covenant-law concerns found in the Torah (e.g., protections for widows, orphans, and strangers, and prohibitions on sorcery and false oaths), supporting a reading of Malachi 3 as invoking Mosaic covenant categories rather than introducing novel ethical content.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · broad thematic continuity accepted; dependence unproven
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Why does the judgment list move from explicitly religious offenses to wages and the rights of socially vulnerable people?
2. How does the repeated language of serving God answer the claim that obedience has brought no visible profit?
3. What changes when the tithing promises are read corporately and agriculturally rather than as an individual formula for wealth?
4. Does the book of remembrance function primarily as literal record imagery, a metaphor for divine knowledge, or both?
5. How can later applications in [[Matthew 11]], [[3 Nephi 24]], and Doctrine and Covenants 128 be appreciated without confusing them with the oracle's immediate historical sense?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
