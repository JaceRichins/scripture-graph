# Malachi 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Malachi 4]] · My notes: [[Malachi 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> At the Second Coming, the proud and wicked will be burned as stubble—Elijah will return before that great and dreadful day.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has three compact movements:

1. **The coming day and its two outcomes (vv. 1-3):** burning for the wicked; healing, growth, and vindication for those who fear the Lord.
2. **Remember Moses (v. 4):** Israel is directed back to the law given at Horeb.
3. **Expect Elijah (vv. 5-6):** a prophetic mission of intergenerational turning precedes the great and dreadful day.

The Moses-Elijah sequence gives the ending a backward-and-forward orientation: remember prior revelation while preparing for promised divine action.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents judgment and deliverance as contrasting aspects of the coming day. Preparation includes remembering previously given law (v. 4) and the promised turning of fathers and children (vv. 5-6).

Later scriptures interpret Elijah's mission in distinct ways. [[Luke 1]] applies part of Malachi's heart-turning language to John the Baptist's preparatory ministry. Within Latter-day Saint scripture, [[D&C 2]] expands Moroni's rendering of the prophecy, [[D&C 110]] presents Elijah's Kirtland Temple appearance as its fulfillment, and [[D&C 128]] relates the intergenerational bond to [[Baptism for the Dead]]. These later applications are important to teachings about [[Priesthood Restoration]], but priesthood keys, sealing ordinances, and proxy baptism are not explicitly described in Malachi 4 itself. The texts support a layered LDS reading, but Malachi does not specify a multiple-fulfillment scheme.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter's metaphors move from vegetation to sunrise, livestock, and ash. “Neither root nor branch” intensifies the wicked's loss by including both source and continuation. By contrast, the healing sunrise and growing calves communicate release, vitality, and security. “Great and dreadful” summarizes the chapter's double perspective: the day is not experienced uniformly.

“Sun of righteousness” is grammatically a solar image of saving righteousness. Reading it as a title for Jesus Christ is a meaningful Christian interpretation, especially in a chapter associated with the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]], but the verse itself does not explicitly name Christ.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Malachi's final verses place Moses and Elijah in succession: Israel is directed backward to remembered covenant instruction and forward to promised prophetic preparation. In Christian canonical ordering, where Malachi stands immediately before the New Testament, this sequence also creates a retrospective bridge to Gospel traditions about John the Baptist and Elijah. That bridge effect depends on the Christian arrangement of the books and does not establish Malachi's original compositional intent.

[[Luke 1]] applies Elijah and part of the heart-turning language to John, but replaces Malachi's children-to-fathers clause with a call for the disobedient to turn to the wisdom of the just. [[Matthew 17]] also associates John with an Elijah role. Latter-day Saint scripture adds the narrated personal appearance of Elijah in [[D&C 110]], permitting a layered interpretation without requiring every text to describe precisely the same event.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Redaction Criticism *(tentative)*
> Many critical scholars regard Malachi 4:4-6 (Hebrew 3:22-24) as a later editorial appendix added to conclude Malachi and possibly the whole Book of the Twelve, rather than part of the prophet's original disputation oracles, based on its shift to direct exhortation and its bridging function between Moses and Elijah.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · Recognized but disputed redactional hypothesis

> [!evidence] Intertextual / Typological Fulfillment *(tentative)*
> The New Testament identifies John the Baptist, not a literal reappearance of the ancient prophet, as filling the Malachi 4:5-6 role: Luke 1:17 says John will go before the Lord 'in the spirit and power of Elias, to turn the hearts of the fathers to the children,' and the Synoptic Gospels have Jesus state that 'Elias is come already,' applying it to John (Matthew 11:14; 17:10-13).
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · John's Elijah role broadly accepted; exclusivity disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- In what sense can one event be both healing and dreadful?
- Why does preparation for the day of the Lord include remembering an already-given law?
- Does “turn the heart” primarily denote family reconciliation, renewed covenant loyalty, work for deceased ancestors, or a combination developed across later scripture?
- What changes when Malachi's wording is compared with Moroni's rendering in [[D&C 2]]?
- Which conclusions come directly from Malachi, and which depend on later Christian or Latter-day Saint interpretation?
