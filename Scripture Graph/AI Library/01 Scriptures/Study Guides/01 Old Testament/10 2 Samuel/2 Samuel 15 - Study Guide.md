## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Absalom cultivates allegiance (verses 1–6):** Display, attention to petitioners, criticism of royal administration, and personal gestures win hearts.

2. **The Hebron conspiracy (verses 7–12):** The journey for a vow is followed by spies, a trumpet signal, proclamation, sacrifice, and growing support.

3. **David evacuates Jerusalem (verses 13–23):** David chooses flight, while Ittai rejects permission to leave him.

4. **The ark returns to Jerusalem (verses 24–29):** David conditions his possible restoration on divine favor and positions the priests and their sons in the city.

5. **Mourning, prayer, and counter-counsel (verses 30–37):** David ascends Mount Olivet in grief, prays concerning Ahithophel, and sends Hushai into Jerusalem. [[2 Samuel 17]] continues the contest of counsel.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
David's handling of the ark provides a restrained portrayal of [[Faith]] and [[Humility]]. He hopes that the Lord will restore him but acknowledges that the opposite outcome is possible. This supports a reading of submission rather than presumption, though the text does not say that theology was David's only motive; preserving the ark in Jerusalem and using the priests as an information channel also matter.

Verses 31–36 place [[Prayer]] beside strategic action. David asks that Ahithophel's counsel be frustrated and then commissions Hushai and arranges a communications chain through the priests and their sons. The chapter depicts prayer and planning together, but it neither supplies a universal formula for providence nor explicitly endorses every deceptive tactic involved.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly contrasts appearance, knowledge, and allegiance. Absalom performs accessibility while secretly organizing rebellion; two hundred companions travel without knowing the plan; and Hushai is sent to appear loyal to Absalom while serving David. Information moves through messengers, counselors, priests, and their sons, making control of knowledge an important source of power.

The narrative also reverses the positions of father and son. Absalom advances from Hebron into Jerusalem while David leaves the capital, crosses the Kidron, and moves toward the wilderness. Israel's allegiance shifts toward Absalom, yet Ittai, a recent foreign exile whom David releases from obligation, chooses to remain. The final verse places Absalom and David's covert ally Hushai in Jerusalem together, preparing a struggle conducted through counsel as well as armies.

David's ascent is marked by communal weeping and covered heads; David himself is also barefoot. These gestures clearly express grief and crisis and may additionally suggest humiliation or penitence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Was Absalom exposing a genuine failure in access to royal justice, exploiting a public perception, or doing both? The narrator condemns his method but does not independently audit David's administration.

- Why did Absalom select Hebron? Its place in David's rise gives it possible dynastic significance, but the chapter states no reason.

- Was David's flight driven chiefly by tactical necessity, concern for Jerusalem, or a combination of motives?

- Why did David return the ark while retaining the priests and their sons as an information channel?

- How should Absalom's concealment and David's counterdeception through Hushai be evaluated ethically when the narrator does not provide a general ruling on wartime deception?

- From what event, if any, should the 'forty years' in verse 7 be counted? The chapter itself does not answer, and proposed textual solutions require manuscript evidence.
