# 2 Samuel 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Samuel 3]] · My notes: [[2 Samuel 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The houses of David and Saul engage in a long war—David grows stronger—Abner joins David but is slain by Joab—David mourns for Abner.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Changing balance of power (vv. 1–5):** Saul's house declines while David's house and family expand.
2. **Abner's break with Ish-bosheth (vv. 6–12):** an accusation concerning Rizpah precipitates Abner's decision to support David.
3. **Terms and negotiations (vv. 13–21):** Michal is returned, tribal leaders are consulted, and David sends Abner away in peace.
4. **Joab's retaliation (vv. 22–30):** Joab recalls and kills Abner without David's knowledge.
5. **Mourning and limited kingship (vv. 31–39):** David repudiates the murder, mourns publicly, wins the people's belief in his innocence, and acknowledges his inability to master the sons of Zeruiah.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter offers a sober, non-idealized picture of political power: legitimate succession is negotiated through marriage claims, tribal lobbying, and violence, even as the text affirms that "the LORD hath spoken of David" (v.18) as the ultimate ground of his kingship (see [[Government and Laws]]). Joab's killing of Abner — carried out by secret recall and a private blow at the gate, after David had already received Abner "in peace" — sits uneasily beside the customary logic of the avenger of blood: Asahel was killed in the heat of battle while pursuing Abner (2 Samuel 2:18-23), a circumstance that complicates rather than simply justifies Joab's later act of vengeance. David's public curse on Joab's house and his own admission that he is too weak to act against him (v.39) leave the moral accounting unresolved within the chapter itself (see [[Justice and Mercy]]). The polygamous household catalogued in vv.2-5 (see [[Marriage]]) is recorded without comment; that three of these sons — Amnon, Absalom, and Adonijah — later become sources of David's gravest family conflict is a pattern worth noting, though the text does not draw an explicit causal line from the marriages themselves to those later events.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repetition of “gone in peace” in verses 21–23 sharpens the violation committed when Joab recalls and kills Abner. Political vocabulary also recurs: “house,” “kingdom,” “throne,” “Israel,” “Judah,” “league,” and “king.” Together these terms make the chapter a study in how a kingdom is transferred through family claims, military power, tribal consent, negotiation, and violence.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 9's oath formula, 'So do God to Abner, and more also,' is echoed — though not repeated verbatim — in David's own oath at verse 35 ('so do God to me, and more also'), linking the ambitious general and the anxious king through shared idiom even as their situations invert. Abner's rhetorical self-deprecation, 'Am I a dog's head' (v.8), is a vivid idiom of aggrieved loyalty. The narrative repeatedly separates David's conduct from Joab's: David feasts with Abner and sends him away 'in peace' (repeated in vv.21-23), Joab acts only after leaving David's presence and recalls Abner 'but David knew it not' (v.26), and David responds with a formal lament (vv.33-34, 'Died Abner as a fool dieth?'), public mourning, and a fast. This pattern is best read as the narrator's deliberate characterization of David's innocence rather than as independent proof of it — the text itself states only that the people 'understood... it was not of the king to slay Abner' (v.37), a claim about public perception, not a historical verdict. Whether David's grief was sincere, politically useful, or both is a question the narrative leaves open.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Narrative Characterization
> The repeated peace formula, the statement that David did not know of Joab's recall, David's public repudiation of bloodguilt, his participation in mourning, and the people's resulting conclusion collectively provide strong literary evidence that this narrative seeks to dissociate David from Abner's assassination.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · accepted as narrative characterization, not historical proof · [[Narrative Characterization in 2 Samuel 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Narrative/Rhetorical Function
> The narrator states directly that David's visible mourning (rending clothes, sackcloth, following the bier, fasting until sundown) produced a specific political effect: 'all the people took notice of it, and it pleased them... all the people and all Israel understood that day that it was not of the king to slay Abner.' This shows the mourning functioned (at least in narrative effect) to publicly clear David of complicity, whether or not the grief itself was also sincere.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.7 · accepted observation / motive left open · [[Narrative/Rhetorical Function in 2 Samuel 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intratextual Pattern *(tentative)*
> Ish-bosheth's accusation implies that taking a former king's concubine could be understood as a claim to royal legitimacy or the throne itself, a pattern that recurs elsewhere in Samuel-Kings (Absalom lying with David's concubines in 2 Samuel 16:20-22 as a public claim to the kingdom; Adonijah's request for Abishag in 1 Kings 2:13-25, which Solomon treats as tantamount to a claim on the throne).
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.4 · disputed; intratextual pattern, not confirmed custom

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Did Abner actually take Rizpah, or does the narrative intentionally leave Ish-bosheth's accusation unresolved?
- How much of Abner's change of allegiance arises from recognition of the LORD's purpose, and how much from anger and political self-preservation?
- What does Phaltiel's silent grief contribute to the account of Michal's return?
- Why does David pronounce a curse yet admit that he cannot presently control Joab and Abishai?
- Does the people's approval validate David's integrity, his political skill, or both?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
