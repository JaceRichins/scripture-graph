## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves through four scenes: (1) campaign and encounter, vv. 1–5 — the army departs for Rabbah, David remains, sees and takes Bathsheba, and she conceives; (2) failed concealment, vv. 6–13 — David twice tries to send Uriah home, first directly and then after making him drunk, but Uriah stays with the king's servants both times; (3) the engineered death, vv. 14–25 — David's letter is carried by Uriah himself, Joab positions him where defenders are strongest, and Uriah dies along with other soldiers; (4) aftermath, vv. 26–27 — Bathsheba mourns, then becomes David's wife, and the narrator supplies the chapter's only explicit moral verdict. Verses 1 and 27 loosely frame the chapter (David's absence from the field at the start, divine displeasure at the close), though this is better read as narrative framing than a formal literary inclusio.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter traces how sin compounds: adultery leads to deception, deception to conspiracy, and conspiracy to murder, with collateral deaths beyond the intended victim (11:17). Uriah — identified throughout as "the Hittite" — keeps a stricter personal loyalty to the ark, his comrades, and his king than David keeps faith with [[Marriage]] and [[Government and Laws]]; this contrast is the reader's inference rather than a stated authorial judgment. Notably, the chapter never shows David repenting — that becomes explicit only after Nathan's rebuke in [[2 Samuel 12]]. The unadorned close, "displeased the LORD," withholds any softening of the verdict.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
A repeated use of "sent" structures the narrative: David sends Joab and the army, sends for Bathsheba, sends for Uriah, and sends the fatal letter; Joab then sends the battle report. The pattern underscores how much David accomplishes through others while remaining physically removed from the field. Uriah's oath, "as thou livest, and as thy soul liveth, I will not do this thing" (11:11), uses phrasing similar to Elisha's refusal formula in [[2 Kings 2]], though this may simply reflect a shared Hebrew oath idiom rather than deliberate allusion. Joab's coaching of the messenger (11:19–21) is an explicit in-story recollection of Abimelech's death at Thebez in [[Judges 9]], invoked to blunt anticipated royal criticism. Separately, [[1 Chronicles 20]] parallels this chapter's opening verse about the Rabbah campaign but omits the entire Bathsheba–Uriah narrative — a notable editorial silence, though its motive is not established by the text alone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the narrator emphasize that this was the season kings went to battle, yet David stayed in Jerusalem — is this a specific criticism, scene-setting, or both? What does Uriah's reference to the ark contribute to his refusal — was it present in the field literally, or is this rhetorical shorthand for Israel's wartime posture? Does the notice that Bathsheba "was purified from her uncleanness" (11:4) bear on the child's paternity, or is it incidental ritual detail? The text never narrates Bathsheba's consent, refusal, or perspective at any point — what can responsibly be inferred, and what cannot, about the power imbalance between a king and a subject's wife he summons? Does "wash thy feet" (11:8) carry a euphemistic sense, or is it a literal courtesy — the text does not disambiguate. And is the Eliam of 11:3 the same man named in [[2 Samuel 23]] as Ahithophel's son — a connection that, if true, would raise questions about Ahithophel's later motives but that the text nowhere confirms?
