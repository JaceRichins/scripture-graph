# 2 Samuel 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Samuel 5]] · My notes: [[2 Samuel 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
2 Samuel 5 completes David's rise to power. After ruling Judah alone from Hebron, "all the tribes of Israel" come to David, and the elders make a league with him "before the LORD" before anointing him king over all Israel (vv.1-5), followed immediately by a regnal summary (age thirty at accession, forty-year reign). David then captures the Jebusite stronghold of [[Zion]] and makes it his capital, the city of David, with building support from Hiram of Tyre (vv.6-12). The chapter records, without moral comment, that David took more wives and concubines and lists sons born to him in Jerusalem, including Solomon (vv.13-16). Finally, the Philistines mobilize against the newly anointed king, and David defeats them in two campaigns in the valley of Rephaim, each time first inquiring of the LORD and receiving a different tactical answer (vv.17-25).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Recognition and covenant (vv. 1–5):** Kinship, demonstrated leadership, and a reported divine commission support David's anointing.

2. **Capital and royal house (vv. 6–12):** Jerusalem is captured, fortified, and supplied with a palace through Hiram's assistance.

3. **Dynastic expansion (vv. 13–16):** Additional wives, concubines, and children are recorded without explicit moral comment.

4. **Threat, inquiry, and victory (vv. 17–25):** Two similar Philistine attacks receive different divine tactical answers.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
David's kingship is grounded in covenant: the elders make a league with him "before the LORD" before anointing him (v.3), and David understands his kingdom as exalted "for his people Israel's sake" (v.12) rather than for his own sake — themes relevant to [[Covenants]] and [[Government and Laws]]. The charge that he would "feed my people Israel" (v.2) casts kingship in shepherding language; later prophetic and Christian tradition develops shepherd-king imagery further, but that extension goes beyond what this chapter itself asserts. David's practice of inquiring of the LORD before each Philistine battle (vv.19, 23) — receiving a different answer each time, and then acting "as the LORD had commanded him" (v.25) — ties the chapter to [[Revelation]] and [[Obedience]], and cautions against treating divine guidance as a fixed, reusable formula.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The commission to “feed” Israel uses the language of shepherding for royal governance, while “captain” emphasizes leadership. Together the terms unite care for the people with responsibility to lead them. David's comparison of victory to a breach of waters explains the naming of Baal-perazim within the narrative. The difficult wording about “the blind and the lame” in verses 6 and 8 should not be generalized into a doctrine about disability without resolving its immediate polemical and textual problems.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The two Philistine episodes (vv.17-21, 22-25) form a repetition-with-variation pattern: the enemy gathers in the same valley, David inquires of the LORD, receives a command, and wins — but the command itself changes, from a direct advance the first time to a flanking maneuver cued by a sound in the treetops the second. This arrangement resists reading divine guidance as a fixed formula. Separately, the account of capturing Jerusalem (vv.6, 8) contains one of the most difficult cruxes in Samuel: the exact sense of "the gutter" and the taunt about "the blind and the lame" remains genuinely disputed. Notably, the parallel account in [[1 Chronicles 11]] omits this material and instead credits Joab with leading the assault — a reminder that Samuel and Chronicles, while narrating the same core events, are not identical texts.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Pattern
> The repeated inquiry-command-action pattern is strong internal literary evidence that the chapter portrays David's victories as dependent upon receiving and following situation-specific divine direction.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-supported internal pattern, honestly hedged · [[Internal Narrative Pattern in 2 Samuel 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Source-Critical Comparison *(tentative)*
> The parallel account of this same capture of Jerusalem in 1 Chronicles omits the 'blind and the lame' exchange found in 2 Samuel 5:6,8 and instead credits Joab with going up first and thereby becoming chief — the two accounts of the same event preserve materially different secondary details while agreeing on the core outcome (the Jebusite fortress falls and becomes 'the city of David').
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed interpretation of a real textual difference

> [!evidence] Archaeological Correlation *(tentative)*
> Some archaeologists (notably Eilat Mazar) have proposed that a 'Large Stone Structure' excavated in the City of David corresponds to the palace/fortification building referenced in 2 Samuel 5:9,11, while others (notably Israel Finkelstein) dispute the dating and scale of tenth-century Jerusalem, arguing it was too small to be the capital of an extensive united monarchy at this time.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.3 · disputed, real debate but no citations

> [!evidence] Historical Chronology / Extrabiblical Synchronism *(tentative)*
> Some scholars note a chronological tension between the biblical picture of Hiram of Tyre as a contemporary ally across both David's and Solomon's reigns and reconstructions of Tyrian regnal chronology preserved through Josephus (citing Menander of Ephesus/Dius), which some argue fit better with a Hiram whose reign centers on the Solomonic era.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.3 · unverifiable as stated

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How do kinship, demonstrated leadership, a reported divine commission, covenant, and anointing (vv.1-3) together establish legitimate kingship?
- What does it mean that David's kingdom was exalted "for his people Israel's sake" (v.12), and what does that suggest about the purpose of political authority?
- Why does David inquire of the LORD again before the second Philistine battle instead of repeating his first, successful strategy?
- What is being described by "the blind and the lame" in verses 6 and 8 — a taunt, a proverb, something else? Which conclusions should stay open given how difficult the passage is?
- How should David's expanding household in Jerusalem (vv.13-16) be read alongside the law in [[Deuteronomy 17]] that Israel's king should not multiply wives?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the accession and Jerusalem account with [[1 Chronicles 11]], and the palace and battle material with [[1 Chronicles 14]]. Read [[1 Samuel 16]] and [[2 Samuel 2]] to distinguish David's earlier anointings from his recognition by all Israel here. Compare the report of additional wives with [[Deuteronomy 17]], taking care not to mistake the narrator's lack of immediate comment for moral approval.
