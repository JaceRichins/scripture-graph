## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The parable and David's verdict (vv. 1-6).** A rich man takes a poor family's only lamb rather than one of his own; David, unaware he is the target, demands death and fourfold restitution.
2. **The prophetic indictment (vv. 7-12).** Nathan identifies David as the man, recounts what God had given him, names his specific offenses (killing Uriah, taking his wife), and pronounces two judgments: ongoing violence within David's house and public disgrace.
3. **Confession, pardon, and loss (vv. 13-23).** David confesses; Nathan announces that his sin is put away and that he will not die — but the child becomes ill and dies despite David's fasting and prayer.
4. **Solomon and Jedidiah (vv. 24-25).** Bath-sheba bears Solomon; the LORD's love for him is stated explicitly, and Nathan conveys the name Jedidiah.
5. **The capture of Rabbah (vv. 26-31).** Joab summons David so the city's capture is credited to the king, and the chapter closes with the spoil and a disputed description of the treatment of Rabbah's inhabitants.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
This chapter is a primary text for [[Repentance and Forgiveness]]: David's confession is met with an immediate pronouncement that his sin is "put away," yet severe consequences continue to unfold. The text holds both together without fully explaining why — it does not supply a complete philosophical account of why the child suffers for David's act, and readers should resist the temptation to manufacture a tidy resolution the passage itself does not offer. This same tension bears directly on [[Justice and Mercy]]: David is spared, while an innocent child dies in connection with his sin.

David's own explanation of his fasting (vv. 22-23) presents [[Fasting]] and [[Prayer]] as earnest, non-coercive petition — he fasted while hoping God might yet be gracious, and stopped once the outcome was fixed. His closing words, "I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me," are genuinely ambiguous: they may express hope of eventual reunion, or simply acknowledge that David too will one day die. The verse alone does not settle which.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Nathan's parable functions as a trap of moral recognition: David judges from a safe distance before Nathan collapses that distance with "Thou art the man." The contrast between the rich man's abundance and the poor man's one beloved lamb exposes not just theft but the absence of pity — and David's self-imposed sentence (fourfold restitution) happens to match the penalty [[Exodus 22]] prescribes for a stolen and slaughtered sheep, without the text ever framing this as a deliberate citation. The chapter also plays on public versus private: David acted secretly, but Nathan warns the consequence will be public, "before all Israel, and before the sun" — language later echoed when Absalom's actions play out publicly in [[2 Samuel 16]].

Two details in the closing verses are worth flagging as genuinely disputed rather than resolved: the crown's weight in verse 30 ("a talent of gold") is heavier than a head could plausibly bear, and the Hebrew consonants translated "their king" are identical to the name of the Ammonite god Milcom, leading some to read "the crown of Milcom." Likewise, the treatment of Rabbah's people in verse 31 ("under saws... under harrows of iron... through the brickkiln") is lexically ambiguous — it has been read as either forced labor or execution/torture, and the Hebrew alone does not decide the question.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Translation Ambiguity *(tentative)*
> The KJV rendering of verse 31, that David 'put them under saws, and under harrows of iron, and under axes of iron, and made them pass through the brickkiln,' is ambiguous in the underlying Hebrew and has been translated by some modern versions as forced labor (setting the Ammonites to work with saws, iron picks, and axes, or at brickmaking) rather than as mass execution or torture.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.5 · disputed translation, unnamed sources

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism *(tentative)*
> The phrase describing the captured king's crown, 'a talent of gold with the precious stones,' set on David's head, is a well-known textual crux: a talent of gold (roughly 75-90 pounds/34-41 kg) is generally regarded as too heavy to be worn as a literal headpiece, and the underlying Hebrew consonants for 'their king' (malkam) are identical to the proper name of the Ammonite god Milcom, so some scholars read the phrase as 'the crown of Milcom' rather than 'their king's crown.'
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, uncited

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- If Nathan says David's sin is forgiven and he "shalt not die," in what sense is the death of the child still a consequence of David's act? Does the text resolve this tension, or leave it genuinely open?
- David sentences the fictional offender in the parable to death as well as fourfold restitution — before learning he is judging himself. Is this narrative irony, or simply how the legal case is framed?
- Does "I shall go to him, but he shall not return to me" (v. 23) express hope of reunion after death, or only recognition that David too will die? The verse alone does not settle it.
- What does David's reasoning in verses 22-23 suggest about the purpose of fasting — a plea made while an outcome is still uncertain, rather than a means of reversing a decree already given?
- Given the real ambiguity in the Hebrew of verse 31, how much should the harshest reading (execution or torture) be assumed as historical fact rather than treated as one of two live translation options?
- Why might the Chronicler have left this entire episode out of his account of the Rabbah campaign (1 Chronicles 20:1-3)? What does that comparison suggest about the different purposes of Samuel and Chronicles as historical works?
