# 2 Samuel 24 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Samuel 24]] · My notes: [[2 Samuel 24 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David sins in numbering Israel and Judah—The men of war total 1,300,000—The Lord destroys 70,000 men by pestilence—David sees an angel, offers sacrifice, and the plague is stayed.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves in four movements:
1. **The census (vv. 1-9)** — David orders a count of Israel and Judah; Joab objects but complies; the survey takes nine months and twenty days and totals 1,300,000 fighting men.
2. **Confession and choice (vv. 10-14)** — David's heart smites him and he confesses unprompted; Gad offers three possible judgments, and David chooses to fall into the LORD's hand rather than man's.
3. **Judgment and mercy (vv. 15-17)** — pestilence kills 70,000; the LORD commands the destroying angel to stay its hand at the threshingfloor of Araunah; David pleads that judgment fall on him and his house rather than the people, calling them sheep.
4. **Purchase and altar (vv. 18-25)** — David buys Araunah's threshingfloor and oxen at full price, builds an altar, offers sacrifice, and the plague is stayed.

The phrase "from Dan even to Beer-sheba" appears both at the start of the census (v. 2) and in the death toll of the plague (v. 15), verbally tying the scope of the count to the scope of its consequence — though the same phrase is a stock idiom for "all Israel" elsewhere in scripture, so this may be conventional geography as much as deliberate literary design.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter is important for [[Sin]], [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], and [[Justice and Mercy]], but it resists a tidy resolution of how they relate. Divine action in verse 1 does not erase David's confession in verse 10, and the death of seventy thousand people is not reduced to a simple lesson — David himself protests, "these sheep, what have they done?" (v. 17).

God offers David an actual choice among three consequences rather than simply imposing one, and David's reasoning — trusting the LORD's mercy over the hand of man (v. 14) — frames [[Justice and Mercy]] as intertwined rather than opposed.

David's refusal to offer the LORD "burnt offerings... of that which doth cost me nothing" (v. 24) is a clear, specific teaching about [[Sacrifice]]: in this episode, an offering that costs the giver nothing does not count as an offering. This should not be over-generalized into a universal rule about all acceptable sacrifice, but it is a direct and quotable teaching within this text.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV statement that the LORD "repented him of the evil" in verse 16 describes a reversal or cessation of the announced destruction within the narrative. The wording should not automatically be expanded into claims that God committed moral evil or had previously made an intellectual mistake; the verse itself does not define the metaphysics of divine repentance. Araunah is called a Jebusite, while [[1 Chronicles 21]] uses the name Ornan for the corresponding landowner.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Many scholars treat 2 Samuel 21-24 as a non-chronological appendix closing out the book, so this event should not be assumed to be the last thing that happened in David's reign — though it could also be read as a deliberately arranged epilogue rather than a purely displaced fragment.

The shepherd-king image in verse 17 ("these sheep, what have they done?") recalls David's own origin as a literal shepherd and reframes kingship as a duty of protection rather than command. The angel's location "by the threshingplace of Araunah the Jebusite" (v. 16) quietly sets up the chapter's final scene and, through the later naming in 2 Chronicles 3:1, the site's significance as the future Temple location — though 2 Samuel itself never uses the name Moriah, and that identification depends entirely on Chronicles, not on anything within this chapter.

The chapter mentions the plague's end twice: the LORD's command to the angel, "It is enough: stay now thine hand" (v. 16), and the later statement that "the plague was stayed" after the altar and offerings (v. 25). These can be read as two connected moments in one resolution, though a simpler reading is that verse 25 is a summary recap of the same reprieve rather than a second, separate cessation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs

> The parallel accounts use different immediate attributions for the impulse to conduct the census: 2 Samuel says the LORD moved David, whereas 1 Chronicles says Satan provoked David. This is strong evidence of distinct narrative or theological framing, but the texts do not explain whether they intend competing or complementary levels of causation.

> [!evidence] Textual/Theological Comparison

> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-documented Samuel/Chronicles divergence · [[Textual/Theological Comparison in 2 Samuel 24|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary-Critical / Redaction-Critical Observation *(tentative)*
> 2 Samuel 21-24 is widely treated by scholars as a non-chronological appendix inserted at the end of the book, interrupting the succession narrative that resumes with the opening of 1 Kings — meaning this chapter's placement at the very end of 2 Samuel is likely an editorial/compositional choice rather than a claim that it happened last in David's life.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · common but uncited scholarly claim

> [!evidence] Intertextual Legal Background *(tentative)*
> Exodus 30:11-16 commands that whenever Israel is numbered, each man must pay a 'ransom' so 'there be no plague among them' — the very consequence (plague) that follows David's census in this chapter, suggesting the narrative may assume that census-specific atonement law as background, even though 2 Samuel 24 never cites it directly.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.5 · minority interpretation, plausible background

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the text say the LORD's anger was already "kindled against Israel" before the census (v. 1) — what prior offense, if any, is presupposed?
- What exactly made this census sinful? The chapter confirms David's guilt but never specifies whether the offense was pride, reliance on military strength, unauthorized purpose, or (per Exodus 30:12) a failure to pay a prescribed ransom when numbering the people.
- How should readers relate 2 Samuel 24:1 ("the LORD... moved David") to David's own confession of guilt in verse 10, and to the different attribution in [[1 Chronicles 21]] ("Satan... provoked David")?
- Why do 70,000 people suffer for an act David identifies as his own sin? The text does not resolve this; David himself raises the question in verse 17.
- Does the later identification of this site with "mount Moriah" (2 Chronicles 3:1) reflect a genuine historical memory connecting it to Genesis 22's Akedah, or a later theological naming?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[1 Chronicles 21]] alongside this chapter and note rather than smooth over the differences: who incites the census, the population totals, the famine's length, and the price David pays for the site are not reported identically. [[Exodus 30]] (the census-ransom law) and [[Numbers 1]] (a divinely commanded census) are useful background and contrast texts, though neither is cited directly within 2 Samuel 24. For the site's later significance, compare 2 Chronicles 3:1, which identifies it as the location prepared for Solomon's [[Temples|Temple]].
