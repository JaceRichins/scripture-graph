# 2 Samuel 20 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Samuel 20]] · My notes: [[2 Samuel 20 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Sheba leads the tribes of Israel away from David—Joab slays Amasa and pursues Sheba—A wise woman intercedes—The death of Sheba ends the insurrection.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Renewed rebellion (20:1-2):** Sheba, a Benjamite, rejects David's dynasty; Israel follows him while Judah remains with David.
2. **Consequences and mobilization (20:3-7):** David secludes the ten concubines Absalom had taken; Amasa misses his three-day deadline to muster Judah, and David sends Abishai after Sheba.
3. **Joab eliminates Amasa (20:8-13):** A feigned greeting conceals a fatal sword thrust; Amasa's body briefly halts the pursuing army until it is moved off the road.
4. **Siege and negotiation (20:14-22):** Joab's forces besiege Abel of Beth-maachah, but a wise woman narrows the conflict to Sheba alone and preserves the city.
5. **Administrative roster (20:23-26):** The chapter closes with a list of David's officials, echoing the earlier list in [[2 Samuel 8]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The wise woman's appeal treats Abel as part of "the inheritance of the LORD" (20:19), placing a limit on purely military reasoning — attacking an Israelite city is not merely a tactical matter. Yet the chapter records no divine command, prophetic oracle, or narratorial verdict on the killings of Amasa or Sheba; their deaths should not automatically be read as endorsed. The chapter's mix of civil war, summary killing without a narrated trial, and restored administration invites study under [[Government and Laws]], [[War and Peace]], and [[Justice and Mercy]].

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Sheba is introduced as "a man of Belial" (20:1), an idiom applied elsewhere to worthless or dangerous men — the sons of Eli, the men of Gibeah, and Nabal — signaling from the outset how the narrator wants Sheba judged, even though the real Israel-Judah tension he exploits (see [[2 Samuel 19]]) may reflect genuine grievance.

The proverb the wise woman cites in v.18 ("they shall surely ask counsel at Abel") is one of the more textually difficult lines in Samuel; its exact sense is debated among translators, but it seems to invoke Abel's reputation as a place where disputes were traditionally settled by seeking counsel rather than by force.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The rebellion is framed by paired trumpet calls: Sheba blows one to call Israel away from David (20:1), and Joab blows one to end the pursuit after Sheba's death (20:22). This creates a tidy narrative bracket, though Joab is shown elsewhere in Samuel ending pursuits with a trumpet call, so the pairing may reflect a reused military convention rather than a device built specifically for this chapter.

A second contrast runs between force and speech: Joab's army batters Abel's wall, but the wise woman's negotiation ends the siege before the wall falls — a counterpart to the wise woman of Tekoa in [[2 Samuel 14]], though the two episodes resolve differently (one preserves a city by surrendering a single man, the other secures a prince's return).

The description of Amasa struck "in the fifth rib" (20:10) repeats the exact wording used for Joab's killing of Abner in [[2 Samuel 3]], underscoring a deliberate resemblance between Joab's two treacherous, close-range killings of rivals. The same phrase appears for Asahel's death in [[2 Samuel 2]], though Asahel died in open pursuit rather than by treachery, so that connection is looser.

Some scholars have noted that the near-duplicate officials lists bracketing chapters 9-20 (2 Samuel 8:16-18 and 20:23-26) may mark the boundaries of an originally distinct "Court History" source; this is a disputed literary hypothesis, not a settled conclusion.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary/Intertextual *(tentative)*
> Sheba's cry to 'tents' as a rejection of the house of David verbally anticipates the language used by the ten tribes when the kingdom permanently splits under Rehoboam, suggesting the editors/narrator frame Sheba's revolt as a preview of the later schism.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · accepted verbal echo / disputed literary intent

> [!evidence] Literary Framing *(tentative)*
> The paired trumpet calls and return-to-tents formulas provide strong internal evidence that the rebellion account is narratively framed: Sheba's trumpet initiates dispersal from David, while Joab's trumpet ends the pursuit after Sheba's death.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · observation solid / 'strong evidence' of deliberate framing overstated

> [!evidence] Narrative Consequence *(tentative)*
> David places the ten concubines Absalom had publicly violated (2 Samuel 16:22) into lifelong seclusion, 'living in widowhood' though their husband is alive — a consequence tied to, but not identical with, Nathan's earlier judgment against David.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · narrative consequence accepted / causal link to Nathan's oracle disputed

> [!evidence] Source Criticism / Literary Structure *(tentative)*
> Biblical scholars (following Leonhard Rost's influential source-critical study) have long noted that the near-duplicate lists of officials in 2 Samuel 8:16-18 and 2 Samuel 20:23-26 bracket the intervening chapters, and have proposed this as a literary seam marking the boundaries of an originally distinct 'Court History' or 'Succession Narrative' source.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does David entrust the army to Amasa — recently Absalom's general — rather than reinstating Joab (see [[2 Samuel 19]]), and how should Amasa's fatal delay (20:5) be weighed in that decision?
- The text gives no stated motive for Joab's killing of Amasa; is rivalry over command the likeliest explanation, or does the narrative deliberately withhold judgment?
- Is the wise woman's solution — the negotiated death of one man to spare a city, with no narrated hearing — presented as straightforwardly righteous, or does the text leave room for discomfort with the bargain?
- Why does Joab remain "over all the host of Israel" (20:23) immediately after killing the commander David had appointed?
- What does the wise woman's appeal assume about the obligations owed to an Israelite city as part of "the inheritance of the LORD" (20:19)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
