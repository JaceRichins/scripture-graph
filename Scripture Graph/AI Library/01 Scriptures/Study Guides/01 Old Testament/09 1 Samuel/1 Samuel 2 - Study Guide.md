# 1 Samuel 2 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Samuel 2]] · My notes: [[1 Samuel 2 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Hannah sings praises to the Lord—Samuel ministers before the Lord—Eli blesses Elkanah and Hannah, and they have sons and daughters—The sons of Eli reject the Lord and live in wickedness—The Lord rejects the house of Eli.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Hannah's prayer (vv. 1–10):** Praise, warning against pride, reversals of human status, and anticipation of the LORD's king.
2. **Contrasting households (vv. 11–26):** The misconduct of Eli's sons is interleaved with Samuel's ministry, Hannah's continued care, and Samuel's growth.
3. **Oracle against Eli's house (vv. 27–36):** An unnamed man of God recalls priestly election, identifies Eli's failure, announces a confirming sign, and promises a faithful priest.

The alternation between the sons' corruption and Samuel's growth makes the contrast cumulative rather than incidental.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents sacred office as accountable stewardship, not immunity from judgment. Eli's sons turn [[Sacrifice]] into an occasion for coercion, and Eli is condemned despite rebuking them because he continues to honor his sons above God. In this setting, [[Priesthood]] faithfulness includes both proper worship and responsible governance.

Hannah's prayer also rejects confidence in unaided strength: the LORD weighs actions, overturns human rankings, and guards his faithful ones. The chapter does not teach that every present inequality will immediately reverse, but it depicts divine judgment as capable of overturning entrenched power.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated image of the **horn** frames Hannah's prayer. In verse 1 it represents Hannah's God-given strength or dignity; in verse 10 it is transferred to the LORD's anointed. The movement from one delivered worshipper to the future king broadens the prayer from personal thanksgiving to national hope.

The saying that the LORD is a God of knowledge and that actions are weighed connects speech, conduct, and judgment. This is immediately relevant to proud claims, but the remainder of the chapter illustrates it through the conduct of priests whose public position conceals no wrongdoing from God.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Hannah's song is built from balanced opposites: bows broken and the stumbling strengthened, the full hungry and the hungry satisfied, the barren fruitful and the fruitful diminished, the poor raised and the powerful brought low. Psalm 113:7–8 closely parallels the dust-and-dunghill imagery, while Luke 1:46–55 develops a similar pattern of divine reversal.

Two growth notices about Samuel punctuate the account of priestly decline. The second—favor with the LORD and people—also closely parallels Luke 2:52. These connections make Samuel's childhood portrayal an important biblical pattern for faithful growth without requiring that every later parallel be treated as a direct prediction.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Explicit Canonical Fulfillment Claim
> The later removal of Abiathar from the priesthood is explicitly interpreted by the Kings narrator as fulfilling the LORD's word concerning Eli's house at Shiloh. This shows that the canonical narrative understands the judgment as extending beyond the deaths of Eli's immediate sons.

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Fulfillment

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Fulfillment
> The oracle's prediction that Hophni and Phinehas would 'die both of them' 'in one day' (2:34) is presented within the book as fulfilled at 1 Samuel 4:11, giving this chapter's prophecy an explicit, checkable fulfillment later in the same narrative.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · explicit internal narrative fulfillment · [[Internal Narrative Fulfillment in 1 Samuel 2|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Cross-Reference / Typological Identification *(tentative)*
> The oracle's promise that the LORD will 'raise... up a faithful priest' and 'build him a sure house' (2:35) is later connected by the text of 1 Kings (2:27, 35) to Solomon's removal of Abiathar and installation of Zadok, which 1 Kings frames as fulfilling 'the word of the LORD... concerning the house of Eli.' This identification is made by the later book's narrator, not stated within 1 Samuel 2 itself, so alternative readings (a generic future faithful priesthood, or later typological application) remain live.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · dynastic connection accepted; Zadok identification inferred

> [!evidence] Internal Textual Tension / Composition History *(tentative)*
> Hannah's song closes by asking the LORD to give strength to 'his king' and exalt the horn of 'his anointed' (2:10), even though no Israelite king exists yet in the surrounding narrative — Saul is not anointed until 1 Samuel 9-10. This royal language sits in tension with the song's stated narrative setting in Hannah's individual thanksgiving for a child.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.9 · textual observation accepted; compositional implications disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrative emphasize that the priests' conduct made other people abhor the offering, rather than describing their offense as merely private misconduct?
- What practical action could Eli have taken beyond verbal rebuke, and how does the oracle's accusation clarify his responsibility?
- Does verse 25 describe judicial hardening after persistent rebellion, or should its causal wording be understood another way?
- Is the faithful priest of verse 35 best identified with Samuel, Zadok, a faithful priestly line, or an ideal figure whose significance exceeds one individual?
- How does the reference to a king and anointed one function before Israel's monarchy is introduced in the narrative?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[1 Samuel 1]] for Hannah's vow and Samuel's dedication; [[1 Samuel 4]] for the deaths of Hophni and Phinehas; and [[1 Kings 2]] for the narrator's connection between Abiathar's removal and the judgment on Eli's house. Compare Leviticus 3:16 and 7:28–34 for the fat and priestly-portion regulations underlying the priests' offense; Deuteronomy 18:3 for another description of priestly portions; Psalm 113:7–8 for the dust-and-dunghill imagery; and Luke 1–2 for later songs and growth notices resembling Hannah's prayer and Samuel's childhood. These comparisons establish meaningful literary and legal context, but they do not by themselves determine direction of literary dependence, composition dates, or supernatural foreknowledge.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
