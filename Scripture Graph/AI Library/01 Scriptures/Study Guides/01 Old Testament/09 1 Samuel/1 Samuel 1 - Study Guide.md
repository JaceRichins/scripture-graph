## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Setting and recurring conflict (verses 1–8):** Elkanah's household travels annually to Shiloh; Hannah's childlessness and Peninnah's provocation produce repeated grief.

2. **Prayer, misunderstanding, and blessing (verses 9–18):** Hannah makes a lifelong vow, Eli mistakes her silent prayer for drunkenness, and then blesses her after hearing her explanation.

3. **Birth and naming (verses 19–20):** The LORD remembers Hannah, and Samuel is conceived, born, and named.

4. **Delay and fulfillment (verses 21–28):** Hannah nurses Samuel until weaning and then presents him at Shiloh with an offering.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays [[Prayer]] as candid disclosure before God: Hannah does not conceal her complaint or grief, describing her act as pouring out her soul. Her vow connects prayer with accountable action; after receiving what she requested, she bears the considerable cost of fulfilling her promise. This makes the chapter relevant to [[Covenants]], [[Sacrifice]], and [[Adversity]], while not implying that vows compel divine action.

The story also places private anguish inside communal worship at Shiloh. Its use of both "house" and "temple" for that sanctuary makes it relevant to [[Temples]], but those English terms alone do not determine the building's precise form.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two theological-language notes are worth flagging with care. The repeated statement that the LORD had "shut up her womb" (1 Samuel 1:5-6) is this narrative's own explanation for Hannah's condition; it is not presented as a universal diagnostic statement about infertility. Likewise "the LORD remembered her" (1 Samuel 1:19) is the hinge between petition and conception — within the story, remembering is manifested through action and reversal, not a claim that God had literally forgotten Hannah. The KJV's "lent him to the LORD" (1:28) is immediately qualified by "as long as he liveth," so whatever nuance "lent" carries, the verse describes a lifelong rather than temporary dedication.

Two further points are genuinely disputed rather than settled. First, "a worthy portion" (1:5), describing what Elkanah gave Hannah, translates a difficult Hebrew phrase; some modern translations render it "a double portion," which would change the picture of Elkanah's favoritism, though the underlying textual question is not resolved here. Second, the narrator's naming note in 1:20 ("Because I have asked him of the LORD") is best read as wordplay on the repeated verb "ask" (vv. 17, 20, 27-28) rather than a strict etymology of "Samuel" itself — the Hebrew root for "ask" (sha'al) more directly underlies the name Saul, which is why some scholars think this naming tradition may echo, or once have been attached to, a Saul birth story. That redactional question is unsettled and should not be treated as established.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repetition gives the chapter much of its force. "Year by year" (1:7) marks Hannah's suffering as prolonged rather than momentary, and the chapter's repeated language of asking and petition ties Samuel's birth directly to Hannah's appeal. There is also a reversal in perception: Eli first sees only moving lips and wrongly infers drunkenness; after Hannah explains herself, he recognizes her petition and blesses her (1:12-17) — the episode quietly distinguishes observable behavior from reliable knowledge of another person's inner state.

Hannah's vow that "there shall no razor come upon his head" (1:11) closely echoes the Nazirite law of [[Numbers 6]] and the prenatal dedication of Samson in [[Judges 13]]. The verbal overlap is close enough to be worth noting, though 1 Samuel 1 never uses the word "Nazirite" and does not repeat every restriction found in Numbers 6, so "Nazirite-style consecration" is a fair description while a formal Nazirite classification is not demonstrated.

The chapter also introduces "the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas" (1:3) in wording nearly repeated when they reappear at the ark's capture in [[1 Samuel 4]]. That repetition may simply be the narrator's conventional way of identifying the same two priests; it is worth watching for as a literary echo, not treated as proof of deliberate foreshadowing.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrator preserve Eli's mistaken accusation instead of moving directly from Hannah's prayer to his blessing?

- What explains Hannah's changed countenance in verse 18: confidence in God, relief at being heard by Eli, resolution produced by prayer, or some combination? The text records the change but does not explicitly explain it.

- Does the no-razor clause alone establish formal Nazirite status, or does the absence of other requirements from [[Numbers 6]] counsel restraint?

- What does Hannah's return to fulfill a costly vow contribute to the chapter beyond the report of Samuel's birth?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the no-razor provision with [[Numbers 6]] and the prenatal dedication in [[Judges 13]]. The similarities support a real thematic connection, but the differences should remain visible: Hannah initiates her vow, whereas Samson's status is announced by an angel.

Compare the broader childlessness-and-birth pattern with [[Genesis 21]] and [[Luke 1]]. Then read [[1 Samuel 2]] to see how Hannah's personal reversal becomes the basis for a wider meditation on the LORD's power to humble and exalt.
