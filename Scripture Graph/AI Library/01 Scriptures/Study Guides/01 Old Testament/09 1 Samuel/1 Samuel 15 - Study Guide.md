# 1 Samuel 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Samuel 15]] · My notes: [[1 Samuel 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter narrates Saul's selective execution of a command against Amalek and Samuel's declaration that Saul is rejected as king. Its best-known principle—"to obey is better than sacrifice"—arises from Saul's attempt to present prohibited spoil as material for worship (1 Samuel 15:15, 21–23). The account also contains severe collective violence, including an explicit command concerning infants (1 Samuel 15:3); responsible study should acknowledge that moral difficulty rather than letting the chapter's lesson about [[Obedience]] obscure it.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Commission and campaign (vv. 1–9):** The command is given, the Kenites are warned, and Saul defeats Amalek but preserves Agag and valuable animals.
2. **Divine response and confrontation (vv. 10–23):** Samuel grieves, Saul claims compliance, and the surviving animals expose selective obedience.
3. **Confession and rejection (vv. 24–31):** Saul admits sin, requests Samuel's public presence, and receives an enacted sign in the torn mantle.
4. **Agag's death and final separation (vv. 32–35):** Samuel executes Agag; Samuel and Saul part, though Samuel continues mourning.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's most quoted teaching is that obedience to revealed command outweighs the outward performance of [[Sacrifice]] (v. 22): Samuel does not condemn sacrifice generally, but rejects Saul's attempt to use intended sacrifice as compensation for withholding what had already been commanded destroyed. Samuel compares rebellion and stubbornness against revealed word to witchcraft and idolatry (v. 23), tying Saul's loss of kingship to his rejection of 'the word of the LORD' rather than to any single tactical failure. The narrative also depicts kingship as accountable to [[Revelation]] and [[Government and Laws]] rather than exempt from it. Saul's confession ('I have sinned,' vv. 24, 30) invites study of [[Repentance]]: he admits fault, yet his simultaneous request for public honor before Israel's elders leaves his motives genuinely ambiguous rather than settled. Any devotional use of this chapter's obedience lesson should also reckon with the severity of the command itself, which explicitly named infants among those to be destroyed (v. 3) — a moral difficulty the text does not resolve.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Saul's self-description shifts across the confrontation: he first claims full compliance ('I have performed the commandment of the LORD,' v. 13), then attributes the retained spoil to 'the people' (vv. 15, 21) even though v. 9 had assigned the decision jointly to 'Saul and the people,' and finally confesses that he 'feared the people, and obeyed their voice' (v. 24) rather than the LORD's. Samuel's verdict is delivered in similar terms three times (vv. 23, 26, 28) and then made visible when the torn mantle becomes an enacted sign of the kingdom being torn from Saul (vv. 27-28). The chapter also juxtaposes the LORD 'repenting' of having made Saul king (vv. 11, 35) with Samuel's declaration that 'the Strength of Israel will not lie nor repent: for he is not a man, that he should repent' (v. 29); a plausible reading distinguishes grief over the appointment from the irrevocability of the verdict, but the chapter does not state that distinction explicitly, and the tension is best presented as genuinely open.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Textual/Theological Tension *(tentative)*
> The chapter contains an internal tension: the narrator twice states the LORD 'repented' of making Saul king (15:11, 15:35), while Samuel's oracle in the same chapter declares 'the Strength of Israel will not lie nor repent: for he is not a man, that he should repent' (15:29). This juxtaposition is a recognized interpretive crux regarding how the Hebrew Bible speaks of divine change relative to divine constancy.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What distinguishes Saul's claim 'I have performed the commandment' (v. 13) from actual obedience, and where else in scripture does partial compliance masquerade as obedience?
- How should readers hold together the LORD's stated 'repenting' over Saul (vv. 11, 35) with the assertion that 'the Strength of Israel... is not a man, that he should repent' (v. 29) within the same chapter?
- Does Saul's appeal to sacrifice reflect self-deception, blame-shifting, or a genuine but mistaken religious rationale, and what does his request for public honor (v. 30) suggest about his confession?
- How should the command's explicit inclusion of infants (v. 3) be weighed alongside the individualized rationale later given for Agag's execution (v. 33), which the text never supplies for the infants?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
