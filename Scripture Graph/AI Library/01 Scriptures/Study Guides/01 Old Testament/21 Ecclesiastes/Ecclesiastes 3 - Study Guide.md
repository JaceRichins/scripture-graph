# Ecclesiastes 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ecclesiastes 3]] · My notes: [[Ecclesiastes 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> To every thing there is a season—Whatever God does, it will be forever—God will judge the righteous and the wicked.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Times and opposites (vv. 1–8):** Verse 1 introduces the theme, followed by fourteen contrasting pairs covering birth and death, grief and celebration, acquisition and loss, speech and silence, and war and peace.
2. **Labor, divine work, and human limits (vv. 9–15):** The speaker questions the profit of labor, acknowledges humanity's inability to comprehend God's work from beginning to end, and identifies enjoyment and doing good as fitting responses.
3. **Injustice, judgment, and mortality (vv. 16–22):** Corruption appears where justice should prevail. The speaker affirms divine judgment, compares the death of humans and animals, asks about the spirit's destination, and returns to enjoyment of one's work.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verse 17 directly supports [[Final Judgment]] by affirming that God will judge the righteous and the wicked, but this chapter does not specify when or how that judgment occurs. Verses 19–21 are relevant to [[Death and the Spirit World]] because they address death, dust, breath or spirit, and possible destinations; verse 21, however, is a question rather than a complete doctrine of the afterlife. [[Ecclesiastes 12]] offers an important later statement about dust returning to earth and spirit returning to God, but it should not erase the uncertainty expressed here.

Verses 12–13 and 22 commend rejoicing, doing good, and enjoying the good of one's labor. That enjoyment is called God's gift. This relates secondarily to [[Work and Self-Reliance]], but the chapter does not teach that wealth, productivity, or occupational success measures divine favor.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
In the KJV, “time” occurs 29 times in verses 1–8: once in the introductory verse and four times in each of verses 2–8. This regularity belongs to the English form of the poem and should not be treated by itself as evidence of hidden numerology.

The KJV phrase “set the world in their heart” in verse 11 is translation-sensitive; many translations use language such as “eternity.” Because the underlying expression can affect interpretation, conclusions about an implanted awareness of eternity—or any specific doctrine—require source-language and translation study beyond the English wording alone. Verse 21 likewise requires care because “spirit,” “breath,” and “wind” may overlap in the underlying language.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The opening poem is constructed from fourteen antithetical pairs. Their cumulative effect presents a broad range of life through opposites: birth and death, planting and uprooting, grief and celebration, acquisition and loss, silence and speech, love and hate, and [[War and Peace]]. The regular antitheses are strong evidence of poetic organization, but the fact that fourteen equals twice seven does not by itself establish symbolic numerical design.

Verse 17 echoes verse 1's language of “a time” for “every purpose,” now adding “every work” in the setting of judgment. This verbal return links the observation of institutional injustice with the chapter's larger treatment of time and humanity's limited view. A proposed progression from private to national life is possible, but the intervening pairs do not sustain it consistently.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does saying that an action has a “time” merely describe what occurs, or can it ever authorize the action morally? What additional principles would be needed before applying “a time to kill” or “a time to hate”?
- How does the confidence that God will judge in verse 17 interact with the question “Who knoweth” in verse 21?
- Is the counsel to enjoy labor an affirmative reception of ordinary gifts, a modest response to uncertainty, resigned acceptance, or some combination of these?
- How does [[Ecclesiastes 12]] clarify the dust-and-spirit imagery without erasing the interrogative form of Ecclesiastes 3:21?
- How should the translation-sensitive phrase “set the world in their heart” be investigated before drawing doctrinal conclusions from it?
- Does “a time to rend, and a time to sew” evoke mourning customs, ordinary cloth-work, or a broader contrast between separation and restoration?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the return-to-dust formula with [[Genesis 3]], and compare the chapter's counsel about eating, drinking, labor, and enjoyment with Ecclesiastes 2:24–26; 5:18–20; and 8:15. [[Ecclesiastes 12]] returns directly to both dust and spirit. [[James 4]] provides a later wisdom parallel about uncertainty concerning the future and dependence on divine will. These comparisons illuminate recurring themes but do not by themselves establish literary dependence or identical theological aims.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
