# Joel 1 — Study Guide

## Overview

> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Call a solemn assembly and gather to the house of the Lord, for the day of the Lord is at hand.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Superscription (v. 1):** The word of the LORD comes to Joel son of Pethuel.
2. **A disaster to remember (vv. 2–4):** The inhabitants must hear, and successive generations must retell the extraordinary, cumulative devastation.
3. **Expanding lament (vv. 5–12):** Drinkers, priests, farmers, and vinedressers mourn as vines, grain, oil, fruit trees, and human joy fail; the destroyer is depicted as an innumerable nation with lionlike teeth.
4. **Communal summons (vv. 13–14):** Priests are to lament, sanctify a fast, call an assembly, and gather the inhabitants at God's house.
5. **The approaching day and the cry (vv. 15–20):** The day of the LORD frames the crisis; a first-person voice cries to God, and even the beasts are portrayed as crying.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins material life and sacred worship. Failed harvests bring hunger and economic loss while also leaving the grain and drink offerings unavailable at the house of God (vv. 9, 13), linking the crisis to [[Sacrifice]] and [[Temples]]. The commanded response is communal: priests, elders, and inhabitants gather, fast, lament, and cry to the LORD (vv. 13–14), supporting the practices of [[Fasting]] and [[Prayer]]. Because Joel 1 neither names a particular offense nor explicitly commands repentance, it does not justify confidently assigning a specific moral cause to this disaster—or, by extension, to every disaster.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV expression “meat offering” in verses 9 and 13 means a grain or meal offering, as illustrated by [[Leviticus 2]], not animal meat in the modern sense. The chapter repeatedly uses vocabulary of depletion—“cut off,” “wasted,” “dried up,” “languisheth,” “perished,” “withered,” and “desolate”—to make scarcity the dominant verbal texture.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The poem creates a sense of totality through accumulation. One devourer consumes what another leaves (v. 4); successive groups are summoned to mourn (vv. 5, 11, 13); and the catalogue expands from cultivated plants to stored seed, livestock, rivers, wilderness pasture, and trees (vv. 10–20). Personification binds the losses together: the land mourns, joy withers, beasts groan, and beasts cry to God. A cry motif moves from the commanded communal cry (v. 14), through a first-person cry that may be prophetic, exemplary, or corporate (v. 19), to the animals' cry (v. 20).

Joel 1:15 closely parallels [[Isaiah 13]]: both announce that the day of the LORD is at hand and will come as destruction from the Almighty. The verbal relationship is strong, but the wording alone cannot determine priority, direct borrowing, date, or whether both texts draw on an existing prophetic formula. [[2 Nephi 23]] preserves the Isaiah wording and is therefore a related, not independent, witness.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Verbal Intertextual Parallel
> Joel 1:15 and Isaiah 13:6 contain a near-verbatim sequence about howling because the day of the LORD is at hand and will come as destruction from the Almighty. This strongly supports shared prophetic wording or literary relationship, but direction of dependence cannot be determined from the parallel alone.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · strong verified parallel; direction of dependence disputed · [[Verbal Intertextual Parallel in Joel 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Lexical/Translation Uncertainty *(tentative)*
> Whether the four Hebrew terms rendered 'palmerworm, locust, cankerworm, caterpiller' denote four distinct insect species, four successive developmental stages of one locust species, or four successive locust swarms/waves is disputed among Hebrew lexicographers and commentators; the KJV translation choices reflect one set of guesses about obscure terms.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · plausible lexical uncertainty; documentation absent

> [!evidence] Genre/Interpretation Dispute *(tentative)*
> Scholars are divided on whether Joel 1 describes a literal locust plague and drought (using military metaphor to intensify the description) or uses locust imagery as an extended metaphor for an actual invading human army; the sustained, concrete horticultural vocabulary across vv.10-20 (vine, fig, corn, barley, oil, cattle, sheep) is often cited as favoring a literal-plague reading, while the 'nation... teeth of a lion' language in vv.6-7 keeps a martial reading alive.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · interpretive alternatives preserved; scholarship uncited

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Dating Dispute *(tentative)*
> Because Joel 1:1 lacks a royal synchronism and the book never names a king, scholars have proposed dates for Joel ranging from roughly the 9th century BC to the post-exilic period (5th-4th century BC); nothing internal to chapter 1 resolves this.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.7 · scholarly dispute asserted without sources

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the “nation” of verse 6 personify the swarm, describe a human army, or intentionally merge the two images?
- Are the fire and flame in verses 19–20 literal wildfire, imagery for severe heat and drought, or part of a compound disaster?
- Is the present catastrophe identified with the day of the LORD, treated as its beginning, or used as a warning of something still approaching?
- Why does Joel make intergenerational retelling the first commanded response before calling for lament and assembly?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Joel's locust devastation and command to transmit its memory with [[Exodus 10]], while noting that the generational sequences are not identical and do not establish direct borrowing. Read [[1 Kings 8]], especially verses 37–40, for a temple-prayer setting that names famine, locust, and caterpillar. Deuteronomy 28:38–40 supplies covenantal background for losses of grain, wine, and oil, although Joel 1 itself does not explicitly call the disaster a covenant curse. Compare Joel 1:15 with [[Isaiah 13]] and [[2 Nephi 23]], then examine Amos 5:18–20 and Zephaniah 1:7 for other prophetic uses of “the day of the LORD.”
