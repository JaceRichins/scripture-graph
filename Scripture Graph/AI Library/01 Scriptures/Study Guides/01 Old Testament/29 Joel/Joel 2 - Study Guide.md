## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Alarm and advancing army (vv. 1-11):** trumpet, invasion, fire, trembling creation, and the terrible day of the LORD.
2. **Call to return (vv. 12-17):** wholehearted repentance, [[Fasting]], total communal assembly, and priestly intercession.
3. **Divine answer and restoration (vv. 18-27):** pity, removal of the threat, renewed rain and crops, restored years, praise, and recognition of God's presence.
4. **Spirit, signs, and deliverance (vv. 28-32):** prophecy across social boundaries, cosmic wonders, and salvation for those who call on the LORD.

The repeated trumpet commands in verses 1 and 15 mark the crucial transition: the first announces danger; the second organizes a covenant community's response.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Wholehearted and communal [[Repentance]].** Joel commands the people to rend their hearts rather than merely their garments, but he does not reject outward practice: fasting, mourning, assembly, and priestly intercession remain integral to the response. The appeal rests on the LORD's gracious and merciful character, while the question “Who knoweth” resists treating mercy as an entitlement.

**Restoration and divine presence.** Renewed land, food, worship, and honor answer the earlier devastation, but the restoration oracle culminates in Israel knowing that the LORD is present and that he alone is their God (v. 27).

**The Spirit broadly poured out.** Verses 28-29 include sons and daughters, old and young, servants and handmaids in the promised outpouring. The groups receive prophecy, dreams, and visions, although Joel does not explicitly assign every manifestation to every group. [[Acts 2]] applies the passage to Pentecost. [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] gives it an additional future horizon in Latter-day Saint reception, supporting a layered reading without establishing a complete chronology.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter uses dense military and destructive imagery without unambiguously identifying the opening force. It appears like horses, moves with the noise of chariots, advances in ordered ranks, crosses walls, and enters houses (vv. 4-9); verse 25 later calls the locust destroyers the LORD's “great army.” These features permit locust, human-invasion, and fused readings, but do not settle the referent.

A broader pattern of reversal links the devastation and restoration units. Eden-like fertility becomes wilderness (v. 3), while wilderness pastures later spring again (v. 22). Fire, scarcity, fear, reproach, and shame give way to rain, grain, wine, oil, gladness, satisfaction, praise, and restored years. The repeated trumpet commands also mark a transition: verse 1 sounds an alarm, while verse 15 summons the community to fasting and assembly.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Reception And Fulfillment
> Canonical reception supplies substantial evidence for a layered Latter-day Saint reading of Joel 2:28-32: Acts applies the prophecy to Pentecost, while Joseph Smith—History reports that Moroni cited the same passage and located its fulfillment in the future relative to 1823. This supports an initial-and-further fulfillment model but does not by itself specify which details belong to each stage.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Reception data accepted; fulfillment model remains interpretive · [[Canonical Reception And Fulfillment in Joel 2|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual Formula
> The description of God in Joel 2:13 as 'gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repenteth him of the evil' closely echoes the same divine-attribute formula found in Jonah 4:2 and Nehemiah 9:17, both of which are flagged as verified text-overlap parallels for this chapter. This is a recognized recurring liturgical formula in the Hebrew Bible (rooted ultimately in the Exodus 34:6-7 self-revelation of God), reused by multiple later biblical authors.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the opening army represent locusts, human invaders, or a poetic fusion of both?
- How does “rend your heart, and not your garments” correct empty ritual without dismissing [[Fasting]] and solemn assembly?
- Which features of Joel 2:28-32 does [[Acts 2]] present as fulfilled at Pentecost, and which may retain a future horizon?
- Why does the chapter place material restoration before the declaration that the LORD is present in Israel?
- How do the two kinds of calling in verse 32—people calling on the LORD and the LORD calling a remnant—belong together?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Joel 2:13 with [[Exodus 34]] and [[Jonah 4]] for the recurring description of God's mercy. Read Joel 2:28-32 beside [[Acts 2]], Romans 10:12-13, and [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] to examine how later scripture receives the prophecy. Compare the day-of-the-LORD imagery with [[Zephaniah 1]] and [[Joel 3]]. A lexical study of the underlying Hebrew of Joel 2:23 is also worthwhile because interpretations differ over whether its first phrase refers only to early rain or may carry an additional sense involving a teacher or instruction associated with righteousness.
