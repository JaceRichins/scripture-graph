# Isaiah 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 6]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Isaiah 6 records the prophet's throne-room vision, dated to "the year that king Uzziah died" (traditionally ~740 BC, though the chapter itself gives no absolute year). Isaiah sees the Lord enthroned in the temple, attended by seraphim crying a threefold "Holy, holy, holy." Overwhelmed by his own uncleanness, Isaiah is symbolically purified by a coal from the altar, then accepts a difficult commission — "Here am I; send me" — to a people who will not perceive, ending in the land's desolation and the survival of a "holy seed." The chapter does not itself name Jesus Christ; the official heading's reading of the hardening oracle as the Jews' rejection of Christ's teaching reflects the New Testament's own later application of these verses (see [[Matthew 13]]; [[Acts 28]]). The chapter is reproduced almost verbatim in [[2 Nephi 16]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Theophany** (vv. 1-4): the Lord enthroned, the seraphim's cry, the shaking temple.
2. **Confession and cleansing** (vv. 5-7): Isaiah's self-disqualification ("unclean lips") met by the altar coal.
3. **Call and commission** (v. 8): "Whom shall I send... Here am I; send me."
4. **Content of the commission** (vv. 9-13): a hard message of hardening and judgment, closing with the difficult "holy seed" image — note the returning tenth is itself said to be "eaten" before the final note of continuity.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Prophetic calling**: a divine summons met with willing response ("Here am I; send me"), part of the larger pattern of being called to serve seen throughout [[Prophets]].
- **Confession and cleansing from sin**: Isaiah's recognition of his own uncleanness precedes his commissioning; the text ties forgiveness (v. 7) to fitness for service, a theme worth reading alongside [[Repentance and Forgiveness]].
- **Agency and consequence**: the hardening commission (vv. 9-10) is genuinely disputed — it can be read as God's direct act or as a description of the predictable result of persistent unbelief; the text does not resolve this. See [[Agency]].
- **Covenant remnant**: v. 13 anticipates that judgment will not be total, though the surviving tenth is itself "eaten" before the closing "holy seed" image — a hopeful but genuinely ambiguous verse, connected to later [[Gathering of Israel]] theology.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"Seraphims" (vv. 2, 6) does not otherwise occur in the Hebrew Bible; the KJV form is a double plural (Hebrew plural *seraphim* + English *-s*). "Train" (v. 1) refers to the flowing hem of a robe, not a procession. "Teil tree" (v. 13) is an archaic English term usually identified with the terebinth, though the underlying botanical identification is uncertain. The singular "Whom shall I send" followed by the plural "who will go for us?" (v. 8) is a genuine shift; a heavenly-council idiom is the leading explanation, though the verse alone does not identify who is included in "us" and should not be read as settling any doctrine of the Godhead.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The vision uses concentrated numerical patterning: six wings in three paired functions, and a threefold "holy." The chapter repeatedly links body parts to perception and speech: seraphim cover face and feet, Isaiah's unclean lips are touched and purged, and the people he is sent to have eyes, ears, and hearts that fail to perceive — his cleansed mouth becomes an instrument of proclamation against the audience's resistance. The scene has the character of a heavenly throne-room theophany, a genre with parallels elsewhere in scripture, though the specific call-narrative sequence sometimes proposed by scholars is not followed rigidly here (Isaiah's self-disqualification precedes rather than follows the commission).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean that Isaiah's uncleanness is located specifically in his "lips," given that his call is to speak?
- Is the hardening of the people's hearts (vv. 9-10) an active divine act, a judicial confirmation, or a description of the predictable result of persistent unbelief? The text does not settle this.
- What temple is envisioned — an earthly sanctuary, a heavenly throne room, or both at once?
- Does the "holy seed" in v. 13 promise renewal, or does it simply name what remains after repeated devastation, given that the returning tenth is itself "eaten"?
- Who is included in the divine "us" of v. 8?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
