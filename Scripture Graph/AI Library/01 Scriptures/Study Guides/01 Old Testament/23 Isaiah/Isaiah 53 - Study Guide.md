## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful four-part reading is:

1. **Unrecognized appearance and rejection (vv. 1-3):** the report is not believed, and the figure lacks the expected marks of attractiveness or status.
2. **The speakers' corrected confession (vv. 4-6):** they had considered him divinely stricken but now say that he bore their griefs and iniquities.
3. **Silent oppression, death, and burial (vv. 7-9):** lamb and sheep imagery emphasizes silence; the text also asserts his innocence.
4. **Purpose and vindication (vv. 10-12):** his soul is an offering for sin; he justifies many, receives an exalted portion, and intercedes for transgressors.

This outline is interpretive rather than a formally marked division. The larger poem likely begins with the exaltation language in [[Isaiah 52]], so studying Isaiah 52:13-15 alongside this chapter prevents the chapter boundary from obscuring the full movement.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's central doctrinal pattern is not suffering in isolation but suffering **for others**. The repeated exchange—his wounds for their transgressions, his chastisement for their peace, his stripes for their healing—provides strong textual grounding for a representative and substitutionary dimension of [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. Verse 10 adds the language of [[Sacrifice]] by calling his soul an offering for sin; verse 11 links his work to justification; and verse 12 joins sin-bearing with intercession.

The text should not be forced into only one later theory of atonement. It includes several effects at once: bearing, healing, peace, justification, intercession, and victory. [[Matthew 8]] notably applies the bearing of griefs to healing, while [[1 Peter 2]] applies the chapter to sin-bearing and righteous conduct under suffering.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The poem is built on reversals. The one thought stricken by God is disclosed as suffering for the speakers; the despised figure is called righteous; the silent victim becomes the one who justifies many; and death and grave give way to prolonged days and triumph. Repetition of first-person plural terms in verses 3-6 turns the speakers from detached observers into implicated confessors.

Animal imagery develops the contrast: the people are sheep who wander voluntarily, while the servant is like a lamb led silently to slaughter. The chapter also shifts perspective. A communal "we" dominates the confession, third-person narration describes the servant, and divine ownership appears in "my righteous servant." These shifts make recognition and reassessment part of the poem's argument.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Early Reception And Intertextual Evidence
> The independent New Testament uses of Isaiah 53 are strong evidence that first-century Christians interpreted the chapter through the suffering, death, healing ministry, and redemptive work of Jesus. They establish early Christian reception of the chapter more directly than they establish the exclusive meaning intended in Isaiah's original historical setting.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted reception-history conclusion · [[Early Reception And Intertextual Evidence in Isaiah 53|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Dating Dispute *(tentative)*
> Mainstream critical scholarship commonly attributes Isaiah chapters 40-55 (including chapter 53) to an anonymous prophetic voice ('Deutero-Isaiah') writing during or near the end of the Babylonian exile in the mid-6th century BC, after Lehi's traditional departure from Jerusalem around 600 BC. Because the Book of Mormon presents this chapter as already inscribed on the brass plates before that departure (quoted by Abinadi in Mosiah 14), this dating question is discussed by both critics and Latter-day Saint scholars as a point of tension for the Book of Mormon's historical claims.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · prevailing critical dating versus minority alternatives

> [!evidence] Textual Variant *(tentative)*
> In Isaiah 53:11, the Masoretic Text underlying the KJV reads 'he shall see of the travail of his soul, and shall be satisfied,' without a stated object for 'see.' Some ancient witnesses (the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran and the Greek Septuagint) are reported to add 'light,' yielding 'he shall see light... and be satisfied,' a reading adopted by several modern translations.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · plausible textual variant requiring apparatus verification

> [!evidence] Textual-Interpretive Dispute *(tentative)*
> The identity of 'the Servant' in Isaiah 53 is genuinely disputed among biblical scholars. While Christian and Latter-day Saint readings identify an individual Messiah fulfilled in Jesus Christ, much Jewish exegesis and a strand of critical scholarship identifies the servant as collective Israel (consistent with an explicit 'Israel' identification for the servant in another Servant Song, Isaiah 49:3), a righteous remnant, or an unnamed historical figure.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · genuine scholarly dispute; supporting survey uncited

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the repeated contrast between “he” and “we/us” shape the chapter's account of representative suffering?
- How might an individual, a righteous remnant, or corporate Israel each fit the distinction between the servant and “my people” in verse 8?
- What readings besides resurrection could account for the movement from death and burial to seeing seed, prolonged days, and receiving a portion among the great?
- How do healing, peace, justification, sin-bearing, sacrifice, and intercession complicate attempts to reduce the chapter to a single theory of [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]?
- What do the uses of Isaiah 53 in [[Matthew 8]], [[John 12]], [[Acts 8]], [[Luke 22]], and [[1 Peter 2]] establish about early Christian reception, and what do they leave unresolved about the passage's original referent?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
