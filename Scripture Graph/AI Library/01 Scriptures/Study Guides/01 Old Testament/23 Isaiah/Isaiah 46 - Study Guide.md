## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **Verses 1–4 — Two kinds of carrying:** defeated idols burden animals; God carries and delivers Israel.

- **Verses 5–7 — The manufactured god:** precious metal becomes an immobile object that cannot answer or save.

- **Verses 8–11 — Remembered acts and effective counsel:** Israel is commanded to remember, while God claims both to declare and to accomplish his purpose.

- **Verses 12–13 — Near salvation:** the stouthearted are addressed, and righteousness and salvation are promised to Zion.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Divine providence across a lifetime**: vv.3-4 frame God's care for covenant Israel ('house of Jacob... the remnant of the house of Israel,' v.3) as continuous from origin ('the belly... the womb') to old age ('hoar hairs') — a relationship of carrying rather than being carried, contrasted with idols that must be carried by others.
- **Monotheism / incomparability of God**: vv.5, 9 assert directly that no comparison or equal exists — 'I am God, and there is none else... none like me.'
- **[[Prophecy]] and divine sovereignty**: vv.9-11 ground God's uniqueness in His claim both to declare 'the end from the beginning' and to bring His declared purposes to pass. This is what the text claims about itself; it does not by itself settle disputed questions about when the oracle was composed or resolve every philosophical question about foreknowledge.
- **[[Salvation]] for Zion**: v.13 closes with a promise that the LORD will 'place salvation in Zion for Israel my glory' — but this follows a rebuke of the 'stouthearted... far from righteousness' (v.12), so it reads as a gracious divine initiative toward a wayward audience rather than an unconditional guarantee detached from that context.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Forms of **bear/carry** organize the chapter. Idols are carried as burdens (vv. 1–2, 7), but Israel is carried by God (vv. 3–4). The same reversal appears in the language of rescue: the idols cannot deliver or save (vv. 2, 7), whereas God says he will deliver Israel and bring salvation near (vv. 4, 13).

The repeated imperatives to “remember” and “bring it again to mind” (vv. 8–9) make recollection part of the argument. The audience is asked to evaluate the claim of divine uniqueness by considering former things, not only by contemplating an abstract assertion.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's central device is an inversion of carrying/bearing vocabulary: idols are burdens carried by weary beasts and worshippers (vv.1-2, 7), while God carries Israel (vv.3-4) — the same reversal extends to 'deliver/save' language (idols 'could not deliver the burden,' v.2; God 'will deliver you,' v.4; salvation 'shall not tarry,' v.13). The rhetorical question of v.5 ('to whom will ye liken me... that we may be like?') echoes similar challenges in [[Isaiah 40]] (40:18, 25), and the idol-manufacture satire of vv.6-7 parallels the longer treatment in [[Isaiah 44]] (44:9-20) and the shorter version in Jeremiah 10 — evidence of a recurring polemical convention in Hebrew prophetic literature, though the exact relationship between these passages (shared convention vs. direct literary dependence) is not established by the parallel alone. The identification of the unnamed 'ravenous bird... from the east' (v.11) with Cyrus depends substantially on [[Isaiah 45]], where Cyrus is named explicitly (45:1, 13); Isaiah 46 itself never names him. That identification is compatible with both traditional (predictive-prophecy) and critical (near-contemporary) readings of Isaiah 40-55 — the two differ on when the oracle was composed, not on who is likely meant.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary-Contextual Identification
> Within the surrounding literary context, Cyrus is the leading identification of the unnamed eastern agent in Isaiah 46:11: the preceding chapter names Cyrus as the Lord's anointed and says he will be raised up in righteousness, while Isaiah 46 describes a man from the east who executes God's counsel. Isaiah 46 itself, however, does not name him.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · standard scholarly identification, authorship-neutral · [[Literary-Contextual Identification in Isaiah 46|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Background *(tentative)*
> The image of idols 'upon the beasts, and upon the cattle' as 'heavy loaden' cargo (v.1) may reflect real historical practice: the Babylonian king Nabonidus is known to have gathered cult statues of various Mesopotamian city-gods into Babylon during the years before its fall, a policy the later Cyrus Cylinder boasts of reversing by returning the gods to their home sanctuaries after Cyrus's conquest in 539 BC. If so, Isaiah's satire may echo an event or practice known to its audience, not merely a generic image.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.4 · minority/speculative historical background, unverified against primary sources

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Interpretation / Authorship Dispute *(tentative)*
> 'A ravenous bird from the east, the man that executeth my counsel from a far country' (v.11) is widely identified, by traditional and critical readers alike, with Cyrus the Great of Persia, given the explicit naming of Cyrus as the LORD's instrument/anointed just one chapter earlier (Isaiah 44:28-45:1). This identification is an inference from context, not a claim the chapter itself makes explicitly, and it feeds directly into the long-running scholarly debate over the authorship and date of Isaiah 40-55.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.6 · disputed authorship implications, standard identification

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the repeated carrying vocabulary shape the contrast between idols and God more precisely than a general statement that idols are false?
- What 'former things of old' would the chapter's first audience most naturally have recalled (v.9)?
- Isaiah 45 names Cyrus explicitly, but Isaiah 46 never does (v.11) — how much interpretive weight should that identification carry, and does it matter for faith whether the prophecy is read as a distant prediction or a near-contemporary reference?
- What 'idols' in modern life require constant maintenance, cannot move on their own, and cannot answer when we cry out to them (v.7)?
- Why might Isaiah pair mockery of false gods so closely with a promise of salvation for Zion (vv.12-13) rather than ending on judgment alone?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the idol-manufacture satire with [[Isaiah 44]] (44:9-20, the fullest version of this genre in Isaiah), Isaiah 40:18-20, and Jeremiah 10:1-16. For the Cyrus material referenced by v.11, see [[Isaiah 45]] (45:1, 13, naming Cyrus directly) and 44:28. Some readers connect v.1's image of idols loaded onto beasts with the report (in the Nabonidus Chronicle and the Cyrus Cylinder) that the Babylonian king Nabonidus gathered provincial cult statues into Babylon before its fall, later restored under Cyrus — an interesting but unproven background resonance rather than a claim the text itself makes, and the specific dates and characterization of that policy should be treated as tentative pending primary-source verification.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
