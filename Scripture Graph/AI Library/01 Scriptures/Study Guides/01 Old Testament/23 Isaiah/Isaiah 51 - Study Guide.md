## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter follows a pattern of repeated imperatives and shifts in address:

- **Verses 1-3:** Hearken; look back to Abraham and Sarah; Zion's waste places will become garden-like.
- **Verses 4-6:** Hearken; divine law, judgment, righteousness, and salvation extend to the peoples and isles.
- **Verses 7-8:** Hearken; those with God's law in their heart are told not to fear mortal reproach.
- **Verses 9-11:** "Awake" — an appeal to the arm of the LORD, recalling the ancient defeat of Rahab/the dragon and the drying of the sea; v.11 closely echoes [[Isaiah 35]]:10 and functions as a hinge before the address turns to Jerusalem.
- **Verses 12-16:** Divine reassurance contrasts the mortal oppressor with the Creator and promises release and protection.
- **Verses 17-20:** Jerusalem is told to awake after drinking the cup of fury; her children cannot guide her.
- **Verses 21-23:** God removes the cup from Jerusalem and gives it to her afflicters.

Exact speaker transitions (especially around vv.9, 12, and 16) are not explicitly marked in the text; the divisions above track content and imperative verbs rather than a certain change of voice.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Covenant memory as antidote to fear.** The chapter repeatedly ties fear of mortal power to forgetting the LORD ("who art thou, that thou shouldest be afraid of a man that shall die...and forgettest the LORD thy maker," vv.12-13) — remembering Abraham and Sarah (vv.1-2), remembering the [[Exodus and Deliverance|Exodus]] (vv.9-10), remembering that "the heavens shall vanish...but my salvation shall be for ever" (v.6).

Hope for Zion is grounded in the [[Abrahamic Covenant]]: the God who called Abraham "alone" and increased him can likewise turn Zion's "waste places" into an Eden (vv.2-3). The permanence of God's [[Salvation]] and righteousness is repeatedly set against the mortality of oppressors and even of creation itself (vv.6,8).

**Reversal, not merely relief.** The chapter's resolution (vv.21-23) is not simply the removal of Jerusalem's suffering but its transfer: the same cup she drank is placed in her oppressors' hands, combining renewal with [[Justice and Mercy|justice]]. The text does not explain how this collective judgment relates to the circumstances of every individual oppressor.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Rahab” in verse 9 appears beside “the dragon,” while verse 10 turns immediately to the sea and the great deep. The combination evokes God's victory over threatening waters and powers. Identifying Rahab exclusively with Egypt, a primordial chaos figure, or one particular historical enemy goes beyond what this chapter alone makes explicit.

The “cup of trembling” and “cup of my fury” are metaphors for experienced judgment, not descriptions of ordinary intoxication; verse 21 expressly calls Jerusalem “drunken, but not with wine.” The transfer of the cup in verses 22-23 makes the metaphor an image of reversed fortunes and judgment.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two doubled "Awake" commands organize the second half of the chapter: an appeal for the arm of the LORD to awake (v.9), and, after God's reassurance ("I, even I, am he that comforteth you," v.12), a call for Jerusalem herself to awake and stand (v.17). Read together, this suggests a movement from appeal toward divine action to the eventual rousing of the afflicted city, though the text does not stage this as an explicit dialogue with marked speakers.

Verse 9's rhetorical question — "Art thou not it that hath cut Rahab, and wounded the dragon?" — sits beside verse 10's description of drying "the sea, the waters of the great deep...for the ransomed to pass over." The pairing moves within two verses from vivid combat/chaos imagery to the memory of the Exodus, though whether the two verses describe one event under different figures or two distinct images placed side by side is not settled by the text itself.

The "garment" and "moth" language of vv.6,8 echoes wording near the end of [[Isaiah 50]] (50:9) — a real verbal link suggesting these chapters were read or transmitted together, though it does not by itself prove they were composed as a single original unit rather than joined editorially.

Verse 11 closely parallels [[Isaiah 35]]:10 in describing the redeemed/ransomed returning to Zion with singing and everlasting joy while sorrow flees — though the wording differs enough ("redeemed" vs. "ransomed," "mourning" vs. "sighing") that no verbatim-match system flags an overlap. Whether this reflects deliberate reprise, shared conventional language, or later editorial relocation is disputed and not resolved by the text.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Ane Comparative-Literature Background *(tentative)*
> Verse 9's rhetorical question, 'Art thou not it that hath cut Rahab, and wounded the dragon?', invokes 'Rahab' and 'the dragon' (Heb. tannin) as defeated cosmic sea-monsters. This is widely read by scholars as drawing on a broader ancient Near Eastern combat-myth idiom (a deity defeating a chaos-sea monster, paralleled in Ugaritic Baal-Yamm/Litan traditions) that Isaiah repurposes as poetic imagery for God's power, not as an endorsement of the underlying myth as history.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed interpretation, unsourced

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the chapter address two different subjects with the same "Awake, awake" call — first God's arm (v.9), then Jerusalem (v.17)?
- Is the imagery of Rahab and "the dragon" in v.9 meant to evoke a specific historical adversary (as in Isaiah 30:7's use of "Rahab" for Egypt), a cosmological motif, or both at once?
- Who is the singular addressee of v.16 ("I have put my words in thy mouth")? The nation collectively, personified Zion, or a prophetic/servant figure?
- Why does the appeal in vv.1-2 name Sarah alongside Abraham, while the explanation that follows speaks only of calling "him" — Abraham — "alone"?
- What does the unusual phrase "plant the heavens" (v.16) contribute to the promise it is part of?
- What should be made of v.11's close resemblance to Isaiah 35:10 — deliberate reprise, shared stock language, or editorial relocation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
