# Isaiah 35 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 35]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 35 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Isaiah 35 answers desolation with restoration: the wilderness itself will blossom and rejoice, weak hands and fearful hearts will be strengthened, blindness, deafness, lameness, and muteness will be reversed, and a protected highway — 'The way of holiness' — will carry the redeemed home to [[Zion]] with everlasting joy. The chapter speaks directly to [[Salvation]], [[Hope]], and [[Gathering of Israel]], and later New Testament use (Matthew 11) reads its healing imagery as a sign of Jesus's identity. But the chapter itself does not assign every image to one fixed historical moment, so readings as a promise to captive Judah, a messianic preview, or a type of latter-day gathering are complementary rather than mutually exclusive.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves through four linked scenes: (1) renewed creation (vv.1-2) — wilderness and desert rejoice and display divine glory; (2) encouragement (vv.3-4) — weak and fearful people are strengthened by the promise that God will come, in both judgment and salvation, to save them; (3) reversal (vv.5-7) — bodily impairments and arid land give way to ability and abundance; (4) return (vv.8-10) — a holy, protected highway, 'The way of holiness,' leads the redeemed to [[Zion]]. This four-part outline is a reading aid, not an explicit division supplied by the text. The chapter is also loosely bookended by joy language — the desert is told to 'rejoice, and blossom' at the outset (v.1-2), and the ransomed arrive with 'songs and everlasting joy... sorrow and sighing shall flee away' at the close (v.10) — though calling this a deliberately engineered inclusio goes beyond what the wording alone establishes.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds divine recompense and salvation together rather than as unrelated themes (v.4), relevant to [[Justice and Mercy]]. Holiness also has a directional quality here: the redeemed do not merely receive relief but travel a designated way toward [[Zion]] (vv.8-10). Verses 3-4 ground [[Hope]] concretely in God's promised coming — weak hands and feeble knees are strengthened by that assurance, not by human effort alone. The passage does not explain the means by which people become clean or ransomed, so a full doctrine of redemption should not be built from this chapter alone.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two KJV word choices are worth flagging for accuracy. In verse 1, 'rose' translates a Hebrew term (chabatzeleth) of uncertain identity — likely a crocus, asphodel, or narcissus rather than the modern rose. In verse 7, 'dragons' translates tannim, which most scholars and modern translations render 'jackals' — the wasteland described was haunted by jackals, not mythical creatures, and its transformation into grass, reeds, and rushes marks a shift from wild desolation to sustainable habitation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Joy vocabulary binds the chapter together: the land rejoices and sings near the beginning (v.1-2), and the returning community comes 'with songs and everlasting joy' at the end (v.10). Verse 10 is also a near-doublet of Isaiah 51:11 ('the redeemed of the LORD shall return, and come with singing unto Zion... sorrow and mourning shall flee away'), a striking verbal parallel whose significance for dating or authorship remains disputed among scholars — it documents shared language, not which passage came first or why. The chapter also reverses [[Isaiah 34]]'s judgment imagery: both chapters use the identical KJV phrase 'habitation of dragons' (34:13; 35:7) for opposite outcomes, ruin versus fertile wilderness, a concrete verbal echo alongside the broader thematic contrast. The healing catalog in verses 5-6 (blind, deaf, lame, dumb) reappears — though not in the same order, and with additions (lepers, the dead raised, good news to the poor, the last drawn from Isaiah 61:1) — in Jesus's answer to John the Baptist's disciples (Matthew 11:4-5; compare Luke 7:22), where it functions as a composite Isaianic sign of his identity rather than a narrow quotation of this chapter alone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Literary Reception
> Matthew 11:4–5 places the restoration of sight, hearing, and mobility among the works reported in answer to John the Baptist's question about Jesus. Its correspondence with Isaiah 35:5–6 is strong evidence that Matthew's presentation participates in an Isaianic restoration framework. It is weaker evidence for the more specific assertion that Isaiah 35 exclusively predicts that single episode.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed scope of application · [[Canonical Literary Reception in Isaiah 35|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Compositional Parallel *(tentative)*
> Isaiah 35:10 closely parallels Isaiah 51:11, which repeats nearly identical content and structure (the redeemed/ransomed of the LORD returning to Zion with singing and everlasting joy, sorrow and sighing/mourning fleeing away). This near-doublet is often cited by scholars as evidence that chapter 35 shares vocabulary and themes with the Deutero-Isaiah material (chapters 40-55), functioning as a literary bridge or preview within the book.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the word “Then” in verses 5–6 connect the healings specifically to God's coming in verse 4, or more generally to the whole promised restoration?
- Is the transformed landscape intended literally, metaphorically, or as poetic language capable of carrying both senses?
- Who are “the redeemed” and “the ransomed” within the chapter's own horizon, and how much identification must come from other texts?
- How does access to “The way of holiness” relate to the exclusion of the unclean in verse 8?
- In what precise sense do Matthew 11:4–5 and Luke 7:22 invoke this chapter: prediction, typology, scriptural allusion, or some combination?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Isaiah 35 with [[Isaiah 34]] (its judgment counterpart, including the shared phrase 'habitation of dragons'), Isaiah 51:11 (the near-doublet of v.10), and Isaiah 43:19 (way in the wilderness, water in the desert). Hebrews 12:12 echoes v.3's 'weak hands' and 'feeble knees' almost verbatim. For the healing-catalog imagery, see Matthew 11:2-6 and Luke 7:18-23, keeping in mind Matthew's list combines material beyond Isaiah 35 alone. D&C 133 uses similar gathering and highway imagery in a latter-day setting but draws more directly on other Isaiah chapters, so treat it as a broad thematic comparison rather than a direct textual reuse.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
