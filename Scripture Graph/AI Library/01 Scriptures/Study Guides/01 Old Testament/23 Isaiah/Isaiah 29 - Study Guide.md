# Isaiah 29 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 29]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 29 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Isaiah 29 begins with judgment against Ariel, the city associated with David, and ends with renewal for the house of Jacob. Between those poles it traces two related crises: hostile nations surround Zion, and God's own people cannot perceive revelation. Both conditions are reversed. The attackers vanish like an unsatisfying dream, while the deaf hear, the blind see, the meek rejoice, and those who erred gain understanding.

The official Latter-day Saint chapter heading reads the voice from the dust, sealed book, and marvelous work through [[2 Nephi 27]], connecting them with [[Apostasy]], the [[Book of Mormon (Gospel Topic)]], and the [[Restoration of the Gospel]]. That canonical reading is important, but it should be distinguished from the narrower observation that Isaiah 29 itself first presents the sealed book as a simile for an unreadable prophetic vision.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful outline is:

1. **Ariel besieged and brought low (vv. 1-4):** Jerusalem faces divine judgment and speaks from the dust.
2. **The besiegers dispersed (vv. 5-8):** the nations' apparent triumph evaporates like a dream.
3. **A people unable to perceive (vv. 9-12):** stupor, closed eyes, covered seers, and the sealed-book comparison depict inaccessible revelation.
4. **False worship and overturned wisdom (vv. 13-16):** lips honor God while hearts remain distant; concealed counsel and creator-denial are exposed.
5. **Renewed creation, perception, and justice (vv. 17-24):** land, senses, social order, Jacob's family, and understanding are transformed.

This progression joins political deliverance to spiritual and ethical renewal rather than treating the sealed book as an isolated prediction.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Isaiah 29 condemns worship that honors God verbally while the heart remains distant and religious fear is reduced to human precept (v.13). Jesus later applies this indictment to disputes over religious tradition in [[Matthew 15]] and [[Mark 7]]. The chapter also presents revelation as dependent on God: neither learning nor lack of learning can by itself overcome the inaccessible vision of verses 11-12.

The promised renewal includes perception, reverence, and justice. The deaf hear, the blind see, the meek and poor rejoice, and those who corrupt accusation and turn aside the just are removed (vv.18-21). Thus [[Revelation]], [[Humility]], and [[Justice and Mercy]] are related themes, although the text does not spell out a simple causal sequence among them.

Through [[2 Nephi 27]], Latter-day Saint interpretation connects the sealed book and marvelous work with [[Apostasy]], the [[Book of Mormon (Gospel Topic)]], and the [[Restoration of the Gospel]]. That is an important canonical interpretation, not an identification made explicitly by Isaiah 29 alone.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several image systems reinforce one another. Vertical language marks humiliation and reversal: Ariel is brought down to the ground, whereas its enemies become dust and passing chaff. Sensory language moves from closed eyes and unreadable words to seeing and hearing. Epistemic language moves from sleep, error, and failed wisdom to understanding and learned doctrine.

The dream simile in verses 7-8 is especially precise. The nations seem to consume Zion as a hungry or thirsty dreamer seems to eat or drink, but awakening reveals that nothing was obtained. The image portrays frustrated conquest, not merely the brevity of the attack.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's reversals are tightly patterned: Ariel is reduced to dust, but its oppressors become dust; the people cannot read the vision, but the deaf later hear the book's words; the wise lose understanding, while those who erred gain it; Jacob's shame gives way to sanctification.

[[2 Nephi 27]] preserves extensive verbal contact with Isaiah 29 while expanding the book imagery into a detailed narrative about a sealed record, learned and unlearned readers, witnesses, and God's marvelous work. The overlap securely demonstrates deliberate textual reuse. What it means historically is a separate question: the correspondence is compatible with Latter-day Saint claims of inspired expansion, but overlap alone cannot establish ancient authorship or exclude modern literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Does "Ariel" emphasize Jerusalem generally, an altar-hearth association, or deliberate wordplay between the city and its coming judgment?
2. How does the initial siege relate to the later crisis of failed religious perception?
3. Why do both the learned and unlearned readers fail, and what does that pairing imply about the limits of human qualification?
4. Should the book imagery be read first as Isaiah's metaphor for inaccessible vision, as a latter-day prophecy through [[2 Nephi 27]], or as both through typology or multiple fulfillment?
5. What does the removal of corrupt accusers in verses 20-21 add to the chapter's account of spiritual restoration?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Isaiah 29:13 with [[Matthew 15]] and [[Mark 7]], where Jesus applies its indictment to outward religion governed by human tradition. Compare Isaiah 29:14 with [[1 Corinthians 1]], where Paul uses its reversal of wisdom in his theology of the cross. Finally, read Isaiah 29 beside [[2 Nephi 27]], marking what is retained, rearranged, and expanded. Separating direct verbal correspondence from interpretive additions helps clarify both the strength and the limits of fulfillment claims.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
