## Overview

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv.1-3 — The LORD's legal defense.** Rhetorical questions expose that no document or creditor forced Israel's estrangement; the text attributes it instead to Israel's own iniquity, while the LORD's continuing power is asserted through the sea/rivers imagery of verse 2.
- **vv.4-9 — The servant's obedience and confidence.** A first-person voice, distinguished by phrases like 'the Lord GOD hath given me,' describes being taught 'morning by morning' (v.4), never rebellious (v.5), submitting to smiting and spitting (v.6), resolute 'like a flint' (v.7), and confident that no accuser can prevail against him (vv.8-9).
- **vv.10-11 — Application to the hearers.** Verse 10 addresses one who fears the LORD yet still 'walketh in darkness,' urging trust; verse 11 turns to a distinct plural group ('all ye that kindle a fire') and warns that reliance on self-made light ends 'in sorrow.'

Speaker changes are signaled by shifts in address and self-reference (the LORD speaking in vv.1-3; a taught servant in vv.4-9; third-person 'his servant' in v.10) but are not formally marked. Verses 10-11 most likely address two different audiences rather than presenting one person a choice between two paths.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Sin and estrangement.** Verse 1 locates the cause of Israel's broken relationship with God in human iniquity — relevant to [[Sin]] and [[Justice and Mercy]] — without settling how far this excludes a disciplinary act by God himself.
- **Obedient suffering.** The servant's description of submitting to abuse without turning back (vv.4-6) is read in Latter-day Saint and Christian tradition as anticipating [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], and models [[Obedience]] grounded in repeatedly 'wakened' listening rather than mere stoic endurance.
- **Trust amid darkness.** Verse 10 portrays a God-fearing, servant-obeying person who still 'walketh in darkness, and hath no light' — a reminder that adversity or uncertainty is not itself evidence of unfaithfulness; the prescribed response is to [[Faith|trust in the name of the LORD]].
- **Self-made light.** Verse 11's warning against those who 'kindle a fire' is often read as a caution against self-reliance as a substitute for trust in God, though the verse may instead (or additionally) address a distinct group of opponents rather than issue a blanket condemnation of human effort.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several image systems reinforce the argument. Verse 1 uses domestic and economic language—divorce, creditors, and sale—to examine estrangement. Verses 4–7 move through parts of the body: tongue, ear, back, cheeks, and face. Verses 8–9 adopt courtroom language: justify, contend, adversary, and condemn. Verses 10–11 close with darkness, light, fire, sparks, and sorrow. The movement from an opened ear to a disciplined tongue suggests that the servant's speech grows from listening.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Isaiah 50 opens and closes with contrasting rhetorical strategies: vv.1-3 use covenant-lawsuit-style language — questions posed as if in a courtroom, then answered by God himself — while vv.10-11 shift to direct address built around a fire/light metaphor. Between them, vv.4-9 form one of Isaiah's 'Servant Songs,' a modern scholarly classification grouping this unit with [[Isaiah 49]] and [[Isaiah 53]] on the basis of a shared pattern: a called, obedient figure who suffers and is vindicated. Legal vocabulary reappears around the servant in vv.8-9 (justify, contend, adversary, condemn), echoing but not exactly repeating the commercial/legal terms of vv.1-3 (creditor, sold, divorcement) — a thematic rather than strictly lexical connection.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs

> The vocabulary of Isaiah 50:6 (smiting, plucking, spitting, shame) is thematically close to Gospel descriptions of Jesus's mocking and scourging before the crucifixion, and later Christian and LDS tradition (including this chapter's official heading, 'Isaiah speaks as the Messiah') reads verses 4-9 as messianic/Christological.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Who is speaking in each unit, and what textual signals mark the transitions? The servant is never named within vv.4-9 itself.
- What is gained by God posing rhetorical questions in vv.1-2 and then answering them himself, rather than simply asserting his faithfulness directly?
- Does the darkness in verse 10 describe outward adversity, inward uncertainty, or both — and does the text support treating it as evidence against, rather than for, faithfulness?
- Is the 'fire' of verse 11 addressed to the same person described in verse 10, or to a separate group? What does that imply about the warning's scope — self-reliance generally, or something more specific?
- Which features of vv.4-9 support a Christological reading, and which remain equally compatible with a prophetic or representative-Israel reading? How does the wording compare with [[2 Nephi 7]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Isaiah 50:2 with biblical sea-deliverance and 'rebuke' traditions (e.g., Exodus 14, Psalm 106) and study the chapter under [[Exodus and Deliverance]]. Compare Isaiah 50:6 with the Gospel Passion accounts of striking and spitting; note that the detail of hair being plucked from the servant's cheeks has no direct New Testament parallel, so the correspondence is partial rather than exact. [[2 Nephi 7]] is the chapter's traditional Book of Mormon counterpart per its own heading, though this job's automated text-overlap check found no exact-matching verses between the two chapters (only a high semantic-similarity score) — a discrepancy worth resolving by direct comparison rather than assuming an exact quotation. [[Isaiah 49]] and [[Isaiah 53]] provide broader context for the Servant Songs, but similarities across these passages should not be assumed to prove an identical speaker or historical referent in each case.
