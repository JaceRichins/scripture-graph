# Isaiah 58 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 58]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 58 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The true law of the fast, with its purposes and attendant blessings, is set forth—The commandment to keep the Sabbath is given.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verse 1:** A trumpet-like summons to expose transgression.
2. **Verses 2–5:** The people's apparent devotion, complaint, and unacceptable fast.
3. **Verses 6–7:** The chosen fast—release from oppression and material care for others.
4. **Verses 8–12:** Conditional promises of light, guidance, nourishment, and restoration, with a renewed demand to remove oppression.
5. **Verses 13–14:** A parallel if-then appeal concerning the [[Sabbath Day]].

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter treats devotion to God and responsibility toward other people as inseparable. Religious acts do not compensate for exploitation or violence. Its account of [[Fasting and Prayer for the Poor]] includes breaking oppressive burdens, sharing food, providing shelter, and clothing the naked.

The promised blessings should be read with care. They express a strong covenantal relationship between reform and renewal, but the poetic assurances do not require a doctrine that every righteous person will receive immediate health or material prosperity.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The recurring **yoke** links verses 6 and 9: it must be broken and removed. **Light** answers **obscurity** and **darkness** in verses 8 and 10, while the watered garden and unfailing spring in verse 11 reverse the condition of drought. In verse 8, righteousness going before the people and the glory of the LORD serving as their "rearward" create an image of protection both ahead and behind.

The word **delight** also changes context. Verse 2 describes people who delight in approaching God despite the chapter's indictment; verses 13–14 locate delight in honoring the Sabbath and in the LORD.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Isaiah repeatedly uses question-and-answer and condition-and-result patterns: the people's questions in verse 3 are answered by the LORD's questions in verses 5-7, and the sequence moves through 'then,' 'if,' and 'then' again in verses 8-10, then repeats a similar conditional movement for the Sabbath in verses 13-14 (vv11-12 continue the promise with plain 'and,' not further conditionals). Vivid imagery reinforces this cause-and-effect logic: bowing 'as a bulrush' in false humility (v5), light breaking 'as the morning' (v8), a 'watered garden' and unfailing spring (v11), and riding 'upon the high places of the earth' (v14). The closing formula, 'for the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it' (v14), is an exact match to the close of [[Isaiah 40]]:5, verified by text-overlap indexing — possibly a stock prophetic refrain reused across oracles, or a deliberate echo tying the two promises together; the text does not decide between these readings.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Textual Structure *(tentative)*
> The explicit "then" and "if" clauses provide strong internal textual evidence that the promised light, divine response, guidance, nourishment, and restoration are presented as consequences of ethical reform rather than of ritual observance alone.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · partially overstated + unjustified confidence figures

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical-Critical *(tentative)*
> Isaiah 58 is not among the Isaiah chapters quoted in the Book of Mormon (unlike Isaiah 2-14, 29, 48-54, which Nephite writers explicitly copy). This means the common Latter-day Saint argument that Nephite quotation of certain Isaiah chapters by c. 550 BC counts as evidence against a later ('Deutero-/Trito-Isaiah') composition date does not extend to this chapter, leaving Isaiah 58's authorship and date open to the same critical-scholarship arguments (thematic fit with a post-exilic audience rebuilding 'the old waste places,' 58:12) that apply to Isaiah 56-66 generally.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, needs sourcing
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does "exact all your labours" in verse 3 refer specifically to coercing or oppressing workers? The KJV wording is archaic enough that comparison with other translations is important.
- Is the rebuilding in verse 12 primarily literal reconstruction, a metaphor for social healing, or deliberately capable of both senses?
- Who is included in "thine own flesh" in verse 7—kin, covenant community, humanity more broadly, or some combination?
- How should "not doing thine own ways" in verse 13 be applied without ignoring the verse's positive command to call the Sabbath a delight?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
