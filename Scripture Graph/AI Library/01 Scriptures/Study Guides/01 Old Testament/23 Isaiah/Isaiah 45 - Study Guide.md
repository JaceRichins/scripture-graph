# Isaiah 45 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 45]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 45 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Isaiah 45 continues the Cyrus oracle begun in [[Isaiah 44]]:28, addressing a foreign king by name and title ("anointed," v.1) as the LORD's chosen instrument to subdue nations, free captives, and enable the rebuilding of Jerusalem (v.13) — even though Cyrus himself, we are twice told, "hast not known" the LORD (vv.4-5). Around this specific historical commission, the chapter builds a sustained argument for the LORD's exclusive divinity, repeating "there is none else" throughout (vv.5, 6, 14, 18, 21, 22), and climaxes in a universal invitation, "Look unto me, and be ye saved, all the ends of the earth" (v.22), and an oath that every knee will bow and every tongue swear (v.23) — language the New Testament later applies to Jesus Christ (Romans 14:11; Philippians 2:10-11).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A defensible four-part reading is:

1. **Cyrus commissioned (vv. 1-8):** the LORD empowers a ruler who has not known him, for Israel's sake and for worldwide recognition.
2. **The creature and the Maker (vv. 9-13):** potter and parent imagery rebukes challenges to divine action; Cyrus's assignment is restated.
3. **Nations, idols, and Israel (vv. 14-19):** foreign peoples acknowledge God, idol makers are shamed, and Israel receives promised salvation.
4. **A summons to the nations (vv. 20-25):** gods that cannot save are contrasted with the just God and Savior; all the earth is invited to look to him.

These divisions describe shifts in address and subject matter; they should not be treated as proof of separate sources.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter grounds saving power in the LORD's identity as sole Creator (vv.7, 12, 18), including the striking claim that he is the source of both light/darkness and peace/calamity (v.7; see Language for the "evil" translation question). [[Salvation]] is not limited to Israel in its invitation — verse 22 addresses "all the ends of the earth" — but verses 24-25 distinguish those who find righteousness in the LORD from those who remain "incensed" and ashamed, so the passage should not be read as teaching automatic universal salvation. Cyrus illustrates divine providence working through someone outside Israel who does not know the LORD (vv.4-5); his title "anointed" here is functional — a commissioning for a specific task — not a claim about his covenant standing. Verse 25's promise that Israel will be "justified" is best read as covenantal vindication before its enemies rather than a developed doctrine of individual justification.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV's "create evil" (v.7) translates Hebrew ra, a word whose range includes calamity and disaster as much as moral wrongdoing, standing in direct parallel with "peace" (shalom) in this verse. Several modern translations render the term as "calamity" or "disaster" rather than "evil"; the NRSV traditionally reads "woe" opposite "weal." Reading the verse as "the LORD is sovereign over calamity" rather than "the LORD authors moral evil" changes its theological weight, though the English parallelism alone cannot fully settle the underlying Hebrew semantic question. Note also that "anointed" in v.1 is the Hebrew mashiach — its application to a foreign king is striking and worth flagging for readers who associate the term exclusively with Israelite kings or the Messiah, though claims about its uniqueness across the whole Hebrew Bible have not been independently verified here.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Isaiah 45 pairs an intensely particular, real-world political event (a foreign king's conquests) with sweeping universal claims ("all the ends of the earth," v.22) — the specific and the cosmic interpret each other throughout the chapter. Two tensions give the chapter its rhetorical force: a foreign ruler who does not know the LORD is nevertheless called, named, and equipped by him (vv.1-5), and the LORD is described as one who "hidest thyself" (v.15) yet denies speaking "in secret" (v.19). The potter/clay and parent/child imagery of vv.9-10 reinforces the Creator-creature distinction, primarily to answer resistance to the particular way the LORD has chosen to act through Cyrus.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Dating And Authorship
> The explicit naming of Cyrus and description of his role are genuine evidence relevant to debates about the oracle's date and authorship. Under an early-Isaiah dating, the details function as a specific prediction; composition or editing during or after Cyrus's rise provides a historical alternative that accounts for the same textual datum.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.8 · accepted datum; disputed interpretation · [[Dating And Authorship in Isaiah 45|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual / New Testament Reception
> Isaiah 45:23's oath — "unto me every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear" — is directly quoted by Paul in Romans 14:11 ("every knee shall bow to me, and every tongue shall confess to God") and echoed in Philippians 2:10-11, where the same universal-submission language originally spoken by/about the LORD (YHWH) is applied to Jesus Christ.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-established NT reception · [[Intertextual / New Testament Reception in Isaiah 45|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical-Critical *(tentative)*
> Isaiah 45 names Cyrus explicitly (continuing from Isaiah 44:28), which traditional readings treat as specific, long-range predictive prophecy delivered roughly 150 years before Cyrus's reign (Isaiah's traditional 8th-century ministry). Critical scholarship, following the "Deutero-Isaiah" hypothesis, instead holds that Isaiah 40–55 was composed by an anonymous exilic-era prophet writing during or near the end of the Babylonian exile, which would place the Cyrus material much closer to (or after) the events it describes.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · disputed; Deutero-Isaiah is the mainstream critical view

> [!evidence] Archaeological / Extra-Biblical Text *(tentative)*
> The historical Cyrus Cylinder (a 6th-century BC Babylonian inscription, British Museum) records Cyrus's general policy of releasing subject peoples and restoring their temples/gods after his conquest of Babylon, a policy broadly consistent with the biblical portrayal of Cyrus releasing captives without price (Isaiah 45:13) and permitting the rebuilding of Jerusalem (cf. Ezra 1:1-4).
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · plausible background, not confirmation
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What does it mean that Cyrus is called the LORD's "anointed" (v.1) while also being told twice that he "hast not known" the LORD (vv.4-5)? Does the chapter's precise naming of Cyrus function as evidence of long-range predictive prophecy, or does it more plausibly reflect a text finalized closer to Cyrus's own time — and can readers hold both possibilities honestly? How should the confession that God "hidest" himself (v.15) be read alongside his claim not to have "spoken in secret" (v.19)? Does the universal invitation of verse 22 imply a universal response, or does verse 24's mention of those "incensed against him" preserve real division? What range of meaning can the KJV's "evil" (v.7) bear given its parallel with "peace"?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
