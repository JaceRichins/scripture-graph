## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jerusalem will be attacked and scourged—The people will be carried captive—The Messiah will hold the key of the house of David, inherit glory, and be fastened as a nail in a sure place.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The threatened city (22:1-4):** public tumult, flight, capture, and Isaiah's grief.
2. **A day of invasion and exposure (22:5-7):** walls fail while Elam and Kir appear among the armed forces.
3. **Preparation without regard for the Maker (22:8-11):** weapons, breaches, water, houses, and walls receive attention.
4. **A rejected call to mourning (22:12-14):** feasting replaces repentance, and judgment is pronounced.
5. **Shebna brought down (22:15-19):** his tomb and glory are answered with displacement and disgrace.
6. **Eliakim installed (22:20-24):** robe, girdle, government, key, and nail imagery describe his new responsibility.
7. **The nail falls (22:25):** the secure-nail image ends with removal and collapse, creating an important interpretive difficulty.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter distinguishes practical preparation from faithful dependence. It lists extensive defensive work but locates the failure in not looking to the Maker (Isaiah 22:8-11). The point need not be that planning is faithless; rather, material competence does not replace accountability to God.

Verses 12-14 portray repentance as an appropriate response to divine warning. The people's feasting may express indulgence, despair, fatalism, or some combination of these, but in every case it stands in direct opposition to the commanded mourning.

Eliakim's appointment also presents authority as entrusted stewardship. His robe, girdle, government, and key are given rather than self-created (Isaiah 22:20-22). This provides a restrained connection to [[Government and Laws]] and [[Pride]].

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly reverses appearances. A joyous city is actually in distress; careful fortification coexists with spiritual neglect; Shebna's impressive tomb anticipates exile rather than lasting honor; and a nail declared secure is finally cut down. These reversals make human confidence a unifying literary concern.

The extended nail image is deliberately weight-bearing: Eliakim is fastened securely, his father's household hangs upon him, and the accumulated burden is finally cut off (Isaiah 22:23-25). The transition from promise to collapse is textually clear, but the identity of the final nail is debated.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why are the city's dead described as neither slain by sword nor dead in battle (Isaiah 22:2)? Does the language imply famine, panic, execution, disease, or another form of catastrophe?
- Is the feast in Isaiah 22:13 defiant pleasure, fatalistic resignation, or both?
- Does the falling nail in Isaiah 22:25 refer to Eliakim, Shebna, Shebna's regime, or the burden placed upon Eliakim? What grammatical and contextual evidence favors each reading?
- How should [[Revelation 3]] inform Christian interpretation without displacing Eliakim from Isaiah's immediate context?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Isaiah 22:15-22 with [[Isaiah 36]] and [[2 Kings 18]], where Eliakim appears over the household and Shebna serves as scribe. Compare Isaiah 22:22 with [[Revelation 3]] to study how later scripture reapplies the key of David to Christ. Compare Isaiah 22:13 with [[1 Corinthians 15]] to see the same feast-before-death wording employed in a distinct argument about resurrection.
