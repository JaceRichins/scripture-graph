# Isaiah 61 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 61]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 61 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Isaiah speaks about the Messiah—The Messiah will have the Spirit, preach the gospel, and proclaim liberty—In the last days, the Lord will call His ministers and make an everlasting covenant with the people.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
One coherent division is:

1. **Isaiah 61:1-3 — Commission and consolation.** The anointed speaker proclaims good news, liberty, divine favor, judgment, and comfort.
2. **Isaiah 61:4-9 — Communal restoration and covenant.** Ruins are rebuilt, social standing is reversed, the people receive priestly titles, and the LORD promises an everlasting covenant.
3. **Isaiah 61:10-11 — Rejoicing and new growth.** Salvation is pictured as festive clothing, while righteousness and praise arise like growth from the earth and a garden.

This division follows real shifts in subject and imagery, but it should not be treated as a proven formal A-B-A design. The identity of the first-person speaker in verse 10 is uncertain.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Salvation]] through consolation, release, rebuilding, joy, righteousness, and public restoration. It also places the LORD's acceptable year beside a day of vengeance, holding divine favor and judgment together without explaining their chronology.

Verse 8 joins the LORD's love of judgment, hatred of robbery, direction in truth, and promise of an everlasting [[Covenants|covenant]]. The KJV expression "robbery for burnt offering" reflects a difficult Hebrew construction, so conclusions about its precise relationship to sacrifice should remain modest.

Verse 6 calls the restored community "Priests of the LORD" and "Ministers of our God." These are genuine corporate priestly titles, but the passage does not determine whether they describe honor, cultic function, mediatorial service, formal authority, or some combination.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The opening follows a commissioning sequence: the Spirit rests upon the speaker, the LORD anoints and sends him, and a series of mission verbs follows—preach, bind up, proclaim, and comfort.

Verse 3 uses three coordinated exchanges to make restoration tangible: beauty for ashes, joy for mourning, and praise for heaviness. Isaiah 61:1-2 also recalls the Jubilee legislation of [[Leviticus 25]] through its proclamation of liberty and its favorable-year language. This is a substantial background, but it does not require a literal fiftieth-year fulfillment.

Two image systems recur: salvation and righteousness are worn as festive garments, while the restored people, righteousness, and praise are pictured through planting and garden growth.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter expands restoration from wounded people and Zion's mourners to ruined cities, descendants, and recognition before the nations. Its repeated reversals move from ashes to beauty, mourning to joy, waste places to rebuilt cities, and shame to a double portion.

Plant imagery links verse 3, where the restored are called the LORD's planting, with verse 11, where righteousness and praise spring forth like growth from the earth. Garment imagery similarly connects the gift of praise in verse 3 with the garments of salvation and robe of righteousness in verse 10.

The bridegroom and bride in verse 10 are parallel images of adornment for one rejoicing speaker. Broader biblical wedding symbolism may enrich the image, but the verse alone is not a marriage allegory or a statement of marriage doctrine.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs

> [!evidence] Canonical Reception And Explicit Application
> Luke reports Jesus reading language from Isaiah 61:1-2 in the Nazareth synagogue and declaring that the scripture was fulfilled in the hearers' presence. This is strong canonical evidence for an early Christian messianic reading of Isaiah's anointed herald, although it does not by itself settle every question about the passage's original historical referent.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted canonical application · [[Canonical Reception And Explicit Application in Isaiah 61|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Lexical And Institutional Scriptural Parallel
> Isaiah's paired language of proclaiming liberty and an acceptable year has a substantial thematic and lexical connection with the Jubilee legislation, which commands liberty to be proclaimed in the fiftieth year and associates it with return and release. This background illuminates Isaiah's deliverance imagery without proving that the prophecy announces a literal calendrical Jubilee.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strong lexical background; scope disputed · [[Lexical And Institutional Scriptural Parallel in Isaiah 61|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Who is speaking in Isaiah 61:1-3, and what can be inferred from the chapter itself as distinct from the application in [[Luke 4]]?
- Does "the day of vengeance" refer to judgment in the speaker's historical horizon, a future eschatological judgment, or more than one horizon?
- How does the Jubilee background in [[Leviticus 25]] illuminate liberty and the acceptable year without forcing a literal-calendar interpretation?
- Does "Priests of the LORD" describe honor, actual religious function, mediatorial service, or some combination?
- How should readers understand the foreign labor and Gentile wealth of verses 5-6 without converting restoration imagery into a timeless endorsement of ethnic domination or coerced labor?
- Is the first-person speaker in verse 10 the earlier herald, Zion personified, or the restored community?
- Why does Luke's reading end before Isaiah's vengeance clause, and what conclusions, if any, can responsibly be drawn from that boundary?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Isaiah 61:1-2 with [[Luke 4]], where Jesus applies the passage to his ministry, while noting that Luke's wording reflects an ancient Greek textual tradition and also resembles Isaiah 58:6. Compare [[Leviticus 25]] for the proclamation of liberty in the Jubilee year. Jeremiah 31:13 offers a mourning-to-joy parallel, while Jeremiah 32:40 supplies a close everlasting-covenant parallel. Finally, compare [[D&C 138]] as a Latter-day Saint canonical reception of Isaiah's captive-and-prison imagery in connection with redemption of the dead; this later application should not be treated as proof of Isaiah's original historical referent.
