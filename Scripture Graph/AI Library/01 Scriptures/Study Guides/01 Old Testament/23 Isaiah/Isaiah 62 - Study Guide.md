# Isaiah 62 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 62]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 62 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Isaiah 62 portrays [[Zion]] as a city whose apparent abandonment is publicly reversed. Righteousness and salvation become visible; God gives the city new names, delights in it, secures its harvest, and commissions unceasing watchmen. The closing commands prepare a highway and raise a standard before a worldwide announcement that Zion's salvation comes.

The official chapter heading reads this as a last-days prophecy of the [[Gathering of Israel]] and Zion's establishment. That is a meaningful Latter-day Saint framing, but the phrase “last days” does not occur in the chapter itself. The poetic promises can therefore be studied both within that latter-day application and within Isaiah's broader vision of Jerusalem's restoration.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter can be read in three broad thematic units, though its poetry revisits themes rather than describing a rigid sequence:

1. **Verses 1–5 — Public vindication and changed identity.** Zion's righteousness and glory become visible; names of abandonment give way to names of delight and belonging.
2. **Verses 6–9 — Watchmen and divine oath.** Tireless watchmen appeal for Jerusalem's establishment, and the LORD promises that outsiders will no longer consume its produce.
3. **Verses 10–12 — Preparation, proclamation, and final names.** Repeated commands prepare a highway and raise a standard. The chapter concludes by calling the people holy and redeemed and the city sought out and not forsaken.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Zion]] and [[Salvation]]:** Restoration includes visible righteousness, a renewed public identity, security from exploitation, enjoyment of labor, and worship (Isaiah 62:1–4, 8–12).
- **Divine promise and human participation:** The LORD names, delights, swears, establishes, and proclaims, while watchmen remain vocal and others prepare the highway (Isaiah 62:2–11). The chapter presents both kinds of action without defining a complete doctrine of their relationship.
- **[[Prayer]]:** The watchmen's refusal to remain silent and their command to give the LORD no rest provide a forceful image of persistent appeal (Isaiah 62:6–7). Identifying them specifically as prayers, prophets, or heavenly figures goes beyond what the text settles.
- **[[Jews and Gentiles]]:** Gentiles and kings witness Zion's righteousness and glory (Isaiah 62:2), but the verse does not by itself establish their conversion or covenant inclusion.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three clusters of language carry the chapter:

- **Light:** righteousness goes forth as brightness and salvation as a burning lamp (verse 1).
- **Names:** “Forsaken” and “Desolate” are replaced by names expressing delight, belonging, holiness, redemption, and renewed attention (verses 2, 4, 12).
- **Speech and rest:** the opening speaker will not be silent or rest; the watchmen never keep silent; and they give God no rest (verses 1, 6–7).

The KJV forms “Hephzi-bah” and “Beulah” are explained by the verse's own parallel clauses: the LORD delights in Zion, and the land is described as married.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Isaiah 62 gathers several metaphors without reducing Zion to only one of them: light, royal ornament, bride, guarded city, productive land, highway destination, and raised standard. The crown image is especially precise—Zion is the diadem in God's hand, not simply its wearer.

The strongest verbal intertext is [[Isaiah 40]]: Isaiah 62:11 repeats the declaration about the coming figure's reward and work from Isaiah 40:10. Isaiah 62:10 also returns to highway preparation, but changes the explicit focus from preparing the LORD's way in Isaiah 40:3 to preparing “the way of the people.” These links join divine coming with the people's return while leaving the historical direction of literary dependence unresolved.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Who is the “I” who refuses silence in verse 1: the LORD, the prophet, or a poetic intercessor?
2. Are the watchmen prophets, intercessors, civic guardians, or a deliberately inclusive image?
3. How do the material promises about grain and wine enlarge a reading focused only on spiritual salvation?
4. What is gained by treating the names in verses 4 and 12 as public verdicts rather than decorative titles?
5. How does the chapter balance God's unilateral oath with the people's commanded work of preparation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Isaiah 60]], [[Isaiah 61]], and [[Isaiah 62]] together, tracking light, righteousness, salvation, nations or Gentiles, kings, glory, bridal imagery, and praise. Then compare Isaiah 62:10–11 with [[Isaiah 40]]. Note the repeated reward-and-work formula in Isaiah 40:10 and Isaiah 62:11, as well as the changed object of highway preparation: the LORD in Isaiah 40:3 and the people in Isaiah 62:10. These correspondences establish literary relationships or shared formulations but do not determine authorship, date, direction of dependence, or a single historical fulfillment.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
