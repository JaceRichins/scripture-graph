# Isaiah 34 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 34]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 34 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Second Coming will be a day of vengeance and judgment—The indignation of the Lord will be upon all nations—His sword will fall upon the world.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Universal summons and judgment (vv. 1-4):** All peoples are called to hear; armies fall and the heavens dissolve.

2. **Judgment focused on Idumea (vv. 5-8):** Bozrah becomes the setting for slaughter described as the LORD's sacrifice and as recompense for Zion.

3. **The land transformed into desolation (vv. 9-15):** Fire, ruined government, overgrown buildings, and wild creatures replace settled human society.

4. **Written assurance and allotment (vv. 16-17):** Readers are told to consult the book of the LORD, while divine command and allotment guarantee the creatures' possession of the land.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter strongly presents divine judgment as purposeful rather than accidental: verse 8 calls it vengeance and recompense connected with [[Zion]]. It therefore contributes to the scriptural portrayal of [[Final Judgment]] and divine justice. Care is needed, however, when moving from the poetry to a detailed end-time scheme. The text does not specify whether its cosmic and perpetual language is wholly literal, symbolic, hyperbolic, typological, or some combination of these.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV's 'unicorns' (v.7) translates the Hebrew re'em; most modern translations render it 'wild ox' or 'wild oxen' rather than a mythical single-horned creature, though the exact species (commonly proposed as the extinct aurochs) remains debated among Hebrew lexicographers. Verse 11's 'the line of confusion, and the stones of emptiness' renders a Hebrew word pair (tohu and bohu) that also appears together in Genesis 1:2 ('without form, and void') — a genuine and rare lexical match, though whether it reflects deliberate allusion to Genesis or simply describes extreme waste and emptiness is disputed and not verifiable from the English text alone. Three clusters of imagery run through the chapter: sacrificial vocabulary (sword, blood, fat, lambs, goats, rams) for the slaughter at Bozrah (vv.5-7), cosmic-dissolution vocabulary (dissolving heavenly host, rolled scroll, falling leaves and figs) for the collapse of the ordered world (v.4), and desolation vocabulary (pitch, brimstone, thorns, ruined palaces, nesting creatures) for the land's transformation (vv.9-17).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The cosmic imagery of verse 4 — the host of heaven dissolved, the heavens rolled up 'as a scroll,' stars falling 'as a falling fig from the fig tree' — is often read as a 'de-creation' motif figuratively undoing the ordered cosmos of Genesis, though the text does not exclude a more literal or conventionally hyperbolic reading of catastrophic judgment. The scroll and fig-tree images reappear closely in Revelation 6:13-14 ('as a fig tree casteth her untimely figs'; 'the heaven departed as a scroll when it is rolled together'), a widely noted intertextual echo, and possibly in D&C 88:87's falling-fig imagery — though the D&C passage may be mediated through Revelation's wording rather than drawing on Isaiah directly. [[Isaiah 63]] revisits Edom and Bozrah in another vengeance scene dominated by blood imagery (63:1-6), reinforcing Edom's recurring association with divine judgment across Isaiah. Verses 11-15's catalog of ruin-dwelling creatures (owl, raven, satyr, vultures) parallels a similar catalog in [[Isaiah 13]]'s oracle against Babylon, suggesting a shared literary convention for depicting irreversible desolation regardless of which nation is judged. Notably, the ending gives this catalog an unusual sense of order rather than pure chaos: verses 16-17 describe the LORD commanding, gathering, and allotting the land to its new inhabitants 'by line' — language echoing covenantal land-allotment (as in Joshua's division of Canaan) — so that judgment produces a reversal, not merely a destruction, of human dominion.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Is Edom in this chapter a specific historical target, a representative case of judgment against nations opposed to Zion, or both at once?
- What does 'the book of the LORD' (v.16) refer to, and does its assurance cover only the immediately preceding catalog of creatures or the prophecy as a whole?
- Do the cosmic-dissolution images (scroll, falling stars) describe a literal cosmic event, a figurative political collapse, or both at different literary levels?
- Why does the ending (vv.16-17) present the land's re-population by wild creatures as an ordered, divinely allotted possession rather than mere chaos?
- How does the judgment described here relate structurally and thematically to the restoration promised in the immediately following [[Isaiah 35]]?
