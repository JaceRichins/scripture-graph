## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Rebellion and recompense (vv. 1-7):** God extends his hands to an unresponsive people and catalogs their offenses.

2. **Preservation and inheritance (vv. 8-10):** The new-wine image explains why the whole people will not be destroyed; servants and elect inherit and dwell.

3. **Choice and contrasting destinies (vv. 11-16):** Those who forsake the LORD are contrasted with servants who eat, drink, rejoice, and receive a new name.

4. **New creation (vv. 17-25):** Jerusalem, human labor, family life, prayer, longevity, and the animal world are renewed.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds judgment and mercy together. Recompense answers persistent rebellion, yet the blessing within the cluster prevents total destruction (vv. 6–10). Verse 12 gives moral weight to hearing, answering, and choosing, themes that resonate with [[Agency]] without reducing the chapter to a systematic treatment of that doctrine. Restored fellowship is pictured through answered [[Prayer]] (v. 24).

The new creation is embodied and communal as well as cosmic. People inhabit what they build and eat what they plant; their work is neither appropriated nor futile (vv. 21–23). The continued references to longevity, death, family, and agriculture make the vision difficult to equate without qualification with a wholly deathless final state. In Latter-day Saint reception, these conditions are importantly associated with the [[Millennium]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's clearest literary feature is reversal. When God calls in verse 12, the rebellious do not answer; in verse 24, God promises to answer before his people finish calling. Verses 13–14 add four coordinated antitheses: servants eat while others hunger, drink while others thirst, rejoice while others are ashamed, and sing while others cry.

Isaiah 65:21–22 also reverses the covenant-curse pattern of building and planting without enjoying the result in [[Deuteronomy 28]], though shared covenant language does not prove direct literary dependence. The animal peace and holy-mountain language of verse 25 closely parallels [[Isaiah 11]], while the new-heavens-and-new-earth expression is received in [[2 Peter 3]] and [[Revelation 21]]. These comparisons illuminate the chapter without making every vision identical or settling the direction of literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does “new heavens and a new earth” in verse 17 denote a wholly new cosmos, a radically renewed historical order, or prophetic language capable of embracing both?

- How should verse 20, which still speaks of death and a sinner, be related to [[Revelation 21]], where death is abolished?

- Is the new name in verse 15 primarily a sign of changed covenant identity, changed reputation, or both?

- How does Paul's application in [[Romans 10]] illuminate Isaiah 65:1-2 without exhausting its original historical meaning?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Isaiah 65:21–23 with the covenant curses in [[Deuteronomy 28]] and the agricultural restoration in [[Amos 9]]. Read Isaiah 65:25 alongside [[Isaiah 11]], [[2 Nephi 21]], and [[2 Nephi 30]] to trace related holy-mountain peace imagery without assuming a single mechanism of dependence. For later reception of the new creation and longevity themes, compare [[2 Peter 3]], [[Revelation 21]], and [[Doctrine and Covenants 101]]. [[Romans 10]] is essential for studying Paul's application of Isaiah 65:1–2, while [[Isaiah 66]] continues several of the chapter's judgment, servant, Jerusalem, and new-creation themes.
