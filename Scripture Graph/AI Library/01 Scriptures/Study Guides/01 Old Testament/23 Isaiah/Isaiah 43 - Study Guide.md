## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **43:1–7 — Creation, redemption, and gathering.** The repeated "Fear not" frames promises of presence and recovery from every direction.
2. **43:8–13 — The assembly of witnesses.** Nations are summoned into a courtroom-like scene, and Israel witnesses to the LORD's unique identity and saving power.
3. **43:14–21 — Deliverance and the new thing.** Action against Babylon is followed by imagery recalling [[Exodus 14]] and anticipating a transformed wilderness.
4. **43:22–28 — Indictment, forgiveness, and judgment.** Israel's failure is charged, forgiveness is announced, and the judicial language resumes.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Covenant identity as creation.** Israel is repeatedly described with creation verbs — "created," "formed," "made" (vv. 1, 7, 15, 21) — tying national identity and vocation to [[Creation]]: Israel is formed for God's glory and to "shew forth my praise" (v. 21).

**Exclusive deity.** Verses 10-11 make one of the Old Testament's starkest monotheistic statements: "before me there was no God formed, neither shall there be after me... beside me there is no saviour." The chapter's statements about [[Salvation]] are emphatically God-centered — Israel's rescue rests on the Lord's identity and initiative, not a rival power.

**Corporate address.** Though the chapter uses singular second-person language ("thee"), the addressee is corporate Jacob/Israel (vv. 1, 5-7). The promise of presence through water and fire (v. 2) should not be read as an unconditional guarantee that every individual escapes physical harm; it speaks to the [[Adversity]] and preservation of the covenant people as a whole.

**Forgiveness amid indictment.** Verse 25's "I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions for mine own sake" is a key text for [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], but it sits between an indictment of Israel's neglected worship (vv. 22-24) and continuing judgment (vv. 26-28). The chapter does not explain how divine initiative in forgiveness relates to ongoing accountability — both are held together without a stated mechanism, and this passage should not be over-generalized into a systematic doctrine of unconditional forgiveness.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly uses legal vocabulary: nations assemble, witnesses testify, parties seek justification, and Israel is invited to plead. A second pattern joins past and future deliverance. Verses 16–17 recall a way through the sea and the defeat of chariots; verses 19–20 announce a way through the wilderness and rivers in the desert. This supports a renewed-exodus reading connected with [[Exodus and Deliverance]], while leaving the exact historical scope of the "new thing" open.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Allusion
> The combination of a way through the sea, mighty waters, chariot and horse, an army brought forth, and enemies who do not rise strongly supports reading Isaiah 43:16–17 as an evocation of Israel's sea deliverance; verses 19–20 then portray the promised new act with reversed geography—a way in the wilderness and water in the desert.

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical / Composition Scholarship *(tentative)*
> The explicit naming of Babylon (v. 14) together with the Exodus-recollection-versus-"new thing" pattern (vv. 16-19) are among the texts cited by historical-critical scholarship to argue that Isaiah 40-55 ("Deutero-Isaiah") addresses an exilic-period audience, in tension with attributing the entire book to the 8th-century prophet alone.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the corporate identity of "Jacob" or "Israel" affect the application of the promises in verses 1-7 — are these guarantees for the nation, not necessarily every individual?
- Why does the text invoke a past sea deliverance (vv. 16-17) immediately before saying not to remember "the former things" (v. 18)? What does the announced "new thing" (v. 19) add to or change from the original Exodus pattern?
- How should the forgiveness announced in verse 25 — grounded in God's own sake — be related to the continued pleading and judgment language of verses 26-28?
- Who is meant by "thy first father" in verse 27 — Jacob, Abraham, Adam, or a more general ancestral figure? The text does not specify.
- What historical referents, if any, best explain the ransom language concerning Egypt, Ethiopia, and Seba (vv. 3-4), and the later action against Babylon and the Chaldeans (v. 14)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Isaiah 43's explicit naming of Babylon (v. 14), together with its "former things"/"new thing" contrast (vv. 18-19), is frequently cited in discussions of Isaiah's composition history: critical scholarship often reads Isaiah 40-55 as addressed to an exilic audience, while traditional readings take the Babylon reference as predictive prophecy from the 8th-century prophet. Isaiah 43 itself is not quoted in the Book of Mormon, so this question cannot be settled by appeal to Nephite chronology; readers interested in the debate should consult critical and traditional commentaries directly rather than assuming either position as settled. Isaiah 48 (quoted in the Book of Mormon as [[1 Nephi 20]]) uses similar "former things"/"new things" language and "for mine own sake" phrasing and is worth comparing, though the exact relationship between the two chapters has not been independently verified here.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
