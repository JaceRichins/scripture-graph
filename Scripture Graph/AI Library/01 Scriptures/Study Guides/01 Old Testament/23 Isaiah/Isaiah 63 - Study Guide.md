# Isaiah 63 — Study Guide

## Overview

> [!info] Chapter heading (official)

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Isaiah 63:1-6 — The warrior from Edom.** Questions about his identity and stained clothing lead to an account of judgment accomplished without human help.

2. **Isaiah 63:7-10 — Mercy, identity, and rebellion.** Praise for the LORD's lovingkindness gives way to the people's rebellion and the grieving of his holy Spirit.

3. **Isaiah 63:11-14 — Remembering the Exodus.** Moses, the sea, the divine arm, and the guiding Spirit recall [[Exodus and Deliverance]].

4. **Isaiah 63:15-19 — The lament.** The speaker appeals to divine compassion, fatherhood, covenant inheritance, and the violated sanctuary. The prayer continues into [[Isaiah 64]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter refuses to separate [[Justice and Mercy]] into unrelated divine attributes. The day of vengeance is also the year of the redeemed (Isaiah 63:4), while the God who judges is remembered for love, pity, and sustaining care (Isaiah 63:7-9).

Covenant memory becomes a form of [[Prayer]]. The petitioners do not appeal to their innocence; they recall both God's former deliverance and their own rebellion. Their hope rests in the LORD's continuing identity as Father and Redeemer (Isaiah 63:16).

References to the holy Spirit are substantial but should not be overread. The Spirit is placed within Israel's history of presence, guidance, rest, and rebellion (Isaiah 63:10-14). This is relevant to [[The Godhead]] and [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], but the passage neither outlines later metaphysical formulations nor describes the ordinance of confirmation.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The dominant images link body, clothing, and power: dyed garments, the winepress, blood, and the LORD's arm. In Isaiah 63:3 the blood belongs to those being trampled, fixing the immediate meaning of the winepress as judgment rather than the shedding of the warrior's own blood.

Two descriptions of time are deliberately paired: "the day of vengeance" and "the year of my redeemed" (Isaiah 63:4). The unequal scale of day and year may rhetorically contrast concentrated judgment with an extended season of redemption, although that nuance is interpretive rather than certain.

The repeated concern for the divine "name" connects the Exodus recital with the final covenant plea. God leads Israel to make himself a glorious name (Isaiah 63:12, 14), and the petitioners contrast themselves with peoples who were not called by that name (Isaiah 63:19).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The opening uses dramatic dialogue: an observer asks who approaches and why his garments are red, and the warrior answers in the first person. The abrupt turn in verse 7 from fury to praise prevents judgment from becoming the chapter's only portrait of God.

Verses 7-19 broadly follow a prayer movement of praise for past mercy, recollection of rebellion, recital of foundational deliverance, description of present distress, and petition for renewed intervention. The Exodus therefore supplies a precedent for the prayer rather than merely decorative background.

Isaiah 63:3-6 has a notable resemblance to [[Isaiah 59]], where the LORD finds no intercessor, saves by his own arm, and is associated with vengeance. This may reflect deliberate intrabook reuse, editorial coordination, or shared prophetic vocabulary; it does not establish common authorship. [[Isaiah 34]] supplies a further Edom-and-vengeance comparison, while [[Revelation 19]] develops related warrior, garment, wrath, and winepress imagery.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the poet place the year of redemption beside the day of vengeance rather than treating them as separate events?

- How does admitting Israel's rebellion in Isaiah 63:10 affect the force of the accusation about divine hardening in Isaiah 63:17?

- What does the appeal to the Exodus assume about the relationship between past revelation and present prayer?

- Why does Isaiah 63:16 appeal directly to the LORD as Father when Abraham and Israel are imagined as failing to recognize the community?

- Which conclusions about the opening warrior come from Isaiah 63 itself, and which depend upon its later use in [[D&C 133]] or [[Revelation 19]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Isaiah 63:1-6 with [[Isaiah 34]], [[Isaiah 59]], [[D&C 133]], and [[Revelation 19]] to trace Edom, divine-warrior, garment, vengeance, and winepress imagery without assuming that every passage assigns identical meaning to each image.

Compare Isaiah 63:7-14 with [[Exodus 14]], [[Psalm 106]], and [[Nehemiah 9]] to examine how prayer can combine deliverance, rebellion, mercy, and the Spirit. Then read Isaiah 63:15 through [[Isaiah 64]] as a continuous lament across the modern chapter division.

Isaiah 63:9 contains a significant Hebrew textual-reading difficulty behind the KJV wording about God being afflicted and the angel of his presence. A critical apparatus and multiple translations should be consulted before building doctrine upon that clause. Isaiah 63:11 and 63:19 also contain grammatical or translation questions that should remain visible in close study.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
