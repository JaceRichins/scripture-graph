## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter does not present faith as denial of danger: the Assyrian flood reaches Judah's neck. Faith instead changes what is treated as ultimate. Isaiah is told not to fear what the people fear but to sanctify the Lord and wait for him even while he hides his face (vv. 11–17). This gives [[Faith]] a disciplined, text-centered form rather than reducing it to optimism.

Verses 19–20 also articulate a principle of discernment. Claims to supernatural guidance are not accepted merely because they sound mysterious; they are measured against the law and testimony. In a modern study setting this supports [[Scripture Study]] and [[Revelation]], though “law and testimony” should first be understood within Isaiah's own prophetic setting rather than automatically equated with a later complete canon.

Christian readers have strong canonical grounds for relating the stumbling stone to [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] because [[Romans 9]] and [[1 Peter 2]] explicitly reuse Isaiah 8:14 in christological contexts. That later application is clear; it does not by itself eliminate the oracle's eighth-century political and covenant setting.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Maher-shalal-hash-baz is conventionally rendered 'hasten to the spoil, hurry to the prey,' making the child's name itself a spoken proclamation of imminent plunder against Damascus and Samaria (8:1-4). The chapter is held together by repeated words and images: gentle 'waters' become an overwhelming imperial river (vv. 6-8); 'Immanuel' in verse 8 is closely echoed by 'God is with us' in verse 10, though in the underlying Hebrew these may be close to the same phrase rather than two separate expressions, so caution is warranted before treating this as deliberate wordplay. The language of breaking appears three times in verse 9 and returns in verses 14-15 through the stumbling stone; 'light' in verse 20 gives way to darkness, dimness, and anguish in verses 21-22.

Several KJV expressions require caution. 'A confederacy' in verse 12 reflects a difficult Hebrew expression (often rendered 'conspiracy') whose precise force is debated; conclusions about a particular political alliance should not rest on the English word alone. 'Familiar spirits' and 'wizards' denote prohibited attempts to obtain information from the dead, as the verse's own closing question indicates.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 9 builds a threefold refrain ('gird yourselves...ye shall be broken in pieces' x2, framed by 'associate yourselves...ye shall be broken in pieces') as a taunt against Judah's enemies. Verse 15 escalates through a chain of verbs — 'stumble, and fall, and be broken, and be snared, and be taken' — dramatizing the stumbling-stone image of v14.

Isaiah and his family function within the message rather than merely reporting it: the son's name, the timing of his infancy, and the declaration that the prophet and his children are signs (vv. 1-4, 18) turn domestic life into enacted prophecy. The Lord's paired roles in verses 13-15 are deliberately severe — the one who is sanctuary is also the stone over which many stumble, a contrast between responses to the same Lord rather than between two different powers. The chapter's movement from a witnessed, written sign (8:1-2) to unrelieved darkness (8:21-22) creates a tonal descent that [[Isaiah 9]] then reverses with 'a great light,' though this may reflect deliberate arrangement of originally distinct oracles rather than proof of single-stage composition.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical Correlation *(tentative)*

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Why does the narrative emphasize writing and named witnesses before describing the child's birth?

2. What does the contrast between Shiloah's gentle waters and Assyria's river imply about rejected and chosen forms of security?

3. Does the flood reaching the neck imply preservation, or only describe the extremity of danger?

4. How can the Lord be both sanctuary and stumbling stone in the same oracle?

5. What did “the law” and “the testimony” most likely denote for Isaiah's first hearers?

6. How should the immediate historical setting and the New Testament's christological uses be held together without collapsing either one?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Isaiah 7]] through [[Isaiah 9]] as a continuous sequence of signs, invasion imagery, darkness, and promised light. Compare [[2 Nephi 18]] closely rather than assuming every wording is identical. For later scriptural interpretation, examine [[Romans 9]], [[1 Peter 2]], and [[Hebrews 2]], noting exactly which portions of Isaiah they reuse and how each author incorporates them into a larger argument.
