## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. **Verse 1 — Victory over Leviathan:** the LORD kills the serpent or dragon of the sea.
2. **Verses 2-6 — The guarded vineyard:** the LORD waters and protects the vineyard; peace is offered, and Israel becomes fruitful.
3. **Verses 7-9 — Measured discipline and purgation:** Jacob's treatment is contrasted with the fate of Jacob's attackers, and sin is removed together with idolatrous objects.
4. **Verses 10-11 — The desolate city:** an unnamed fortified habitation is abandoned and burned.
5. **Verses 12-13 — Gathering and worship:** Israelites associated with Assyria and Egypt are gathered individually to Jerusalem.

The identity of the city in verses 10-11 and its precise relationship to the vineyard section are not explicit.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Gathering of Israel]]**: vv.12-13 describe Israelites gathered individually ("one by one") from [[Scattering of Israel|dispersion in]] Assyria and Egypt at the sound of a great trumpet, to worship at Jerusalem's holy mount.
- **[[Justice and Mercy]]**: the chapter holds judgment and mercy together — the LORD has "no fury" toward the vineyard and burns only hostile briers and thorns (v.4), while offering peace to anyone who will "take hold of my strength" (v.5); Jacob's affliction is also distinguished from the fuller destruction of his attackers (v.7).
- Verse 9 ties the purging of Jacob's iniquity to a concrete sign: the pulverizing of altar stones and the removal of groves and images. This connects to [[Repentance]], though the chapter never uses that word and describes only the removal of outward idolatrous objects, not an inward response.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter's images should not be collapsed into a single literal scene. Leviathan, a cultivated vineyard, briers and thorns in battle, an abandoned city, agricultural beating or gleaning, and a great trumpet form a succession of poetic pictures. Verse 8 is especially difficult in the KJV: the expressions "in measure," "shooteth forth," "debate with it," and "stayeth his rough wind" leave the pronoun referents and exact action unclear. Interpretations resting heavily on that verse should therefore be held cautiously.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The vineyard song (vv.2-6) is best read against [[Isaiah 5]]'s earlier Song of the Vineyard: both share vineyard and "briers and thorns" imagery, but the outcome is inverted — protection, continual watering, and an offer of peace replace the earlier judgment and abandonment. This reuse of a common prophetic image is a plausible deliberate reversal, though shared vocabulary alone cannot prove it was engineered as such.

Verse 1's stacked epithets for a sea-serpent/dragon ("leviathan the piercing serpent," "leviathan that crooked serpent," "the dragon that is in the sea") echo other biblical Leviathan/sea-dragon texts, most closely [[Psalm 74]]:13-14, which shares the same cluster of sea, dragon, and Leviathan vocabulary in a divine-victory scene. [[Job 41]] and Psalm 104:26 also feature Leviathan, but in different literary settings (a divine speech on human limits, and calm praise of creation) rather than combat narratives, so they illustrate the breadth of Leviathan tradition rather than a single uniform combat motif. This cluster of imagery is often linked to the wider ancient Near Eastern combat-myth idiom attested in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle's defeat of the sea-serpent Lotan, though this is comparative literary background, not proof of direct dependence or of what Isaiah's audience specifically had in mind.

The refrain "in that day" (vv.1, 2, 12, 13) loosely structures the chapter, though vv.12-13 likely form one continuous gathering scene rather than two fully separate units.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Scriptural Intertext
> Isaiah 27:1 and Psalm 74:13-14 share a cluster of sea, dragon, Leviathan, and divine-defeat imagery. This is strong evidence of a shared biblical image complex, but only moderate evidence for a direct literary relationship between the passages.

> [!evidence] Explicit Prophetic Statement

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does Leviathan symbolize a particular political power, a cosmic enemy, or poetic chaos? What details in verse 1 actually constrain the answer?
- Who or what is the unnamed fortified city in verses 10-11, and should it be identified with Israel's enemy, Israel itself, or neither?
- Does "one by one" emphasize individual divine attention, a gleaning process, or simply the completeness of the gathering?
- How should the invitation to peace in verse 5 be read alongside the declarations of destruction in verses 1, 4, and 10-11?
- What evidence would be required to distinguish a historical return from Assyria and Egypt from a typological or future fulfillment?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the vineyard units in [[Isaiah 5]] and [[Isaiah 27]], noting who acts, what fruit is produced, and whether the vineyard is protected or exposed. Then compare the gathering language in [[Isaiah 11]] with Isaiah 27:12-13, especially the shared references to Assyria, Egypt, and dispersed Israelites. For Leviathan, read [[Psalm 74]] and [[Job 41]] while keeping their differing literary settings in view.
