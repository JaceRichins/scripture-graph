## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Prophecy and divine initiative**: God offers Ahaz an open-ended sign (7:11) and gives one even after refusal (7:14), showing signs proceed from divine initiative rather than human merit or demand.
- **Sovereignty over geopolitics**: "It shall not stand, neither shall it come to pass" (7:7) asserts the LORD's control over international events — see [[Government and Laws]].
- **Faith versus fear**: hearts "moved... as the trees of the wood are moved with the wind" (7:2) give way to the principle that stability depends on belief (7:9), framing [[Faith]] as consequential under political pressure — though the chapter's own later judgment on Judah (7:17-25) shows this is not a blanket promise that faith prevents all temporal disaster.
- **Immanuel and later Christology**: the name "Immanuel" ("God with us") is applied to Jesus in Matthew 1:23; within Isaiah 7 itself the sign is explicitly timed to the child's early years and the collapse of the Syro-Ephraimite threat (7:16), so the chapter supports both a near-term reading and a later messianic application without requiring either be Isaiah's exclusive meaning.
- **Naming as prophetic sign-act**: both Shear-jashub (7:3) and Immanuel (7:14) accompany the oracle as named figures, a pattern continued with Maher-shalal-hash-baz in Isaiah 8.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV's "virgin" in v.14 translates Hebrew 'almah, a term for a young woman of marriageable age that does not by itself specify virginity; the more specific Hebrew term for virginity, 'bethulah,' is not used here, though even that word's usage is not entirely free of context-dependence. The Greek Septuagint rendered 'almah' as 'parthenos' (virgin), the wording Matthew's Gospel relies on. This is a standard, largely uncontested point among Hebraists, and it qualifies both a contemporary-sign reading (an ordinary young woman known to Isaiah's audience) and the messianic reading built on the Greek and English translations. Commentators have also long noted a wordplay in the Hebrew of v.9 between the roots behind "believe" and "be established" (both from 'aman) — a pun invisible in the KJV's English but reinforcing the verse's logic that national stability and trust are bound together in Isaiah's own language.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The closing section (7:18-25) is structured by a fourfold refrain — "and it shall come to pass in that day" (vv.18, 21, 23) and "in the same day" (v.20) — each introducing a distinct image of coming devastation: foreign forces summoned like a hissed-for fly from Egypt and bee from Assyria (18-19), national humiliation likened to a hired razor stripping head, feet, and beard (20), a pastoral diet replacing cultivated agriculture (21-22), and vineyards reverting to briers and thorns (23-25). "Butter and honey" appears twice (7:15, 22), first as the Immanuel child's food and again in the description of survivors — but the two occurrences are not simply opposite: v.22 explicitly describes "abundance of milk," so the repetition more likely evokes shared pastoral simplicity than a clean irony of prosperity turned scarcity. Readers should hold that ambiguity rather than resolve it in either direction.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Chronological Marker
> The child-development deadline in Isaiah 7:16 is strong internal evidence that the sign has relevance to the political crisis narrated in the chapter: before the child reaches the stated level of discernment, the land associated with the two hostile kings will be forsaken. This does not, by itself, identify the child or exclude a later typological or messianic application.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strong internal marker, well hedged · [[Internal Chronological Marker in Isaiah 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Canonical Reception And Intertextual Quotation
> Matthew 1:22-23 expressly applies Isaiah 7:14 to the conception and birth of Jesus. This is strong evidence for a canonical Christian messianic reading, but it is not by itself proof that Isaiah's sign had no nearer relevance or referent in the crisis of Ahaz.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-hedged, correctly limits scope · [[Canonical Reception And Intertextual Quotation in Isaiah 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Consistency / Historical Geography *(tentative)*
> Alma 7:10 in the Book of Mormon states that the Messiah's mother would conceive and bring forth her son "at Jerusalem which is the land of our forefathers." Because the New Testament places Jesus' birth at Bethlehem rather than the city of Jerusalem (Matthew 2:1; Luke 2:4-7), this wording has drawn both apologetic and critical attention as a genuine point of tension connected to the Immanuel prophecy of this chapter.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · genuine disputed tension, fairly presented

> [!evidence] Prophecy/Historical Correlation And Textual Criticism *(tentative)*
> The clause "within threescore and five years shall Ephraim be broken, that it be not a people" (v.8) is a specific, checkable-sounding chronological prediction. Some interpreters correlate it with Assyria's later resettlement of foreign populations into former Israelite territory (the background to 2 Kings 17:24) roughly two generations after Ahaz's crisis, while many critical scholars instead regard the clause as a later scribal insertion, since it breaks the otherwise tight structural parallel between the Syria/Damascus/Rezin sequence (v.8a) and the Ephraim/Samaria/Remaliah's-son sequence (v.9a).
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · disputed; proposed correlation chronologically shaky

> [!evidence] Translation And Linguistics *(tentative)*
> The Hebrew word behind the KJV's "virgin" in v.14 is generally identified as 'almah,' a term for a young woman of marriageable age that does not by itself specify virginity the way the more precise Hebrew term 'bethulah' would; the Greek Septuagint nonetheless renders it 'parthenos' (virgin), the wording Matthew's Gospel relies on to present the verse as fulfilled in Jesus' birth.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.6 · core lexical point valid, bethulah overstated

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean that God gives Ahaz a sign even after Ahaz refuses to ask for one — what does that say about the relationship between divine signs and human faith or merit?
- The Hebrew word behind "virgin" in v.14 ('almah) means "young woman" and does not by itself require virginity — how does that affect your reading of the sign's original meaning versus its later application in Matthew 1:23?
- Why does Isaiah bring his son Shear-jashub to meet Ahaz, and what might the boy's presence and name communicate even though the chapter never explains it?
- Ahaz frames his refusal to ask a sign as reverence ("I will not tempt the LORD"); how do you distinguish genuine humility from a refusal that masks unbelief?
- Does the repeated phrase "butter and honey" (7:15, 22) describe the same kind of life in both places, or two different situations that happen to share a stock phrase?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
