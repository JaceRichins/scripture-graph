## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **Isaiah 28:1-4:** Ephraim's proud and fading crown is condemned.

- **Isaiah 28:5-6:** The LORD becomes the residue's crown, spirit of judgment, and strength.

- **Isaiah 28:7-13:** Priests and prophets err through drink; refused speech culminates in falling, breaking, ensnaring, and capture. The speakers and tone of verses 9-10 are disputed.

- **Isaiah 28:14-22:** Jerusalem's scornful rulers trust false security; God answers with a sure foundation, measured righteousness, and an unavoidable scourge.

- **Isaiah 28:23-29:** An agricultural discourse describes varied cultivation and threshing under divine instruction.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter contrasts false confidence with trust in God's sure foundation. Jerusalem's rulers claim safety through falsehood, but their refuge and covenant cannot withstand judgment (vv. 15-20). God places a tested stone in [[Zion]] (v. 16); [[1 Peter 2]] explicitly applies that cornerstone to [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]], though Isaiah 28 does not specify the stone's sole original historical referent.

The repeated “line upon line” formula concerns divine speech and human reception. In Isaiah 28 its immediate effect is judicial: the people refuse to hear and are broken and taken (vv. 12-13). Its positive use for continuing [[Revelation]] in [[2 Nephi 28]] and [[D&C 128]] is a later canonical reuse, not a reason to erase Isaiah's severe context.

The closing agricultural discourse may also be read in relation to [[Justice and Mercy]]: different crops receive different treatment, and threshing is not endless (vv. 24-28). That application is suggestive rather than explicit because verse 23 begins a fresh unit.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated wording in verses 10 and 13 should be read with its narrative effect in view. Verse 12 says the people would not hear; verse 13 then associates the repeated formula with falling, breaking, ensnaring, and capture. Whether verses 9-10 voice mockers or the prophet's description of elementary instruction is disputed, but the immediate context is more severe than the phrase's familiar positive use in [[2 Nephi 28]] and [[D&C 128]].

The chapter repeatedly converts boastful images into judgment: a crown becomes a fading flower, a refuge becomes lies, a protective agreement is annulled, and a supposedly adequate bed and covering prove unusable.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Three image systems organize the chapter. First, the crown motif contrasts transient human glory with the LORD as the remnant's crown (vv. 1-6). Second, storm, flood, foundation, measuring line, and plummet contrast destructive judgment with sound construction (vv. 2, 15-18). Third, the farming discourse moves from breaking soil to sowing and threshing (vv. 23-29). Its varied methods provide a measured counterpart to the chapter's overwhelming storm imagery.

The Perazim and Gibeon allusions recall occasions when God fought Israel's enemies. Here those victory memories intensify the warning to Jerusalem: covenant identity does not make persistent mockery and falsehood immune from judgment.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Canonical Intertextual Reception

> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · Established Christian canonical interpretation · [[Canonical Intertextual Reception in Isaiah 28|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Original Vs Devotional Readings *(tentative)*
> Many scholars read the repeated 'precept upon precept... line upon line' (Isaiah 28:10, 13) as the drunken priests' mocking, sing-song imitation of Isaiah's preaching — echoing v11's 'stammering lips and another tongue' — rather than as a straightforward description of a divine teaching method; the same phrase is nonetheless repurposed devotionally elsewhere (e.g., 2 Nephi 28:30) to describe gradual revelation.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Who is speaking in verses 9-10: the prophet, the intoxicated leaders mocking him, or a deliberately ambiguous voice?

- What did the cornerstone first signify to Isaiah's audience, and how does the explicit christological use in [[1 Peter 2]] develop that earlier image?

- How does the differentiated treatment of crops in verses 24-28 qualify the preceding announcement of a determined consumption?

- In what sense is God's work at Perazim and Gibeon a precedent for the "strange work" threatened here?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Isaiah 28:11-12 with [[1 Corinthians 14]] to examine how Paul uses Isaiah's unheeded foreign speech. Compare Isaiah 28:16 with [[Isaiah 8]], [[Romans 9]], and [[1 Peter 2]] to trace the developing stone imagery. Read [[Isaiah 30]] alongside the covenant-with-death passage, while noting that Isaiah 28 itself never explicitly identifies the rulers' policy with Egypt.
