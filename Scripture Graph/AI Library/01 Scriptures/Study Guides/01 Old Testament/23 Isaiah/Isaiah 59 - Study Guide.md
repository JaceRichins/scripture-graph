# Isaiah 59 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 59]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 59 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The people of Israel are separated from their God by iniquity—Their sins testify against them—The Messiah will intercede, come to Zion, and redeem the repentant.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has four principal movements:

1. **Indictment (vv. 1–8):** Sin separates the people from God; violence, falsehood, and crooked paths destroy peace.
2. **Lament and confession (vv. 9–15a):** The voice changes to “we,” acknowledging that transgression has left justice, light, and salvation distant.
3. **Divine intervention (vv. 15b–20):** Finding no intercessor, the LORD arms himself with righteousness and salvation, repays adversaries, and promises that the Redeemer will come to [[Zion]] and to the repentant.
4. **Covenant (v. 21):** The Spirit and the LORD's words are promised to the covenant community across generations.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verses 1–2 connect separation from God with human iniquity rather than any inability in God. In context, this diagnosis concerns a society marked by bloodshed, lies, oppression, and failed justice; it should not be generalized into a claim that every experience of divine silence results from a particular personal sin.

The saving turn begins with divine initiative in verse 16, but verse 20 also names turning from transgression. The chapter therefore holds divine action and [[Repentance]] together. Its presentation of recompense and redemption likewise joins [[Justice and Mercy]] rather than reducing God to either judgment alone or unconditional deliverance.

Verse 21 presents [[Covenants]] as involving both the Spirit and preserved divine words. Its generational language portrays durable covenant continuity, although it does not by itself guarantee individual faithfulness irrespective of response.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter maps disorder across the body: hands and fingers enact violence, lips and tongue speak falsehood, thoughts devise iniquity, and feet hurry along destructive paths. Social conditions are also personified: judgment retreats, justice remains distant, truth falls in the street, and equity cannot enter. These choices make injustice concrete, embodied, and public.

The KJV wording of verse 19 pictures an enemy coming “like a flood” and the Spirit raising a standard. Because the Hebrew syntax and modern translations vary, detailed conclusions about the identity of the flood or standard should be held cautiously.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several recurring images help organize the chapter. The LORD's hand is declared able to save in verse 1, and his arm brings salvation in verse 16. The people's webs cannot become garments in verse 6, whereas the LORD wears righteousness, salvation, vengeance, and zeal in verse 17. This clothing contrast is suggestive, though the text does not explicitly mark it as a deliberate reversal.

The shift from accusation to first-person lament at verse 9 becomes explicit acknowledgment of transgression in verses 12-13. Judgment, justice, truth, and equity are personified as retreating, standing far away, falling in the street, or being unable to enter.

The armor in [[Ephesians 6]] probably recalls Isaiah 59:17, especially its breastplate of righteousness and helmet of salvation, but Ephesians combines several scriptural images and places God's armor on believers. [[Romans 3]] reuses Isaiah 59:7-8 within a composite indictment of sin, while [[Romans 11]] adapts verses 20-21 together with other Isaianic language.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the speaker move from accusing “you” and “they” to confessing “we”? Does this represent the prophet identifying with the community, a communal liturgy, or another rhetorical strategy?

How does the unsafe position of the person who departs from evil in verse 15 deepen the chapter's picture of systemic injustice?

What changes when divine-warrior armor from Isaiah 59 is placed on believers in [[Ephesians 6]]?

How do repentance in verse 20 and divine initiative in verse 16 qualify one another?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the divine warrior here with [[Isaiah 11]] and [[Isaiah 63]], especially that the close recurrence within Isaiah does not by itself establish common authorship. Trace the violence-and-peace language into [[Romans 3]], where it appears in a composite scriptural catena. Compare the Redeemer and covenant promise with [[Romans 11]], noting its adapted wording and additional sin-removal language. Finally, examine the armor in [[Ephesians 6]], distinguishing its close breastplate and helmet parallels from the other armor elements drawn from a wider scriptural tradition.

For historical context, many scholars distinguish Isaiah 56-66 from material associated with the eighth-century prophet Isaiah. That widely held post-exilic dating remains a scholarly reconstruction rather than a fact stated within the chapter.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
