# Isaiah 64 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 64]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 64 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Isaiah 64 is a communal lament asking God to become present again. The speakers remember an overwhelming divine descent, confess collective sin, appeal to God as Father and Potter, and point to the devastation of Zion, Jerusalem, and the holy house. The chapter heading's [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]] reading has a strong Latter-day Saint canonical basis in [[D&C 133]], which reuses verses 1–2 in an explicitly latter-day setting. Historically and literarily, however, the chapter also speaks from within a crisis involving a ruined city and sanctuary; the eschatological application need not erase that immediate lament.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **64:1–3 — Petition and remembered manifestation:** God is asked to rend the heavens and descend as he did in earlier, unexpected acts.

2. **64:4–5 — The God who meets the faithful:** Waiting, rejoicing, righteousness, and remembrance are set beside divine wrath and human sin.

3. **64:6–7 — Corporate confession:** Repeated first-person plurals portray communal uncleanness, fading strength, unchecked iniquity, and God's hidden face.

4. **64:8–9 — Appeal from relationship:** Father, Potter, clay, handiwork, and "thy people" become grounds for a plea that wrath and remembered iniquity not last forever.

5. **64:10–12 — Devastation and unresolved question:** Zion, Jerusalem, and the holy house lie waste; the prayer ends by asking whether God will remain silent.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Repentance]] to trust in divine relationship. Confession is candid: the community does not excuse its iniquities. Yet guilt is not its final identity; it still addresses the Lord as Father, Potter, and owner of a people fashioned by his hand. This creates a productive [[Justice and Mercy]] tension: divine wrath is acknowledged, while mercy is sought on the basis of belonging, dependence, and the hope that judgment is not endless.

Verse 6 should be read within this communal lament. Its comparison of "all our righteousnesses" to filthy rags intensifies the confession of a compromised people; it should not be isolated from verses 4–5, which affirm waiting for God, rejoicing, working righteousness, and remembering him. The passage supports humility and dependence on divine mercy more directly than a systematic claim that righteous action has no value.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three repetitions of "at thy presence" dominate verses 1–3. Later expressions reverse that desired presence: "thou hast hid thy face" (verse 7) and "wilt thou hold thy peace" (verse 12). The movement from **come down** to **hidden face** and **silence** supplies the lament's central emotional tension.

The repeated collective terms—"we," "our," and "all"—also matter. The prayer is voiced by a wounded community, not merely an isolated penitent. Its metaphors progress from fading leaves driven by wind to clay held by a potter, shifting from helpless dispersal to purposeful formation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The opening uses theophanic imagery: heaven is rent, God descends, mountains flow, fire burns, water boils, and nations tremble. Exodus 19:18 offers a useful comparison because the Lord descends amid fire while the mountain reacts, though such imagery also belongs to a wider biblical vocabulary of divine appearance and does not prove a deliberate Sinai allusion.

The prayer recalls earlier deeds beyond expectation as grounds for seeking renewed intervention. Within [[Isaiah 64]], the final questions receive no stated answer, preserving the experience of waiting and protest characteristic of lament. The divine speech beginning in [[Isaiah 65]] may be read as a response to the larger prayer, but the precise literary relationship should not be reduced to a simple question-and-answer pair. [[Prayer]] here includes urgent questioning alongside confession and reverence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Textual Reception
> D&C 133:40–42 closely reproduces the opening petition and imagery of Isaiah 64:1–2 within an explicitly latter-day scene of the Lord's coming. This is strong evidence for Isaiah 64's canonical reception as Second Coming language in Latter-day Saint scripture, but it does not establish that the historical sense of Isaiah 64 was exclusively eschatological.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · mechanically verified canonical reception evidence · [[Canonical Textual Reception in Isaiah 64|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Quotation Comparison *(tentative)*
> Isaiah 64:4 is echoed in the New Testament by 1 Corinthians 2:9 ('Eye hath not seen, nor ear heard...'), but Paul's wording there differs noticeably from both the Hebrew (Masoretic) and Greek (Septuagint) forms of Isaiah 64:4, a divergence long discussed in New Testament textual scholarship.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · echo and textual divergence accepted; explanation unsettled

> [!evidence] Internal Textual/Historical Dating Evidence *(tentative)*
> Isaiah 64:11's past-tense description of the temple as already 'burned up with fire' is cited by many biblical scholars as internal evidence that this oracle (part of Isaiah 56-66, sometimes called 'Trito-Isaiah') reflects a setting after the 586 BC destruction of Solomon's temple, rather than exclusively the eighth-century ministry of Isaiah ben Amoz. This bears on longstanding authorship/dating debates about the book of Isaiah.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · historical association widely held; composition inference disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the repeated contrast between God's presence and hiddenness shape the prayer?
- Why does the community appeal both to its guilt and to its identity as God's handiwork and people?
- How do verses 4–5 qualify an interpretation of verse 6 that dismisses every form of human righteousness?
- What changes when the opening is read in its ruined-temple context, in the Second Coming setting of [[D&C 133]], or in both settings typologically?
- What does the unanswered question in verse 12 teach about faithful lament?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Isaiah 63]] with [[Isaiah 64]] as a continuous prayer, especially Isaiah 63:15–19. Compare the descent imagery cautiously with [[Exodus 19]], recognizing that similar theophanic imagery appears elsewhere. Compare the potter metaphor with [[Jeremiah 18]], where the emphasis falls more clearly on the potter's authority to reshape a nation.

Trace later scriptural reception by comparing Isaiah 64:4 with [[1 Corinthians 2]] and Isaiah 64:1–3 with [[D&C 133]]. The Pauline passage echoes Isaiah but is not a verbatim reproduction of the surviving Hebrew or Greek forms; D&C 133:40–42 provides a verified Latter-day Saint reuse in a Second Coming setting. [[Psalm 44]] may also be explored as a thematic comparison for communal lament and divine hiddenness, not as an established textual dependency.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
