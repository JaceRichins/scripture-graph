# Isaiah 2 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 2]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 2 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter moves from an ideal vision to a severe indictment. Isaiah 2:1–5 depicts nations ascending to the LORD's house to receive instruction, followed by international judgment and peace. Verses 6–22 accuse Jacob of misplaced abundance, idolatry, and pride, then portray the day of the LORD as the humiliation of everything lofty. The official Latter-day Saint heading connects these scenes with latter-day temples, gathering, millennial peace, and the Second Coming. Those are coherent theological readings, but the chapter itself does not identify a particular modern temple or give a detailed end-time chronology.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. **Superscription (v. 1):** Isaiah's vision concerning Judah and Jerusalem.
2. **The exalted house and international peace (vv. 2–4):** nations seek divine teaching; judgment leads to disarmament.
3. **Invitation and indictment (vv. 5–9):** Jacob is summoned to walk in light, then accused of foreign practices, material and military accumulation, and idolatry.
4. **The day of the LORD (vv. 10–21):** proud humanity and other lofty things are lowered; idols disappear; people flee into rocks.
5. **Concluding admonition (v. 22):** mortals should not receive ultimate reliance or valuation.

Verse 5 is especially important: it can be read both as the response demanded by the opening vision and as the transition to the ensuing rebuke.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The opening movement joins [[Temples]], [[Zion]], instruction, judgment, and [[War and Peace]]. Peace is not presented merely as the absence of combat: people learn the LORD's ways, walk in his paths, submit disputes to judgment, repurpose weapons, and cease learning war. The second movement challenges [[Pride]] and confidence in human products, power, and wealth. Its final imperative stresses mortal frailty.

Readings involving the [[Gathering of Israel]], [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]], and [[Millennium]] belong to a broader canonical and Latter-day Saint interpretive framework. They should not be represented as though every detail were explicitly stated in Isaiah 2.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Vertical language unifies much of the chapter. The LORD's house is “exalted” in verse 2, while proud people and elevated objects are brought low in verses 11–17 so that the LORD alone is exalted. The repeated description of the land as “full” in verses 7–8 links wealth, military resources, and idols without necessarily declaring that material possessions are intrinsically evil. The image of nations “flowing” upward to a mountain is striking and may suggest an irresistible collective movement, although the English wording alone cannot settle the image's precise force.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
[[Micah 4]] contains the closest biblical parallel to Isaiah 2:2–4. The overlap securely indicates shared tradition, but several historical models remain possible: borrowing in either direction, use of a common source, or later editorial alignment. [[2 Nephi 12]] parallels nearly the entire chapter and should be compared verse by verse, including its differences rather than only its agreements.

The chapter's strongest internal contrast is between legitimate exaltation and illegitimate loftiness: the LORD's house and the LORD are exalted, while human pride and its symbols are lowered. The three rock-refuge scenes intensify the judgment section through repetition.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does verse 5 conclude the nations vision, introduce the indictment of Jacob, or deliberately perform both functions?
- Is the mountain primarily the Jerusalem temple mount, a symbol of divine rule, or imagery capable of bearing both senses?
- What relationship does the text imply among receiving instruction, divine judgment, disarmament, and lasting peace?
- Why are wealth, horses, chariots, and idols placed in a single repeated “full of” sequence?
- Does verse 22 warn against all human trust, or specifically against treating mortal power as ultimate?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Isaiah 2:2–4 closely with [[Micah 4]], noting both agreement and Micah's continuation beyond the shared material. Compare the whole chapter with [[2 Nephi 12]], documenting variants before proposing explanations for them. Isaiah 2:20 may also be compared with [[Isaiah 31]], where silver and gold idols are again cast away. Reading [[Isaiah 1]] and [[Isaiah 3]] alongside this chapter helps situate its vision and indictment within the surrounding sequence.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
