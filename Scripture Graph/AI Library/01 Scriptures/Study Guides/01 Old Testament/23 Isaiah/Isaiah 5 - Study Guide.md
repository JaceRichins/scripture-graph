# Isaiah 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 5]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Lord’s vineyard (Israel) will become desolate, and His people will be scattered—Woes will come upon them in their apostate and scattered state—The Lord will lift an ensign and gather Israel—Compare 2 Nephi 15.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **5:1-7 — Song of the vineyard.** Careful cultivation (vv1-2), appeal for judgment (vv3-4), sentence and self-identification of the vineyard as Israel/Judah (vv5-7).
2. **5:8-24 — Six woes.** Land accumulation (v8); drunken feasting that ignores 'the work of the LORD' (vv11-12); defiant unbelief (vv18-19); moral inversion (v20); self-conceit (v21); bribery and corrupted judgment (vv22-23) — each followed or capped by a specific judgment (desolate houses, collapsed yields, captivity, Sheol's hunger, humbling of the proud).
3. **5:25-30 — Anger and the distant army.** V25 ends with the refrain 'his hand is stretched out still,' repeated verbatim at [[Isaiah 9]] 9:12, 17, 21 and [[Isaiah 10]] 10:4 — a real textual link, though the refrain itself only marks v25; vv26-30 form their own vivid unit describing an efficient, remorseless invading force summoned by an ensign and a hiss.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Justice and Mercy]]** — v7's pivot from expected 'judgment'/'righteousness' to actual 'oppression'/'a cry' makes the gap between hoped-for and actual justice the oracle's central point; v16 answers it: the LORD himself 'shall be exalted in judgment' and 'sanctified in righteousness' even when His people fail to be.
- **[[Apostasy]]** — v24 traces the coming judgment directly to the people having 'cast away the law of the LORD' and 'despised the word of the Holy One of Israel.'
- **[[Pride]]** — v21's woe on those 'wise in their own eyes' names self-sufficiency as its own covenant failure alongside land-grabbing, drunkenness, and bribery.
- Covenant accountability — the vineyard is given every advantage and still yields bad fruit; careful cultivation intensifies rather than removes responsibility, and the woes extend that accountability beyond private vice into public social and judicial conduct (vv8-10, 20-23).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Isaiah 5:7 reaches its climax through Hebrew sound-play: *mishpat* (justice/judgment) is answered by *mispach* (commonly rendered bloodshed or oppression), and *tsedaqah* (righteousness) by *tseaqah* (a cry). The similar sounds and opposite outcomes make Israel's failure audible as well as conceptual.

In Isaiah 5:14, the KJV's “hell” represents Hebrew *Sheol*. The verse personifies the realm of death as an insatiable mouth swallowing Jerusalem's splendor and population. Reading the English word as a complete definition of final postmortem punishment would place more doctrinal specificity on the verse than its immediate imagery supplies.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The song of the vineyard shifts genre as it unfolds — love song, legal appeal, verdict in the owner's own voice, then decoding of the metaphor — a juridical-parable structure loosely comparable to Nathan's parable to David (2 Samuel 12:1-7), though unlike David, Isaiah's audience is never shown rendering a verdict.

Isaiah 5:7's climax turns on Hebrew sound-play: mishpat ('judgment') answered by mispach ('bloodshed'/oppression), and tsedaqah ('righteousness') by tse'aqah ('a cry') — pointed correspondences paired with opposite meanings, though this feature belongs to the underlying Hebrew and can't be confirmed from the KJV English alone.

Both [[Matthew 21]] and Mark 12 open their parable of the wicked husbandmen with the same distinctive cluster of details as Isaiah 5:1-2 (vineyard, hedge, winepress, tower), a widely-recognized deliberate evocation — though the Gospel parables redirect blame to the tenants rather than the vineyard itself, and shouldn't be read as one-to-one allegorical matches with Isaiah's song.

Some readers additionally note that the sixth woe (v22-23) is followed one chapter later by Isaiah's own 'Woe is me' (Isaiah 6:5) and ask whether this forms a seventh, self-directed woe — an intriguing but minority literary observation with no named scholarly source behind it, offered here as a question rather than an established structure.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Hebrew Phonetic Wordplay
> The Hebrew conclusion of Isaiah 5:7 uses two conspicuous sound-pair contrasts: mishpat/mispach and tsedaqah/tseaqah. The KJV represents the semantic contrasts as judgment/oppression and righteousness/a cry; many modern translations render mispach as bloodshed. The phonetic resemblance sharpens the reversal between what the Lord expected and what he found.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted; drop the unverified Landy citation · [[Hebrew Phonetic Wordplay in Isaiah 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Hebrew Lexical Clarification
> The word translated “hell” in the KJV at Isaiah 5:14 is Hebrew Sheol. In this verse it is personified as opening its mouth without limit while Jerusalem's glory and multitude descend. The immediate image is the voracious realm of death; the verse alone does not define a later Christian doctrine of final punishment.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · uncontroversial lexical clarification · [[Hebrew Lexical Clarification in Isaiah 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Philological/Literary *(tentative)*
> A similar sound-play reportedly opens the song: the Hebrew word for the 'grapes' hoped for (anabim) and the 'wild grapes' actually produced (be'ushim, closer to 'stinking/rotten things') are commonly noted in commentaries as another instance of Isaiah using near-rhyme to underline the gap between expectation and result, bracketing the song (v2) and its climax (v7) with the same rhetorical device.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · weakly sourced, phonetic similarity overstated

> [!evidence] Evidence *(tentative)*
> In Isaiah 5:7, Isaiah deliberately uses wordplay between the similar-sounding Hebrew words mishpat ('justice') and mishpach ('bloodshed'/'oppression'), and between sedaka ('righteousness') and tsa'aqah ('outcry'), to underscore how close justice and injustice can sound while being opposite in meaning.
> — confidence medium · secondary-claim

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Isaiah frame his message as a song about someone else's vineyard, addressed to Jerusalem and Judah as judges, rather than a direct accusation — what does the delayed reveal in v7 accomplish?
- How do the six woes (vv8-23) give concrete social and legal content to the 'wild grapes' of v2 and the 'oppression'/'cry' of v7?
- Does 'because they have no knowledge' (v13) describe missing information, failure to recognize the Lord's work (echoing v12), or deeper culpable blindness?
- Isaiah 11:12 later uses 'ensign' for gathering scattered Israel — how does that compare with the judgment-ensign of 5:26, which summons an invading force against Judah?
- Some readers read Isaiah's 'Woe is me' (Isaiah 6:5) as completing the sixfold woe series of this chapter into seven. Does the text support that reading, or are these independent units?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare the vine/vineyard image for Israel's covenant relationship across scripture: [[Psalm 80]] (a vine brought out of Egypt), [[Jacob 5]] (the allegory of the tame olive tree in the Lord's vineyard), and [[Matthew 21]] / Mark 12 (the parable of the wicked husbandmen, opening with vocabulary drawn directly from Isaiah 5:1-2).
- Read [[Isaiah 9]] and [[Isaiah 10]] alongside this chapter's closing refrain ('his hand is stretched out still,' v25) — the identical wording recurs four more times in that judgment sequence.
- For contrast, [[Isaiah 27]] returns to vineyard imagery but describes the Lord keeping and watering it toward eventual fruitfulness — worth reading against this chapter's desolation.
