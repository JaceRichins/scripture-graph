# Isaiah 48 — Study Guide

## Overview

> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Lord reveals His purposes to Israel—Israel has been chosen in the furnace of affliction and is to depart from Babylon—Compare 1 Nephi 20.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A coherent reading follows five movements:

1. **Isaiah 48:1-8 — Summons and accusation:** Israel's religious identity is contrasted with obstinacy, while prior and new declarations prevent either idols or the audience's supposed prior knowledge from receiving credit.
2. **Isaiah 48:9-11 — Restrained judgment:** Israel is not cut off; affliction and preservation are tied to the LORD's name and glory.
3. **Isaiah 48:12-16 — Divine identity and the agent against Babylon:** the first and last, creator of earth and heaven, calls an unnamed agent to act against Babylon. Verse 16 introduces a debated speaker transition.
4. **Isaiah 48:17-19 — Instruction and forfeited blessing:** peace, righteousness, and posterity are presented as consequences Israel could have enjoyed through [[Obedience]].
5. **Isaiah 48:20-22 — Departure and warning:** redeemed Jacob leaves Babylon in a new-exodus scene, but the chapter ends by denying peace to the wicked.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents redemption as morally serious grace. Israel is called treacherous and subjected to a furnace of [[Adversity]], yet is not annihilated. Divine teaching identifies the profitable way, while verses 18-19 preserve meaningful moral consequence: disobedience has cost Israel peace and covenant blessings. The text also connects divine sovereignty with [[Creation]] and rejects any transfer of divine glory to idols.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Repeated hearing language organizes the chapter: Israel is told to hear in verses 1, 12, 14, and 16, even though verse 8 says its ear had not been opened. Repetition intensifies key assertions: "I, even I" in verse 15 and "for mine own sake, even for mine own sake" in verse 11. Verses 18-19 use counterfactual grammar, so their images of river-like peace, sea-like righteousness, and sand-like posterity express lost possibilities rather than present conditions.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The strongest literary pattern is the new-exodus sequence in verses 20-21: departure from Babylon, leading through deserts, freedom from thirst, and water from the rock. This portrays redemption through imagery associated with Israel's foundational deliverance, without requiring every Mosaic-exodus event to recur literally. The sand imagery in verse 19 also recalls the [[Abrahamic Covenant]], but appears within a lament over blessing forfeited through disobedience.

Within the Latter-day Saint scripture collection, [[1 Nephi 20]] is the direct textual parallel to the complete chapter. The extensive agreement establishes a textual relationship, not its date or mechanism. Ancient-source, translation, KJV-mediated, and modern-composition explanations remain disputed. Historical-critical dating of Isaiah 40-55 to the late Babylonian exile also creates a chronological tension with the Book of Mormon narrative's placement of the brass plates before Lehi's departure; the overlap itself cannot resolve that issue.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical/Authorship *(tentative)*
> Isaiah 48's explicit, repeated naming of 'Babylon' and 'the Chaldeans' as a specific future judgment target, combined with the announcement of an unnamed loved one who will act against them, is cited by many biblical scholars as evidence for a sixth-century BCE authorial/redactional setting (so-called 'Deutero-Isaiah'), since the passage presupposes the Babylonian exile as a lived reality; this reading is a minority position within LDS interpretive tradition, which more often affirms single Isaianic authorship spanning the eighth century BCE, treating the material as genuine predictive prophecy.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · major methodological and confessional dispute

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the LORD cite his own name and glory as the reason for restraining anger in verses 9-11?
- Is the agent of verses 14-15 also the speaker of verse 16, or does the voice change?
- Does verse 21 predict a renewed miracle, poetically portray the departure from Babylon, or recall the original exodus as assurance?
- How does the closing denial of peace reinterpret the river-like peace offered in verse 18?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
