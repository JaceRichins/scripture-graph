# Isaiah 57 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 57]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 57 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Isaiah 57 opens with the largely unrecognized death of the righteous, who are described as being taken from coming evil into peace and rest (vv. 1–2). It then indicts pervasive idolatry and infidelity expressed through sexual, cultic, and possibly diplomatic imagery (vv. 3–13). The final movement promises a prepared way, revival, healing, comfort, and peace, while closing with the warning that the wicked remain like a restless sea (vv. 14–21). The chapter therefore places [[Apostasy]], [[Humility]], and [[Justice and Mercy]] within a sustained contrast between false securities and trust in God.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The righteous enter peace (vv. 1–2):** Their deaths go largely unrecognized but are described as removal from coming evil.

2. **Infidelity and failed securities (vv. 3–13):** The chapter catalogs idolatrous places, sacrifices, intimate imagery, distant journeys, forgotten loyalty, and powerless alternatives. Verse 13 contrasts these with trust in the LORD.

3. **Revival, healing, and peace (vv. 14–19):** A way is prepared; the exalted God dwells with the contrite; wrath is limited; healing, guidance, and comfort are promised.

4. **The wicked without rest (vv. 20–21):** The troubled sea closes the chapter, followed by the 'no peace' refrain also found in [[Isaiah 48]].

Peace and rest form a strong thematic frame, though calling the arrangement a formal inclusio remains an interpretation.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Isaiah 57:15 joins divine majesty with compassionate nearness: the One who inhabits eternity and the high and holy place also dwells with the humble and contrite to revive them. This gives the chapter a direct connection to [[Humility]].

Verses 16–19 hold judgment and mercy together. God was angry and struck the offender, yet will not contend forever because human spirits and souls are his creations. Healing, guidance, comfort, and peace then follow. The sequence emphasizes divine restorative initiative, but it does not explicitly narrate the offender's repentance or prove unconditional restoration; the relationship between the contrite of verse 15 and the stubborn figure of verses 17–19 remains interpretive.

Verse 13 is also relevant to [[Faith]]: competing sources of deliverance are swept away, while the one who trusts in the LORD inherits the land and holy mountain.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter accumulates sexual and relational vocabulary: adulterer, whore, exposure, bed, lover, and covenant. Because the accused exposes herself 'to another than me,' the imagery strongly suggests infidelity toward the LORD, while potentially retaining reference to literal sexual, cultic, or political conduct.

A suggestive high-and-low contrast also develops. Illicit sacrifice occurs on a 'lofty and high mountain' (v. 7), whereas the true 'high and lofty One' dwells with the humble and contrite (v. 15). This may deliberately challenge the assumption that ascent to a cultic high place produces nearness to God.

Peace includes rest, revival, healing, and restored wholeness in this chapter. Its opposite is portrayed through the turbulent sea, which cannot rest and continually casts up mire and dirt.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
A peace-and-rest motif unifies the received chapter. The righteous enter peace and rest near the beginning; verse 19 proclaims 'Peace, peace' to the far and near; and the wicked cannot rest and have no peace at the end. This is a strong thematic frame, although it does not by itself establish single authorship or a particular editorial history.

Isaiah 57:14 belongs to Isaiah's recurring way-preparation imagery. Its doubled command closely parallels [[Isaiah 62]], while 'prepare the way' more loosely recalls [[Isaiah 40]].

The proclamation of peace to the far and near in verse 19 is probably echoed in [[Ephesians 2]]. Paul's Jew-and-Gentile application should not be read back as Isaiah's only possible original meaning, and Ephesians may combine Isaiah 57:19 with other Isaianic peace language.

The chapter's infidelity and child-sacrifice imagery also has a useful thematic parallel in [[Ezekiel 16]], without proving direct literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Verbal Allusion
> The shared combination of peace with those who are “far off” and “near” is strong textual evidence that Ephesians 2:17 reuses or alludes to Isaiah 57:19. Ephesians applies the wording within its argument about the reconciliation of Gentiles and Jews through Christ.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · probable Isaianic allusion; exact route debated · [[Canonical Verbal Allusion in Isaiah 57|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual Echo *(tentative)*
> Isaiah 57:19's phrase 'Peace, peace to him that is far off, and to him that is near' resembles Paul's statement in Ephesians 2:17 that Christ 'preached peace to you which were afar off, and to them that were nigh,' describing the reconciliation of Jew and Gentile. Some scholarship treats this as Paul echoing Isaiah's language. However, the chapter's own automated text-overlap check found zero verbatim overlap with Ephesians 2, so this should be treated as a possible conceptual/verbal echo rather than an established quotation.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · probable echo; precise source relationship debated

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Thematic Parallel *(tentative)*
> Isaiah 57:3-9 develops a sustained marriage/adultery metaphor for covenant unfaithfulness combined with child-sacrifice imagery; Ezekiel 16 develops the same governing metaphor (Israel as an unfaithful wife) at much greater length, and it too explicitly mentions sacrificing children (Ezekiel 16:20-21). This supports treating the two chapters as thematically parallel witnesses to the same prophetic idiom, but does not establish direct literary dependence of one on the other.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Does 'taken away from the evil to come' refer primarily to a particular approaching catastrophe, or does it also offer a broader consolation concerning righteous death?

2. What are the 'companies' expected to deliver the accused in verse 13—idols, allies, or a broader collection of false securities?

3. How should the promised healing in verses 18–19 relate to the contrite spirit in verse 15, given that verse 17 says the offender continued stubbornly?

4. Does the high mountain of false sacrifice in verse 7 intentionally anticipate the contrast with the true high and holy dwelling in verse 15?

5. How does [[Ephesians 2]] preserve and extend Isaiah 57:19's language of peace to the far and near?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Isaiah 57:14 with [[Isaiah 40]] and [[Isaiah 62]] to distinguish the chapter's immediate command to remove a stumbling block from Isaiah's broader way-preparation motif. Compare Isaiah 57:15 with [[Psalm 51]] and [[Isaiah 66]] for related treatments of the contrite spirit.

Isaiah 57:19 and [[Ephesians 2]] share the proclamation of peace to those far off and near. The correspondence is substantial, but further study should consider whether Ephesians directly alludes to Isaiah 57, combines it with Isaiah 52's peace proclamation, or draws on an inherited scriptural formula.

The composition and dating of Isaiah 56–66 remain disputed. Literary coherence within the received chapter does not by itself resolve whether the material comes from one prophetic voice or an editorially arranged collection.
