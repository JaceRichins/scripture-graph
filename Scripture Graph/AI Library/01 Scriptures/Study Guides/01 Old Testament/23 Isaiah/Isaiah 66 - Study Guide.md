# Isaiah 66 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Isaiah 66]] · My notes: [[Isaiah 66 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Isaiah 66 closes Isaiah with paired visions of judgment and restoration. The LORD cannot be contained by a human building, yet he looks toward the poor and contrite person who trembles at his word. Rebellious worshippers face recompense, while marginalized servants receive vindication. Zion then appears as a mother who gives birth, nourishes, and comforts her children. The final movement expands outward to all nations and tongues, sends witnesses to distant peoples, gathers dispersed brethren to Jerusalem, and anticipates enduring new creation and worldwide worship. The last verse deliberately leaves judgment, as well as consolation, in view.

The official Latter-day Saint heading interprets the nation's sudden birth and fiery advent in relation to latter-day Israel and the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]]. That is a coherent theological reading, but it should be distinguished from the text's direct statements: Isaiah 66 speaks of Zion's children, the coming of the LORD in fire, worldwide gathering, and judgment without explicitly using the later Christian expression Second Coming.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful reading divides the chapter into four movements:

1. Isaiah 66:1-6 — true responsiveness to God contrasted with rebellious ritual; rejected servants are promised vindication.
2. Isaiah 66:7-14 — Zion's astonishing birth and Jerusalem's maternal consolation.
3. Isaiah 66:15-17 — the LORD's fiery judgment against enemies and illicit worshippers.
4. Isaiah 66:18-24 — nations gathered to see divine glory, witnesses sent abroad, brethren brought to Jerusalem, new creation, universal worship, and the continuing disgrace of transgressors.

These movements are tied together by recurring contrasts: hearing versus refusing to hear, God's servants versus his enemies, acceptable offering versus abomination, and joy versus judgment.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Isaiah 66 places the worshipper's disposition beside questions of temple and sacrifice. The LORD looks to the poor and contrite person who trembles at his word, while verses 3-4 condemn people who choose evil and refuse his call (Isaiah 66:1-4). This supports connections with [[Humility]], [[Obedience]], [[Sacrifice]], and [[Temples]]. It does not settle whether verse 3 primarily condemns insincere performance, syncretistic rites, or a corrupt contemporary cultic establishment.

The restoration scene joins [[Zion]], [[Gathering of Israel]], and [[Jews and Gentiles]]. Nations see divine glory, witnesses carry that proclamation outward, and brethren are brought from all nations to Jerusalem as an offering (Isaiah 66:18-20). The international scope does not establish universal salvation, since the chapter continues to distinguish servants from enemies and worshippers from transgressors.

Verse 21 does not make the antecedent of “them” sufficiently explicit to establish whether the promised priests and Levites come from returned Israelites, Gentile participants, or the gathered community more generally. Verse 23 uses new moons and Sabbaths to portray recurring worship, but the language alone does not determine whether Mosaic calendar institutions continue literally or function as inherited imagery for regular worship.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The verb *choose* creates a precise moral reversal in Isaiah 66:3-4: the people choose their own ways and what God does not delight in, so God chooses their delusions. Hearing language forms another contrast. The rebellious do not answer when God calls, whereas faithful servants tremble at his word (Isaiah 66:2, 4-5).

The phrase *all flesh* functions in two sharply different settings. In Isaiah 66:16, all flesh stands within the scope of judgment; in Isaiah 66:23, all flesh comes to worship. This repeated expression gives both accountability and worship a universal horizon, without proving that the same outcome applies to every individual.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's most sustained image is maternal. Zion gives birth with impossible suddenness; Jerusalem nurses, carries, and dandles her children; and the LORD says that he will comfort them as a mother comforts her child (Isaiah 66:7-13). The image moves from national restoration to intimate consolation without requiring that every element be read literally.

Cultic imagery changes direction across the chapter. Early sacrifices are treated as abhorrent because the offerers choose evil (Isaiah 66:3-4). Later, gathered people are brought as an offering in a clean vessel to the house of the LORD (Isaiah 66:20). The contrast suggests that acceptable worship depends on a transformed and gathered people, not merely on performance of ritual.

The conclusion is intentionally severe. New creation, continued seed and name, and recurring worship are followed by the sight of unburied transgressors (Isaiah 66:22-24). Any reading that presents the ending only as comfort misses this final warning.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the chapter first question the house built for God but later speak positively of the house of the LORD? How do Isaiah 66:1-4 and 66:20 distinguish inadequate ritual from acceptable offering?

Does the pronoun them in Isaiah 66:21 refer to Gentiles, returned Israelites, or the gathered population as a whole? What conclusions remain possible if the antecedent is left unresolved?

How should the two uses of all flesh in Isaiah 66:16 and 66:23 be held together? Does the phrase indicate identical individuals, a worldwide scope, or prophetic generalization?

What is gained—and what is potentially obscured—when the sudden birth of Zion's children is summarized as Israel becoming a nation in one day?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
