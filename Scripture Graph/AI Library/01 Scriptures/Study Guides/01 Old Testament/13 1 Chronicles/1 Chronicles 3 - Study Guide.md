## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv. 1-4** — Six sons born to David in Hebron, one per named wife; closes with David's total reign of 40 years (7½ in Hebron, 33 in Jerusalem).
- **vv. 5-9** — Sons born in Jerusalem, led by Solomon (son of Bath-shua/Bathsheba), a further list of nine names, and a closing note on concubines' sons and Tamar.
- **vv. 10-14** — An unbroken, one-name-per-generation line from Solomon to Josiah.
- **vv. 15-16** — The pattern breaks: Josiah's four sons are listed, then Jehoiakim's line to Jeconiah — including a genealogically difficult second 'Zedekiah' (see Questions).
- **vv. 17-24** — Jeconiah's descendants branch through Pedaiah to Zerubbabel, then continue several more generations into sibling groups reaching the Persian period.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several names in this chapter appear differently elsewhere in scripture — worth noting as genuine textual variation rather than harmonizing away. 'Daniel' (3:1), David's second Hebron-born son, is called 'Chileab' in [[2 Samuel 3]]:3 — almost certainly the same person under two names, though the text doesn't say why they differ (this Daniel is not the prophet Daniel of the exile, despite the shared name). Solomon's mother is 'Bath-shua the daughter of Ammiel' here (3:5) but 'Bathsheba the daughter of Eliam' in 2 Samuel 11:3 — the same individual under alternate name forms; the exact relationship between the two spellings isn't established by the text. 'Assir' (3:17) is ambiguous in Hebrew and may be a title ('the captive,' describing the exiled Jeconiah) rather than a son's personal name — translations differ. And the Jerusalem-sons list has its own internal puzzle: 'Elishama' and 'Eliphelet' each appear twice (3:6, 3:8) to reach a stated total of 'nine' (3:8), while the parallel list in [[2 Samuel 5]]:15-16 has only one 'Elishua' and one 'Eliphalet' — suggesting a name was duplicated or altered somewhere in transmission.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter shifts from a horizontal list format (multiple sons per wife, vv. 1-9) to a vertical single-line succession (vv. 10-14), then branches horizontally again for Jeconiah's scattered descendants (vv. 17-24). Comparison with [[2 Samuel 3]] and [[2 Samuel 5]] shows Chronicles arranging material also known from narrative contexts, but stripped of the stories of rivalry and violence attached to these same names elsewhere — a selectivity that concentrates attention on descent and continuity rather than implying the omitted narratives were unknown or unimportant. Readers may find the narrowing-then-branching shape a fitting picture of a kingdom that concentrated into one royal line, was broken by exile, and then continued as a dispersed but still-tracked family — though that reading is an interpretation of the form, not something the text states outright.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary And Genealogical Evidence
> The preservation of several generations after Jeconiah is strong internal evidence that the genealogy's interest continues beyond the sequence of named royal successors. It supports an interpretation of continuing concern for Davidic descent, but it does not by itself demonstrate the historical accuracy of every relationship or a promise of restored kingship.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · well-calibrated, best claim in either set · [[Internal Literary And Genealogical Evidence in 1 Chronicles 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual-Critical *(tentative)*
> The Jerusalem-born-sons list in 1 Chronicles 3:6-8 diverges from the parallel list in 2 Samuel 5:14-16: 'Nogah' is absent from Samuel's version, and Samuel reads 'Elishua' where Chronicles has a second 'Elishama,' pointing to independent transmission or scribal variation between the two source lists.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed — comparison misaligned per critique

> [!evidence] Translation Ambiguity *(tentative)*
> 'Assir' in 1 Chronicles 3:17, listed first among Jeconiah's descendants, may be a common noun meaning 'the captive' describing Jeconiah himself (who was taken captive to Babylon per 2 Kings 24:15) rather than the personal name of a distinct son; the Hebrew is genuinely ambiguous and translations differ.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · genuine translation ambiguity

> [!evidence] Compositional Dating Argument *(tentative)*
> The genealogy extends at least six generations beyond Zerubbabel — through Hananiah, Shechaniah, Shemaiah, Neariah, and Elioenai's seven sons (1 Chronicles 3:21-24) — which some scholars use to argue for a compilation date for (this portion of) Chronicles well into the Persian period, though the exact number of generations and their spacing is debated due to textual variants between the Masoretic Text and the Septuagint at verse 21.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · speculative, uncited

> [!evidence] Textual Crux *(tentative)*
> 1 Chronicles 3:16 lists 'the sons of Jehoiakim: Jeconiah his son, Zedekiah his son,' which sits in tension with 2 Kings 24:17, where the king renamed Zedekiah (originally Mattaniah) is identified as Josiah's son and Jehoiakim's brother, not Jehoiakim's son — a recognized genealogical crux in this verse.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.5 · disputed textual crux — do not assume identity of the two Zedekiahs
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does 1 Chronicles 3:1 call Abigail's son Daniel, when 2 Samuel 3:3 calls him Chileab — alternate personal names, or a difference in transmission?
- 1 Chronicles 3:15 names Zedekiah as Josiah's third son; 3:16 names a 'Zedekiah' as Jehoiakim's son. Are these the same person, two different men who share a name, or does the text reflect corruption or telescoping? (2 Kings 24:17 complicates a simple reading, since it makes the reigning Zedekiah Josiah's son and Jehoiakim's brother, not his son.)
- Why is Zerubbabel called 'son of Pedaiah' here (3:19) but 'son of Shealtiel' throughout Ezra, Haggai, and Zechariah — and why do Matthew 1 and Luke 3 each name a different father for Salathiel/Shealtiel in their own genealogies?
- What does it mean that this line survives, generation after generation, through the trauma of exile — and how might that shape a reader's confidence in God's providence over the covenant line even when no miracle is narrated in this chapter?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
