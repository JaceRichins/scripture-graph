# 1 Chronicles 22 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David prepares gold, silver, brass, iron, stone, and cedar wood for the temple—He charges Solomon to do the work of building it.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv. 1–5** — David identifies the site and gathers materials and labor (stone, iron, brass, cedar) "in abundance," explaining that he prepares in advance because Solomon is "young and tender" and the house must have unmatched "fame and glory."
- **vv. 6–13** — Charge to Solomon: David explains why he himself may not build (bloodshed and wars, v. 8), reports the oracle naming Solomon and promising him rest and an established throne (vv. 9-10), and makes Solomon's future prosperity conditional on keeping "the statutes and judgments which the LORD charged Moses with" (v. 13).
- **vv. 14–16** — David inventories the resources already prepared (gold, silver, brass, iron, timber, stone, and skilled workers) and commands Solomon to begin.
- **vv. 17–19** — David charges "all the princes of Israel" to help Solomon, grounding the appeal in the rest God has already given the nation (v. 18) and defining the sanctuary's purpose as bringing in "the ark of the covenant... and the holy vessels of God" (v. 19).
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Temples]] with both divine authorization and human preparation. Sacred work requires materials, skill, leadership, and collective assistance, but David still describes success as dependent upon God's presence and Solomon's [[Obedience]] to the [[Law of Moses]].

The contrast between David and Solomon contributes to the chapter's treatment of [[War and Peace]]. David's accomplishments do not erase the bloodshed associated with his reign; the designated builder is instead associated with rest, peace, and quietness. This is a narrative-specific judgment and should not automatically be turned into a universal rule about every warrior or leader.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter uses repeated abundance language: materials are prepared "in abundance," brass and iron are "without weight," and precious metals are said to have "no number." This repetition makes overwhelming provision part of the literary portrait, even if the historical magnitude of the figures remains difficult to assess.

Rest is also repeated strategically. Solomon will receive rest from his enemies (v. 9), while the princes are told that God has already given rest on every side (v. 18). The statements may distinguish David's achieved subjugation from the more settled peace expected under Solomon, but the text does not explain the temporal relationship explicitly.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 9 pairs the naming of Solomon with the promise of "peace and quietness" for Israel — in Hebrew, the name Shelomoh shares its root with shalom ("peace"), so the verse likely encodes a Hebrew name-wordplay. An English reader can see the thematic pairing of name and promise, but the wordplay itself depends on the underlying Hebrew, not on anything visible in translation alone.

David's charge in verse 13 — "be strong, and of good courage; dread not, nor be dismayed" — echoes the stock succession formula used for Joshua's commissioning in [[Deuteronomy 31]]:6-8, 23, and recurs again in David's own later charge to Solomon in 1 Chronicles 28:20. This looks like a conventional formula for commissioning a leader to an unfinished, divinely authorized task, rather than a single unique literary borrowing; it need not imply that temple-building and the conquest of Canaan are being equated in scale.

The resources David reports preparing (v. 14) — "an hundred thousand talents of gold, and a thousand thousand talents of silver" — are extraordinary by any standard measure of ancient talents. The chapter's repeated language of things done "in abundance," "without weight," and having "no number" (vv. 3, 14, 16) suggests the numbers function partly as rhetorical emphasis on the temple's unmatched glory; whether they also reflect a literal accounting, a different unit convention, or textual corruption in transmission cannot be settled from this chapter alone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should the already-given rest of verse 18 be related to the future rest promised to Solomon in verse 9?

- Does the contrast between David and Solomon condemn warfare generally, excessive bloodshed specifically, or simply explain the divinely assigned division of roles?

- What can be responsibly concluded from the enormous resource figures in verse 14, and what requires historical or textual evidence beyond this chapter?

- What social status and working conditions are implied by the term "strangers" in verse 2? The chapter does not provide enough information to answer fully.
