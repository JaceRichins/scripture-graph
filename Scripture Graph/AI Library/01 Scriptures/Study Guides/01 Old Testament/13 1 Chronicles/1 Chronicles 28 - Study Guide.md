# 1 Chronicles 28 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Chronicles 28]] · My notes: [[1 Chronicles 28 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David assembles the leaders of Israel—Solomon is appointed to build the temple—David exhorts Solomon and the people to keep the commandments—David gives Solomon the pattern and materials for the temple.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Public assembly and covenant setting (vv. 1–8):** David gathers Israel's leaders, explains why he cannot build, identifies Solomon as God's choice, and exhorts the congregation to keep the commandments.

2. **Direct charge to Solomon (vv. 9–10):** Solomon must know, serve, and seek God, then act courageously on his assignment.

3. **Transfer of the temple pattern (vv. 11–19):** David supplies architectural, organizational, liturgical, and material specifications that the narrative attributes to divine disclosure.

4. **Renewed commission and communal support (vv. 20–21):** The command to be strong returns, accompanied by assurance of God's presence and the support of priests, Levites, skilled workers, leaders, and people.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter places divine choice beside continuing responsibility. Solomon is chosen as son, king, and temple builder, yet the establishment of his kingdom is expressed conditionally and he is warned not to forsake God (vv. 5–9). This supports the limited conclusion that election does not eliminate [[Obedience]]; the chapter does not by itself settle every question about the wider Davidic covenant.

Verse 9 gives service an inward dimension: Solomon must serve with a complete heart and willing mind because God searches hearts and understands thoughts. Verse 21 also presents [[Service]] as communal. A chosen leader remains dependent on worship officers, artisans, rulers, and people. David's exclusion should be treated as a differentiation of assignments, not automatically generalized into a doctrine that all warfare or bloodshed is morally equivalent.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrases "house of rest," "footstool of our God," and "kingdom of the LORD" connect the temple and monarchy with divine presence and rule (vv. 2, 5), though their wider theological implications remain interpretive.

The pattern is described as received "by the spirit" and understood "in writing by his hand upon me" (vv. 12, 19). These expressions attribute the design to [[Revelation]] but do not clearly identify its medium: the text may refer to a received document, inspired written plans, or understanding granted while God's hand was upon David.

Verse 18 explicitly uses "chariot" imagery for the cherubim. The wording is distinctive and worth comparison, but the verse alone does not establish a developed throne-chariot theology.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The technical inventory in verses 11–19 stands between two related commands to be strong and act (vv. 10, 20). This creates a visible frame around the temple pattern, although a deliberately constructed inclusio remains an interpretation.

The closing charge resembles the commissioning language in [[Deuteronomy 31]] and [[Joshua 1]]: strength and courage, freedom from fear or dismay, and assurance that God will not fail or forsake the successor. A still closer internal comparison is [[1 Chronicles 22]], where David gives Solomon a similar charge. These parallels make commissioning tradition a useful lens, but they do not prove direct borrowing or an exclusive Solomon-Joshua analogy.

The revealed "pattern" also invites comparison with [[Exodus 25]]. The comparison connects the temple with Israel's earlier sanctuary tradition without erasing the differences between tabernacle and temple or establishing a particular direction of literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Cumulative Narrative Presentation
> Within the narrative, Solomon's authority is reinforced by public assembly, explicit divine choice, Davidic endorsement, a revealed plan, allocated resources, organized priests and Levites, skilled workers, and support from leaders and people. This cumulative presentation strongly supports interpreting the chapter as a literary legitimation of Solomon's succession and temple commission.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strong cumulative interpretation · [[Cumulative Narrative Presentation in 1 Chronicles 28|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Unique Vocabulary / Imagery *(tentative)*
> 1 Chronicles 28:18 uniquely describes the cherubim pattern as 'the chariot of the cherubims,' framing the ark's cherubim in explicitly vehicular/throne-chariot terms not used in the tabernacle cherubim description elsewhere in the Torah.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · wording explicit; uniqueness and theology unverified

> [!evidence] Verbal And Narrative Parallel *(tentative)*
> The combination of "be strong and of good courage," the prohibition against fear or dismay, and the assurance that God will neither fail nor forsake the successor closely parallels the Moses-to-Joshua commissioning language. This is strong evidence for a deliberate literary analogy between Solomon's succession and Joshua's succession, although it cannot by itself establish direct borrowing or its direction.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · verbal cluster accepted; deliberate analogy disputed

> [!evidence] Comparative Textual Analysis *(tentative)*
> The explicit conditional clause in 1 Chronicles 28:7 ('if he be constant...') stands in some tension with the more unconditional-sounding wording of the parallel Davidic covenant promise in 2 Samuel 7, which the Chronicler is generally understood to be drawing on.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · difference in emphasis accepted; theological tension disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should the conditional promise in verse 7 be related to the language of a kingdom established "for ever"?

Why does David distinguish knowing God from serving God in verse 9, and how are the "perfect heart" and "willing mind" related?

What does David's exclusion from construction—but extensive participation in preparation—suggest about the difference between receiving a calling and supporting another person's calling?

How do divine revelation and ordinary administration work together in verses 11–21?

Does the language borrowed from a military succession setting acquire a different force when applied to building a sanctuary?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

Compare the commissioning language with [[Deuteronomy 31]] and [[Joshua 1]], and the revealed sanctuary pattern with [[Exodus 25]].
