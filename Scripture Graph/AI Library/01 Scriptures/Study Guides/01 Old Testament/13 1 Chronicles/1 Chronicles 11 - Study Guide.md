## Overview

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Accession at Hebron (vv. 1–3):** Israel recognizes David, the elders covenant with him, and he is anointed.

2. **Capture and rebuilding of Jerusalem (vv. 4–9):** David takes Zion, Joab gains command, and the city is developed.

3. **Leading heroes and remembered exploits (vv. 10–25):** The narrative recounts the first mighty men, the defense of the barley field, the Bethlehem-water episode, Abishai, and Benaiah.

4. **Extended warrior roster (vv. 26–47):** Men from many regions and backgrounds are named.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's account of kingship joins [[Covenants]], [[Prophecy]], and public action. The LORD's word authorizes David, but Israel must still gather, covenant, anoint, fight, and build. Likewise, the defenders stand in the field and fight, while the narrator declares that the LORD gives the deliverance. The text therefore resists reducing providence either to passive inevitability or to unaided human achievement.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The promise that David will "feed" Israel uses pastoral language for royal leadership alongside the verb "rule." The phrase "bone and flesh" expresses solidarity or kinship as part of Israel's appeal. In the Bethlehem-water episode, David's reference to the warriors' "blood" interprets the water through the mortal risk required to obtain it; it need not mean that he regarded the liquid as literally transformed.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The repeated phrase "all Israel" (vv.1, 4, 10) links David's accession, the capture of Jerusalem, and the warrior tradition into a single account of national support; v.10 functions as a bridge verse, since the mighty men "strengthened themselves with him... to make him king," yet his kingship remains "according to the word of the LORD." The roster preserves an inherited puzzle: "the three" are referenced as an established group in v.15, but only two individuals (Jashobeam and Eleazar) receive named exploits in vv.11-14 — the parallel account in [[2 Samuel 23]] supplies the missing third man, Shammah, whom Chronicles omits. A further silence is worth noting without over-reading it: Uriah the Hittite appears in the honor roll (v.41) with no hint elsewhere in Chronicles of his death at David's arrangement, since the book omits the Bathsheba/Uriah narrative entirely — this may reflect the Chronicler's generally favorable presentation of David, though the silence alone cannot prove intent. The roster also names warriors identified as Ammonite, Hittite, Reubenite, and Moabite alongside Israelites (vv.39, 41-42, 46); these labels show the roster's breadth but do not by themselves establish birthplace or the circumstances of each man's service. Comparison with [[2 Samuel 23]] also shows only partial overlap: roughly sixteen names in 1 Chronicles 11:41b-47 have no counterpart there, suggesting the Chronicler supplemented an inherited roster with additional material.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Textual Variant
> The kill-count for David's chief mighty man differs between the two biblical accounts of this roster: Chronicles gives three hundred (1 Chronicles 11:11), while the parallel figure in the Samuel roster is given as eight hundred (2 Samuel 23:8). This is a recognized numeral discrepancy between the two textual traditions of the same list.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · confirmed numeral variant between parallel traditions

> [!evidence] Argument From Silence / Redaction Pattern *(tentative)*
> Uriah the Hittite appears in Chronicles only here, listed with honor among David's mighty men (v.41), with no acknowledgment anywhere in the book that David later arranged his death — because Chronicles omits the entire Bathsheba/Uriah episode found in 2 Samuel 11-12. The silence is notable given the Chronicler's broader tendency to present David's reign in an idealized light.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · accepted observation, disputed motive

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Chronicles retain Uriah's name among David's honored warriors while omitting every trace of the Bathsheba affair that led to his death?
- What accounts for the numeral difference between Jashobeam's three hundred (11:11) and the parallel figure of eight hundred in 2 Samuel 23:8 — scribal error, independent traditions, or something else?
- If "the thirty" is meant as a literal headcount, why does the full list in vv.26-47 include well over thirty names — turnover, replacements, or a fixed corps title?
- Does "all Israel" describe literal universal attendance, representative national action, or a literary emphasis on unity?
- Does David's treatment of the Bethlehem water primarily express reverence toward God, respect for his warriors' lives, royal self-restraint, or all three?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

Note agreements as well as changes in names, numbers, sequence, and attribution. [[1 Chronicles 12]] should also be read as a continuation of the theme of diverse supporters gathering around David.
