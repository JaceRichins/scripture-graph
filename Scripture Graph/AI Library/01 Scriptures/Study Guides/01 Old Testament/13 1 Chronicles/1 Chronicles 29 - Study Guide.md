# 1 Chronicles 29 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Chronicles 29]] · My notes: [[1 Chronicles 29 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
1 Chronicles 29 brings together the Chronicler's temple-preparation narrative and the account of David's reign. The chapter moves in three parts: (1) a public offering scene in which David gives from his own private wealth and invites Israel's leaders to give likewise (vv. 1–9); (2) David's extended prayer of blessing, the chapter's theological center (vv. 10–19); and (3) the transition of the kingdom to Solomon, David's death, and a closing citation of sources (vv. 20–30). Its central concern is less the size of the gifts than the disposition behind them — willingness and wholeness of heart — expressed in the deeper claim that everything offered to God was already God's own (v. 14). The chapter brings [[Temples]], [[Consecration]], and [[Prayer]] together with the narrative of royal succession.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Preparation and voluntary gifts (vv. 1–9):** David describes the project's scale and sacred purpose (vv.1–2), gives an additional personal gift of 3,000 talents of gold and 7,000 talents of silver (vv.3–4), and challenges the leaders — "who then is willing to consecrate his service this day unto the LORD?" (v.5). The chief fathers, tribal and military leaders, and administrators respond with willing offerings (vv.6–8), and the people and David rejoice in the willingness of the giving (v.9).
2. **David's prayer (vv. 10–19):** praise of God's sovereignty (10–12), thanksgiving (13), acknowledgment of human dependence and transience (14–16), self-examination before the God who "triest the heart" (17), and intercession for the people and for Solomon (18–19).
3. **Public worship and succession (vv. 20–25):** the congregation blesses the LORD and sacrifices are offered (20–21); Solomon is anointed king "the second time" and Zadok is anointed priest (22); Solomon takes the throne, wins the submission of Israel's leaders, and is magnified by the LORD (23–25).
4. **Close of David's reign (vv. 26–30):** reign length (40 years: 7 in Hebron, 33 in Jerusalem), David's death, Solomon's succession, and a citation of records associated with Samuel, Nathan, and Gad.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Consecration]] as willing action arising from an upright heart. Its theology of possessions is summarized in verse 14: the people give God what already came from God. This makes generosity an expression of dependence and [[Gratitude]], rather than evidence that worshippers have enriched God.

David's petitions also connect inward disposition with outward [[Obedience]]. He asks that the people's thoughts remain directed toward God and that Solomon receive a perfect heart to keep the commandments and complete the temple. In context, "perfect" most plausibly describes wholehearted loyalty; Solomon's later conduct should caution against treating the prayer as a prediction that he would remain morally flawless.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated vocabulary of willingness and the heart links the offerings with motive (vv. 5–9, 17–19). Material abundance receives extensive attention, yet the prayer denies autonomous human ownership: "all things" come from God (v. 14), and the prepared store is God's own (v. 16).

The expression "throne of the LORD" in verse 23 is a particularly strong statement of delegated kingship. It does not identify Solomon with God; within the prayer's logic, the LORD remains ruler over all, while Solomon governs under that sovereignty.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The movement from inventory to prayer changes how the opening lists are read. Gold, silver, stones, and sacrifices could display royal wealth, but David's prayer redirects attention from possession to divine ownership and willing hearts. The voluntary-offering pattern also resembles [[Exodus 35]], where valuable materials are freely supplied for sacred construction. The parallel is meaningful without requiring the stronger claim that one passage directly copied the other.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrator devote so much space to quantities, and how should readers distinguish the literary message of abundance from claims about exact historical totals?
- What does "perfect heart" mean when the same biblical tradition later records Solomon's failures?
- How does calling Solomon's seat "the throne of the LORD" limit as well as authorize royal power?
- What is implied by Solomon being made king "the second time," and how does [[1 Kings 1]] clarify the statement?
- Were the works associated with Samuel, Nathan, and Gad separate lost documents, sources incorporated into extant books, or conventional source designations?
