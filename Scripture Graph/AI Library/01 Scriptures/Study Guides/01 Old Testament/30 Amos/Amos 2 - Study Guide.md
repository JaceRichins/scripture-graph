## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Moab (2:1–3):** desecration of the king of Edom’s remains; fire, tumult, and the removal of rulers.
2. **Judah (2:4–5):** rejection of the Lord’s law and commandments; fire upon Jerusalem.
3. **Israel’s offenses (2:6–8):** exploitation of the vulnerable, sexual wrongdoing, and abuse connected with worship.
4. **The Lord’s prior gifts (2:9–11):** victory over the Amorite, deliverance from Egypt, wilderness guidance, land, prophets, and Nazarites.
5. **Israel’s reversal of those gifts (2:12):** Nazarites are given wine and prophets are silenced.
6. **Inescapable defeat (2:13–16):** speed, strength, weapons, horses, and courage all fail.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects accountability with both received divine benefits and the treatment of other people. Judah is charged with despising the Lord's law and failing to keep his commandments. Israel's indictment emphasizes harm to the righteous, poor, and meek, while pledged garments appear beside altars and disputed wine is consumed in a house of worship. The passage therefore presents social conduct and worship as inseparable aspects of faithfulness.

Israel's remembered deliverance, land, prophets, and Nazarites sharpen the indictment because the people actively work against the latter two. This supports a theology of responsibility following divine gifts, although Amos 2 does not state a universal rule measuring punishment by knowledge. Moab's judgment for an act against Edom also supports a limited vision of divine accountability beyond Israel without defining identical covenant obligations for every nation.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The formula "for three transgressions ... and for four" is repeated for each nation. Since the ensuing material does not consistently list four separate offenses, the wording is better treated as a rhetorical expression of accumulated transgression than as a numbered inventory. Verse 9 uses paired tree imagery—cedar height, oak strength, fruit above, and roots beneath—to portray the Amorite as formidable and its destruction as comprehensive. Verse 13 is unusually difficult: the King James wording depicts the LORD as "pressed," but the precise force and direction of the cart metaphor should not be made the basis of a major conclusion without consulting the Hebrew and multiple translations.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The Israel oracle turns on a strong contrast between divine action and human response. The Lord declares that he destroyed, brought, led, and raised up (2:9–11); Israel responds by giving Nazarites wine and commanding prophets not to prophesy (2:12). The received text therefore presents Israel's conduct as a reversal of divine provisions, though prophets and Nazarites retain distinct functions.

The conclusion removes every presumed avenue of military security. Speed, strength, might, archery, running, horsemanship, and courage all fail. Seven defeat clauses create a comprehensive picture of helplessness, but the chapter supplies no basis for assigning symbolic significance to the number seven.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the national-oracle sequence in [[Amos 1]] and [[Amos 2]] shape an Israelite audience's response?
- Why does Israel's oracle occupy substantially more space than the judgments against Moab and Judah?
- What does Judah's undefined term “lies” denote, and what cannot be established from this chapter?
- What economic practices may lie behind pledged garments and the “wine of the condemned”?
- What was the status of the “maid” in verse 7, and which proposed interpretations go beyond the wording?
- Does “their god” in verse 8 refer to the Lord, another deity, or deliberately ambiguous compromised worship?
- How do different translations of the cart image in verse 13 affect the transition from accusation to punishment?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Amos 1]] with Amos 2 as one continuous sequence of national judgments. Compare Amos 2:8 with Exodus 22:26–27 and Deuteronomy 24:12–13, Amos 2:11–12 with Numbers 6:1–4, and the command “Prophesy not” with Amos 7:12–13. Amos 5:11–12 further develops the book’s indictment of economic oppression.
