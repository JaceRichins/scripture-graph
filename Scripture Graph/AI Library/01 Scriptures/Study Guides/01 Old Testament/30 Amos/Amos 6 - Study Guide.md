## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Complacent confidence challenged (verses 1–3):** Zion, Samaria, neighboring kingdoms, and the effort to postpone the evil day.
2. **Luxury without grief (verses 4–6):** a cumulative catalogue ending with indifference to Joseph's affliction.
3. **Reversal and devastation (verses 7–11):** captivity replaces banqueting; the city and its houses are struck.
4. **Corrupted justice and false strength (verses 12–14):** images of absurd action on rock lead to the charge that justice has been poisoned; confidence in human power is answered by invasion.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter warns that sacred association, political prominence, and human strength do not substitute for righteousness. Being "at ease in Zion" becomes part of the indictment when ease accompanies violence, indifference to Joseph's affliction, and the corruption of judgment and righteousness.

The passage does not establish that wealth, music, wine, feasting, or physical comfort is intrinsically sinful. Nor should its judgment oracle be generalized into a claim that every historical disaster is divine punishment. Its stated targets include complacency, violence, failed communal concern, corrupted justice, and pride in self-acquired power.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly overturns the elite's self-description. Those "at ease" face woe; those considered first become first in captivity; Jacob's "excellency" becomes an object of divine abhorrence; an apparent military achievement is called "a thing of nought"; and claimed strength is answered by a stronger nation raised against Israel. These reversals make the rhetoric itself enact the collapse of false confidence.

"Joseph" in verse 6 most likely names the northern Israelite community or its Joseph tribes collectively. "Horns" in verse 13 is best read as an image of power, but the chapter does not specify the particular victories behind the boast.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Amos moves from sensory abundance to deprivation. Beds, couches, livestock, music, wine, and ointments fill verses 4–6; captivity, silence, bones, breached houses, poisoned justice, and invasion dominate verses 7–14. Verse 7 marks the reversal: those associated with privileged banqueting become the first taken captive, and their feast ends. The judgment answers the whole preceding indictment, not merely the possession of comforts.

The questions in verse 12 compare corrupted judgment and righteousness with actions on rock that are impracticable, futile, or contrary to sound practice. The poison imagery closely parallels [[Amos 5]], where corrupted judgment is likewise associated with bitterness.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What makes the comforts in verses 4–6 part of an indictment: the objects themselves, their social setting, or the revelers' failure to grieve?
- How does putting the evil day "far away" help bring violence "near"?
- Why does Amos direct the opening woe toward both Zion and Samaria?
- What forms of self-confidence are successively dismantled in verses 1–14?
- Does the reference to great and little houses broaden the threatened consequences beyond the elite who are initially addressed?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Amos 6 with [[Amos 5]], especially Amos 5:7, 11–12, and 21–24. The parallels show that poisoned justice, elite prosperity, and music detached from righteousness belong to a broader argument rather than an isolated criticism. Amos 8:4–6 further develops the connection between prosperity and disregard for the needy. Isaiah 22:12–14 offers a useful canonical parallel in which feasting and drinking replace the response demanded by an approaching crisis.
