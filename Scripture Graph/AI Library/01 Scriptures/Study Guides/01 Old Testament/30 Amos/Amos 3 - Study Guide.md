# Amos 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Amos 3]] · My notes: [[Amos 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Lord reveals His secrets unto His servants the prophets—Because Israel rejects the prophets and follows evil, the nation is overwhelmed by an adversary.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Covenant accusation (vv. 1–2):** deliverance and distinctive relationship establish accountability.

2. **Divine speech and prophetic warning (vv. 3–8):** seven rhetorical questions explore necessary connections among events; verses 7–8 connect divine counsel and speech with prophetic proclamation.

3. **Witnesses and sentence (vv. 9–12):** Ashdod and Egypt observe Samaria's oppression; an adversary will spoil its palaces, and the shepherd image portrays catastrophic loss with only fragmentary remains.

4. **Judgment on worship and wealth (vv. 13–15):** Bethel's altar and the elite houses are condemned together.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Covenant accountability:** Israel's distinctive relationship with God is explicitly joined to punishment for iniquity (vv. 1–2). Covenant privilege does not provide immunity from judgment.

- **Prophetic revelation:** The Lord reveals His counsel to His servants the [[Prophets]], and Amos portrays prophecy as an urgent response to divine speech (vv. 7–8). In this chapter the disclosure concerns warning associated with judgment; broader universal applications require additional theological argument.

- **Justice:** Samaria is condemned for oppression, violence, and robbery stored in palaces (vv. 9–10). The passage targets wealth connected with exploitation rather than declaring possessions inherently sinful.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The verb "known" in verse 2 is best read relationally in context: God plainly knows that other families exist, but Israel alone is described as occupying this distinctive relationship. The word "therefore" is decisive—the relationship brings responsibility.

In verse 6, the KJV word "evil" occurs in a sequence about trumpet alarm and impending invasion. Here it is naturally read as calamity or disaster, not moral wickedness committed by God. Verse 12 is less transparent: the references to the corner of a bed and a couch in Damascus have generated differing explanations, so detailed reconstructions should be treated cautiously.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
- **Rhetorical questions:** Verses 3–6 present seven questions about relationships among actions, conditions, and consequences. Verse 3 may function differently from the more naturalistic examples that follow, so the series should not be treated as a formal causal syllogism.

- **Lion motif:** The lion appears with prey (v. 4), as an analogy for the compelling divine voice (v. 8), and with Israel in its mouth (v. 12), linking warning with judgment.

- **Palace progression:** Foreign palaces are summoned as witnesses, Samaria's palaces store violence and robbery, and its palaces are sentenced to plunder (vv. 9–11).

- **Fragmentary rescue:** The shepherd recovers only legs or part of an ear (v. 12). The image emphasizes catastrophic loss while leaving room for an extremely small remnant.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary Pattern
> The recurrence of the lion image gives the chapter literary coherence: verse 4 considers a lion with prey, verse 8 equates the Lord's speech with a lion's roar, and verse 12 depicts Israel in the lion's mouth. This strongly supports reading verses 3–12 as a coordinated argument about warning and judgment.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong internal pattern with appropriate limitations · [[Internal Literary Pattern in Amos 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Cumulative Textual Evidence
> The convergence of oppression, violence, robbery, palaces, ivory houses, and great houses provides strong internal evidence that systemic social injustice is central to the chapter's indictment, alongside its condemnation of the Beth-el altar.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong cumulative internal evidence · [[Cumulative Textual Evidence in Amos 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Biblical Background *(tentative)*
> In Israelite religious practice, altar horns (projections at each corner, cf. Exodus 27:2) could serve as a place of asylum for someone who grasped them (1 Kings 1:50; 2:28); the announced removal of the horns in Amos 3:14 signals the destruction of the altar's ritual function at Bethel, though the verse itself does not invoke the asylum motif explicitly.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · Background texts relevant; intended asylum allusion uncertain

> [!evidence] Archaeology *(tentative)*
> Archaeological excavations at Samaria (Harvard Expedition, 1908-1910; Joint Expedition, 1931-1935) recovered a large cache of carved ivory plaques and inlays, commonly called the 'Samaria ivories,' demonstrating that ivory-decorated furnishings existed among the northern kingdom's elite, consistent with the social critique in Amos 3:15 and with 1 Kings 22:39's reference to Ahab's 'house of ivory.'
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · Archaeological background credible but insufficiently sourced here

> [!evidence] Historical Corroboration *(tentative)*
> Amos 3:11's unnamed adversary is conventionally identified by biblical historians with the Neo-Assyrian empire, whose forces destroyed Samaria and ended the Northern Kingdom within a few decades of Amos's ministry (recorded in 2 Kings 17:5-6), though the chapter itself names no nation.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · Historically plausible retrospective identification; not internally established
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does Amos 3:7 intend a universal rule about every divine action, or is its immediate claim specifically about revealed warning before covenant judgment?

- Is the shepherd image in verse 12 intended to preserve a small remnant, to prove that destruction occurred, or to evoke both ideas?

- Why are Ashdod and Egypt—foreign societies often condemned elsewhere—made witnesses against Samaria?

- What does the joint destruction of Beth-el's altar and the houses of the wealthy suggest about the relationship between worship and social ethics?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

These parallels illuminate Amos 3 but do not establish direct literary dependence.
