# Amos 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Amos 1]] · My notes: [[Amos 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Amos 1 opens with a superscription locating Amos at Tekoa and dating his words by two royal reigns and an earthquake. The LORD's roar from Zion introduces five patterned judgments against neighboring peoples. Their stated offenses involve severe violence, forced transfer of captives, violated relationship, sustained vengeance, and territorial aggression. Political boundaries therefore offer no exemption from accountability in the chapter, although Amos does not present a complete theory of universal law. The sequence continues in [[Amos 2]], where the rhetoric reaches Judah and Israel.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Amos 1:1:** Amos, Tekoa, the two kingdoms, and the earthquake marker.
2. **Amos 1:2:** The LORD roars from Zion; shepherding lands and Carmel suffer.
3. **Amos 1:3–5:** Damascus—violence against Gilead, destroyed rule, and captivity.
4. **Amos 1:6–8:** Gaza—captives delivered to Edom and Philistine centers destroyed.
5. **Amos 1:9–10:** Tyrus—captives delivered to Edom and a brotherly covenant disregarded.
6. **Amos 1:11–12:** Edom—persistent violence against a brother.
7. **Amos 1:13–15:** Ammon—atrocity for territorial expansion, battle, and royal captivity.

Every oracle uses the three-and-four formula. Fire devouring palaces is the principal punishment refrain. The structure continues without a major break in [[Amos 2]].

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays national power as no exemption from accountability: the foreign peoples are judged for identifiable acts of violence, betrayal, displacement, vengeance, and territorial aggression rather than for explicitly stated violations of Israelite ritual law. This makes the chapter relevant to [[Justice and Mercy]], [[War and Peace]], [[Sin]], and [[Prophecy]]. The Tyrus oracle also touches [[Covenants]], although the "brotherly covenant" is not defined. The Edom oracle's charge that he "pursue[d] his brother with the sword" plausibly draws on the traditional Jacob–Esau kinship between Israel and Edom (see [[Genesis 27]]), though Amos 1 itself does not name Jacob, Israel, or Esau. Amos 1 is not a complete doctrine of judgment: it does not explain repentance, mercy, individual responsibility, or the suffering of innocent people within condemned societies, nor does it independently verify each accusation as history.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The formula "for three transgressions ... and for four" does not introduce an itemized list of exactly four offenses. It probably intensifies the accusation, perhaps expressing accumulated or climactic guilt, following a graduated-numerical pattern attested elsewhere in Hebrew wisdom sayings (compare Proverbs 30's "three things ... yea, four"); the wording may also simply reflect a conventional numerical saying, and the chapter does not settle which. "I will not turn away the punishment thereof" gives each oracle a tone of settled judgment. The KJV phrase "whole captivity" concerns a captive group transferred to Edom, but the English alone does not establish its exact scale or whether the transfer involved deportation, extradition, enslavement, or sale. "Brotherly covenant" and Edom's pursuit of "his brother" invoke breached relationship without identifying the parties fully within this chapter. Two names in this chapter can mislead an English reader: the "house of Eden" associated with Damascus (Amos 1:5) is a place name and should not be assumed to mean the Genesis Garden of Eden, and "the children of Ammon" (Amos 1:13) is a national designation, not the Book of Mormon missionary Ammon.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Amos 1 is highly patterned: five units repeat a messenger formula, numerical accusation, named offense, and punishment, while fire that devours palaces recurs in every unit. Gaza and Tyrus form a close pair through their nearly parallel accusations about captives delivered to Edom; Edom then becomes the next accused nation. The chapter's opening line, "The LORD will roar from Zion, and utter his voice from Jerusalem" (Amos 1:2), closely parallels [[Joel 3]] 3:16, one of the clearest verbal echoes among the Minor Prophets, though the chapter does not establish which text is prior or whether both draw on a shared idiom. These features strongly support deliberate arrangement in the received text. Reading onward into [[Amos 2]] reveals the pattern's turn toward Judah and Israel and discourages using the foreign-nation oracles merely to validate hostility toward outsiders. The regularity does not establish a single author or compositional stage; conventional formulas or editorial arrangement remain viable explanations.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the three-and-four formula express accumulation, climax, a conventional numerical saying, or some combination of these?
- What historical relationship lies behind Tyrus's “brotherly covenant”?
- How should the national judgments be understood in relation to individual responsibility and civilian suffering?
- Does the recurring fire language predict particular conquests, employ conventional judgment imagery, or do both?
- How does the turn toward Judah and Israel in [[Amos 2]] reshape the audience's response to Amos 1?
- Which, if any, of the chapter's accusations can be independently correlated with securely dated historical events?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Amos 2]] as the continuation and rhetorical culmination of the oracle sequence. Compare the earthquake notice with [[Zechariah 14]], the treatment of captives with [[Joel 3]], the shared royal dating framework with [[Hosea 1]], and the overlapping foreign-nation oracles in [[Jeremiah 49]] and [[Ezekiel 25]]. Amos 1:5's prediction that Syria would go "into captivity unto Kir" also invites comparison with [[2 Kings 16]]'s account of Assyrian deportation from Damascus, and the Edom oracle's fraternal language invites comparison with the Jacob–Esau narratives in [[Genesis 27]]. These comparisons illuminate recurring prophetic themes and conventions, but similarity alone does not establish that the passages describe the same events, that one text directly depends on another, or that any prediction is thereby confirmed as fulfilled.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
