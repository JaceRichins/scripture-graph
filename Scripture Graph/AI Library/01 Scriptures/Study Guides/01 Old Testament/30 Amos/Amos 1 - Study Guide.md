# Amos 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Amos 1]] · My notes: [[Amos 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Amos 1 opens with a dated superscription, the LORD's roar from Zion, and five patterned judgments on neighboring peoples. The stated accusations concern brutal violence, the transfer of captive populations, broken obligation, sustained vengeance, and territorial aggression. The chapter portrays political boundaries as no exemption from divine judgment for human conduct, while stopping short of a systematic theory of universal law. The sequence continues in [[Amos 2]], where the same rhetoric turns toward Moab, Judah, and Israel.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Amos 1:1:** Superscription—Amos, Tekoa, the two kingdoms, and the earthquake marker.
2. **Amos 1:2:** Programmatic announcement of the LORD's voice and its effects.
3. **Amos 1:3–5:** Damascus—violence against Gilead, destruction, and captivity.
4. **Amos 1:6–8:** Gaza—transfer of captives and the fall of Philistine centers.
5. **Amos 1:9–10:** Tyrus—transfer of captives and disregard of a brotherly covenant.
6. **Amos 1:11–12:** Edom—persistent violence against a brother.
7. **Amos 1:13–15:** Ammon—atrocity for territorial expansion, followed by battle and royal captivity.

Each national oracle begins with the three-and-four formula and announces punishment; fire consuming palaces is the dominant refrain. The sequence continues in [[Amos 2]].

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays national power as no exemption from accountability: the foreign peoples are judged for identifiable acts of violence, betrayal, displacement, vengeance, and territorial aggression rather than for explicitly stated violations of Israelite ritual law. This makes the chapter relevant to [[Justice and Mercy]], [[War and Peace]], [[Sin]], and [[Prophecy]]. The Tyrus oracle also touches [[Covenants]], although the “brotherly covenant” is not defined. Amos 1 is not a complete doctrine of judgment: it does not explain repentance, mercy, individual responsibility, or the suffering of innocent people within condemned societies, nor does it independently verify each accusation as history.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The formula “for three transgressions ... and for four” does not introduce an itemized list of exactly four offenses. It probably intensifies the accusation, perhaps expressing accumulated guilt or moving toward a climactic named offense; the wording may also reflect a conventional numerical saying. “I will not turn away the punishment thereof” gives each oracle a tone of settled judgment. The KJV phrase “whole captivity” concerns a captive group transferred to Edom, but the English alone does not establish its exact scale or whether the transfer involved deportation, extradition, enslavement, or sale. “Brotherly covenant” and Edom's pursuit of “his brother” invoke breached relationship without identifying the parties fully within this chapter.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Amos 1 is highly patterned: five units repeat a messenger formula, numerical accusation, named offense, and punishment, while fire that devours palaces recurs in every unit. Gaza and Tyrus form a close pair through their nearly parallel accusations about captives delivered to Edom; Edom then becomes the next accused nation. These features strongly support deliberate arrangement in the received text. Reading onward into [[Amos 2]] reveals the pattern's turn toward Judah and Israel and discourages using the foreign-nation oracles merely to validate hostility toward outsiders. The regularity does not establish a single author or compositional stage; conventional formulas or editorial arrangement remain viable explanations.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does the three-and-four formula accomplish if it is not an exact itemized count?
- Why are acts against people, rather than foreign ritual practices, the stated grounds for judgment?
- How does the continuation in [[Amos 2]] alter an Israelite audience's response to the opening oracles?
- What relationship is designated by Tyrus's “brotherly covenant,” and who is Edom's unnamed “brother”?
- Does the repeated fire language describe particular conquests, conventional judgment imagery, or both?
- What questions about rulers, civilians, collective punishment, and individual responsibility remain unanswered?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Amos 2]] as the continuation and rhetorical culmination of the oracle sequence. Compare the earthquake notice with [[Zechariah 14]], the treatment of captives with [[Joel 3]], the shared royal dating framework with [[Hosea 1]], and the overlapping foreign-nation oracles in [[Jeremiah 49]] and [[Ezekiel 25]]. These comparisons illuminate recurring prophetic themes and conventions, but similarity alone does not establish that the passages describe the same events or that one text directly depends on another.
