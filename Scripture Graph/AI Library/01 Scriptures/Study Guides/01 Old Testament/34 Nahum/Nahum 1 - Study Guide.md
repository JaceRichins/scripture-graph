# Nahum 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Nahum 1]] · My notes: [[Nahum 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Nahum speaks of the burning of the earth at the Second Coming and of the mercy and power of the Lord.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Superscription (v. 1):** a burden concerning Nineveh and the vision of Nahum.
2. **The LORD’s character and appearance (vv. 2-6):** patience and power accompany judgment; storm, drought, earthquake, fire, and collapsing terrain portray his presence.
3. **Refuge and destruction contrasted (vv. 7-8):** the LORD is good and a stronghold to those who trust him, while flood and darkness pursue his enemies.
4. **Opposition brought to an end (vv. 9-11):** plots against the LORD fail, and an unnamed “wicked counsellor” is introduced.
5. **Release and condemnation (vv. 12-14):** an afflicted addressee is promised freedom from yoke and bonds, while another addressee—or a shifted aspect of the oracle—is condemned through the ending of a name, images, and life. The KJV leaves some pronoun assignments uncertain.
6. **Good tidings to Judah (v. 15):** peace is announced, and Judah is commanded to keep its feasts and perform its vows.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Nahum 1 presents divine patience and moral accountability together. The LORD is “slow to anger,” good, and a refuge to those who trust him; he is also powerful and does not acquit wickedness. This is relevant to [[Justice and Mercy]], though the chapter itself emphasizes patience, refuge, and judgment rather than explaining how mercy and justice are reconciled for one offender.

The chapter also connects deliverance from oppressive rule with covenant worship. The LORD promises to break a yoke and burst bonds, and Judah is then commanded to keep its feasts and perform its vows. This is conceptually related to [[Exodus and Deliverance]], but the passage does not explicitly call Judah’s release a new exodus.

The official chapter heading applies the earth-shaking imagery to the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]]. That latter-day application should be distinguished from the chapter’s immediate literary setting: judgment concerning Nineveh and relief for Judah.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly uses images of irresistible natural force: whirlwind, storm, drought, earthquake, melting hills, fire, shattered rocks, flood, darkness, thorns, and dry stubble. These images do more than predict military defeat; they portray the LORD as sovereign over the created order and make human resistance appear futile.

The metaphorical pair in verse 13—"yoke" and "bonds"—turns foreign domination into bodily servitude. Verse 15 reverses that condition with the vocabulary of good tidings and peace.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The theological center is the contrast between verses 7 and 8. The LORD's power is not represented as directionless destruction: he is a stronghold to those who trust him and an overwhelming end to his enemies. The surrounding shifts between Nineveh, an unnamed adversary, and Judah dramatize that double outcome.

Nahum 1:15 closely parallels [[Isaiah 52]], especially Isaiah 52:7. [[Mosiah 12]] reproduces Isaiah's announcement, and [[Mosiah 15]] interprets the publishers of peace. This is a strong textual connection, but it should not be turned into a claim that Mosiah directly quotes Nahum.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual/Philological Parallel *(tentative)*
> Nahum 1:15 ('Behold upon the mountains the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace!') shares close phraseology with Isaiah 52:7, confirmed by mechanical text-overlap detection. Both proclaim a herald's feet 'upon the mountains' bringing 'good tidings' and 'publisheth peace.' If much of Isaiah 40-55 is dated by critical scholarship to the Babylonian exile (mid-6th century BC), that would place it after Nahum's likely composition window (commonly bracketed between Thebes' fall, 663 BC, and Nineveh's fall, 612 BC), meaning Isaiah could not be Nahum's literary source under that dating; conservative/unified-Isaiah authorship instead places all of Isaiah in the 8th century BC, before Nahum, allowing the reverse. Most likely, both draw on a shared prophetic 'herald of good tidings' formula rather than one directly quoting the other.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · Verbal parallel certain; dependence disputed

> [!evidence] Verbal And Theological Intertext *(tentative)*
> Nahum 1:3 closely reuses the scriptural description of the LORD as slow to anger yet unwilling simply to clear guilt, found in Exodus 34:6-7 and Numbers 14:18. This supports reading Nahum's judgment oracle within an established biblical theology in which patience and accountability coexist.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · Hebrew formula plausible; direct reuse unverified here
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Who is addressed by each occurrence of "thee," "they," and "his" in verses 11-14? The alternating words of doom and consolation make the general contrast clear, but some individual pronouns remain difficult to assign with certainty.

Does "affliction shall not rise up the second time" describe the completeness of Nineveh's defeat, reassurance that Judah will not face the same oppressor again, or a broader principle of final judgment? The immediate context favors decisive historical deliverance, while later readers may legitimately see an additional eschatological pattern.

How does reading [[Jonah 4]] beside [[Nahum 1]] change the portrait of Nineveh? Together they invite reflection on both divine mercy toward repentant people and judgment when violence or opposition persists.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Nahum 1:3 with [[Jonah 4]], noting how both books connect Nineveh with the LORD being “slow to anger” while emphasizing different outcomes. Study Exodus 34:6-7 and Numbers 14:18 as possible examples of a broader scriptural formula joining patience with accountability, while noting that the KJV wording is not identical. Finally, trace the mountains, good tidings, and peace motif through [[Isaiah 52]], [[Mosiah 12]], and [[Mosiah 15]], keeping the Mosiah connection explicitly mediated through Isaiah.
