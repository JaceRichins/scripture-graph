## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has three principal movements:

1. **Named sealers (10:1–27):** Nehemiah, priests, Levites, and chiefs of the people.
2. **The participating community and general oath (10:28–29):** the wider community joins the leaders and commits itself to the [[Law of Moses]].
3. **Specific obligations (10:30–39):** marriage boundaries, sacred-time observance, seventh-year practices, and an increasingly detailed program for supporting worship and its personnel.

The movement from names to oath to recurring practices shows how a public [[Covenants|covenant]] is translated into communal administration.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays covenant keeping as more than verbal assent. Sacred commitments govern family alliances, buying and selling, treatment of land and debt, and the use of material resources. Its treatment of [[Sabbath Day]], [[Tithing]], [[Sacrifice]], and [[Temples]] is specifically embedded in Israel's postexilic application of the [[Law of Moses]]; responsible modern application should distinguish the enduring principle of covenant fidelity from the chapter's particular legal and institutional forms.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The long roster gives the covenant public and corporate weight. Beginning in verse 30, repeated first-person commitments shift attention from who sealed to what the community promised to do. References to “the house of our God” recur through the closing regulations, and the final pledge not to forsake that house can be read as a concise thematic conclusion, although it may also be a conventional summary clause. [[Nehemiah 13]] provides a sobering counterpoint by returning to temple support, Sabbath trade, and intermarriage as areas requiring renewed correction. This correspondence may reflect both remembered communal failure and deliberate literary arrangement.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary Correspondence
> The recurrence in Nehemiah 13 of neglected temple support, Sabbath trade, and intermarriage strongly supports reading the promises in Nehemiah 10 as a thematic agenda for the book. It also shows that recording a covenant does not demonstrate sustained compliance.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Strong literary evidence with alternatives properly retained · [[Internal Literary Correspondence in Nehemiah 10|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical Discrepancy *(tentative)*
> The community obligates itself to a temple tax of one-third of a shekel per year (v.32), a different amount than the half-shekel sanctuary tax legislated in Exodus 30:13. This numeric discrepancy is noted by commentators, though its cause is disputed.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.8 · Numeric contrast secure; discrepancy interpretation disputed

> [!evidence] Textual Tension *(tentative)*
> Verse 34 institutes a lot-assigned "wood offering" for fueling the altar, and explicitly claims this practice is "as it is written in the law" — yet no wood-offering ordinance of this kind is preserved in the extant Pentateuch (Leviticus/Numbers/Deuteronomy). The verse's own appeal to written law is therefore in tension with what survives in the Torah text as we have it.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · Possible textual tension; grammatical scope and corpus claim disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrative preserve such an extensive list of names before stating the covenant's terms?
- What does the pairing of a curse and an oath communicate about the seriousness of the agreement?
- Why are land rest and debt release placed beside Sabbath commerce?
- How does [[Nehemiah 13]] affect the interpretation of this covenant: as an achievement, an ideal, a failed program, or some combination of these?
- Which principles in the chapter are broadly theological, and which practices belong specifically to the historical administration of the [[Law of Moses]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Nehemiah 10:31 with Exodus 23:10–11 and Deuteronomy 15:1–2; 10:32 with Exodus 30:13; 10:36 with Exodus 13:2, 13, and 15; and 10:38 with Numbers 18:26. Then trace the chapter's principal commitments through [[Nehemiah 13]]. These comparisons clarify both the Torah foundations of the covenant and the community's particular postexilic applications.
