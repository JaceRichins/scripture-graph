# Exodus 1 — Study Guide

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Continuity and growth (vv. 1–7):** Jacob's household is recalled, Joseph's generation passes, and Israel becomes numerous and mighty.

2. **Political fear and forced labor (vv. 8–14):** A new king portrays Israel as a possible wartime danger and imposes burdens and rigorous service. The chapter nevertheless reports continued multiplication.

3. **The midwives' resistance (vv. 15–21):** Pharaoh orders Shiphrah and Puah to kill sons at birth. They fear God, preserve the children, and receive divine favor.

4. **The decree broadens (v. 22):** Pharaoh addresses all his people and commands that newborn sons—Hebrew sons in the narrative context—be cast into the river while daughters are kept alive.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The clearest theological judgment concerns the midwives: they fear God, refuse a command to kill, preserve the sons, and are treated well by God. Their conduct exemplifies [[Agency]] exercised under coercive [[Government and Laws]]. The episode is tightly bounded, however; it concerns resistance to a lethal command and does not teach that every personal disagreement licenses civil disobedience.

The narrator associates divine favor with the midwives' fear of God, but does not explicitly evaluate the precise truthfulness of their explanation in verse 19. Readers should therefore distinguish the text's approval of their God-oriented allegiance and preservation of life from broader claims about [[Honesty]] that the passage does not expressly settle.

Israel's multiplication fits naturally within the [[Abrahamic Covenant]], especially promises of numerous descendants. That connection is plausible and important, but Exodus 1 itself does not name the covenant or explicitly label each growth notice its fulfillment.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three expressions invite caution. The statement that the new king “knew not Joseph” marks a break with Joseph's former standing, but Exodus 1 does not explain whether this means literal ignorance, lack of acknowledgment, or a political refusal to honor his legacy.

Pharaoh's invitation to “deal wisely” presents the policy from his own strategic perspective. The following narrative reports that affliction does not achieve his stated aim of limiting Israel's growth.

The expression “he made them houses” is brief and unexplained. It may refer to establishing households or families for the midwives rather than providing physical buildings. The causal wording explicitly connects this action with their fear of God.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repetition, escalation, and reversal shape the chapter. Israel's multiplication is emphasized in verses 7, 12, and 20. Pharaoh fears further growth, yet the policies narrated through verse 20 are followed by continued growth notices.

The coercion also broadens: taskmasters impose forced labor, the named midwives receive an order to kill sons at birth, and Pharaoh finally commands all his people to cast newborn sons into the river. The text does not say that Shiphrah and Puah were the only practicing midwives.

Shiphrah and Puah are named while Pharaoh remains unnamed. This permits a reading in which the subordinate women receive greater personal memorialization than the ruler, although narrative or source convention may also explain the naming pattern.

The fruitfulness, multiplication, and filling language of Exodus 1:7 recalls [[Genesis 1]], but shared vocabulary does not prove deliberate quotation. The final decree provides the narrative setting that [[Exodus 2]] transforms into part of the means of Moses's preservation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does Pharaoh move from a hypothetical wartime concern to forced labor and lethal policy?

- Why does the narrative repeat Israel's multiplication after attempts to suppress it?

- What is gained literarily by naming Shiphrah and Puah while leaving the king's personal name unstated?

- Does verse 19 describe ordinary circumstances, a strategic generalization, or protective deception? Which conclusions does the narrator actually state?

- What changes when the order to two midwives becomes a command addressed to all Pharaoh's people?

- How do Pharaoh's concern about Israel and the midwives' fear of God generate opposite responses to human life?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Exodus 1:1–5 with [[Genesis 46]] to examine how the family list and corporate total of seventy are resumed, while noting that Joseph was already in Egypt. Compare Exodus 1:7 with [[Genesis 1]], treating deliberate allusion as a question rather than a certainty. Read [[Genesis 15]] alongside the chapter for the earlier pattern of foreign residence, service, and affliction. Psalm 105:24–25 offers a later poetic summary of Israel's increase and Egyptian hostility. Continue into [[Exodus 2]] to trace how Moses's mother, sister, and Pharaoh's daughter extend the pattern of women frustrating the death policy.
