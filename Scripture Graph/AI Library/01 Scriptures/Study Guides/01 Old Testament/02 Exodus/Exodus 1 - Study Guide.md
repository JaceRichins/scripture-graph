## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Continuity and growth (vv. 1–7):** Jacob's household is recalled, Joseph's generation dies, and Israel multiplies.

2. **Political fear and forced labor (vv. 8–14):** A new king depicts Israel as a security threat and subjects the people to burdens and rigorous service, yet their growth continues.

3. **The midwives' resistance (vv. 15–21):** Pharaoh orders Shiphrah and Puah to kill sons at birth. They fear God, preserve the children, and receive divine favor.

4. **A broadened decree (v. 22):** Pharaoh addresses all his people and commands that newborn sons be cast into the river while daughters are kept alive.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents a conflict between divine allegiance and royal authority. The midwives exercise [[Agency]] by refusing a command to kill children, and the narrator explicitly attributes their refusal to fear of God. God then deals well with them. This makes the episode relevant to [[Government and Laws]], but it does not supply an unrestricted rule that personal disagreement always justifies disobedience; the command in this account is specifically lethal.

Israel's repeated multiplication may be read in light of the [[Abrahamic Covenant]], but Exodus 1 does not explicitly name that covenant or say that God directly caused every growth notice. The covenant connection is therefore a plausible intertextual interpretation rather than an observation stated by the chapter.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repetition, escalation, and reversal shape the chapter. Israel's multiplication is emphasized in verses 7, 12, and 20. Pharaoh fears further growth, yet the measures narrated through verse 20 do not stop it. His policy moves from forced labor to an order given to the midwives and then to a decree addressed to all his people.

The contrast between the named midwives and the unnamed Pharaoh permits a reading in which the courageous subordinate figures receive greater personal memorialization than the ruler, but the reason for the naming difference is not stated. Likewise, the fruitfulness, multiplication, and filling language of Exodus 1:7 recalls [[Genesis 1]], though shared vocabulary does not prove deliberate quotation. The closing decree provides the narrative setting that [[Exodus 2]] transforms into the means of Moses's preservation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why are Israel's multiplication and strength emphasized repeatedly in verses 7, 12, and 20?

- How does Pharaoh transform a hypothetical security concern into increasingly coercive policy?

- What changes when the order given to the midwives becomes a decree addressed to all Pharaoh's people?

- Why might the narrative preserve the names of Shiphrah and Puah while leaving the king personally unnamed? What alternative explanations are possible?

- Does verse 19 report ordinary circumstances, a strategic generalization, or protective deception? What does the narrator explicitly identify as the grounds for divine favor?

- How do Pharaoh's fear and the midwives' fear of God produce opposite kinds of action?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
