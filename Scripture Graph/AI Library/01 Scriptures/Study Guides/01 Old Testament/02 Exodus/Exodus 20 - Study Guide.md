# Exodus 20 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Exodus 20]] · My notes: [[Exodus 20 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Exodus 20 joins moral instruction to a narrative of divine self-disclosure. The Lord identifies himself as the one who delivered Israel from Egyptian bondage (Exodus 20:2) before giving what is customarily counted as the [[Ten Commandments]] (Exodus 20:3-17) — see [[Exodus and Deliverance]]. Terrified by the thunder, lightning, trumpet-blast, and smoking mountain (Exodus 20:18), the people withdraw and ask Moses to mediate rather than hear God's voice directly (Exodus 20:19), and Moses alone approaches "the thick darkness where God was" (Exodus 20:21). The chapter closes by renewing the prohibition on images and describing a simple, unhewn altar (Exodus 20:24-26) for [[Sacrifice]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves through four units: God's self-identification as Israel's deliverer (vv. 1-2); the commandments themselves (vv. 3-17); the people's fearful response and Moses's approach to God (vv. 18-21); and supplementary instructions about images and altars (vv. 22-26). Within the commandments, readers have traditionally grouped verses 3-11 as duties toward God (other gods, images, the divine name, the [[Sabbath Day]]) and verses 12-17 as duties toward other people (parents, life, marriage, property, truthful testimony, and desire). This is a useful and common way to organize the material, though the text itself does not label the division this way — honoring parents, for instance, sits at the boundary between religious and social obligation, and different traditions number the same seventeen verses differently.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The commandments follow, rather than precede, the declaration of deliverance in verse 2 — see [[Exodus and Deliverance]] — an ordering that supports reading these obligations as belonging to an already-existing covenant relationship, though the chapter alone does not settle a complete theology of law and grace. Love of God and keeping his commandments are explicitly linked in verse 6, but the text does not reduce every commandment to that single motive.

Verses 5-6 pair a limited generational consequence ("third and fourth generation") for those who hate God with mercy "unto thousands" for those who love him and keep his commandments — a real asymmetry favoring mercy, though the chapter does not define the exact scope of "thousands" and it should not be described as unlimited. See [[Justice and Mercy]].

The Sabbath command (Exodus 20:8-11) is grounded in the pattern of [[Creation]] and extends rest to sons, daughters, servants, cattle, and the resident stranger, not only the household head. Deuteronomy 5:15 later grounds the same commandment in the memory of deliverance from bondage instead of creation; both rationales appear in scripture and are best presented as distinct emphases rather than harmonized away.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Exodus 20:20 places two uses of "fear" side by side — "Fear not" and "that his fear may be before your faces" — a wording that plausibly distinguishes disabling terror (which Moses tells the people not to have) from an ongoing reverence meant to restrain sin, though the chapter uses one English word for both and does not formally define the distinction.

The altar law's language of a tool "polluting" the stone and nakedness being "discovered" (Exodus 20:25-26) uses purity and modesty language similar to later priestly regulation, such as the linen garments required of officiating priests (Exodus 28:42); this is a thematic resemblance worth noting without assuming one text intentionally anticipates the other.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The scene at Sinai is built around sensory theophany — thunder, lightning, trumpet, smoking mountain (Exodus 20:18) — followed by the people's retreat and Moses's solitary approach into "the thick darkness where God was" (Exodus 20:21). This darkness-as-presence motif recurs elsewhere in scripture describing divine nearness as veiled rather than openly visible. Read alongside Mosiah 13, where Abinadi recites this same Decalogue almost verbatim to a hostile audience, the chapter's original setting (Israel fleeing from direct divine speech) is inverted: Abinadi insists on speaking the commandments precisely because his hearers, like Israel, resist hearing them.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual-Comparison
> Exodus 20:11 grounds the sabbath commandment in the Creation week (God rested the seventh day), whereas the parallel Decalogue in Deuteronomy 5:15 grounds the same commandment in the memory of deliverance from Egyptian bondage. The two accounts give different theological warrants for the identical command.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted textual comparison · [[Textual-Comparison in Exodus 20|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Comparative/Form-Critical Scholarship *(tentative)*
> The chapter's opening move — self-identification of the divine sovereign ('I am the LORD thy God') followed by a historical prologue ('which have brought thee out of the land of Egypt') and then stipulations — matches the recognized form of ancient Near Eastern suzerain-vassal treaties, a form-critical observation associated especially with George Mendenhall's mid-20th-century work relating biblical covenant texts to Hittite treaties.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed comparative scholarship

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the declaration of deliverance in verse 2 shape the way the commands beginning in verse 3 should be read?
- Why does the Sabbath command explicitly include servants, livestock, and the stranger, and not only the household head?
- What kind of fear does Moses reject in verse 20, and what kind does he want to remain before the people?
- What does the prohibition on coveting add that the prohibitions on adultery and stealing do not?
- How should the asymmetry between "third and fourth generation" and mercy "unto thousands" (vv. 5-6) shape how [[Justice and Mercy]] are understood together, without overstating what "thousands" means?
- Why might a law about altar construction (vv. 24-26) immediately follow the Ten Commandments?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Exodus 20 with [[Deuteronomy 5]], especially the differing rationale given for the Sabbath (creation here, deliverance from bondage there). [[Mosiah 13]] preserves the Book of Mormon's most extensive citation of this Decalogue, in a very different narrative setting. [[Exodus 16]] narrates Sabbath observance earlier in the wilderness account and is worth reading alongside this chapter's fuller Sabbath law.
