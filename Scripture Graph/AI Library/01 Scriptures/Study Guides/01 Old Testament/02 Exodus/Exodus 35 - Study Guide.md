# Exodus 35 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Exodus 35]] · My notes: [[Exodus 35 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Exodus 35 moves from sacred rest to communal preparation for sacred work. Moses commands the [[Sabbath Day]] (vv. 1–3), requests materials and skilled makers for the tabernacle (vv. 4–19), and receives a willing response from men, women, rulers, and textile workers (vv. 20–29). Bezaleel and Aholiab are then identified as divinely enabled artisans and teachers (vv. 30–35). The chapter combines firm divine specification with voluntary human participation and recognizes several different forms of useful contribution.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Sabbath command (vv. 1–3):** The seventh day is declared holy rest before the appeal for sanctuary labor.

2. **Materials and work requested (vv. 4–19):** Moses lists the required offerings, structures, furnishings, garments, and skilled makers.

3. **Request answered (vv. 20–29):** The congregation supplies the named materials. Men and women give, skilled women spin, and rulers bring stones, spices, and oil.

4. **Artisans commissioned (vv. 30–35):** Bezaleel and Aholiab are associated with divine wisdom, specialized craftsmanship, design, and teaching.

The sequence leads directly into construction in [[Exodus 36]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Divine command and willing response operate together. The LORD specifies the sacred project, while the offerings repeatedly come from hearts described as willing or stirred (vv. 5, 21–22, 29). This supports responsible connections to [[Agency]] and [[Consecration]], although those are later interpretive categories rather than the chapter's own labels. The willingness language governs the offerings and should not be generalized to every obligation or project role.

The chapter associates practical capacities with divine enabling. Bezaleel's wisdom, understanding, knowledge, design, metalwork, stonecutting, and woodworking are linked with the spirit of God (vv. 30–33), and teaching is added in verse 34. This supports connections to [[Service]], [[Spiritual Gifts]], and [[Education and Learning]] within the narrative. It does not establish that every talent or successful craft is direct revelation.

Because the [[Sabbath Day]] command immediately precedes the sanctuary appeal, the sequence can be read as placing sacred work under the obligation of sacred rest. That interpretation is plausible, but Exodus 35 does not explicitly explain the placement.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated KJV expressions “willing heart,” “wise hearted,” and “wisdom of heart” connect inward disposition with concrete action, but they are not exact synonyms. Some occurrences concern willingness to give; others concern technical ability, textile production, or teaching.

In this setting, “wise hearted” denotes skilled or capable workers rather than moral goodness alone. Likewise, “curious works” and “cunning work” refer to sophisticated design and craftsmanship; modern senses of merely strange or deceitful would distort the passage.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The clearest literary correspondence is between request and response. Metals, textiles, skins, wood, oil, spices, and stones requested in verses 5–9 recur in the contribution report in verses 22–28. This presents Israel's response as concrete fulfillment of the announced request and demonstrates careful organization. Routine implementation reporting or editorial alignment could also explain the repetition, so it does not determine authorship, date, compositional history, or historicity.

A broader correspondence connects [[Exodus 31]] and [[Exodus 35]]. Exodus 31 places the artisans before its Sabbath material; Exodus 35 places the Sabbath before the offerings and artisans. [[Exodus 32]], [[Exodus 33]], and [[Exodus 34]] intervene with covenant rupture and renewal. The reverse ordering is observable, but whether it deliberately frames that crisis cannot be determined from sequence alone.

Gold jewelry used for the calf in [[Exodus 32]] can also be compared with the jewelry and gold offered to the LORD in Exodus 35:22. This permits a reading about similar resources directed toward rival forms of worship, but the narrative does not explicitly identify the later offering as restitution or announce the contrast.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why is the [[Sabbath Day]] command placed immediately before the tabernacle appeal?

- How does the chapter combine a commanded project with voluntary offerings?

- What different contributions come from men, women, rulers, artisans, teachers, and priests?

- Why are design, teaching, and manual craftsmanship associated with divine wisdom?

- How does the matching order of requested and contributed materials shape the account?

- Does the gold offered in this chapter intentionally contrast with the gold used in [[Exodus 32]], or is that connection primarily produced by narrative proximity?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Exodus 25]] for the earlier sanctuary offering and construction instructions, [[Exodus 31]] for the artisan material followed by Sabbath legislation, and [[Exodus 36]] for the beginning of actual construction and the continued abundance of offerings. The golden-calf crisis and covenant renewal in [[Exodus 32]], [[Exodus 33]], and [[Exodus 34]] intervene between the initial instructions and their implementation; Exodus 35 resumes the project after that rupture and renewal. [[Exodus 28]] gives fuller context for the priestly garments, and [[Exodus 39]] reports their completion.
