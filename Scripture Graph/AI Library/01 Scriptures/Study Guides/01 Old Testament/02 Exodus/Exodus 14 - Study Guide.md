# Exodus 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Exodus 14]] · My notes: [[Exodus 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview

> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Israel goes out of Egypt—Israel passes through the Red Sea on dry ground—The Lord overthrows the Egyptians in the midst of the sea.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The apparent trap (vv. 1–9):** Israel encamps by the sea; Pharaoh interprets its position as weakness and mobilizes his chariots.
2. **Fear, complaint, and promise (vv. 10–15):** Israel cries out and accuses Moses; Moses promises that the LORD will fight, and God commands the people to advance.
3. **Protection and passage (vv. 16–22):** The pillar moves behind Israel, the wind drives back the sea, and Israel enters on dry ground.
4. **Pursuit and reversal (vv. 23–29):** Egypt follows into the sea, its chariots fail, and the returning waters distinguish the fate of pursuer and refugee.
5. **Recognition (vv. 30–31):** Israel sees the deliverance, fears the LORD, and believes the LORD and Moses.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter places divine action beside commanded human response. Moses says that the LORD will fight while Israel holds its peace (14:13–14), and the next command is for the people to go forward (14:15). In a canonical reading these instructions can be understood as complementary: Israel cannot create the path but must enter it when commanded. The text itself does not turn this sequence into a universal formula, and some source-critical readings understand the adjacent instructions as traces of interwoven traditions.

The chapter also does not portray [[Faith]] as an uninterrupted emotional state. Israel is fearful and accusatory before the deliverance, while verse 31 reports belief after the people see the LORD's work. Hebrews 11:29 later emphasizes faith in the crossing; the differing emphases should not be flattened into a claim that every Israelite displayed constant confidence.

Divine hardening in verses 4, 8, and 17 raises a serious question for [[Agency]]. The chapter attributes hardening to God while also narrating Pharaoh and the Egyptians as acting and pursuing. It does not provide a philosophical reconciliation of divine sovereignty and human responsibility.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Repeated language helps trace the narrative's reversals. The purpose that the Egyptians may “know that I am the LORD” appears in verses 4 and 18; in verse 25 the Egyptians recognize that the LORD fights for Israel. Israel first sees the approaching Egyptians and fears (v. 10), but finally sees the LORD's great work and believes (v. 31).

Several kinds of “hand” language also occur: Israel leaves with a “high hand” (v. 8), Moses stretches out his hand over the sea (vv. 16, 21, 26–27), and Israel is saved from the Egyptians' hand (v. 30). These expressions may contribute to a transfer-of-power motif, but they also have distinct idiomatic and narrative functions, so deliberate unification should not be assumed.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 22 and 29 repeat the description of Israel walking on dry ground with water on both sides. The repetition can be read as framing the Egyptian pursuit and defeat in verses 23–28, but it may also be ordinary recapitulation, an Israel–Egypt contrast, or evidence of editorial arrangement.

Wind, divided waters, and dry ground resemble motifs in [[Genesis 1]] and [[Genesis 8]], making a new-creation reading plausible without establishing direct literary dependence. The coexistence of the wind, Moses' outstretched hand, “stand still,” and “go forward” can be read coherently in the received narrative; source-critical scholarship has also treated such features as traces of interwoven traditions. The chapter itself does not settle its compositional history.

[[Exodus 15]] immediately retells the event in praise. [[Psalm 106]], [[Joshua 4]], [[Isaiah 43]], [[1 Corinthians 10]], and [[Hebrews 11]] provide later liturgical, prophetic, baptismal, and faith-centered receptions. These uses document the chapter's influence but do not automatically establish its original authorial intent.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How do “stand still” in verse 13 and “go forward” in verse 15 together define faithful action?
- Why is recognition of the LORD attributed to Egyptians as well as Israelites?
- What does the movement of the pillar from front to rear contribute to the portrayal of divine presence?
- How should readers hold together liberation from enslavement, divine hardening, and the death of the pursuing army?
- Does verse 31 portray faith as the prerequisite for deliverance, the result of deliverance, or part of a more complex process?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read the chapter with [[Exodus 15]], its immediate poetic interpretation. Compare [[Exodus 7]] with Exodus 14:4, 17–18 to trace the hardening-and-recognition theme. Then compare [[Psalm 106]], [[Joshua 4]], [[Isaiah 43]], [[1 Corinthians 10]], and [[Hebrews 11]] to distinguish the narrative from its later liturgical, prophetic, baptismal, and faith-centered receptions. A critical commentary can also help readers evaluate proposals that the wind and hand descriptions or the “stand still” and “go forward” instructions reflect interwoven literary traditions.
