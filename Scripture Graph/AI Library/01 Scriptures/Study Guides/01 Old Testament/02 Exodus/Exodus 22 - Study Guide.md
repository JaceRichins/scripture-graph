# Exodus 22 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Exodus 22]] · My notes: [[Exodus 22 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Exodus 22 moves from losses involving property to obligations involving worship, vulnerable people, offerings, and holiness. Its legal reasoning is often circumstantial: a stolen animal sold or killed is treated differently from one recovered alive; entrusted property differs from borrowed property; and liability can turn on an oath, physical evidence, or the owner's presence. The later commands place strangers, widows, fatherless children, and poor debtors under explicit protection. The collection therefore holds material accountability, exclusive worship, and restraint toward vulnerable neighbors within the same covenant framework.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. **Theft and property damage (vv. 1–6):** graded restitution for livestock theft, differing bloodshed rulings around sunrise, grazing damage, and spreading fire.
2. **Custody, disputed possession, and borrowing (vv. 7–15):** judicial inquiry, double payment, oaths, evidence, and liability affected by cause or the owner's presence.
3. **Seduction and severe religious or sexual offenses (vv. 16–20):** marriage or payment following enticement, then severe sanctions concerning sorcery, bestiality, and sacrifice to another god.
4. **Protection from exploitation (vv. 21–27):** strangers, widows, fatherless children, poor borrowers, interest, and garment pledges.
5. **Authority, offerings, and holiness (vv. 28–31):** speech concerning divine and human authority, first produce and firstborn, and flesh torn by beasts.

Verses 1–17 predominantly use conditional cases, while direct commands become more prominent afterward. The shift is not absolute: verses 19–20 retain case-like formulations, and conditional language continues later. These divisions are study aids rather than headings supplied by the text.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents justice as calibrated responsibility. Restitution varies with the kind of loss and its circumstances, while oaths and evidence address cases without eyewitnesses. This supports a connection with [[Justice and Mercy]], although the laws should not simply be equated with a modern legal system.

God is represented as more than a distant source of rules. He hears the cries of afflicted widows, fatherless children, and a poor neighbor deprived of necessary clothing (vv. 23, 27). The statement “for I am gracious” grounds the garment-pledge rule in divine character. Israel's former life as strangers in Egypt similarly turns [[Exodus and Deliverance]] into an ethical obligation toward strangers.

The same chapter also contains sale for unpaid theft, paternal authority in the marriage case, and severe sanctions. Those features prevent an idealized claim that it is a modern egalitarian code. The chapter instead shows protection and accountability operating within the household, economic, and religious structures of [[Law of Moses]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Restitution language recurs with meaningful variation: fivefold, fourfold, double, or replacement of the loss. The variations show that “restitution” is a family of remedies rather than one fixed penalty.

The KJV's “breaking up” in verse 2 refers within the immediate context to a thief entering unlawfully; the sunrise clause then changes the blood-liability ruling. The precise rationale for the day-night distinction is unstated.

In verse 25, “usury” should not automatically be restricted to the modern sense of an exorbitant interest rate. The English text prohibits laying usury upon the poor borrower in view. A precise account of the Hebrew term and its range requires lexical evidence beyond the supplied text.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 21-27 form the chapter's clearest rhetorical concentration. The cry of the afflicted is heard in verses 23 and 27, and divine hearing culminates in “for I am gracious.” The threatened judgment in verse 24 reverses the offense: those who afflict widows and fatherless children would leave their own wives widowed and children fatherless.

Torn flesh also recurs in two distinct settings. In verse 13, a torn animal can supply evidence that excuses a custodian from repayment; in verse 31, flesh torn in the field is forbidden as food to a people called holy. The recurrence is meaningful, but by itself it does not prove that the entire chapter was designed as a unified literary composition.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the law require five oxen for an ox but four sheep for a sheep when the stolen animal has been killed or sold?
- What rationale best explains the sunrise distinction in Exodus 22:2–3, and how much of that rationale is actually stated?
- How do judges, oaths, and physical evidence distribute responsibility when no eyewitness exists?
- What protection does the remedy in verses 16–17 provide, and what limitations follow from the fact that the maid's own response is not stated?
- How should protection of vulnerable people be understood alongside sale for unpaid theft, paternal household authority, and severe sanctions?
- How does Israel's remembered vulnerability in Egypt become a rule for using power toward strangers?
- Does the holiness declaration in verse 31 explain primarily the food rule that follows, the closing commands, or the collection more broadly?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the stranger command with [[Exodus 23]] and [[Deuteronomy 10]]. Compare the poor-loan rule with [[Leviticus 25]], and the garment pledge with [[Deuteronomy 24]] and [[Amos 2]]. [[Exodus 13]] gives fuller context for firstborn consecration, while [[Acts 23]] records later reception of the ruler-command. These parallels establish recurring scriptural concerns, but they do not by themselves determine literary dependence, date, or authorship.
