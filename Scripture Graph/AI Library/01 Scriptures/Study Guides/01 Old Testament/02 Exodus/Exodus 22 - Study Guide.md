# Exodus 22 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Exodus 22]] · My notes: [[Exodus 22 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Exodus 22 combines civil disputes, religious prohibitions, protection of vulnerable people, offerings, and holiness. Its opening cases concentrate on theft, damaged property, entrusted goods, and borrowing. The later commands address sexual conduct and exclusive worship, then give particular attention to strangers, widows, fatherless children, and poor debtors. The chapter closes with offerings and a food restriction associated with being holy to God.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. **Property offenses and restitution (vv. 1–6):** theft, grazing damage, and spreading fire.
2. **Entrusted or borrowed property (vv. 7–15):** judges, oaths, evidence, and differing liability according to circumstances.
3. **Sexual and religious offenses (vv. 16–20):** seduction, acts assigned capital sanctions, and exclusive sacrifice to the Lord.
4. **Protection from exploitation (vv. 21–27):** strangers, widows, fatherless children, and poor debtors.
5. **Authority, offerings, and holiness (vv. 28–31):** speech concerning divine and human authority, first produce and firstborn, and meat torn by animals.

This outline reflects a real shift in subject and form, but the boundaries are editorial aids rather than divisions stated by the text.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Exodus 22 places accountability and protection within the same covenant law. Property wrongs ordinarily receive differentiated restitution, while strangers, widows, fatherless children, and poor borrowers receive explicit safeguards. God is represented as lawgiver and as one who hears the cries of afflicted people and is gracious (Exodus 22:23, 27). Israel's former condition in Egypt supplies a stated reason not to oppress strangers, joining [[Exodus and Deliverance]] to ethical obligation.

These observations support a relationship between [[Justice and Mercy]], but they do not make the chapter a modern humanitarian or egalitarian code. Its provisions remain embedded in ancient household structures and include sale for unpaid theft, paternal authority over marriage, and severe sanctions. The chapter also places property dealings, exclusive worship, [[Sacrifice]], offerings, and food practice within [[Law of Moses]]. Reading all of these as one deliberately integrated theology of holiness is plausible but disputed.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated restitution formulas—fivefold, fourfold, double, or full replacement—show that the chapter does not apply one uniform sanction to every property loss. The recurrent conditional form, often beginning with “if,” tests responsibility under different circumstances.

The KJV term “usury” in verse 25 should not automatically be read as referring only to an excessive modern interest rate: within the English wording of the verse, the poor Israelite in view is not to have usury laid upon him at all. Precise conclusions about the underlying Hebrew require lexical study beyond this supplied text.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 1–17 predominantly use conditional cases, while verses 18–31 predominantly use direct commands and prohibitions, though the transition is not absolute. Repeated restitution formulas connect the property cases. In verses 21–27, cries and divine hearing recur, and the threatened judgment would leave an oppressor's family widowed and fatherless—a reversal of the harm condemned.

The chapter also gives explicit motivations: Israel formerly lived as strangers in Egypt (Exodus 22:21), the pledged garment is needed for sleep (Exodus 22:27), and God hears because he is gracious (Exodus 22:27). Torn animal flesh appears both as evidence that can relieve a custodian of repayment and as food forbidden to Israel (Exodus 22:13, 31). These patterns are observable, but recurrence and placement alone do not prove unified authorship or deliberate literary composition.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why do the restitution amounts differ when livestock is killed or sold rather than recovered alive?
- What unstated rationale lies behind the sunrise distinction in Exodus 22:2–3?
- How do oaths and physical evidence function when no eyewitness exists?
- What protections and limitations are visible in the treatment of the unbetrothed maid, whose own wishes are not stated?
- How should the chapter's protection of vulnerable people be understood alongside sale for unpaid theft, paternal household authority, and severe sanctions?
- Does the holiness declaration in verse 31 illuminate the whole collection or primarily introduce the rule about torn flesh?
- How does remembered oppression become a reason not to oppress others?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the stranger command with [[Exodus 23]] and [[Deuteronomy 10]], the garment-pledge rule with [[Deuteronomy 24]] and [[Amos 2]], and the poor-borrower interest rule with [[Leviticus 25]]. [[Exodus 13]] supplies context for firstborn consecration and redemption, while [[Acts 23]] shows later scriptural use of the ruler-command in Exodus 22:28. These comparisons illuminate recurring concerns and later reception, but similarity alone does not prove direct literary dependence.
