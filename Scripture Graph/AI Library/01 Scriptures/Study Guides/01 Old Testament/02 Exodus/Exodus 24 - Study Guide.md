# Exodus 24 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Exodus 24]] · My notes: [[Exodus 24 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Exodus 24 narrates Israel's public acceptance and ritual ratification of the Sinai covenant. Moses reports and records the LORD's words; the people twice undertake to obey; sacrifices and blood solemnize the covenant. Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and seventy elders then ascend, see the God of Israel, survive the encounter, and eat and drink. Moses subsequently enters the cloud-covered summit to receive written law and commandments. The chapter closely joins [[Covenants]], [[Obedience]], [[Sacrifice]], [[Records and Sacred Writings]], and [[Revelation]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Approach prescribed (24:1–2):** Moses, Israel's representatives, and the people receive differentiated instructions.
2. **Covenant publicly ratified (24:3–8):** Moses reports and writes the words; Israel assents; offerings are sacrificed; the covenant book is read; Israel again assents; and blood is applied to altar and people.
3. **Representatives behold and eat (24:9–11):** Israel's leaders ascend, see God, are not struck, and eat and drink.
4. **Moses enters the cloud (24:12–18):** Moses and Joshua begin the ascent, arrangements are made for disputes below, and Moses enters the cloud for forty days and forty nights.

The narrative narrows from the assembled people to selected representatives and then to Moses. It also moves from spoken words to a covenant record and finally toward divinely written tables. The relationship between the exclusive approach prescribed in verse 2 and the elders' vision in verses 9–11 remains less explicit than this broad progression might suggest.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Covenant commitment responds to disclosed instruction: Moses speaks and reads, and the people undertake to do and obey. Their repeated response makes obedience public and communal rather than merely private. The chapter also joins revelation with preservation and teaching. Moses writes the words and reads the book publicly; he is then promised written tables so that he may teach (24:4, 7, 12).

The blood is explicitly called "the blood of the covenant" (24:8). In the immediate narrative it solemnizes the LORD's covenant with Israel. [[Matthew 26]], [[Luke 22]], and [[Hebrews 9]] later reuse this language. That reception is theologically significant, but it should not be treated as proof that every feature of the Sinai ceremony had an exclusively Christian predictive meaning for its earliest audience.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Forms of "all" concentrate around the covenant words and Israel's response: Moses reports "all the words" and "all the judgments"; the people promise to do "all the words"; Moses writes "all the words"; and the covenant concerns "all these words" (24:3–4, 8). The second response adds "and be obedient" (24:7), sharpening the movement from hearing to action.

The manifestation is described through comparisons. What lies under God's feet is "as it were" sapphire and heavenlike in clarity, while the visible glory is "like" devouring fire (24:10, 17). The language communicates brilliance and danger without offering a detailed description of God's form.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter intensifies spatially and visually. The people remain below; selected leaders ascend and encounter sapphire-like clarity; Moses ultimately enters the cloud while the glory appears like devouring fire. This supports a pattern of differentiated sacred access, though extending it into a complete later temple scheme goes beyond what the chapter specifies. Joshua accompanies Moses partway up the mountain (24:13), but the text does not say where he stopped or whether he later remained on the mountain rather than in the camp below.

The vision report is both direct and restrained: the leaders "saw the God of Israel," but the description focuses on what was under his feet and stresses that he did not lay his hand upon them (24:10–11). This creates a genuine interpretive tension with [[Exodus 33]], where seeing the LORD's face is associated with death. A mediated theophany, or a distinction between seeing God and seeing his face, may resolve the tension, but the text does not state either resolution.

Aaron and Hur are the same pair Moses relies on again in [[Exodus 17]], though there they physically support his hands in battle while here they are left to hear disputes in his absence — the recurrence suggests a trusted association rather than proof of a fixed office.

The six days of cloud followed by a seventh-day summons (24:16) may recall a creation-week rhythm, but the chapter uses no creation, rest, or Sabbath vocabulary, and a similar wait-then-decisive-day pattern appears elsewhere in the Torah (e.g., the flood's seven-day intervals) without creation resonance; the connection remains speculative. Moses's own forty days and nights in the cloud (24:18) echoes a recurring, but not uniform, biblical use of forty-day periods (his second ascent in [[Exodus 34]], the flood, Elijah's journey, Jesus's fast) marking extended divine encounter — the shared number does not imply one shared meaning across these very different narratives.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does the second public commitment add after the covenant book is read?
- Which preceding words belong to the "book of the covenant," and can its boundaries be established from this chapter alone?
- What does the placement of blood on the altar and people signify beyond the narrator's explicit identification of it as covenant blood?
- Does the meal express covenant fellowship, emphasize survival in God's presence, or serve another unstated purpose?
- How should the elders' sight of God be held together with [[Exodus 33]]?
- How should Moses's exclusive nearness in verse 2 be related to the elders' vision and Joshua's accompaniment later in the chapter?
- Does the seventh-day summons deliberately evoke creation, or does it simply report elapsed time?
- How does this ratification prepare for the covenant's breach and renewal in [[Exodus 32]]–[[Exodus 34]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Exodus 19]] for Sinai's boundaries, cloud, fire, and controlled approach; [[Exodus 20]]–[[Exodus 23]] for the laws preceding the ceremony; and [[Exodus 32]]–[[Exodus 34]] for covenant breach, intercession, renewal, replacement tables, and another forty-day period. [[Exodus 17]] supplies an earlier scene of Aaron and Hur assisting Moses, useful background for their role here in verse 14. [[Deuteronomy 9]] later recalls the ascent and stone tables. [[Matthew 26]], [[Luke 22]], [[Hebrews 9]], and [[Hebrews 12]] demonstrate later reception and development of covenant-blood and Sinai imagery; comparison should attend to both continuity and changed context, since Hebrews expands the ritual details (goats, water, scarlet wool, hyssop) well beyond what Exodus 24 itself records. For the sapphire pavement of 24:10, [[Ezekiel 1]]'s throne-vision uses comparable imagery, though this is best read as shared theophanic idiom rather than established literary dependence.
