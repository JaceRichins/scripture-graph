# Exodus 2 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Exodus 2]] · My notes: [[Exodus 2 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Exodus 2 narrows the national crisis of [[Exodus 1]] to the life of one endangered child. Moses is preserved through the initiative and compassion of women, raised as Pharaoh's daughter's son, drawn toward the suffering of the Hebrews, and forced into exile after killing an Egyptian. In Midian he again intervenes on behalf of people being driven away, then enters a household, marries Zipporah, and names a son in recognition of his own displacement. The final three verses widen the focus back to Israel: their bondage continues, but God hears, remembers, looks, and takes notice. The chapter therefore prepares for [[Exodus and Deliverance]] without yet narrating God's commission to Moses.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Preservation and adoption (2:1-10):** Moses's mother, sister, and Pharaoh's daughter cooperate—without any stated prior plan—to preserve him.

2. **Violence, exposure, and flight (2:11-15):** Moses sees oppression, kills an Egyptian, is challenged by a Hebrew, and flees Pharaoh.

3. **Refuge and household in Midian (2:16-22):** Moses assists Reuel's daughters, receives hospitality, marries Zipporah, and becomes Gershom's father.

4. **Israel's cry and God's covenant attention (2:23-25):** The viewpoint shifts from Moses's private exile to the continuing bondage of Israel and the [[Abrahamic Covenant]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins human compassion with covenantal divine attention. Moses is preserved through ordinary decisions made by his mother, sister, and Pharaoh's daughter (2:2-10); the text does not label these actions miraculous, though a providential reading is possible. At the close, Israel's suffering is neither ignored nor treated abstractly: God hears their groaning and looks upon them (2:23-25). God's remembrance of the covenant should be read as the narrative signal for covenant action, not as proof that God had previously forgotten information.

The killing in 2:12 deserves moral care. Moses's opposition to oppression does not make every method he uses righteous. Because the narrator gives no explicit divine approval, the scene invites serious reflection on [[Justice and Mercy]] rather than a simple endorsement of violence.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two naming explanations interpret major changes in Moses's life. Pharaoh's daughter names him Moses because she drew him from the water (2:10); later Moses names his son Gershom because he has been a stranger in a strange land (2:22). These explanations function clearly within the narrative, but they should not automatically be treated as exhaustive linguistic proofs of each name's historical origin.

The ending gains force through repeated clauses beginning with God as subject: God heard, God remembered, God looked, and God had respect unto Israel (2:24-25). This compressed sequence changes the horizon of the story from human improvisation to impending divine action.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly turns danger into preservation. The river associated with Pharaoh's death decree becomes the location of Moses's rescue; Pharaoh's daughter recognizes a Hebrew child but makes him her son; Moses's mother is paid to nurse the child she had been forced to relinquish. These reversals connect Exodus 2 tightly with [[Exodus 1]].

Water also marks both major geographical movements. Moses is drawn from water in Egypt (2:3-10), while his life in Midian begins at a well where he draws water for a flock (2:15-19). The Midian scene resembles the well-and-marriage narratives of [[Genesis 24]] and [[Genesis 29]]. That resemblance may reflect a conventional biblical scene type; it does not require direct copying.

Moses's identity remains deliberately unsettled. He is the son of Pharaoh's daughter, regards Hebrews as his brethren, is perceived as Egyptian in Midian, and calls himself a stranger. This tension fits his later narrative role as a mediator who must move among several communities.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Philological *(tentative)*
> Alongside the Hebrew folk etymology given in the text (v.10), some scholars propose that 'Moses' derives from the Egyptian element ms(y)/'mose,' meaning roughly 'is born' or 'son of' (as in royal names like Thutmose or Rameses), which would fit a child raised in Pharaoh's household. The biblical text itself only supplies the Hebrew wordplay and does not address an Egyptian derivation.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Genuine scholarly proposal; formulation needs Egyptological support

> [!evidence] Literary Structure *(tentative)*
> The concentration of reversals, water scenes, identity transitions, rescue episodes, and the final sequence of divine-response verbs is meaningful evidence of deliberate literary shaping. It supports reading Exodus 2 as a coherent narrative unit rather than as an accidental list of events.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · Coherence evident; mode and degree of deliberate shaping disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does Pharaoh's daughter's action constitute conscious resistance to Pharaoh, or does the text permit only the narrower conclusion that she acted compassionately toward this child?

- Why does the narrator withhold an explicit moral evaluation of Moses's killing of the Egyptian?

- How do the three conflict scenes in 2:11-17 distinguish impulsive violence, verbal confrontation, and protective service?

- What changes when God becomes the explicit subject of the final clauses in 2:24-25?

- How does Gershom's name express Moses's location between Egyptian upbringing, Hebrew kinship, and Midianite residence?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the death decree in [[Exodus 1]] with Moses's river rescue, then follow the verbs of seeing, hearing, and covenant remembrance into [[Exodus 3]] and [[Exodus 6]]. For later scriptural interpretations of Moses's choices, compare [[Acts 7]] and [[Hebrews 11]]. The ark imagery can also be compared cautiously with [[Genesis 6]], while the Midian well scene can be studied alongside [[Genesis 24]] and [[Genesis 29]].
