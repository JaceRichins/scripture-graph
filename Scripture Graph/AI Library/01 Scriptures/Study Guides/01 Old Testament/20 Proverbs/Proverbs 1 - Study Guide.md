# Proverbs 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Proverbs 1]] · My notes: [[Proverbs 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge—If sinners entice you, do not consent—Those who hearken to wisdom will dwell safely.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Purpose and foundation (vv. 1–7).** The opening identifies the collection and states its educational aims for inexperienced and wise learners. Verse 7 gives the foundational contrast between fear of the LORD and foolish rejection of instruction.
2. **Parental warning (vv. 8–19).** The son is told to heed both parents and refuse a violent group's invitation. Verses 18–19 explicitly describe the attackers' way as self-destructive; the precise function of the net image in verse 17 remains disputed.
3. **Wisdom's public appeal (vv. 20–33).** Personified Wisdom calls in public places, invites the simple to turn, recounts rejected counsel, and contrasts ruin with security.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Fear of the LORD and knowledge.** Verse 7 presents fear of the LORD as the beginning of knowledge, and verse 29 again joins rejection of knowledge with failure to choose that fear.
- **Responsible response.** The hearer can consent or refuse, turn or persist, and hearken or disregard. This supports a connection to [[Agency]] without defining a complete philosophical theory of agency.
- **Conduct and consequence.** The image of eating “the fruit of their own way” links choices with outcomes. The speech also contains active judgment language, so it should not be reduced to automatic consequences alone.
- **Parental instruction.** Father and mother appear together as sources of moral teaching, giving the chapter a limited but meaningful [[Family]] dimension.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The lexical family **simple / simplicity** appears in verses 4, 22, and 32. The simple can receive instruction and are invited to turn, but loving simplicity or turning away leaves them endangered. The recurrence creates thematic continuity, though deliberate structural placement cannot be proved.

Verse 6's “dark sayings” indicates that some wisdom requires interpretation. Proverbs 1:7 also shares the formula “The fear of the LORD is the beginning of...” with [[Proverbs 9]] and [[Psalm 111]], while differing in its final word: Proverbs 1 says “knowledge,” and the parallels say “wisdom.”
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter sets the enticers' speech beside Wisdom's appeal. The violent group says “Come with us,” lurks privately, and promises shared plunder; Wisdom cries openly in streets, gathering places, gates, and the city. This supports a literary contrast between concealed predation and public correction, although a formally designed spatial opposition cannot be demonstrated.

Wisdom is personified as a woman who calls, counsels, reproves, and responds. [[Proverbs 8]] develops this figure at greater length. Personification establishes a literary role here but does not by itself identify Wisdom as a separate divine being.

Verses 18–19 clearly reverse violence upon its planners. Verse 17's bird-and-net image may contribute to that reversal, but its precise logic is disputed.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Verbal Parallel
> The maxim in Proverbs 1:7 has close canonical parallels in Psalm 111:10 and Proverbs 9:10. This recurrence is strong evidence that fear of the Lord is a framing theme in biblical wisdom discourse and especially in Proverbs, but it does not by itself determine the literary dependence or authorship of the passages.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 1.0 · Parallel accepted; significance moderated · [[Canonical Verbal Parallel in Proverbs 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Composition/Authorship *(tentative)*
> Proverbs 1:1 attributes the collection to Solomon, but Proverbs contains multiple internal superscriptions elsewhere in the book (e.g., 'the proverbs of Solomon' recurs at 10:1 and 25:1, and other sections are headed 'the words of Agur' and 'the words of king Lemuel'), which is commonly read as evidence that the book is a compiled anthology assembled under Solomon's name and associated wisdom tradition rather than a single continuous composition penned entirely by one author.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · Anthology model common but insufficiently sourced here
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why are the father's instruction and the mother's law placed in parallel in verse 8?
- What makes the sinners' invitation attractive besides material gain?
- How does the invitation to turn in verse 23 relate to the severe judgment language that follows?
- What is the force of the net-and-bird saying in verse 17, and how does it connect with the reversal in verse 18?
- Should verse 33 be read as an absolute guarantee against temporal harm or as a general wisdom contrast with the self-destructive way of verses 31–32?
