## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A marked literary transition occurs at verse 17.

- **Verses 1–16:** Mostly short, self-contained sayings contrasting wisdom and folly, wealth and reputation, prudence and carelessness, generosity and oppression.
- **Verses 17–21:** A first-person instructional prologue. The pupil is told to hear, apply the heart, retain the teaching, trust the LORD, know truth, and become able to answer others.
- **Verses 22–29:** Direct admonitions about exploiting the poor, angry companions, debt guarantees, inherited boundaries, and diligent work.

The chapter therefore joins memorable observations about life with explicit instruction in how wisdom is received and practiced.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Proverbs 22 recognizes economic inequality without treating it as morally self-justifying. The rich exercise power and borrowers can become dependent (verse 7), yet rich and poor share one Maker (verse 2), generosity takes the concrete form of sharing bread (verse 9), and the LORD pleads the cause of the afflicted against exploitation (verses 22-23). Together these sayings illuminate [[Justice and Mercy]], though verse 2 is not grammatically presented as the explicit premise of every later saying.

Verses 17-21 portray [[Education and Learning]] as attentive hearing, inward retention, trust in the LORD, knowledge of truth, and readiness to answer. Verse 6 similarly emphasizes the enduring influence of childhood formation within [[Family]]. Because these are proverbs, their characteristic outcomes should not be converted into exceptionless guarantees.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several sayings use concrete images to make moral consequences memorable: prudence hides from approaching evil (verse 3); the froward path contains thorns and snares (verse 5); iniquity is seed producing a harvest (verse 8); the strange woman's mouth is a deep pit (verse 14); companionship with anger becomes a snare (verse 25); and unpaid liability can cost a debtor his bed (verse 27). These images translate abstract wisdom into dangers that can be seen, entered, harvested, or lost.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 17-21 coordinate ear, heart, and lips: wisdom is heard, retained inwardly, and fitted for truthful speech. The movement gives the instructional prologue coherence and connects [[Education and Learning]] with [[Truth]]. Proverbs 22:3 also repeats the wording of Proverbs 27:12, illustrating the preservation of duplicate sayings within Proverbs.

The instructional material beginning at verse 17 is widely compared with the Egyptian *Instruction of Amenemope*. Parallels in appeals to hear and retain instruction and in teachings about the poor, angry companions, and landmarks are real and historically important. They support literary relationship or participation in a shared ancient wisdom environment, but do not establish direct copying, direction of influence, authorship, or date.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Structure
> The change at verse 17 from largely self-contained, third-person observations to sustained second-person instruction is strong internal evidence for a literary boundary between verses 1–16 and 17–29.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted literary boundary · [[Literary Structure in Proverbs 22|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Comparative Ancient Near Eastern Literature *(tentative)*
> Proverbs 22:17-23:11 shows substantial thematic and structural resemblance to the Egyptian wisdom text 'The Instruction of Amenemope' (a scribal father-to-son instruction, likely composed sometime in the New Kingdom / early first millennium BC): both open with an appeal to 'give thine ear'/'hear' and 'apply thine heart,' and share a run of specific parallel topics (do not move the ancient boundary-marker/landmark; do not rob the poor because he is poor; do not associate with a hot-tempered/passionate man) in a similar order. This has led many biblical scholars to argue for a literary relationship between the Hebrew and Egyptian texts.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · parallels accepted; sequence and dependence disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How do verses 2 and 7 work together—one affirming a common Maker and the other acknowledging unequal social power?
- Why does verse 9 define generosity through the concrete act of sharing bread?
- What distinction should be made between a proverb's typical outcome and an unconditional promise, especially in verse 6?
- How does the ear–heart–lips sequence in verses 17–21 deepen the meaning of [[Education and Learning]]?
- What practices help prevent anger, scorn, and financial entanglement from becoming a "snare"?
