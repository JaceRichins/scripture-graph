## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter is a collection of individual proverbs rather than a continuous argument, but several local groupings are visible:

- Verses 1–2: limits on boasting and self-praise.
- Verses 3–4: escalating images of wrath, anger, and envy.
- Verses 5–10: correction, counsel, loyalty, and the practical value of nearby friendship.
- Verses 11–16: prudence, financial exposure, intrusive speech, and domestic contention.
- Verses 17–22: analogies concerning formation, service, self-knowledge, desire, praise, and resistant folly.
- Verses 23–27: a coherent closing poem on flocks, impermanent riches, seasonal renewal, and household provision.

These divisions are descriptive rather than proof of a single designed outline; repeated themes cross their boundaries.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Proverbs 27 offers practical wisdom rather than a systematic doctrinal exposition. Verses 1–2 support [[Humility]] by warning against boasting about an unknown tomorrow and praising oneself. Verses 5–6 and 9 emphasize faithful correction and heartfelt counsel, connecting most directly with [[Honesty]] while also requiring care: not every hurtful statement is a faithful wound, and verse 14 shows that even positive words can become intrusive through poor timing or manner.

Verses 23–27 strongly support [[Work and Self-Reliance]]. Diligence includes knowing the actual condition of one's resources and attending continually to household provision. Because riches and rank do not endure automatically, stewardship cannot be reduced to passive confidence in present wealth. The passage gives proverbial counsel, not a guarantee that diligent people will never suffer poverty, loss, or disaster.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several KJV expressions need care. In verse 16, “bewrayeth” means “reveals,” “discloses,” or “gives away”: the ointment makes its presence known. In verse 22, “bray” means to pound or crush in a mortar, not the sound of a donkey. The hyperbole is that even such extreme processing does not remove the fool's ingrained foolishness.

Verse 17 is less certain. “Countenance” refers to the face, but “sharpeneth the countenance” may describe enlivening, provoking, alerting, or more broadly influencing a friend. Familiar applications about improving another's character or intellect are plausible, but the wording does not establish one precise application. Verse 19 is likewise compact and may concern self-disclosure, correspondence between human hearts, or interpersonal recognition; it should not be reduced to a single uncontested paraphrase.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly reasons through concrete comparison: stone and sand convey the weight of wrath (verses 3–4), hunger changes the experienced taste of food (verse 7), wandering resembles a bird leaving its nest (verse 8), contention resembles incessant rain (verses 15–16), and personal interaction is compared with iron sharpening iron (verse 17). Verses 17–21 are especially dense with images drawn from iron, horticulture, reflected faces, insatiable realms, and metal refining.

Praise appears in two separated sayings: verse 2 rejects self-praise, while verse 21 compares a person's relation to praise with silver and gold in refining vessels. This thematic recurrence is worth noticing, but it does not by itself establish a deliberate literary frame, and the precise force of verse 21 remains uncertain.

Proverbs 27:12 repeats Proverbs 22:3 in the KJV. This verified repetition shows that the same maxim was preserved in more than one location within Proverbs, but it does not determine authorship, date, priority, or a particular compilation model. A proposed parallel between Proverbs 27:13 and Proverbs 20:16 should remain tentative until the discrepancy with the verified overlap index is resolved.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- When does correction become a faithful wound, and what safeguards keep verse 6 from being used to excuse cruelty?
- How does the ill-timed blessing in verse 14 complicate the assumption that good words always have good effects?
- Does verse 19 primarily concern self-knowledge, similarity between human hearts, or the way one heart responds to another?
- What qualities make a nearby neighbor more useful in calamity than distant kin, and how should that observation coexist with obligations to [[Family]]?
- How does the agricultural economy of verses 23–27 illustrate stewardship without becoming a simplistic promise of material success?
