# Proverbs 26 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Proverbs 26]] · My notes: [[Proverbs 26 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Honor is not fitting for a fool—Answer not a fool according to his folly—Where there is no talebearer, the strife ceases.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1–12 — Sayings centered mainly on fools and folly:** Honor, responsibility, and even a proverb can become unfitting, ineffective, or dangerous in connection with foolishness. Verse 2 is a partial outlier concerning a causeless curse.
2. **Verses 13–16 — The sluggard:** Excuses and motion without progress culminate in confidence in one's own wisdom.
3. **Verses 17–22 — Meddling and inflamed conflict:** Reckless interference, harmful deception, talebearing, and contentiousness create or sustain danger.
4. **Verses 23–28 — Concealed hostility and destructive speech:** Pleasant words can conceal hatred and deceit, which lead toward exposure, reversal, and ruin.

These are useful descriptive divisions based on repeated vocabulary and subject matter. The chapter does not explicitly mark them, and they may reflect topical collection of independent sayings rather than a continuous argument.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The repeated warning against being wise in one's own conceit connects [[Pride]] with resistance to correction (verses 5, 12, 16). Verses 4–5 place two dangers side by side: answering may draw a person into folly, while silence may leave the fool confirmed in self-conceit. A plausible practical reading is that wise response requires attention to circumstances, though the pair gives no complete decision rule.

The portrait of the sluggard supports reflection on [[Work and Self-Reliance]]. Verses 18–28 connect [[Honesty]] not merely with pleasant wording but with whether speech corresponds to inward purpose and conduct.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase **“wise in his own conceit”** recurs in verses 12 and 16, linking self-assured folly with the sluggard's resistance to reason. Verse 23 compares speech joined to a wicked heart with a potsherd covered by silver dross; the general contrast between attractive appearance and a hostile interior is clear, but the precise senses of “burning lips” and the metallic coating should not be pressed without lexical evidence. Verse 25's **“seven abominations”** may convey fullness or conventional numerical rhetoric, but Proverbs 6:16–19 does not by itself prove that the number is nonliteral here.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Concrete comparisons drive much of the chapter: untimely weather, animal restraints, impaired legs, a stone in a sling, a thorn in a drunkard's hand, a dog returning to vomit, a door turning on hinges, a dog seized by the ears, fire deprived of or supplied with fuel, and coated pottery. The concentration is conspicuous within this chapter, although no corpus-wide count establishes that it exceeds most other chapters.

Verses 4–5 form the most striking pair. Their repeated opening and opposite commands create a controlled tension, and each closing clause names a different danger. Reading the pair as instruction in situational discernment is plausible; alternatively, independent sayings may have been placed together through shared catchwords. Verses 12 and 16 link neighboring clusters through the repeated language of being wise “in his own conceit.” Verse 27 also parallels the reversal imagery of Psalm 7:15–16 and Ecclesiastes 10:8.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. What features of a conversation indicate that answering will expose folly rather than reproduce it (verses 4–5)?
2. Why does self-conceit leave even less hope than ordinary folly (verse 12)?
3. How do excuses, inactivity, and confidence in one's own judgment reinforce one another in verses 13–16?
4. In verses 20–21, which participants supply the 'wood' that keeps a conflict alive?
5. How does the silvered-potsherd image help distinguish sincere kindness from flattering or deceptive speech (verses 23–28)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

- Compare Proverbs 26:27 with Psalm 7:15–16 and Ecclesiastes 10:8 without assuming a particular direction of borrowing.
- Consult multiple translations and a reputable textual commentary before building an interpretation on Proverbs 26:10, whose wording is rendered substantially differently across translations.
- Investigate Proverbs 26:22 and Proverbs 18:8 directly before describing them as an exact duplicate, because that claim conflicts with the supplied mechanical overlap index.
