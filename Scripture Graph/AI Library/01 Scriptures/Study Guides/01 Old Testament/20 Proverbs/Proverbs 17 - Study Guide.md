## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter is a collection of associated sayings rather than a demonstrated continuous outline. A provisional reading map is: verses 1–6, household order and intergenerational relationships; verses 7–16, speech, gifts, correction, conflict, justice, and desire for wisdom; verses 17–26, loyalty, reckless commitments, strife, inner condition, family grief, and public judgment; and verses 27–28, two adjacent sayings about restrained speech. The clearest internal repetition is the near-doublet about parental grief in verses 21 and 25. These groupings are study aids, not evidence of a formal original structure, and the modern chapter boundary does not establish deliberate literary closure.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter grounds conduct in divine and creational accountability: the LORD tests hearts (verse 3), mocking the poor reproaches their Maker (verse 5), and opposite distortions of judgment are an abomination to the LORD (verse 15). Its strongest connection to [[Justice and Mercy]] is on the justice side, especially impartial judgment and resistance to bribery. Verse 9 associates love with not needlessly publicizing or reviving an offense, but it does not require concealing abuse, danger, or injustice. The chapter presents [[Family]] realistically as a source of honor, loyalty, grief, and bitterness. Verse 17 locates loyal relationship within [[Adversity]], while verses 10 and 16 show that [[Education and Learning]] requires receptivity and inward desire, not merely punishment, access, or resources. Its warnings about false and perverse speech also connect directly with [[Honesty]].

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several concrete images compress complex judgments. A refining vessel pictures divine testing (verse 3); a bereaved bear pictures the danger of entrenched folly (verse 12); released water pictures rapidly escalating strife (verse 14); a price held by a heartless fool pictures resources without desire for wisdom (verse 16); medicine and dried bones picture the embodied effects of inner condition (verse 22); and wandering eyes picture unfocused folly (verse 24). The repeated English word "gift" in verses 8 and 23 deserves contextual reading because only the latter explicitly identifies judicial corruption.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Antithetical parallelism gives many sayings their force: quietness versus strife, wise responsiveness versus stubborn folly, covering versus repeating an offense, acquitting the wicked versus condemning the just, merriment versus brokenness, and focused wisdom versus wandering attention. Concrete images make abstract judgments memorable: refining vessels for divine testing (verse 3), a bereaved bear for dangerous folly (verse 12), and released water for escalating conflict (verse 14). Verses 27–28 form an adjacent speech-themed pair: genuine knowledge restrains words, while silence can at least give a fool the appearance of wisdom. Their adjacency is meaningful, but it does not prove that they were designed as the conclusion to a chapter.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Closure And Thematic Reinforcement *(tentative)*
> The final two verses intensify a shared lesson about disciplined speech: knowledge spares words, and even a fool can be regarded as wise by remaining silent. Their adjacency provides meaningful evidence for a deliberate closing emphasis on verbal restraint.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · shared theme affirmed; deliberate closure disputed

> [!evidence] Lexical And Contextual Contrast *(tentative)*
> Within the chapter's canonical wording, the term "gift" appears first in a saying about its perceived power to prosper and later in an explicitly corrupt judicial transaction. The contrast is evidence that a gift's moral evaluation depends upon its purpose and context, although verse 8 may itself be describing influence cynically rather than approving it.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · lexical recurrence accepted; moral contrast disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- When does covering an offense serve love, and when would silence instead protect ongoing wrongdoing?
- Does verse 8 describe an ordinary gift, the recipient's biased perception, or the apparent effectiveness of a bribe?
- What makes reproof penetrate more deeply than severe punishment?
- How do verses 1 and 14 challenge habits that allow tension to develop into strife?
- How can verse 22 be applied as wisdom about embodied well-being without turning it into a universal medical promise?
- What might the 'exalted gate' in verse 19 signify, and what remains uncertain without stronger lexical or historical evidence?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
