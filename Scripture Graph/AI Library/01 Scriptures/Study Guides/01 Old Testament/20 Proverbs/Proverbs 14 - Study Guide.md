<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter is a collection of short sayings rather than a continuous argument, but several thematic clusters provide cohesion:

- Verses 1–9 contrast wisdom and folly in households, speech, testimony, and conduct.
- Verses 10–15 emphasize hidden inward experience, misleading appearances, and prudent self-examination.
- Verses 16–18 and 29 connect anger, confidence, and self-control with folly or understanding.
- Verses 20–21 and 31 move from the social vulnerability of the poor to a clear obligation of mercy.
- Verses 26–27 develop the fear of the LORD as confidence, refuge, and a fountain of life.
- Verses 28, 34, and 35 apply moral wisdom to rulers and nations.

These groupings are useful thematic observations, not proof of a comprehensive formal outline.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Proverbs 14 connects reverence for God with conduct and its consequences. Walking uprightly expresses fear of the LORD (verse 2), while verses 26–27 associate that fear with confidence, refuge, and a “fountain of life.” Verse 16 may describe general caution rather than explicitly referring to fear of the LORD.

The chapter's treatment of poverty links [[Justice and Mercy]] with accountability to God. Verse 20 reports the social isolation of the poor; verses 21 and 31 then condemn contempt and oppression and commend mercy. Oppressing the poor “reproacheth his Maker,” while mercy honors him. This establishes theological accountability without requiring the stronger claim that the verse states a developed doctrine of universal human dignity.

Verses 5 and 25 connect [[Honesty]] with consequences for others through faithful or deceitful witness. Verse 32 says in the supplied KJV that the righteous has [[Hope]] in death, but the verse does not explain that hope or provide a developed doctrine of the afterlife.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Way” language carries an epistemic warning. The prudent understands his way (verse 8), the apparently right way can terminate in death (verse 12), and the prudent watches his going (verse 15). Wisdom is therefore represented as disciplined moral navigation.

The “fountain of life” in verse 27 contrasts with “snares of death.” The paired images present reverence for the LORD as both a positive source of life and a means of avoiding destructive traps.

Verse 4 uses concrete agricultural imagery to express a practical tradeoff: an unused crib may remain clean, but an ox supplies the strength that produces increase. Cleanliness or convenience is not automatically evidence of fruitfulness.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Antithetical parallelism dominates Proverbs 14: wise and foolish, upright and perverse, faithful and false, prudent and simple, and righteous and wicked are repeatedly contrasted. Some couplets use more complex contrasts, so the presence of “but” should not be treated as a complete classification of their poetic form.

Verses 5 and 25 form a notable witness recurrence: the first contrasts faithful and false witnesses, while the second emphasizes testimony's consequences. Because witness sayings are common throughout Proverbs, this recurrence is not strong evidence of a designed chapter-wide structure.

Proverbs 14:12 closely parallels [[Proverbs 16]] 25, and Proverbs 14:27 similarly echoes [[Proverbs 13]] 14. These parallels establish repetition of proverbial formulas within the book but do not determine authorship, editorial purpose, chronology, or direction of dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How do verses 8, 12, and 15 distinguish confidence from genuine discernment?
2. Why might the text place the social observation of verse 20 beside the moral correction of verse 21?
3. In what sense can fear produce “strong confidence” and function as a “fountain of life” in verses 26–27?
4. What costs of genuine productivity are illustrated by the ox and the crib in verse 4?
5. How do verses 10 and 13 complicate judgments based solely on another person’s outward mood?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Proverbs 14:12 with Proverbs 16:25, noting both the repetition and the small wording difference. Trace the chapter’s three major ethical clusters: truthful testimony (verses 5 and 25), mercy toward the poor (verses 20–21 and 31), and disciplined anger (verses 16–17 and 29). These provide focused entry points for studying [[Honesty]], [[Justice and Mercy]], and wisdom as self-government.
