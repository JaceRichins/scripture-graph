# Proverbs 28 — Study Guide

## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter opens (v.1) and closes (v.28) with contrasts between the wicked and the righteous, giving loose bracketing, though the two verses do not share imagery beyond that broad contrast. It is better approached as a collection of related sayings than as a single continuous argument, but recurring threads provide cohesion: verses 4, 7, and 9 concern responses to the law; verses 2, 12, 15-16, and 28 concern rulers and public conditions; and verses 3, 6, 8, 11, 15, 19-22, and 27 repeatedly return to poverty, riches, labor, and generosity. The closely related wording of verses 12 and 28 ('when the wicked rise...') gives the chapter's ending a possible summarizing function, though repetition alone does not prove a deliberately engineered literary frame.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verse 13 distinguishes genuine repentance from concealment: confession is joined with forsaking sin before mercy is promised, aligning with [[Repentance and Forgiveness]] as taught elsewhere in scripture. Verse 9 adds that religious speech cannot substitute for [[Obedience]]: deliberately refusing to hear the law makes [[Prayer]] itself unacceptable. Verses 25-26 oppose [[Pride]] and uncritical self-trust to trust in the Lord and walking wisely. The chapter's extended attention to the poor (vv.3, 6, 8, 11, 19, 21-22, 24, 27) — condemning oppression, unjust gain, and deliberate indifference while commending generosity — places economic conduct within [[Justice and Mercy]] rather than treating it as mere practical advice.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Most sayings use antithetic parallelism: upright and perverse ways, confession and concealment, faithful steadiness and haste for riches, trust in the Lord and trust in one's own heart. Animal imagery is morally flexible rather than fixed: the righteous are 'bold as a lion' in verse 1, but a wicked ruler is compared to 'a roaring lion, and a ranging bear' in verse 15 — the image itself conveys power or intensity whose moral force depends on its subject, not necessarily a deliberate authorial callback. Verses 12 and 28 share the near-identical clause 'when the wicked rise,' a refrain-like repetition whose compositional intent cannot be confirmed from the text alone. Verses 20 and 22 form an internal doublet warning against haste to riches, and per the verified index, verse 6 closely duplicates [[Proverbs 19]]:1 — evidence of shared proverbial material adapted across the wisdom collection, though this need not indicate direct literary dependence in either direction.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How do verses 3, 6, and 11 prevent a simplistic identification of either poverty or wealth with righteousness? What distinction does verse 13 make between admitting wrongdoing and abandoning it, and how does that relate to repentance as taught elsewhere in scripture? Why might verses 20 and 22 object specifically to haste for riches rather than to possession of wealth itself? What do verses 12, 15-16, and 28 imply about the social consequences of a ruler's character? What does the near-duplication of Proverbs 28:6 and 19:1 suggest about how individual proverbs circulated and were adapted before being fixed in their present chapters? How should the categorical promises in verses 18, 20, and 27 be read alongside real cases where upright or generous people still suffer?
