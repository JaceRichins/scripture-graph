## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful, provisional outline is:

- **25:1:** Superscription and attributed transmission.
- **25:2-7:** Divine concealment, royal inquiry, purification of the court, and conduct before rulers.
- **25:8-15:** Restraint in disputes and examples of fitting, faithful, empty, or persuasive speech.
- **25:16-20:** Moderation, social boundaries, false testimony, unreliable support, and emotional insensitivity.
- **25:21-22:** Material kindness toward an enemy and the disputed coals-of-fire image.
- **25:23-26:** A loose group of social and relational comparisons.
- **25:27-28:** Excessive pursuit of glory and rule over one's spirit.

These clusters are reading aids, not proof of a continuous argument or unified original composition; Proverbs is an anthology, and several boundaries remain porous.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter brings public responsibility and personal virtue into close proximity. [[Government and Laws]] is relevant to the honor of searching out a matter and to the ideal of a throne established in righteousness (25:2, 4-5). [[Humility]] opposes self-placement among the great and pursuit of personal glory (25:6-7, 27). [[Honesty]] is implicated in warnings about secrets, false promises, false witness, and backbiting (25:9-10, 14, 18, 23). [[Charity]] appears concretely in giving food and water to an enemy (25:21-22), while [[Justice and Mercy]] offers a broader framework for considering righteous rule alongside benevolence. [[Romans 12]] later quotes the enemy-feeding instruction within a non-retaliation argument; that reuse does not settle the meaning of the coals metaphor.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter reasons heavily through simile. Refined silver explains a purified court (25:4-5); precious ornaments explain fitting correction (25:11-12); harvest coolness explains a faithful messenger (25:13); empty storm clouds explain false promises (25:14); weapons explain false testimony (25:18); damaged body parts explain misplaced confidence (25:19); polluted water explains righteous collapse (25:26); and a breached city explains lack of self-control (25:28). These comparisons translate moral qualities into consequences that can be seen, felt, or experienced.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly converts moral judgments into concrete images: dross removed from silver (25:4-5), precious ornaments (25:11-12), harvest coolness (25:13), rainless clouds (25:14), weapons (25:18), damaged body parts (25:19), polluted water (25:26), and a breached city (25:28). Some sayings gain force through tension or reversal. A soft tongue breaks a bone (25:15); desirable honey becomes harmful in excess (25:16, 27); and kindness toward an enemy is followed by the severe but unexplained image of fiery coals (25:21-22). These patterns support close literary reading but do not by themselves prove deliberate chapter-wide composition.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Editorial Superscription
> The superscription presents this collection as Solomonic proverbs copied by officials associated with King Hezekiah. It is internal evidence for an editorial or scribal stage distinct from the sayings' attributed origin.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 1.0 · accepted observation / historical interpretation disputed · [[Internal Editorial Superscription in Proverbs 25|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Canonical Intertextual Reuse
> Romans 12:20 reuses the commands to feed and give drink to an enemy together with the image of heaping coals of fire upon the enemy's head. This is strong evidence that Proverbs 25:21-22 became part of early Christian ethical instruction.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted canonical reuse · [[Canonical Intertextual Reuse in Proverbs 25|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Textual/Authorship Claim
> Proverbs 25:1's claim that Hezekiah's men copied out this collection is internal textual evidence for a stage of editorial compilation in the book's literary history, but does not by itself establish the historical accuracy of that attribution.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.9 · accepted internal evidence; historical interpretation disputed · [[Internal Textual/Authorship Claim in Proverbs 25|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What kinds of concealment and searching are commended in 25:2, and how might verse 3 complicate the contrast?
- How does the removal of dross illuminate the removal of wicked influence before a king (25:4-5), and what does the analogy leave unspecified?
- What distinguishes responsible privacy in a dispute from harmful disclosure or backbiting (25:9-10, 23)?
- Do the coals of fire in 25:22 suggest shame, repentance, judgment, or an image whose cultural background is no longer clear? How does the reuse in [[Romans 12]] shape without settling the question?
- How do the repeated honey images distinguish healthy enjoyment from excess (25:16, 27)?
- In 25:26, does the righteous person yield morally, suffer defeat, or lose public integrity before the wicked?
