## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A rigid outline would overstate the chapter's continuity, but several local groupings are visible:

- Verses 1–4 warn against impaired judgment, provoking rulers, strife, and neglected labor.
- Verses 5–12 concern counsel, integrity, judgment, purity, honest measurement, conduct, and perception.
- Verses 13–21 collect warnings about sleep, bargaining, surety, deception, planning, gossip, family dishonor, and hasty wealth.
- Verses 22–30 turn toward retaliation, divine direction, vows, kingship, inward searching, age, and painful correction.

The repetition of dishonest weights in verses 10 and 23 is clearer than any proposed comprehensive literary pattern.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins religious accountability to ordinary behavior. Dishonest measurement is an offense against the LORD, not merely poor business practice (verses 10, 23). Human beings cannot credibly claim complete moral purity (verse 9), and their paths remain partly opaque under divine direction (verse 24). Verse 22 directs the injured person away from private vengeance and toward trust in the LORD; it should not automatically be expanded into a ban on lawful protection or public justice.

The royal material is also balanced: judgment restrains evil (verses 8, 26), while mercy and truth preserve both ruler and throne (verse 28). This supports study under [[Government and Laws]] and [[Justice and Mercy]].

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter frequently makes consequences concrete through physical images: strong drink rages, concealed counsel is deep water, deceptive bread becomes gravel, a dishonored person's lamp goes dark, and the human spirit is the LORD's candle. These images communicate moral effects vividly without necessarily supplying technical definitions of the soul, conscience, inspiration, or punishment.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 27's image of 'the spirit of man' as 'the candle of the LORD, searching all the inward parts of the belly' is a striking metaphor for the LORD's capacity to search a person's inward life, without resolving into a developed doctrine of conscience on its own. Several sayings use contrast or delayed reversal: claimed goodness versus rare faithfulness (20:6-7), immediate sweetness versus later gravel (20:17), and a hastily gotten inheritance versus an unblessed end (20:21). Verse 15's comparison of wisdom's worth ('the lips of knowledge are a precious jewel') to gold and rubies echoes the same rubies-versus-wisdom imagery used elsewhere in the book (Proverbs 3:15; 8:11), suggesting a recurring stock image rather than unique phrasing — and verse 6's rhetorical question ('a faithful man who can find?') uses a construction that also appears in Proverbs 31:10, though whether this is a deliberate echo or a common idiom is unclear. Proverbs 20:16 also has a near-verbatim parallel in [[Proverbs 27]] (27:13), consistent with the reuse of individual sayings across the book's collections.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How do verses 8, 26, and 28 hold judgment and mercy together in their portrait of kingship? What distinction does verse 6 make between announcing goodness and proving faithfulness? Does verse 24 primarily encourage trust, humility about providence, or both? How should the severe disciplinary image in verse 30 be interpreted without turning a descriptive ancient proverb into an unrestricted mandate for violence?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
