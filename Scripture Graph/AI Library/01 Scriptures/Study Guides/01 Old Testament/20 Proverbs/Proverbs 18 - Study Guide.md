## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
Proverbs 18 does not state a continuous argument, so any outline is approximate. Loose local concentrations can nevertheless guide study:

- Verses 1–9: understanding, foolish speech, injustice, gossip, and negligent work.
- Verses 10–12: refuge, wealth, pride, and humility.
- Verses 13–19: hearing, knowledge, testimony, dispute settlement, and offended relationships.
- Verses 20–24: consequential speech, [[Marriage]], social inequality, and friendship.

Several verses resist these labels, so the divisions describe thematic proximity rather than a demonstrable four-part design.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verse 10 presents the name of the LORD as refuge. The adjacent image of wealth as a strong city and a high wall “in his own conceit” may contrast divine safety with perceived or exaggerated material security; it does not teach that wealth is inherently evil. Verse 12 links [[Pride]] with destruction and [[Humility]] with honor. Verse 22 values finding a wife as good and as favor from the LORD, supporting a positive view of [[Marriage]] without supplying a complete theology of marriage or a guarantee about every marriage. The chapter also commends hearing and examination before confident judgment.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Speech is the chapter's most persistent lexical field, expressed through “words,” “mouth,” “lips,” “answereth,” “heareth,” “ear,” and “tongue.” Verse 4 compares a man's words with deep waters and the wellspring of wisdom with a flowing brook. Verses 20–21 use fruit and eating imagery for the consequences of what the mouth produces. Fortification vocabulary also recurs: the LORD's name is a “strong tower” (verse 10), wealth a “strong city” and perceived “high wall” (verse 11), and an offended relationship a strong city barred like a castle (verse 19). The KJV's “wounds” in verse 8 and the first clause of verse 24 are translation-sensitive.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several nearby sayings reinforce or contrast with one another. Verses 10–11 place two fortification images side by side; verse 12 balances haughtiness and destruction against humility and honor; and verses 20–21 form a close pair through mouth, lips, fruit, eating, death, and life. Verses 13, 15, and 17 can be read together as an ethic of cautious inquiry, although they may remain independent maxims. The return of tower, city, wall, and castle-bar imagery in verses 10, 11, and 19 is a genuine motif, but neither this recurrence nor the local pairings prove a single author, a deliberate chapter-wide progression, or a precise compositional history.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How does the qualification "in his own conceit" alter the apparent similarity between the two kinds of security in verses 10-11? What practices would satisfy the demand to hear a matter before answering it in verses 13 and 17? When should "death and life" in verse 21 be read as concrete social consequences, and when as deliberate proverbial intensification? Does verse 19 mainly warn against giving offense, describe the difficulty of reconciliation, or both?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Proverbs 18:8 with its exact KJV parallel in [[Proverbs 26]], and compare the shared wealth-as-strong-city clause in Proverbs 18:11 with [[Proverbs 10]]. [[Proverbs 16]] closely parallels the connection between pride and destruction, though it should not be cited as an exact repetition of the complete pride/humility balance in Proverbs 18:12. For [[Education and Learning]], compare the fool's preference for self-disclosure over understanding (verse 2), the wise pursuit of knowledge (verse 15), and the testing of an initially persuasive case (verse 17). These comparisons illuminate recurring wisdom themes without establishing authorship or direction of borrowing.
