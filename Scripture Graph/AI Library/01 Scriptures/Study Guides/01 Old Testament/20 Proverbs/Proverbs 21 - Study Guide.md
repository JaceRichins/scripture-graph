# Proverbs 21 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Proverbs 21]] · My notes: [[Proverbs 21 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Proverbs 21 gathers short sayings about divine sovereignty, moral self-deception, justice, work, wealth, speech, pride, household conflict, and preparation for danger. Its clearest theological thread is that the LORD judges beneath appearances: human ways may seem right to their owners, but hearts, intentions, and conduct remain subject to divine evaluation. The chapter repeatedly places [[Justice and Mercy]] above merely outward religious or social advantage.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter is not a continuous argument but a loosely ordered collection of independent couplets, typical of Proverbs 10-29. Several recurring clusters give it coherence rather than a formal outline:
- **Divine sovereignty and judgment:** vv.1-3, 30-31
- **Work, wealth, and consumption:** vv.5, 17, 20, 25-26
- **Justice and care for others:** vv.3, 7, 13, 15, 21, 26-27
- **Speech and testimony:** vv.6, 23, 28
- **Pride and hardened conduct:** vv.4, 24, 29

One concrete literary feature stands out beyond thematic grouping: vv.9 and 19 form a **doublet**, both preferring a harsher solitary dwelling to life with a contentious woman, and v.9 is a near-verbatim match to [[Proverbs 25]] 25:24 — confirmed by this chapter's verified text overlap with Proverbs 25. This shows a saying was preserved and reused across collections; it is not evidence that the chapter itself has a deliberate macro-structure.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter does not permit ritual to compensate for injustice. Proverbs 21:3 and 21:27 connect acceptable [[Sacrifice]] with moral conduct and right intent. Proverbs 21:13, 21, and 26 place mercy and generosity within righteous life, while verse 2 warns that sincere self-approval is not the final measure of goodness.

Proverbs 21:31 also gives a restrained account of providence: the horse may be prepared, but safety is attributed to the LORD. The saying need not condemn planning; it limits confidence in human means. Similarly, verse 1 affirms divine sovereignty over rulers without explaining the precise relationship between providence and [[Agency]].

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Much of the chapter uses antithetical parallelism: one course of life is set against another so that the contrast carries the instruction. Examples include human self-assessment versus the LORD's examination (verse 2), diligence versus haste (verse 5), and military preparation versus divine safety (verse 31). Verses 9 and 19 employ repeated “better … than” comparisons and exaggerated living conditions to portray the cost of domestic contention.

Several expressions remain opaque in the supplied English wording, especially “the plowing of the wicked” in verse 4, “a ransom for the righteous” in verse 18, and “the man that heareth speaketh constantly” in verse 28. Strong conclusions from these phrases require comparison with other translations and the underlying Hebrew.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The near-identical 'brawling woman' sayings (vv.9, 19; cf. [[Proverbs 25]] 25:24) show how individual proverbs could circulate and be redeployed across different collections within the book — a concrete, textually-verified instance of literary reuse. The chapter's opening (v.1, the king's heart) and closing (v.31, the war horse) both subject impressive human power to the LORD, an echo worth noting for readers; but given the loosely-ordered nature of Proverbs 10-29, this should be read as a reader-level pattern rather than evidence of deliberate authorial framing.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Intra-Canonical Parallel/Motif *(tentative)*
> Verse 22 ('A wise man scaleth the city of the mighty, and casteth down the strength of the confidence thereof') articulates the wisdom-outweighs-might motif, closely paralleled by the account in Ecclesiastes 9:14-15 of a poor wise man who delivers a besieged city by his wisdom.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · generic wisdom-over-might motif

> [!evidence] Intra-Canonical Parallel *(tentative)*
> Verse 18 ('The wicked shall be a ransom for the righteous, and the transgressor for the upright') describes a this-worldly pattern in which calamity meant for or generated by the wicked ends up sparing the righteous, closely paralleling Proverbs 11:8 ('The righteous is delivered out of trouble, and the wicked cometh in his stead').
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · disputed mechanism, correctly rejects atonement eisegesis

> [!evidence] Internal Textual Convergence *(tentative)*
> Proverbs 21:3 and 21:27 provide strong internal textual evidence that this chapter rejects ritual sacrifice as a substitute for justice or as a cover for wicked intent.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How do verses 3 and 27 distinguish opposition to corrupt worship from opposition to [[Sacrifice]] itself?
2. How can verse 31 preserve both responsible preparation and reliance on God?
3. Are the material outcomes in verses 5, 17, 20, and 25 intended as universal promises or as ordinary tendencies?
4. How should the gendered household sayings in verses 9 and 19 be studied without turning their rhetoric into a blanket judgment about women?
5. Does verse 14 approve the secret gift, merely describe its effect, or assume a legitimate conciliatory gift rather than a bribe?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

A comparison with [[Proverbs 10]] is also warranted given this chapter's verified text overlap there, though the exact matching verse pair has not yet been independently confirmed and should be resolved before drawing conclusions about direct reuse.
