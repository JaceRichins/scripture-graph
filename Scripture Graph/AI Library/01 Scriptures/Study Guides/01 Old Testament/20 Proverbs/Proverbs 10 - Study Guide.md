# Proverbs 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Proverbs 10]] · My notes: [[Proverbs 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> A wise son makes a glad father—The mouth of a righteous man is a well of life—He who utters slander is a fool—The desire of the righteous will be granted.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter is composed chiefly of short, self-contained contrasts. Verses 1-5 emphasize family conduct, gain, and work; verses 6-21 concentrate heavily on reputation, teachability, and speech; verses 22-30 emphasize blessing, desire, duration, and stability; verses 31-32 return to the mouth and lips. These are useful thematic groupings, not a certain formal outline, because several subjects recur across the proposed boundaries.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The sayings associate receiving correction with the way of life (Proverbs 10:8, 17), restrained and nourishing speech with wisdom (10:19-21), and fear of the LORD with prolonged days (10:27). They also present righteousness as more valuable than illicit treasure when confronting death (10:2). Verse 22's attribution of wealth to "the blessing of the LORD" sits alongside verse 4's teaching that "the hand of the diligent maketh rich" — a tension worth noticing rather than resolving into a single formula. Because proverbial sayings are compressed, their stated outcomes should not automatically be treated as exceptionless guarantees about every person's present circumstances.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Speech vocabulary is unusually dense: "mouth," "lips," "tongue," and "words" recur throughout Proverbs 10:6-21 and 10:31-32. The text distinguishes speech by both source and effect: righteous speech can be a "well of life" and can "feed many," whereas lying, slander, uncontrolled words, and froward speech bring harm.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Antithetical parallelism dominates the chapter, placing opposed moral types or consequences on either side of "but." Two exact repeated half-lines deserve attention: "but a prating fool shall fall" (10:8, 10) and "but violence covereth the mouth of the wicked" (10:6, 11). These repetitions link nearby sayings, although they do not prove a comprehensive hidden structure or single compositional setting.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does reading these verses as proverbs rather than unconditional predictions affect the interpretation of Proverbs 10:3, 24, 27, and 30?
- Does "love covereth all sins" in Proverbs 10:12 describe forgiveness, restraint from publicizing faults, conflict prevention, or some combination of these?
- How do the speech sayings distinguish wise silence from speech that actively gives life and nourishment?
- Does Proverbs 10:15 endorse economic inequality, merely describe its practical effects, or warn how poverty itself magnifies vulnerability?
- Does the repetition of full lines (10:6/11, 10:8/10) change how you read the chapter's origin — as one composition, or a woven anthology?
