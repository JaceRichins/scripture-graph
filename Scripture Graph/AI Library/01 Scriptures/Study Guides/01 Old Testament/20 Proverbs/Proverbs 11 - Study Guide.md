# Proverbs 11 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Proverbs 11]] · My notes: [[Proverbs 11 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The state and rewards of the righteous and the wicked are contrasted—When a wicked man dies, his expectations perish—He who wins souls is wise.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
Proverbs 11 is better read as a collection of related sayings than as a continuous argument. Approximate thematic concentrations include:

- Verses 1–8: integrity, righteousness, wealth, death, and deliverance.
- Verses 9–14: neighbors, the city, speech, discretion, secrets, and counsel.
- Verses 15–23: surety, honor, mercy, reward, life and death, divine approval, and desire.
- Verses 24–31: generosity, commerce, trust in wealth, household consequences, flourishing, and recompense.

Antithetical parallelism repeatedly places contrasting characters and trajectories side by side. These groupings are study aids, not demonstrated editorial divisions.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter makes moral character concrete. A just balance belongs to [[Honesty]]; pride is associated with shame and lowliness with wisdom; restraint, faithfulness, and counsel protect relationships and communities; and mercy benefits the merciful person. These themes are relevant to [[Pride]], [[Humility]], and [[Justice and Mercy]].

Verses 4 and 28 deny that riches provide dependable deliverance, while verses 24–26 commend openhanded conduct and access to needed goods. These sayings neither define a complete economic system nor guarantee a fixed monetary return. Their reward-and-consequence language affirms moral order but should not be converted into exceptionless promises of immediate prosperity. References to wrath, death, and recompense may contribute to reflection on [[Final Judgment]], but the chapter does not itself present a complete doctrine of final judgment.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several KJV expressions require care because modern readers may hear meanings narrower than the passage warrants. Terms such as "hypocrite," "naughtiness," "froward," "surety," "liberal," and "made fat" merit comparison with historical English, the underlying Hebrew, and other translations before narrow lexical conclusions are drawn.

The phrase "he that winneth souls is wise" in verse 30 is especially uncertain. It can be applied devotionally to [[Missionary Work]], but the English verse does not explicitly mention preaching or conversion. Lexical meaning in the proverb should therefore be distinguished from later missionary application.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Antithesis is the chapter's dominant technique: false and just measures, pride and lowliness, integrity and perverseness, righteousness and wickedness, and scattering and withholding. The repeated form invites comparison of both conduct and expected trajectories without proving that the chapter was composed as one continuous argument.

The chapter also uses vivid images of sowing, watering, flourishing branches, inherited wind, and a [[Tree of Life]]. Verse 22 employs a deliberately grotesque and gendered simile, comparing beauty without discretion to gold in a swine's snout. Verses 24–25 use paradox: scattering can accompany increase, and one who waters others can also be watered.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Does the abomination/delight language in verses 1 and 20 deliberately frame the intervening sayings, or does it reflect recurring wisdom vocabulary?
2. Which consequences are presented as ordinary effects within mortal society, and which invite an ultimate judgment reading?
3. How do verses 10-14 expand wisdom from private conduct to civic health?
4. What does verse 26 condemn: scarcity profiteering, refusal to sell needed food, hoarding generally, or some combination?
5. How does the ancient meaning behind "winneth souls" compare with its common modern missionary application?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Proverbs 11:1 with Leviticus 19:35–36 and Deuteronomy 25:13–16 for legal and ethical parallels concerning honest measurement; similarity does not by itself prove direct dependence. Compare [[Proverbs 10]] and [[Proverbs 12]] for surrounding righteous–wicked contrasts. For the tree-of-life motif, compare Genesis 2:9, [[Proverbs 3]], [[Proverbs 13]], and [[Proverbs 15]], distinguishing shared imagery from demonstrated allusion. First Peter 4:18 is relevant to the reception of Proverbs 11:31, particularly through comparison with the ancient Greek form. Study the Hebrew and major translations of Proverbs 11:30 before assigning "winneth souls" a narrow missionary meaning.
