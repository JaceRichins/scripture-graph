# Proverbs 24 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Proverbs 24]] · My notes: [[Proverbs 24 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> In a multitude of counselors there is safety—Fret not yourself because of evil men—It is not good to show partiality in judgment.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv.1-2** — do not envy or desire the company of evil men, whose hearts plot destruction.
- **vv.3-9** — wisdom builds, strengthens, and counsels; folly and scorn are rebuked.
- **vv.10-12** — adversity tests strength; a duty to rescue those drawn toward death, with no ignorance-excuse before the God who weighs hearts.
- **vv.13-20** — wisdom compared to honey; the just recover from repeated setbacks while the wicked collapse (v.16, though "falleth" may denote calamity as much as moral failure); warnings against gloating over an enemy's fall (vv.17-18) and against envying the wicked, whose future prospects will fail (vv.19-20, echoing vv.1-2).
- **vv.21-22** — fear the LORD and the king; avoid those "given to change" (a phrase whose precise referent — political rebels, the fickle, or something else — isn't settled by the English text alone).
- **vv.23-29** — a second set of sayings introduced "these things also belong to the wise": impartial judgment (23-26), ordered preparation (27), honest witness, and rejection of personal retaliation (28-29).
- **vv.30-34** — a first-person eyewitness vignette on a neglected field, closing with a couplet duplicated verbatim at [[Proverbs 6]] 6:10-11 — a mechanically verified textual parallel.

This is a thematic outline rather than proof of a single continuous argument; v.23's heading plausibly opens a second collection, but that is an editorial judgment, not something the text states outright.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Active responsibility for the vulnerable**: it is not enough to be personally innocent; failing to rescue those "drawn unto death" invites accountability before a God who weighs the heart and "render[s]... according to his works" (vv.11-12).
- **Resilience under adversity**: "a just man falleth seven times, and riseth up again" (v.16) is best read, alongside the surrounding attack-on-the-righteous imagery (v.15) and the wicked's collapse, as repeated recovery from calamity or reversal rather than a statement about repeated sin — though the verse doesn't rule out a broader devotional reading.
- **Restraint toward the wicked**: rejoicing over an enemy's fall risks displeasing the LORD, who "may" turn his wrath away from the enemy instead (vv.17-18); the text doesn't spell out a full doctrine of how divine wrath operates. Personal retaliation ("I will do so to him as he hath done to me") is likewise forbidden in favor of an unspecified "render[ing]... according to his work" (v.29) — language that echoes v.12's recompense wording without the text explicitly assigning that judgment to God.
- **Dual loyalty**: fearing the LORD is paired with fearing the king, alongside a warning against joining those "given to change" (v.21).
- **Impartial judgment**: favoring the wicked in judgment invites public curse; honest rebuke invites blessing (vv.23-26).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The sayings rely on memorable images: wisdom builds and furnishes a house (verses 3–4), wisdom is sweet like honey (verses 13–14), a person's future is a candle that can be extinguished (verse 20), and poverty approaches like a traveler while want comes like an armed man (verse 34). These images make abstract instruction concrete without requiring every detail to function as an allegorical symbol.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is mostly composed of direct second-person admonitions and third-person maxims, but vv.30-32 shift into first-person eyewitness narration ("I went by... I saw, and considered it well... I received instruction"), framing the neglected field of the sluggard as personally observed wisdom. This vignette closes (vv.33-34) with a couplet that is word-for-word identical, in the canonical text, to [[Proverbs 6]] 6:10-11 — one of the few mechanically verified textual parallels in the chapter, showing the book reusing a fixed wisdom saying rather than composing every proverb fresh.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- In verses 11–12, who are those being drawn to death, and what forms of intervention does the saying envision?
- Does “falleth” in verse 16 mean suffering calamity, committing sin, or admit both applications?
- What kind of “change” is condemned in verse 21: political rebellion, disloyal instability, moral novelty, or something broader?
- Does “build thine house” in verse 27 refer to literal construction, establishing a household, or both?
- How does the prohibition on enjoying an enemy's fall relate to the stated possibility that divine wrath may be turned away from that enemy?
