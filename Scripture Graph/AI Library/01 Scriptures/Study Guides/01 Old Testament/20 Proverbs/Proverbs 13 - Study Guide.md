## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The strongest formal observation is that 22 of the 25 verses contain the KJV conjunction “but”; verses 7, 14, and 22 are the exceptions. Most sayings use antithetic parallelism, placing contrasting conduct, conditions, character, or outcomes side by side. The exceptions are principally exceptions to the explicit conjunction pattern: verse 7 remains a semantic contrast expressed through two uses of “yet,” and verse 22 still juxtaposes a good man with a sinner while using “and.” Verse 14 is a single sentence presenting the law of the wise as a fountain of life. This repeated form does not by itself establish uniform Hebrew syntax, a rigid outline, or a continuous argument.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter depicts wisdom as teachable rather than merely intelligent. A wise person hears instruction, accepts counsel, acts with knowledge, regards reproof, and chooses wise companions. This makes [[Education and Learning]] a moral and relational process.

Its sayings about diligence support [[Work and Self-Reliance]], but verses 7, 8, and 23 resist an overly simple equation of wealth with righteousness or poverty with laziness. Material circumstances involve appearance, vulnerability, inheritance, productive labour, and judgment.

Proverbs 13:24 connects parental love with timely correction. Read alongside the chapter’s instruction theme, it opposes neglectful refusal to correct. The verse alone, however, does not provide a complete theology or safe modern procedure for discipline; Ephesians 6:4 and Hebrews 12:5–11 supply important additional context.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Life and death are expressed through concrete images: guarded lips preserve life, righteous light rejoices, fulfilled desire becomes a tree of life, wise law becomes a fountain, and death sets snares. These metaphors make wisdom appear life-giving before they define it abstractly.

The repeated vocabulary of desire in verses 4, 12, and 19 distinguishes wanting from receiving and from moral willingness. Desire may remain empty through sloth, become painful through delay, or become sweet through fulfillment; nevertheless, a fool may still refuse the change that wisdom requires.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Antithetic parallelism is the chapter’s dominant literary device: one line presents a disposition, act, condition, or outcome, and the second places a contrasting alternative beside it. This form invites comparison between rival paths without proving that the sayings constitute a continuous argument.

Verse 7 memorably reverses apparent and actual possession, although whether it concerns pretense, generosity, concealment, or another kind of self-presentation remains open. Verse 12 calls fulfilled desire “a tree of life,” while verse 14 calls the law of the wise “a fountain of life.” The latter has a particularly close verbal counterpart in [[Proverbs 14]], where the fear of the Lord is likewise a fountain of life enabling departure from the snares of death. Verse 23 should be handled cautiously: its first clause recognizes productivity in the poor person’s tillage, but its second clause is difficult and should not be made to support an elaborate social theory without further evidence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Which sayings describe consequences as immediate, and which leave the timing unstated?
2. How do verses 7, 8, 11, 22, and 23 complicate a simple claim that material wealth always measures virtue?
3. What is the difference between desiring something, patiently hoping for it, and being morally willing to depart from evil?
4. How should Proverbs 13:24 be interpreted alongside the chapter’s emphasis on hearing instruction and alongside scriptural warnings against provoking children?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Proverbs 13:14 with Proverbs 14:27 and trace other “fountain of life” and “tree of life” sayings within Proverbs. Study verses 1, 10, 13–14, 18, 20, and 24 together under [[Education and Learning]]. For ethical application of the wealth sayings, examine them alongside [[Work and Self-Reliance]], [[Honesty]], and [[Pride]]. Because the wording of verses 8 and 23 is difficult in the King James Version, comparing several careful translations and their textual notes would be useful before building detailed interpretations on either verse.
