# Proverbs 16 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Proverbs 16]] · My notes: [[Proverbs 16 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> It is better to get wisdom than gold—Pride goes before destruction—The gray hair of the righteous person is a crown of glory.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful, though not necessarily original, reading divides the chapter into five movements:

- Verses 1–9: human purposes under the LORD's evaluation and direction.
- Verses 10–15: ideals and dangers of kingship, judgment, and royal power.
- Verses 16–24: wisdom, upright conduct, teachable speech, and pleasant words.
- Verses 25–30: deceptive ways and the social damage caused by corrupt people.
- Verses 31–33: righteous age, mastery of anger, and divine disposal of uncertain outcomes.

The opening concern with divine direction returns in verse 33, giving the collection a thematic frame even if it does not prove a formal literary design.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds [[Agency]] and divine sovereignty together: a person devises a way, but the LORD directs the steps (v. 9). This tension discourages both arrogant self-sufficiency and passive refusal to plan. Verses 2 and 25 also challenge moral self-certainty; sincerity or an inward sense of being right is not presented as a sufficient test of goodness.

Verse 6 joins mercy, truth, fear of the LORD, and departure from evil. It supports a close relationship among reconciliation, faithful conduct, reverence, and [[Repentance]], but the verse alone does not specify a complete doctrine of atonement or the precise mechanism by which iniquity is purged.

Verse 32 gives self-government exceptional dignity. Mastering anger and ruling one's spirit are valued above military achievement, connecting [[Humility]] and moral discipline with a deeper form of strength.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated contrast between what is visible to a person and what the LORD knows is crucial. Ways may appear clean or right to their traveler (vv. 2, 25), but the LORD weighs the spirits and the end of a way reveals its character. Likewise, the chapter repeatedly connects heart and speech: wise inward character teaches the mouth (v. 23), while corrupt intention emerges through inflammatory or divisive communication (vv. 27–30).

The word "better" organizes the chapter's values in verses 8, 16, 19, and 32. These comparisons do not merely condemn possessions or power; they insist that wealth and victory are inferior when separated from righteousness, wisdom, humility, and self-command.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Proverbs 16:25 closely repeats the saying found in [[Proverbs 14]]. That recurrence is strong evidence of shared proverbial material within the book, but it does not reveal whether one occurrence depends on the other or whether both draw on oral or written tradition. The chapter also contrasts two kinds of speech: wise and pleasant words nourish learning and life (vv. 21–24), while ungodly and secretive words burn, divide, entice, and enact evil (vv. 27–30).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How do verses 2 and 25 challenge confidence in personal moral intuition?
2. How can planning and labor remain meaningful if the LORD directs steps and disposes the lot (vv. 9, 33)?
3. Does verse 10 describe the ideal function of a king, or should it be read as an unconditional statement about rulers?
4. What does the word "if" contribute to the praise of old age in verse 31?
5. How does treating verse 7 as a wisdom maxim rather than an exceptionless promise affect its application?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
