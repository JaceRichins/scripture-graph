# Deuteronomy 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 3]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Deuteronomy 3 completes Moses's retrospective account of Israel's victories east of the Jordan, describes the distribution of that territory, and prepares for the campaign west of the river. The chapter joins divine deliverance with Israelite military action and explicitly reports the destruction of men, women, and children, a morally severe feature that responsible interpretation must not conceal (Deuteronomy 3:6). The closing movement shifts to succession: Moses's request to cross is refused, but he must strengthen Joshua to lead the people onward.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Og and Bashan defeated (3:1-11):** The LORD promises deliverance; Israel defeats Og, destroys the cities' inhabitants, takes spoil, and surveys the conquered territory.

2. **Eastern territory distributed (3:12-17):** Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh receive specified lands.

3. **Inheritance and intertribal obligation (3:18-20):** The eastern fighting men must assist their brethren until the latter also receive rest and possession.

4. **Past victory applied to future conflict (3:21-22):** What Joshua has seen becomes the stated basis for confidence concerning the kingdoms ahead.

5. **Moses denied and Joshua prepared (3:23-29):** Moses may view the land but not cross it; he is commanded to charge, encourage, and strengthen Joshua.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents divine help and human responsibility together. The LORD is said to deliver and fight, yet Israel still acts, and tribes that have already received land must assist their brethren until the LORD gives them rest (Deuteronomy 3:2-3, 18-22). This can inform reflection on [[Service]], though it first concerns a specific intertribal military obligation.

Moses's experience provides a significant example for [[Prayer]]. His reverent petition is explicitly denied, showing that sincere prayer does not guarantee the requested outcome (Deuteronomy 3:23-27).

The transition to Joshua also bears on [[Prophets]] and leadership. After learning that he will not cross the Jordan, Moses is commanded to charge, encourage, and strengthen the person who will lead Israel onward (Deuteronomy 3:28).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated language of giving and possession ties military victory to territorial allocation. In verses 18–20, the land is said to be given by the LORD, given by Moses, and possessed by the tribes; these expressions operate at different levels of the narrative rather than necessarily competing explanations. The phrase "unto this day" in verse 14 signals a narrator looking back from a later reference point, but this chapter alone does not identify that point or determine the passage's date.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The repeated prohibitions of fear create a strong thematic correspondence. In verse 2 Moses is told not to fear Og because the LORD will deliver him; in verses 21-22 the eastern victories are applied to future kingdoms as the reason Israel need not fear. This repetition need not be treated as a demonstrable formal frame.

Sight also functions suggestively in the closing movements. Joshua's eyes have seen victories that prepare him to advance (verse 21), while Moses is told to behold a land he may not enter (verse 27). The contrast suits their differing leadership roles, though deliberate symmetry cannot be proven from repetition alone.

The notices about Hermon's alternative names, Og's bedstead, and Jair's place-name provide geographic and cultural specificity. They may preserve recollection, inherited tradition, editorial explanation, or literary realism; by themselves they establish neither date nor historicity.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should readers evaluate the chapter's attribution of conquest to God alongside its explicit report that men, women, and children were destroyed? Responsible study should neither conceal the wording nor assume that narrative attribution settles every historical, theological, or moral question. Ancient war rhetoric may use totalizing language, but that possibility does not remove the surface meaning of Deuteronomy 3:6.

Why does Moses say the LORD was angry with him 'for your sakes' (Deuteronomy 3:26), while [[Numbers 20]] emphasizes Moses's and Aaron's own failure? Corporate and personal responsibility may be compatible, but the passages retain different emphases and do not provide a single uncomplicated explanation.

What is the function of Og's bedstead? It may evoke unusual stature, royal magnificence, commemoration, or legendary amplification. Because the verse measures an object rather than Og's body, precise claims about his height go beyond the text.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the Og narrative with [[Numbers 21]] and its retrospective summary in [[Joshua 12]]. Trace the eastern tribes' agreement in [[Numbers 32]], their territorial allotments in [[Joshua 13]], their obligation during the crossing in [[Joshua 1]], and their eventual release in [[Joshua 22]]. For Joshua's succession, continue with [[Deuteronomy 31]]. Read [[Numbers 20]] beside [[Deuteronomy 1]] and Deuteronomy 3:26 when studying the differing emphases surrounding Moses's exclusion. These canonical correspondences clarify the received narrative but should not automatically be counted as independent historical witnesses.
