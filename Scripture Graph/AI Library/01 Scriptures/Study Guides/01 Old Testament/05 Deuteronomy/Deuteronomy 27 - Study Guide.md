# Deuteronomy 27 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 27]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 27 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Deuteronomy 27 prescribes a covenant ceremony for Israel's entry into the land. Inscribed stones make the law visible; an altar, offerings, eating, and rejoicing place worship beside instruction; and the Gerizim–Ebal assembly makes Israel answer publicly to the announced sanctions. The chapter moves from receiving and recording the law to affirming responsibility to do it.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1–8 — Inscription and worship:** Israel is to plaster large stones, write the law plainly, build an altar of whole stones on Ebal, offer sacrifices, eat, and rejoice.

2. **Verses 9–10 — Covenant identity and obligation:** Israel is declared the Lord's people and is therefore directed to hear and obey him.

3. **Verses 11–14 — The assembled nation:** Six tribes are assigned to Gerizim, six to Ebal, and the Levites are appointed to proclaim aloud.

4. **Verses 15–26 — Twelve responsive curses:** Particular offenses are followed by a comprehensive sanction against failing to uphold the law.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Israel's declaration as the Lord's people is immediately followed by the command to obey his voice (Deuteronomy 27:9–10). The chapter therefore places [[Covenants]] and [[Obedience]] together while leaving unresolved whether this moment creates, renews, or publicly reaffirms Israel's covenant identity.

The chapter's commands join [[Sacrifice]], eating, and rejoicing with duties concerning worship, [[Family]], property, sexual conduct, vulnerable people, judgment, and innocent life. This breadth makes the chapter important for studying obligation under the [[Law of Moses]], but it does not by itself supply a complete theology of covenant membership or of the offerings.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“All the words of this law” recurs in verses 3, 8, and 26. The first two occurrences command inscription; the last concerns confirming the law by doing it. This progression connects [[Records and Sacred Writings]] with enacted obedience while leaving the exact scope of “this law” undefined.

The repeated “And all the people shall say, Amen” gives verses 15–26 a formal, responsive cadence. The people do not merely overhear the sanctions; the prescribed response gives them an audible role. That observation does not prove the inward agreement of every participant.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter coordinates durable objects, worship, geography, proclamation, and response: plastered stones display words, an altar receives offerings, tribes occupy assigned mountains, Levites speak loudly, and the assembly repeatedly answers. Together these features strongly support reading the passage as a formal ritual prescription.

The arrangement also contains a notable asymmetry. Gerizim is assigned for blessing, yet this chapter preserves only the curse formulas. [[Deuteronomy 28]] continues the blessing-and-curse material, but the text does not explicitly identify that chapter as the missing Gerizim response.

[[Joshua 8]] presents a close narrative correspondence to the prescription. That relationship is textually strong, but it permits several historical and compositional explanations and should not be treated by itself as proof of when or how the ceremony occurred.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Lexical And Structural Evidence
> The recurrence of "all the words of this law" in commands to write and in the final demand to do provides strong internal evidence that the chapter presents law as both a recorded standard and an enacted obligation.

> [!evidence] Canonical Narrative Correspondence
> Joshua 8:30–35 narrates a closely corresponding event involving an altar on Mount Ebal, unworked stones, offerings, writing the law on stones, the Gerizim–Ebal arrangement, and a reading of blessings and curses. This is strong evidence that the Deuteronomic command and the Joshua narrative were understood as corresponding texts.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong canonical correspondence; historical conclusions disputed · [[Canonical Narrative Correspondence in Deuteronomy 27|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intracanonical Narrative Correspondence
> Joshua 8 presents a later narrative enactment of this chapter's instructions: Joshua builds an unhewn-stone altar on Ebal, offers sacrifices, writes a copy of the law, positions Israel in relation to Ebal and Gerizim, and reads the blessing and curse.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · Strong validated narrative correspondence · [[Intracanonical Narrative Correspondence in Deuteronomy 27|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Textual Pattern
> As internal literary evidence, the prescribed speakers, locations, loud proclamation, fixed response, and twelvefold repetition strongly support identifying the passage as a formal communal ceremony rather than an unstructured list of prohibitions.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong internal literary evidence · [[Internal Textual Pattern in Deuteronomy 27|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Literary And Ritual Evidence
> The convergence of inscribed stones, an altar and offerings, geographically assigned groups, loud proclamation, fixed formulas, and repeated communal responses provides strong internal evidence that the chapter presents a prescribed communal covenant ceremony.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong internal evidence; historical implications limited · [[Internal Literary And Ritual Evidence in Deuteronomy 27|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism *(tentative)*
> The Masoretic Text (followed by the KJV) reads 'Ebal' as the site for the altar in Deuteronomy 27:4, matching the mountain later assigned to curse (27:13). The Samaritan Pentateuch is widely reported to read 'Gerizim' at this point instead, aligning the altar with the blessing-mountain. This textual variant sits at the center of the ancient Jewish-Samaritan dispute over the legitimate site of worship (compare John 4:20, where a Samaritan refers to 'this mountain' as the proper place of worship).
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Known textual issue, but inadequately sourced here

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What material is encompassed by “all the words of this law” in verses 3, 8, and 26?

- Are the plastered inscription stones and the whole altar stones two distinct installations, and how does the grammar of “the stones” in verse 8 relate to them?

- Why does Levi appear among the tribes on Gerizim while the Levites also proclaim the curses?

- Why are blessing and cursing both assigned to mountains when only the curse formulas appear here?

- Does “this day thou art become the people of the LORD” describe a new covenant status, a renewal, or a formal declaration of an existing relationship?

- What precise force does the people's “Amen” carry in this setting?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Deuteronomy 11]] for the earlier Gerizim–Ebal instruction, [[Deuteronomy 26]] for the reciprocal declarations concerning the Lord and Israel, and [[Joshua 8]] for the corresponding narrative ceremony. [[Exodus 20]] provides a close parallel concerning unworked altar stones and also contains relevant commands about images and parents. [[Deuteronomy 28]] extends the broader blessing-and-curse material but should not automatically be identified as the missing Gerizim responses. [[Galatians 3]] later applies Deuteronomy 27:26 within a Christian argument about law and curse; its wording is not identical to the KJV rendering of Deuteronomy.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
