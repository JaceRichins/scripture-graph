# Deuteronomy 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 6]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Moses proclaims, The Lord our God is one Lord, and, Thou shalt love the Lord thy God—The children of Israel are commanded to teach their children—Moses exhorts them to keep the commandments, testimonies, and statutes of the Lord that they may prosper.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv.1-3** — Introduction: purpose of the commandments (to be done in the land, so Israel and future generations may fear God, keep his statutes, and prosper).
- **vv.4-5** — The Shema: the LORD's oneness/exclusive claim, and the command to love him wholly.
- **vv.6-9** — Internalization and transmission: heart, children, speech, hand, eyes, doorposts.
- **vv.10-15** — Warning: prosperity in an inherited, unearned land (cities, houses, wells, vineyards Israel did not build or plant) risks forgetting the LORD and following the gods of neighboring peoples.
- **vv.16-19** — Further warning: do not test the LORD as at Massah; do what is "right and good" in order to possess the land.
- **vv.20-25** — Catechism: a son's question about the law's meaning is answered with the Exodus narrative, and obedience is named "our righteousness" (v.25).

The phrase "that it may be well with thee" recurs at v.3 and v.18; whether this is a deliberate literary bracket or simply Deuteronomy's conventional blessing formula is uncertain.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Love of God as comprehensive covenant allegiance.** Verse 5's command to love God with all heart, soul, and might is later identified by Jesus as the first/great commandment ([[Mark 12]]; [[Matthew 22]]). In Deuteronomy 6 itself, "love" is not offered as sentiment alone: it is immediately unpacked as [[Obedience]] — internalizing, teaching, and marking the commandments (vv.6-9), remembering, serving, and refusing other gods (vv.12-14). This matches the covenant-loyalty sense "love" carries elsewhere in ancient Near Eastern treaty language, without excluding an affective dimension.

**Covenant memory and the order of grace and law.** The chapter repeatedly ties obedience to remembering what God has already done (vv.12, 20-23). The catechism of vv.20-25 narrates bondage, deliverance, and gift of land (vv.21-23) before naming the statutes' purpose and calling obedience "our righteousness" (v.25). This sequence is consistent with reading obedience as a response within an already-established covenant relationship, but should not be overdrawn into a strict grace-versus-law scheme: a historical prologue preceding stipulations is also the standard form of ancient Near Eastern treaties, and the chapter elsewhere makes future well-being and possession of the land explicitly conditional on keeping the commandments (vv.2-3, 18-19).

**The Shema and [[The Godhead]] (disputed).** Verse 4's declaration that "the LORD our God is one LORD" is the central creedal text of later Jewish and Christian worship, but the underlying Hebrew is read variously — as a claim about the LORD's exclusive claim on Israel ("the LORD alone"), as a claim about his unity or incomparability, or, in some traditions, as consistent with a unity of purpose among distinct divine persons. In its immediate context (vv.14-15's prohibition on "other gods... round about you"), the verse most clearly establishes Israel's exclusive covenant loyalty; it does not by itself settle later metaphysical questions about God's inner nature.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
- **Expanding spheres of memory (vv.6-9)**: the commanded words move from the heart, to speech with children, to the rhythms of daily life (sitting, walking, lying down, rising), to the body (hand, eyes), and to the household and gates — a cumulative pattern of continual remembrance. Whether the hand/eyes/doorpost language was meant literally or figuratively at the time of writing is not settled by the text alone; later Second Temple practice (tefillin, mezuzot) shows some Jews read it literally, but that doesn't establish the original audience's understanding.
- **Question-and-answer form (vv.20-25)**: a son's legal question ("what mean the testimonies...") is answered with narrative rather than definition, suggesting the law's meaning is inseparable from Israel's story of deliverance.
- **Singular and plural address**: the chapter alternates between singular ("thou," "thee," e.g. vv.2, 5-9) and plural ("ye," "you," e.g. vv.3, 14, 16-17) forms of address — the covenant addresses corporate Israel and the individual household both, rather than one exclusively.
- The repeated phrase "that it may be well with thee" (vv.3, 18) may loosely bracket the central material, though the same formula recurs elsewhere in Deuteronomy and may simply be conventional.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Narrative And Theological Sequence
> The narrative order in the parental answer supports interpreting obedience as a response within a relationship already defined by divine deliverance: the LORD brings Israel out and in before the answer explains the statutes.

> [!evidence] Cumulative Textual Pattern
> The concentration of commands to internalize, speak, teach, bind, and write the words strongly supports reading Deuteronomy 6:6-9 as an intentional strategy for embedding covenant memory throughout ordinary life.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · well-hedged evidence claim · [[Cumulative Textual Pattern in Deuteronomy 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Confession Reinforced By Prohibitions
> The confession that the LORD is one, reinforced by commands to serve him and not follow surrounding gods, strongly supports exclusive allegiance to the LORD. The precise semantic force of 'one LORD' should not be made to carry more than the surrounding passage establishes.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · well-hedged on the 'one' semantic range · [[Confession Reinforced By Prohibitions in Deuteronomy 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Archaeological/Historical *(tentative)*
> Ancient tefillin (phylacteries) recovered from Judean Desert sites, including Qumran, contain the text of Deuteronomy 6:4-9 among the passages bound into them, indicating that by the Second Temple period at least some Jews interpreted vv.8-9 ('bind them for a sign upon thine hand... frontlets between thine eyes') as a literal physical practice rather than only a figure of speech.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What is the relationship between "one LORD" (v.4) and the repeated warnings against other gods (vv.14-15)? Is the Shema primarily a claim about God's nature, or a covenantal claim about Israel's exclusive loyalty?
- Was the instruction of vv.8-9 understood literally (as in later tefillin/mezuzah practice) or figuratively at the time of writing?
- Why does the chapter treat prosperity, rather than only hardship, as a threat to covenant faithfulness?
- What is gained when the son's question about the law is answered with a story of deliverance rather than a definition (compare the similar child's-question passage in Exodus 12:26-27 and 13:14)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

- Exodus 17:1-7 — narrates the Massah incident referenced in v.16.

- [[Matthew 4]]; [[Luke 4]] — Jesus answers wilderness temptation with v.16 (and echoes v.13).
