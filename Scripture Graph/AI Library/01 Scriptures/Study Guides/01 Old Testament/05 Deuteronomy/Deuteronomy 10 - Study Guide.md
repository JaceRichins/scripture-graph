# Deuteronomy 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 10]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The tables of stone containing the Ten Commandments are placed in the ark—All that God requires is that Israel love and serve Him—How great and mighty is the Lord!
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Restored tablets and ark** (vv1-5) — Moses, in first person, cuts new tablets, builds the ark, and deposits the tablets in it.
2. **Itinerary and priestly succession** (vv6-9) — third-person material on Israel's stations, Aaron's death, Eleazar's succession, and Levi's separation to bear the ark, minister, and bless.
3. **Intercession and resumed journey** (vv10-11) — first-person narration resumes: forty days on the mountain, the LORD's forbearance, and the command to journey on.
4. **What the LORD requires** (vv12-16) — introduced by the rhetorical question of v12, this section calls for fear, love, service, obedience, and a circumcised heart.
5. **God's character and Israel's ethics** (vv17-22) — divine impartiality and justice for the vulnerable ground the command to love the stranger, closing with a reminder of Israel's growth from seventy ancestors.

Verses 6-9 sit as third-person subject matter bracketed by Moses's first-person narration in vv1-5 and 10-11 — a real, checkable shift in what is being narrated, though whether it reflects Moses's own aside or a later editorial insertion cannot be settled from the text alone.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Covenant renewal**: the replacement tablets bearing the [[Ten Commandments]] show that broken covenant obligations can be restored and the journey continued (vv1-5, 10-11).
- **Levi's inheritance**: Levi receives no tribal land because 'the LORD is his inheritance' (v9); [[Priesthood]] service is given theological meaning apart from land, though Levites elsewhere still receive material support through offerings and towns.
- **The heart of covenant religion**: vv12-13 summarize what is required — fear, walk, love, serve with all heart and soul, keep the commandments — for Israel's own good.
- **Circumcision of the heart** (v16): covenant language applied to inward disposition, opposing stubbornness rather than replacing outward covenant identity.
- **Divine impartiality and justice**: God 'regardeth not persons, nor taketh reward' and executes judgment for the fatherless, widow, and stranger (vv17-18), grounding [[Justice and Mercy]] in God's own character.
- **Memory as ethical motive**: Israel is to love the stranger 'for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt' (v19), tying [[Obedience]] to remembered experience.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
'Circumcise therefore the foreskin of your heart' (v16) transfers a physical covenant sign onto an inward, dispositional target, paired with the command to be 'no more stiffnecked' — outward identity is not treated as a substitute for responsive obedience. The doubled title 'God of gods, and Lord of lords' (v17) is a superlative construction asserting the LORD's supremacy, comparable to Psalm 136:2-3; later titles applied to Christ in Revelation echo the same idiom thematically, though not as a verbatim quotation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 14-19 move from the widest scope to concrete obligation: heaven and earth belong to the LORD (v14), yet Israel is chosen (v15), called to inward change (v16), and shown a model of impartial justice that extends to the fatherless, widow, and stranger (vv17-19). Verse 19 exhibits the classic 'motive clause' pattern — a command grounded in remembered experience — that recurs in Exodus 22:21, Exodus 23:9, and Leviticus 19:34. Verses 6-9 form a self-contained itinerary and priestly notice within the surrounding first-person narrative, an excursus whose compositional relationship to its context is not settled by the text alone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Textual Comparison *(tentative)*
> Deuteronomy 10:6 states that Aaron died and was buried at Mosera, but Numbers 20:22-29 and Numbers 33:38 place Aaron's death and burial at Mount Hor, after the itinerary had advanced beyond Moseroth (Numbers 33:30-31); the two accounts differ on the location of the same event.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · real crux, imprecisely stated

> [!evidence] Inner-Biblical Intertextuality *(tentative)*
> The rhetorical formula in Deuteronomy 10:12 ('what doth the LORD require of thee') is echoed nearly verbatim in Micah 6:8, which poses the same question and answers with its own triad (do justly, love mercy, walk humbly), suggesting either direct literary dependence or a shared covenant-summary convention in ancient Israelite religious rhetoric.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · parallel real, dependence disputed

> [!evidence] Literary/Source-Critical Observation *(tentative)*
> The person-shift identified in c2 is the textual basis for a common scholarly view that Deuteronomy 10:6-9 is an itinerary/priestly notice inserted into Moses's first-person retelling, interrupting the ark narrative (vv1-5) from its natural continuation (vv10-11); a traditional reading instead treats vv6-9 as Moses's own parenthetical aside within his address.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, unsourced
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What would it look like, practically, to 'circumcise the heart' rather than keep only external commandments?
- Why does Moses ground love for the stranger in Israel's own memory of being strangers, rather than in an abstract principle?
- How do the command to circumcise the heart here (v16) and the LORD's promise to circumcise the heart in [[Deuteronomy 30]] relate to each other?
- How should readers weigh the differences between this itinerary and Aaron's death notice against the account in Numbers, without concealing the difficulty or assuming it is easily resolved?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
