> If the children of Israel are obedient, they will be blessed temporally and spiritually—If they are disobedient, they will be cursed, smitten, and destroyed; diseases, plagues, and oppression will come upon them; they will serve false gods and become a byword among all nations; fierce nations will enslave them; and they will eat their own children and be scattered among all nations.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has two sharply asymmetric halves:

- **Blessings (vv.1-14):** brief and formulaic — blessed 'in the city,' 'in the field,' in 'basket and store,' in the 'fruit of thy body,' in victory over enemies, and in surplus (lending, not borrowing).
- **Curses (vv.15-68):** roughly four times longer and far more graphic, moving through disease (vv.20-29, 35, 59-61), domestic and agricultural loss (vv.30-34, 38-42), foreign conquest and siege (vv.49-57), and finally scattering and re-enslavement (vv.62-68). These categories recur and intensify rather than following a single one-way ladder — plague language, for instance, returns in force at vv.58-61, well after the invasion material of vv.45-57.

Several verses are precisely, verbally paired across the two halves, turning much of the curse section into a deliberate inversion of the blessing section:

| Blessing | Curse |
|---|---|
| 'Blessed...in the city...in the field' (v.3) | 'Cursed...in the city...in the field' (v.16) |
| 'thou shalt lend unto many nations, and thou shalt not borrow...the LORD shall make thee the head, and not the tail' (vv.12-13) | 'He shall lend to thee, and thou shalt not lend to him: he shall be the head, and thou shalt be the tail' (vv.43-44) |
| enemies flee 'one way...seven ways' (v.7) | Israel flees 'one way...seven ways' (v.25) |

The chapter also repeatedly returns to 'the voice of the LORD' as its hinge condition (vv.1-2, 15, 45, 62) — less a formal inclusio than a recurring refrain marking each major turn of the argument.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter ties Israel's national fortunes to covenant [[Obedience]] under a conditional [[Covenants]] relationship: the LORD is portrayed as directly and actively blessing or afflicting (v.8, vv.20-21, v.63), not merely allowing consequences to unfold on their own. Verse 47 adds an inward dimension — Israel is charged not only with disobedience but with failing to serve God 'with joyfulness, and with gladness of heart, for the abundance of all things,' suggesting covenant service concerns disposition as well as outward compliance, though this single verse does not by itself define the full relationship between emotion and obedience.

Because the chapter's promises and warnings are framed in corporate, territorial, and political terms — land, harvest, army, king, siege, exile — they describe the stakes for Israel as a covenant people under the [[Law of Moses]] rather than settling every question about an individual's health or prosperity. The scattering described here (vv.25, 36, 63-68) sets up the conditional restoration promised in Deuteronomy 30 and the wider [[Gathering of Israel]] theme.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The same verb describes blessings and curses as coming upon Israel and "overtaking" it (vv. 2, 15, 45), emphasizing consequences that cannot simply be evaded. Paired expressions—city/field, coming in/going out, head/tail, above/beneath—express comprehensiveness through opposites. The curse section repeatedly converts an earlier good into its negative counterpart, making reversal one of the chapter's controlling devices.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The curse list's specific afflictions — blindness, 'astonishment of heart,' groping at noonday, unburied corpses left for birds and beasts, boils 'that cannot be healed' — resemble curse formulas known from other ancient Near Eastern state treaties, most often compared to the Neo-Assyrian Vassal Treaties of Esarhaddon (672 BCE; discussed at length in Moshe Weinfeld's *Deuteronomy and the Deuteronomic School*, 1972). Scholars disagree on whether this reflects direct literary contact, a shared older Near Eastern treaty-curse tradition (also attested in Sefire and other treaties), or common ancient anxieties about disease and abandonment — the resemblance situates the chapter within a recognizable ancient genre without settling questions of date or authorship.

A second literary thread is the deliberate return to [[Exodus and Deliverance|Egypt]]: 'the botch of Egypt' (v.27) and 'the diseases of Egypt...which thou wast afraid of' (v.60) recast Egyptian bondage as the fate awaiting a disobedient Israel, culminating in the curse of being sold 'into Egypt again with ships' (v.68) — a rhetorical undoing of the exodus deliverance. Whether this verse also specifically alludes back to the king's law of Deuteronomy 17:16 or simply echoes the broader exodus promise of Exodus 14:13 is unresolved.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Is the extreme length and detail of the curse section (vv.15-68) best read as rhetorical emphasis (a treaty convention meant to deter), as genuine prophecy, or both?
- How does the chapter's corporate, territorial framing change how its promises and warnings should be applied to individuals?
- How should this chapter's stark cause-and-effect language be reconciled with cases (e.g., Job) where suffering is not tied to personal or national sin?
- What is the significance of verse 47's added charge — serving God 'without joyfulness' — alongside the more obvious charge of disobedience?
- Verse 63 says the LORD will 'rejoice...to destroy you' as he once rejoiced to prosper you. How should this stark language be read alongside other scriptural depictions of divine mercy, without softening or over-resolving it?
- Does the parallel with the Vassal Treaties of Esarhaddon (c. 672 BCE) meaningfully constrain when this material was composed, or is the treaty-curse genre too widespread to date the text by?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read Deuteronomy 27 for the covenant-ceremony setting that precedes this chapter, and Deuteronomy 30 for the later appeal to choose life and the promise of restoration after scattering. Deuteronomy 7 offers a close comparison for covenant blessing tied to obedience. Exodus 23 contains thematically related promises about provision, health, and protection from enemies, though its connection here rests on topical similarity rather than verified textual overlap.
