# Deuteronomy 21 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 21]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 21 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> How amends are made for murders by unknown persons—Equity is required in dealing with wives and children—Stubborn and rebellious sons will be put to death.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter contains five case laws, each introduced with "if" or "when":
1. **Unsolved murder** (21:1-9) — elders of the nearest city break a heifer's neck in unworked ground, wash their hands, and disclaim guilt before the LORD.
2. **Captive wife** (21:10-14) — a soldier may marry a captured woman only after a month of mourning; he may not later sell her.
3. **Firstborn's inheritance** (21:15-17) — a father may not shift the firstborn's double portion to a favored wife's son.
4. **Stubborn and rebellious son** (21:18-21) — both parents bring an incorrigible son before the city elders at the gate; the penalty is communal stoning.
5. **Burial of the executed** (21:22-23) — a hanged criminal's body must be buried the same day, "for he that is hanged is accursed of God."

Cases 1 and 5 bracket the chapter with concern for the land; cases 2-4 each regulate a household relationship (a wife, an heir, a son) by public law rather than private discretion.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The unsolved-murder rite treats innocent blood as a liability on the whole community, not just the unknown perpetrator: "lay not innocent blood unto thy people of Israel's charge" (21:8). [[Justice and Mercy]] appear together — the community petitions for mercy while still performing an act of accountability. The captive-wife, inheritance, and rebellious-son laws each place some real constraint on a household head's private power (no resale of a rejected wife, no arbitrary transfer of the firstborn's portion, no private execution of a son), without erasing the coercion of captivity or the severity of the penalties described.

Deuteronomy 21:23 ("he that is hanged is accursed of God") is quoted explicitly in Galatians 3:13 and applied to Christ's death, connecting this verse to the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] and [[Crucifixion of Jesus Christ]]. This is a clear, textually verified New Testament citation of a Deuteronomic law — but the vicarious-atonement reading is Paul's later theological application, not a claim the Deuteronomic text itself makes about its own meaning.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The unsolved-murder heifer is called in Hebrew *eglah arufah*, "the heifer whose neck is broken" — distinct from ordinary sacrificial vocabulary. The verb "put away ... from among you" recurs exactly in verses 9 and 21 (guilt of innocent blood; evil), functioning as a legal formula for removing communal liability; verse 23 uses different language (the land "not be defiled") for a related but distinct concern.

Describing one wife as "hated" (21:15) most likely marks relative disfavor between the two wives rather than a claim about the strongest modern sense of the word; the English text alone cannot settle the nuance. Verse 22's sequence — "be to be put to death, and thou hang him on a tree" — most naturally reads as two steps (execution, then display of the body), a reading also supported by the Joshua 8:29 and 10:26-27 narratives, though some ambiguity remains.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's cases are loosely bound by a shared refrain ("put away ... from among you," vv. 9, 21) and by land language opening and closing the chapter (v. 1, v. 23) — a real but modest literary framing device, not proof of single-author chapter design. The heifer's disqualification as one "which hath not drawn in the yoke" (21:3) echoes the red heifer's qualification in Numbers 19:2; the verbal overlap is worth noting even though the two rites serve different purposes and the relationship between them (shared convention vs. direct dependence) is uncertain. The captive woman's shaved head, pared nails, and discarded "raiment of her captivity" (21:12-13) form a compact sequence marking her passage out of her former status, though the text does not specify whether the acts signify mourning, purification, humiliation, or some combination.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Intertextual Citation
> Paul explicitly quotes the 'cursed' language of Deuteronomy 21:23 in Galatians 3:13 and applies it to Christ's crucifixion, making this one of the clearest direct New Testament citations of a Deuteronomic law.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted, textually explicit · [[Intertextual Citation in Deuteronomy 21|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary And Thematic Pattern
> The repeated removal formula links the unknown-homicide procedure with the rebellious-son law: one removes the guilt of innocent blood and the other removes evil. Together with the warning against defiling the land, this supports reading the chapter as concerned with communal accountability and the integrity of Israel's covenant land.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation, disputed compositional claim · [[Literary And Thematic Pattern in Deuteronomy 21|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Legal-Ethical Provision
> The captive-wife law imposes a mandatory month-long mourning/transition period and explicitly forbids selling her or treating her as merchandise, 'because thou hast humbled her'—a real but limited restraint on a victor's treatment of a war captive, operating within, rather than abolishing, the underlying practice of taking captive women as wives.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Was the broken-necked heifer ever understood as a sacrifice, or deliberately kept distinct from one — and what does that distinction suggest about how ancient Israel handled unresolved guilt?
- Is there evidence the "stubborn and rebellious son" penalty was ever carried out, or did it function mainly as a deterrent even in ancient Israel?
- Does Paul's use of Deuteronomy 21:23 in Galatians 3:13 draw on an existing Second Temple-period reading of "accursed of God," or is it a fresh application?
- What genuine restraints does the captive-wife law place on a victor, and which modern claims about consent or protection does the text not support?
- Why are both parents required to bring the rebellious-son case, and why to the city elders rather than a private family decision?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
