# Deuteronomy 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 15]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Deuteronomy 15 regulates the periodic release of claims against fellow Israelites, openhanded care for poor people, release and provisioning of Hebrew servants, and the disposition of firstling animals. The sections are connected by a recurring demand that covenant blessings not be converted into hardhearted economic advantage. Blessing is represented both as God's gift and as a reason to share with others (Deuteronomy 15:4-10, 14, 18). The chapter therefore brings [[Law of Moses]], [[Charity]], [[Freedom and Liberty]], and [[Sacrifice]] into one economic and worshipful framework.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The seventh-year release (15:1-6):** Creditors cease exacting claims from a neighbor or brother, with a stated distinction for foreigners.
2. **Openhanded care for the poor (15:7-11):** Approaching release must not become an excuse to refuse aid.
3. **Hebrew servants (15:12-18):** Release follows six years of service; the released servant receives substantial provisions. A procedure is supplied for one who chooses to remain.
4. **Firstling males (15:19-23):** Suitable firstlings are sanctified and eaten at the chosen place; blemished animals are not sacrificed, though they may be eaten locally without consuming their blood.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The text grounds social responsibility in divine redemption. Israel's memory of bondage in Egypt is not merely historical background; Deuteronomy 15:15 makes it the explicit reason for generous treatment of a released servant. This closely joins [[Exodus and Deliverance]] with obligations toward another person's [[Freedom and Liberty]].

The command reaches motives as well as transactions. A shut hand, hardened heart, evil eye, and grieving heart describe failures surrounding the same outward refusal (15:7-10). In the chapter's own terms, care for the poor is therefore more than an optional feeling: withholding aid in the situation described can become sin (15:9).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated contrast between a shut hand and an open hand organizes the poverty section (15:7-11). References to the heart and eye add an interior dimension: the law addresses calculation, resentment, and perception as well as giving.

The KJV word “release” is defined within the passage by the creditor's duty not to “exact” from a neighbor or brother (15:1-3). The English text does not by itself settle every later legal question about whether all underlying debt was permanently cancelled or collection was suspended; that issue requires lexical and comparative legal study.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Deuteronomy's characteristic motive clauses recur throughout the chapter: "because it is called the LORD's release" (v.2), "thou shalt remember that thou wast a bondman" (v.15), and the promise of blessing "in all that thou doest" (v.18) — each binding legal obligation to covenant memory or future blessing rather than bare command. The Hebrew-servant release law (vv.12-18), including the ear-piercing rite, closely parallels [[Exodus 21]] (Exodus 21:2-6), with Deuteronomy adding a requirement of liberal provisioning and explicitly extending the ear-piercing option to a maidservant. A looser thematic connection to the sabbatical land-rest legislation of Leviticus 25 is also worth noting, though whether these represent one shared legal tradition, independent developments, or distinct literary layers remains disputed.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should the conditional vision of “no poor among you” in 15:4 be read alongside the prediction that the poor will never cease in 15:11? What social boundaries are created by the different treatment of a “brother” and a “foreigner” in 15:2-3? How freely could a servant choose permanent attachment in the unequal relationship described in 15:16-17? What does the distinction between sacrificial and local consumption of firstlings reveal about holiness and ordinary food?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the servant legislation with [[Exodus 21]] and the broader treatment of vulnerable people in [[Deuteronomy 24]]. Compare the firstling rules with the central-worship instructions in [[Deuteronomy 12]] and [[Deuteronomy 16]]. These comparisons may clarify how release, provision, and sacred consumption operate within the larger [[Law of Moses]], but similarity alone should not be treated as proof of identical legal circumstances.
