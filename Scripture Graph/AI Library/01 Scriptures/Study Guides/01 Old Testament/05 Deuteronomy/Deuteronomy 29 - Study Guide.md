# Deuteronomy 29 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 29]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 29 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The children of Israel make a covenant with the Lord under which they will be blessed if they are obedient, and cursed if they are disobedient—If they are disobedient, their land will be as brimstone and salt.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Remembered acts and required response (vv. 1-9):** The Moab covenant is introduced; Egypt, wilderness preservation, and victories east of the Jordan lead to the command to keep and do its words.

2. **Assembly, oath, and identity (vv. 10-15):** The whole social range of the assembly stands before the LORD. The covenant establishes Israel as his people in continuity with the patriarchal oath and includes persons not present, whose identity is not specified.

3. **The defiant individual or subgroup (vv. 16-21):** A heart turns toward other gods, promises itself peace, and is threatened with individual separation and the written curses.

4. **Collective abandonment, land, and exile (vv. 22-28):** Later observers see the ruined land and explain it as the consequence of the people's forsaking the covenant.

5. **Secret and revealed things (v. 29):** What is revealed belongs to the community and its children so that the law may be obeyed.

This is one defensible literary outline; other section divisions are possible.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Covenants]] as creating communal identity and requiring practiced [[Obedience]]. Leaders, men, little ones, wives, strangers, and laborers all stand within the covenant assembly, although the text does not claim equal social status or specify an identical legal role for every group.

The chapter also distinguishes individual and corporate accountability. Verses 18-21 threaten a defiant person or subgroup; verses 22-28 describe devastation after the people collectively forsake the covenant. [[Apostasy]] is therefore treated at several social scales without making one hidden offender the stated cause of national ruin.

The oath to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob connects the chapter with the [[Abrahamic Covenant]]. Verse 28 anticipates [[Scattering of Israel]], while verse 29 relates [[Revelation]] to responsibility: matters revealed to the community and its children are given so that the law may be done.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several repeated expressions organize the chapter. “This day” makes the covenant immediate, while references to people who are absent, the coming generation, and children “for ever” extend its horizon. “Heart” marks three stages of the spiritual problem: lack of perception, turning away, and self-assured persistence in one's own imagination. Written-language terms—“book,” “book of the law,” and curses “written” therein—make the covenant record an instrument of accountability.

The expression “to add drunkenness to thirst” in verse 19 is difficult and should not bear a detailed doctrinal conclusion without lexical study. Its surrounding context is clearer than the idiom itself: the speaker expects peace while intentionally following his own heart.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly connects seeing with interpretation. Israel has seen signs in Egypt but lacks a perceiving heart (vv. 2-4); later generations and foreigners see the devastated land and ask what it means (vv. 22-24). The text then supplies a covenantal explanation for the latter scene.

The image of a root bearing gall and wormwood depicts apostasy through bitter or poisonous growth. The immediate sequel concentrates judgment on the offender, so the image should not automatically be treated as a claim that one person infects the whole nation. Hebrews 12:15 later adapts closely related root language to communal trouble and defilement.

Verses 22-27 employ an imagined dialogue: observers ask why the land was devastated, and the answer identifies collective covenant abandonment and idolatry. Similar formulas appear in [[Jeremiah 22]] and [[2 Chronicles 7]], whether through literary reuse or shared covenant-judgment rhetoric.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should verse 4's statement that the LORD had not given Israel a perceiving heart be related to the command and responsibility to obey in verse 9?

- Does “him that is not here” in verse 15 refer to absent contemporaries, future generations, or both? How do verses 22 and 29 affect that reading?

- What does the movement from an individual or subgroup in verses 18-21 to collective abandonment in verses 22-28 clarify about personal and corporate accountability?

- Which matters does verse 29 call “secret,” and what can be concluded without claiming that the verse identifies them more precisely?

- How does [[Deuteronomy 30]] develop the themes of scattering, return, and the heart?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the Sihon and Og narrative in [[Deuteronomy 3]] and the direct continuation concerning return and restoration in [[Deuteronomy 30]]. [[Judges 2]] narrates the later apostasy pattern, while [[Jeremiah 11]], [[Jeremiah 22]], and [[2 Chronicles 7]] reuse related covenant, curse, and public-question language. Romans 11:8 combines language resembling Deuteronomy 29:4 with language from Isaiah, and Hebrews 12:15 closely recalls the poisonous-root image of Deuteronomy 29:18. Genesis 19 supplies the destruction narrative for Sodom and Gomorrah, while Genesis 14 explicitly places Admah and Zeboim among the cities of the plain.
