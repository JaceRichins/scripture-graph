# Deuteronomy 12 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 12]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 12 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Israel is to destroy the Canaanite gods and places of worship—The Lord will designate where His people will worship—The eating of blood is forbidden—Israel’s worship must conform to the divine standard.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has a framed, recursive movement:

- Verses 1-4: entry into the land and rejection of Canaanite worship.
- Verses 5-14: the chosen place, offerings, households, and rejoicing.
- Verses 15-19: local meat consumption distinguished from sacred meals; care for the Levite.
- Verses 20-28: the distinction is restated for an enlarged territory, with special emphasis on blood and holy things.
- Verses 29-31: a renewed warning against imitating Canaanite worship.
- Verse 32: a concluding command neither to add nor diminish.

This is coherent framing and repetition, though not a perfectly symmetrical chiasm.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins exclusive worship, [[Sacrifice]], [[Tithing]], family participation, and care for landless Levites. Holiness does not require every meal to occur at a sanctuary: ordinary slaughter is permitted locally, while holy offerings remain attached to the chosen place. The distinction protects both centralized worship and ordinary life. The repeated invitation to rejoice also shows that covenant worship is envisioned as shared fellowship, not merely ritual obligation.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase about placing or causing the LORD's "name" to dwell at a chosen location is central. The text identifies no city, so the chapter itself should not simply be made to say "Jerusalem," even though later writings connect the motif with Jerusalem and the temple. Also, the KJV's "groves" in verse 3 represents Hebrew *asherim*, cult objects associated with Asherah, not necessarily groves of living trees.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several literary features deserve notice:

- The names of other gods are to be erased, while the LORD places his name at the chosen site. This is a strong verbal and theological contrast, though deliberate authorial design cannot be proved from juxtaposition alone.
- Doing what is right in one's own eyes (v.8) is set beside doing what is good and right in the LORD's sight (v.28). The first expression has close canonical parallels in [[Judges 17]] and [[Judges 21]], but shared wording does not establish literary dependence.
- In the supplied English translation, the address is predominantly plural in vv.1-12 and predominantly singular from v.13 onward, with mixed forms and exceptions. Scholars disagree over whether such number shifts reflect rhetoric, transmission, editing, or compositional layers; the English pattern alone cannot decide among those explanations.
- The anti-Canaanite warnings in vv.2-4 and vv.29-31 frame repeated treatments of chosen-place worship and local slaughter. This supports a coherent reading of the final form without proving single-stage composition or a formal chiasm.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Scholarship *(tentative)*
> The centralization law of Deuteronomy 12 (worship restricted to one chosen place) is widely cited in critical Old Testament scholarship as textually consonant with the cult-centralizing reform attributed to King Josiah (2 Kings 22-23), and is a cornerstone of arguments (going back to Wellhausen and De Wette) for dating the core legal material of Deuteronomy to the seventh century BCE. This is a long-standing critical-scholarly position, not a claim this chapter makes about itself.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · Major historical-critical position; dating remains disputed

> [!evidence] Source-Critical/Theological Scholarship *(tentative)*
> The chapter's language of the LORD choosing a place 'to put his name there' / 'to cause his name to dwell there' is often labeled by scholars as 'Name theology,' and is sometimes contrasted with Priestly-source language elsewhere in the Pentateuch describing God's glory (kavod) filling the tabernacle. This distinction is used in some source-critical frameworks as a marker separating Deuteronomic ('D') theology from Priestly ('P') theology, though the significance of the distinction is debated.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · Recognized scholarly category; source-critical inference disputed

> [!evidence] Literary/Source-Critical Scholarship *(tentative)*
> Some biblical scholars treat the plural/singular alternation documented in c3 as one indicator that Deuteronomy 12's law code was assembled from more than one source-layer or underwent successive redaction (the pattern recurs elsewhere in Deuteronomy's legal core). This is a minority-to-mixed position within critical scholarship, not a consensus finding, and is not part of any LDS doctrinal position on authorship.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · Disputed and insufficiently sourced
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Because the chosen place is unnamed, at what stage do later texts identify it with Jerusalem?
2. Does verse 8 condemn general moral autonomy, irregular worship during the wilderness period, or both?
3. How does the permission for local slaughter qualify the chapter's centralization policy?
4. Why are rejoicing, household inclusion, and Levite support repeated alongside sacrificial regulations?
5. Does "ye shall not do so" in verse 4 chiefly contrast Israel's sanctuary practice with the destruction just commanded, or does it anticipate the prohibition on copying Canaanite rites in verse 31?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
