# Deuteronomy 30 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The scattered Israelites will be gathered from all nations when they remember the covenant—Moses places life or death, blessing or cursing, before the people.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter divides into three movements:

1. **Restoration cycle (30:1-10)** — Israel remembers the blessing and curse while scattered, returns and obeys, and God responds with compassion, gathering, restoration to the land, renewed prosperity, and a promised circumcision of the heart.
2. **Accessibility of the commandment (30:11-14)** — rhetorical denials (not hidden, not in heaven, not beyond the sea) culminate in the claim that the word is near, in mouth and heart.
3. **The formal choice (30:15-20)** — matching life/death, good/evil formulas (30:15, 19) bracket the appeal; heaven and earth are summoned as covenant witnesses, and Moses commands Israel to "choose life."

A refrain, "with all thine heart, and with all thy soul," recurs in verses 2, 6, and 10, binding the first movement together — though in verse 6 it names the *result* of God's promised action rather than a condition Israel must first meet.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Human response and divine action.** The chapter holds both together without fully systematizing their relationship: Israel is called to "return" and obey (30:2, 8, 10), while God has compassion, gathers, restores, and promises to "circumcise" the heart (30:6) so the people can love him wholly. Neither human initiative nor divine enabling is presented as sufficient alone, and the text does not specify their exact order or mechanism.

**[[Gathering of Israel]] and [[Scattering of Israel]].** The promise of gathering "from the outmost parts of heaven" (30:4) is among the Old Testament's most expansive restoration statements, conditioned on remembrance and return.

**[[Abrahamic Covenant]].** The chapter's final appeal (30:20) is explicitly anchored in the oath sworn "to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob," showing Moses' covenant renewal continues the patriarchal promise.

**"Choose life" (30:15-20).** This is not generic moral advice; the text defines it concretely as loving the LORD, walking in his ways, keeping his commandments, obeying his voice, and cleaving to him — set within a covenant that carries real sanctions (30:17-18), not a scenario of unconstrained preference.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The recurring language of turning organizes the chapter: Israel returns to the LORD, God turns Israel's captivity, Israel again obeys, and the contrasting danger is that the heart turns away. Mouth and heart likewise appear together in verse 14, joining confession or recitation with inward reception and practical obedience.

The referent of “this commandment” in verse 11 is not identified with complete precision. It may summarize the covenant instruction generally, the command to return and obey, or the immediately renewed appeal.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter reverses the movement of judgment: scattering is answered by gathering, estrangement by return, curses by renewed blessing, and threatened death by the invitation to choose life. Verses 15 and 19 bracket the climax with closely matching two-way formulas ("life and good, and death and evil" / "life and death, blessing and cursing"), a plausible envelope pattern framing the appeal. Verse 19's summons of "heaven and earth" as witnesses gives the appeal solemn, formal weight. The rhetorical questions of verses 12-13 ("Who shall go up... who shall go over the sea") are answered and reversed in verse 14 — a pattern striking enough that Paul reuses its shape in [[Romans 10]] (10:6-8), reworking the questions christologically while echoing the "in thy mouth, and in thy heart" language; Paul's application should be read as adaptation, not as the passage's original Deuteronomic sense.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does "this commandment" in verse 11 refer to the whole covenant instruction, the command to return, or the choice in verses 15-20?
- How does the promise that God will circumcise the heart (30:6) relate to the repeated commands that Israel itself return and obey (30:2, 8, 10)?
- Why might Moses frame the commandment's accessibility (30:11-14) as a rebuttal to imagined objections? What similar objections do people raise today about the commandments being too hard or too remote?
- What is the effect of calling "heaven and earth" as witnesses (30:19) rather than simply stating the choice plainly?
- In verses 15-20, which aspects of "life" are national and temporal, and what (if anything) supports extending the term further?
- How does Paul's use in Romans 10:6-8 preserve, adapt, or redirect the function of Deuteronomy 30:12-14?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Romans 10:6-8 — Paul's direct reuse and christological adaptation of Deuteronomy 30:12-14.
- Deuteronomy 10:16 — the earlier command to circumcise one's own heart, later promised as God's own act in 30:6.
- Deuteronomy 6:4-5 — the Shema's command to love God "with all thine heart, and with all thy soul," echoed as a refrain in 30:2, 6, 10.
- Deuteronomy 26 — the study guide's highest semantic match and a verified 4-verse overlap chapter, worth comparing for shared covenant-renewal language.
- 1 Kings 8:46-50 — Solomon's dedicatory prayer, anticipating a similar captivity-repentance-restoration sequence.
- Jeremiah 4:4 — a related but distinct use of heart-circumcision language, addressed to the people as a command rather than a divine promise.
- 2 Nephi 2:27 — a thematically similar (though not textually dependent) choice between liberty/eternal life and captivity/death.
