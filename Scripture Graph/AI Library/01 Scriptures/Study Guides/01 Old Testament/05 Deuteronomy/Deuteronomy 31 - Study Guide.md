# Deuteronomy 31 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 31]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 31 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Deuteronomy 31 prepares Israel for life after Moses. Joshua is publicly commissioned, but the chapter locates Israel's ultimate security in the LORD, who goes before the people. Moses also provides institutions of communal memory: a recurring public reading of the law, a written book kept beside the ark, and a song taught to Israel. These measures are especially significant because the chapter anticipates future apostasy. Leadership changes, but covenant instruction is to remain publicly accessible and intergenerationally transmitted. See [[Joshua 1]], [[Covenants]], and [[Records and Sacred Writings]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Transition and encouragement (vv. 1-8):** Moses announces that he will not cross the Jordan and publicly commissions Joshua.
2. **Recurring reading of the law (vv. 9-13):** The written law is entrusted to priests and elders and is to be heard by the entire community every seven years.
3. **Divine charge and warning (vv. 14-23):** The LORD appears at the tabernacle, predicts covenant breach, and commands a song to serve as a witness.
4. **The completed book as witness (vv. 24-29):** Moses directs the Levites to place the book beside the ark and summons Israel's leaders.
5. **Introduction to the song (v. 30):** Moses begins the words continued in [[Deuteronomy 32]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins divine presence with human covenant responsibility. Israel and Joshua are told not to fear because the LORD goes with and before them (vv. 3-8, 23), yet the same chapter warns that turning to other gods will bring covenant consequences (vv. 16-18). Courage here is trust grounded in God's promised action, not a denial of danger. The public reading also portrays learning as ordered toward reverence and obedience: the people hear, learn, fear the LORD, and observe the law (vv. 11-13). This makes the passage particularly relevant to [[Faith]], [[Obedience]], [[Covenants]], and [[Education and Learning]].

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The threefold repetition of 'be strong and of a good courage' (31:6, 7, 23) functions as a structuring refrain binding Moses's public farewell, his personal charge to Joshua, and a further charge to Joshua into a connected act of leadership transition — one of the strongest verbal links between Deuteronomy and [[Joshua 1]]. A witness motif also organizes the chapter's second half: the song is a witness (31:19, 21), the book of the law is a witness (31:26), and heaven and earth are called to record (31:28) — writing, recitation, and cosmic testimony together resist communal forgetfulness. The chapter also holds assurance and warning side by side without fully resolving the tension: God promises not to fail or forsake Joshua in the task immediately ahead (31:6, 8, 23), while Israel is later warned that God will forsake it and hide his face after apostasy (31:16-18).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary And Institutional Pattern
> The coordinated placement of public succession, written-law preservation, communal recitation, and a mnemonic song supports reading the chapter as a deliberate covenant-transition framework designed to preserve communal identity beyond Moses's death.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · well-hedged, alternatives listed · [[Literary And Institutional Pattern in Deuteronomy 31|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary/Theological Pattern *(tentative)*
> The pattern predicted here — prosperity leading to apostasy, followed by 'evils and troubles' the people will recognize as divine withdrawal — matches the recurring apostasy-affliction-deliverance cycle that structures the book of Judges and much of the later historical books, a pattern scholars commonly describe as the 'Deuteronomistic' theological framework running from Deuteronomy through Kings.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed scholarly framework

> [!evidence] Later Religious/Legal Tradition *(tentative)*
> This command for a septennial public reading of the law at the Feast of Tabernacles is the biblical basis later Jewish tradition points to for the Hakhel ('assembly') ceremony, in which rabbinic sources (Mishnah, tractate Sotah 7:8) describe the king publicly reading portions of Deuteronomy to the gathered nation every seventh year.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · real tradition; continuity disputed

> [!evidence] Comparative Ancient Near Eastern Literature *(tentative)*
> Invoking heaven and earth as witnesses to a covenant proceeding is a recurring feature elsewhere in Deuteronomy (e.g., the calling of heaven and earth to witness in Deuteronomy 4:26 and 30:19, and the song's opening address to heaven and earth in Deuteronomy 32:1), a rhetorical pattern that some biblical scholars connect to ancient Near Eastern treaty and covenant-lawsuit conventions in which cosmic elements serve as permanent, impartial witnesses to a sworn agreement.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · minority scholarly lens, needs sourcing

> [!evidence] Internal Textual Consistency *(tentative)*
> Placing the 'book of the law' at the side of the ark, distinct from the object(s) inside it, is consistent with the later statement in 1 Kings 8:9 that the ark contained only the two tables of stone Moses put there at Horeb — together these texts support a picture in which the law scroll was a separate physical object stored alongside the ark rather than one of its contents.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed interpretation, well hedged already

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Who is the antecedent of "he" in 31:23 — Moses or the LORD — and how should that ambiguity shape reading the charge to Joshua?
- What is signified by placing the law "in the side of" the ark rather than inside it?
- Why does the chapter preserve both a written law and an orally taught song? What distinct forms of memory might each serve?
- How does including children and strangers in the public reading shape the chapter's picture of covenant community?
- How should the assurances of verses 6-8 be read alongside the warnings of verses 16-18?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
