# Deuteronomy 7 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 7]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 7 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Deuteronomy 7 combines election, covenant fidelity, promised blessing, and conquest. Israel is called holy and specially chosen, but the text expressly denies that this status rests on national size or strength; it rests on the LORD's love and oath to the fathers (vv. 6-8). Israel's answering obligation is exclusive loyalty expressed through [[Obedience]] and the rejection of [[Apostasy]]. The chapter's severe conquest commands must remain visible in interpretation rather than being softened by its devotional themes.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves through five connected units:

1. Verses 1-5: commands concerning conquest, covenants, marriage, and rival worship.
2. Verses 6-11: Israel's holiness and election, grounded in divine love, oath, and redemption.
3. Verses 12-16: conditional covenant blessings and renewed warnings against idolatry.
4. Verses 17-24: fear answered by remembrance of the Exodus, followed by a gradual-dispossession sequence.
5. Verses 25-26: destruction of divine images and refusal of their precious metals.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Israel's election is grounded in the LORD's love and ancestral oath rather than numerical greatness (vv. 6-8). Within verses 6-11, divine love, oath, redemption, and election provide the setting for the renewed command to obey, although the chapter as a whole interweaves commands and theological motives rather than presenting a simple grace-then-law sequence.

The blessings in verses 12-15 are conditional, corporate covenant promises involving fertility, agriculture, livestock, and health. They should not be converted into a universal guarantee that every obedient individual will always prosper or avoid illness. The chapter holds together [[Covenants]], [[Obedience]], and [[Justice and Mercy]] without erasing the moral severity of its conquest commands.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated word “snare” connects two dangers: serving the nations' gods (v. 16) and taking silver or gold attached to their images (v. 25). The latter warning concerns valuables associated with objects the chapter calls abominable and cursed; it should not be generalized into a complete teaching about material desire.

The chapter also alternates divine and human agency. The LORD casts out, delivers, and puts out the nations, while Israel is commanded to smite, consume, and destroy. The rare “hornet” image in verse 20 admits more than one interpretation, but detailed lexical claims about its frequency, cognates, or historical symbolism require external scholarly support.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 9-10 share covenant-loyalty language with the Decalogue's idolatry sanction in [[Deuteronomy 5]], including love, commandment keeping, mercy across generations, and repayment for hatred.

Verse 22 establishes a phased process: the nations are put out “by little and little” so that wild animals do not multiply. [[Exodus 23]] contains the chapter's clearest parallel sequence of hornet, gradual expulsion, and concern about wild animals. This gradual pace stands beside totalizing destruction language in verses 2, 16, 23, and 24. The relationship may be understood as gradual stages leading to a comprehensive result, as conventional war rhetoric, or through other literary explanations; the chapter does not adjudicate among them.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should the gradual process in verse 22 be related to the commands to destroy utterly in verses 2 and 16? Does the chapter use totalizing ancient war rhetoric, describe different stages of conquest, or preserve more than one formulation of the tradition?

What does it mean for election to rest on love and oath rather than national strength? How does that gracious basis relate to the demanding conditions stated in verses 9-12?

How should modern readers acknowledge the moral severity of commands withholding mercy while avoiding the assumption that an ancient, narratively specific command supplies a general rule for religious violence?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
