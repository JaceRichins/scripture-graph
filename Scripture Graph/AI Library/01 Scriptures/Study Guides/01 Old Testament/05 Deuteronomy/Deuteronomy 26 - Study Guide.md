# Deuteronomy 26 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Deuteronomy 26]] · My notes: [[Deuteronomy 26 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The children of Israel are to offer to the Lord a basket of the firstfruits of Canaan—They are commanded to keep the law of tithing—They covenant to keep the commandments, and the Lord promises to make them a holy people and a great nation.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Firstfruits and remembered deliverance (vv.1-11)** — produce from the land is brought in a basket to the place the LORD chooses, given to the priest, and set before the altar; the worshiper then recites Israel's history: an endangered ancestor, descent into Egypt, affliction, deliverance, and possession of the land (vv.5-9), followed by presentation of the firstfruits (v.10) and a command to rejoice with the household, the Levite, and the stranger (v.11).
2. **Third-year tithe and declaration of compliance (vv.12-15)** — the tithe is given to the Levite, stranger, fatherless, and widow so that they may eat and be filled (vv.12-13); the worshiper declares that the commandments regarding its use were kept (vv.13-14) and petitions the LORD to look down from heaven and bless Israel and the land (v.15).
3. **Reciprocal declarations (vv.16-19)** — Israel "avouched" the LORD to be their God and commits to walk in his ways (v.17); the LORD "avouched" Israel to be his "peculiar people" and promises to set them high above the nations (vv.18-19).

Only the second declaration (vv.12-15) follows a report-then-petition pattern; the first (vv.5-10) recounts history and presents the offering, with no parallel petition.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays material abundance as a divine gift that creates obligations in two directions: worship toward God and provision for people without an ordinary land-based support system. Rejoicing includes the Levite and stranger (v. 11), while the third-year tithe includes the Levite, stranger, fatherless, and widow (v. 12). This is more specific than a general appeal to generosity: within the [[Law of Moses]], distribution is commanded and reported before God.

Covenant identity is likewise paired with covenant conduct. Verses 17–19 combine belonging to God with walking in his ways, hearing his voice, keeping his commandments, and becoming holy. The passage supports a close relationship between election and obligation, though it does not offer a complete theology of either subject.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The confession shifts from the singular "my father" (v.5) to the communal "us" (vv.6-9) and then to the worshiper's individual "I" and "me" (v.10), tying one household's harvest to the remembered history of the whole people. Deuteronomy 26:5 does not name this "father"; identifying him with Jacob is a common contextual inference, not something the verse states.

"Peculiar people" (v.18) does not mean "odd" in the modern sense but "treasured possession" (Hebrew segullah) — the same term used of Israel at Sinai (Exodus 19:5, "a peculiar treasure unto me above all people"). The KJV verb "avouched" is used in both directions in this passage — Israel avouches the LORD (v.17), and the LORD avouches Israel (v.18) — a deliberate repetition that makes reciprocity central to the chapter's close.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The historical recital of vv.5-9 is sometimes called Israel's "little credo" — a compressed, formulaic confession moving from a vulnerable ancestor ("a Syrian ready to perish was my father," v.5) through affliction to deliverance and land. Gerhard von Rad's influential, still-debated form-critical proposal treated this and similar passages as evidence of an ancient fixed liturgical confession underlying Israel's larger salvation narrative; other scholars read it instead as a literary composition by the author(s) of Deuteronomy summarizing themes developed earlier in the book, not proof of a separate pre-existing oral form. Either way, the passage is a notable place in the Torah where an ordinary worshiper is given words to say, turning national history into personal testimony ("I profess this day," v.3).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What is the difference between an offering that is simply given and one that must be verbally "professed" (v.3) before it is given? What might the spoken part add for the worshiper?
- Why does the law require the tithe-payer to explicitly deny certain uses of the tithe (v.14) rather than simply commanding its proper use?
- Why are the Levite and stranger included both in rejoicing (v.11) and in material distribution (v.12)?
- In what sense can a covenant be "mutual" (vv.17-18) if one party (the LORD) also established its terms?
- What can safely be concluded from this chapter's ritual declarations, and what about their historical origin and performance remains uncertain?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the third-year tithe declaration with [[Deuteronomy 14]], especially 14:28-29 and the "peculiar people" language of 14:2. Compare inclusive rejoicing with [[Deuteronomy 16]], wholehearted obedience ("with all thine heart, and with all thy soul," v.16) with [[Deuteronomy 6]] (6:5), and the Sinai covenant language of "peculiar treasure"/holy nation with Exodus 19:5-6. These comparisons clarify recurring themes without proving that every parallel belongs to a single ceremony or literary source.
