# Psalm 78 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 78]] · My notes: [[Psalm 78 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Israelites are to teach the Lord’s law to their children—Disobedient Israel provoked the Lord in the wilderness—The Egyptian plagues are recounted—The Lord chooses and blesses Judah and David.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The psalm moves through five movements, though this outline tracks changes of subject rather than a proven chiasm:

1. **Verses 1-8 — The purpose of remembered history.** Teaching God's 'testimony' and 'law' (v.5) to future generations is framed as a covenant duty.
2. **Verses 9-39 — Wilderness rebellion and compassion.** An isolated note on Ephraim's battlefield retreat (v.9) opens a retelling of the Exodus, wilderness provision, and repeated unbelief; the cycle closes with insincere seeking (vv.34-37) met by real divine compassion (vv.38-39).
3. **Verses 40-55 — Deliverance reconsidered.** The psalm returns to Egypt for a second, selective retelling of the plagues (vv.42-51), then the sea-crossing and conquest.
4. **Verses 56-66 — Rebellion in the land.** Idolatry leads to the abandonment of Shiloh, military defeat, and the Lord's eventual response.
5. **Verses 67-72 — Judah, Zion, and David.** The rejection of Ephraim's tribal leadership gives way to the choice of Judah, Zion, and David as shepherd of Israel.

The verb 'tempted'/tested God recurs at vv.18, 41, and 56, marking renewed provocation at several points in the survey, though the three occurrences do not mark equally weighted or symmetrical cycles.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Intergenerational covenant memory.** Verses 1-8 make teaching children God's 'testimony' and 'law' (v.5) a covenant obligation: children should 'set their hope in God' and 'not forget' his works (v.7), connecting to [[Family]], [[Education and Learning]], [[Hope]], and [[Obedience]].

**Insincere versus real repentance.** Verses 34-37 show Israel 'seeking' God only under pressure — flattering him 'with their mouth' while their heart was 'not right with him' — which complicates any simple reading of [[Repentance and Forgiveness]]: crisis-driven return is not automatically portrayed as a changed heart.

**Persistent divine compassion amid repeated apostasy.** Verses 38-39 ground God's restraint not in Israel's merit but in his own compassion and 'remembered' awareness that 'they were but flesh.'

**Consequences remain real.** The psalm does not let compassion erase consequence: it also narrates real judgment — the abandonment of Shiloh, captivity, and battlefield loss (vv.59-66) — holding [[Justice and Mercy]] in unresolved tension rather than resolving it simply.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Remember” and “forget” carry moral force throughout the psalm. Forgetting is not presented as an innocent lapse: it accompanies unbelief, covenant refusal, and renewed provocation. The description “deceitful bow” likewise portrays Israel as unreliable rather than merely weak. The word “tempted” in the KJV commonly means putting God to the test in this context; readers should not assume the narrower modern sense of enticement to wrongdoing.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The psalm retells events out of strict chronological order: it narrates the wilderness rebellion (vv.9-39) before circling back to a second, selective retelling of the plagues of Egypt (vv.42-51) that historically preceded it. That second plague list omits lice, boils, and darkness and does not match the Exodus 7-12 order verse-for-verse, which is consistent with a poetic, theological retelling rather than a chronological transcript — though it does not by itself prove or disprove literary dependence on the Exodus text.

The psalm's wilderness section also compresses several distinct traditions (water from the rock, manna, and quail) that appear at greater length in Exodus 16-17 and Numbers 11. The closing shepherd imagery (vv.70-72) turns David's former occupation into an ideal of rule, pairing 'integrity of heart' with 'skilfulness of hands.'

A secondary source reports a Jewish tradition treating verse 38 (or, per the Masorah, verse 36) as the numerical midpoint of the Book of Psalms; the two figures disagree with each other and neither is independently verified here, so this remains an open question rather than an established fact (see Questions).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What specific historical event does Psalm 78:9 (Ephraim's retreat 'in the day of battle') refer to? It is not identified elsewhere in the material examined here.
- Why does the psalm retell the plagues of Egypt (vv.42-51) *after* the wilderness rebellion narrative rather than before it, and what effect does that reordering have?
- How should the recurring 'tempted'/tested-God language (vv.18, 41, 56) be distinguished from other scriptural uses of 'temptation' as enticement to sin — and does the chapter's existing link to [[Satan and Temptation]] accurately represent that difference?
- What is the difference between Israel's remembering in verse 35 (which proves insincere) and God's remembering in verse 39?
- Does the movement from Ephraim/Shiloh to Judah/Zion/David (vv.9, 67-72) primarily explain sanctuary succession, defend Davidic kingship, critique northern Israel, or some combination?
- What would faithful remembrance look like beyond simply repeating the story?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the psalm's wilderness recital with Exodus 16-17 and Numbers 11, paying attention to what is omitted, combined, or reordered. Compare its plague list with Exodus 7-12. First Samuel 4 supplies important context for the fall of Shiloh and capture of the ark, although Psalm 78 calls the captured object God's “strength” and “glory” rather than explicitly naming it. [[Psalm 105]] and [[Psalm 106]] provide useful controls: they rehearse much of the same sacred history while distributing emphasis differently among divine faithfulness, human rebellion, confession, and mercy.
