## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
One plausible outline: vv.1-3 trust, waiting, and the danger of shame; vv.4-7 petitions for guidance and merciful remembrance; vv.8-14 reflection on whom the LORD teaches and his covenant; vv.15-21 renewed lament over entrapment, affliction, sin, and enemies; v.22 a closing petition for Israel. These divisions are a reading aid, not an explicit structure marked in the text itself.

Two literary features are visible even in translation. An inclusio (verbal envelope) frames most of the prayer: "I trust in thee: let me not be ashamed" (v.2) is echoed by "let me not be ashamed; for I put my trust in thee" (v.20), with "wait on thee" appearing near both ends (vv.3, 5, 21) - though this pattern is also common to individual laments generally and need not reflect a design unique to this psalm.

In the Hebrew source text, Psalm 25 is also constructed as an (irregular) alphabetic acrostic, each verse beginning, with some omissions and doublings, with a successive letter of the Hebrew alphabet (see Craigie, *Psalms 1-50*; Alter, *The Book of Psalms*). This is invisible in the KJV translation. Whether verse 22 - which shifts from individual to national petition - belonged to the acrostic's original form or was added later cannot be settled from the English text alone.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The psalm joins [[Prayer]], [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], and [[Covenants]]. God teaches sinners rather than merely abandoning them (Psalm 25:8), yet the promised guidance is especially associated with meekness, reverence, and covenant keeping (Psalm 25:9–14). The speaker's appeal for pardon is grounded in God's mercy, goodness, and name. At the same time, verse 10 should not be isolated as a complete theory of mercy; the chapter is devotional poetry, not a systematic account of every condition or operation of divine forgiveness.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated verb “remember” shapes verses 6–7. The speaker asks God to remember enduring mercies, not youthful sins, and to remember the person according to mercy. Repetition of “way,” “ways,” and “paths” turns moral instruction into a journey metaphor. “Shame” is also recurrent; because it is paired with enemies triumphing and with deliverance, it likely includes defeat or failed vindication rather than only an inward feeling.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The vocabulary of "shew," "teach," "lead," and "guide" clustered in verses 4-9 and recurring in verse 12 makes divine instruction one of the psalm's dominant images: prayer here doubles as a request to be taught "the way," not only to be rescued.

The psalmist names enemies who "hate me with cruel hatred" (v.19) without asking that they be cursed, punished, or destroyed - restraint worth noting relative to more overtly imprecatory laments elsewhere in the Psalter. The psalm is not entirely free of adverse petitions, however: verse 3 does ask that causeless transgressors generally 'be ashamed.'
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Verse 8 says God "will teach sinners in the way," while verses 9, 12, and 14 tie teaching and guidance to meekness, fearing the LORD, and keeping covenant. Are these in tension, or does one produce the other?
- What is the relationship in verse 10 between "mercy and truth" as divine attributes and "covenant and testimonies" as the condition for experiencing them?
- Does "integrity and uprightness" in verse 21 describe the speaker's own present fidelity, a quality God supplies, or something the speaker hopes to maintain - especially given that the same prayer confesses "great" iniquity (v.11)?
- Why might a personal, first-person prayer of confession and guidance (vv.1-21) close with a one-line petition for the whole nation, "Redeem Israel" (v.22)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
