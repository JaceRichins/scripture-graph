# Psalm 37 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 37]] · My notes: [[Psalm 37 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David counsels, Trust in the Lord and do good—Rest in the Lord and wait patiently for Him—Cease from anger and forsake wrath—The meek will inherit the earth—The Lord loves justice and does not forsake His Saints.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
In the Masoretic Hebrew text, Psalm 37 is an alphabetic acrostic (compare Psalms 9-10, 25, 34, 111-112, 119, 145): successive units, mostly spanning two verses, begin with successive Hebrew letters. This scaffolding is invisible in English, and the acrostic itself is irregular — commentators note a disruption near the expected letter around vv. 28-29. Three features survive in translation:

- **A refrain.** 'Fret not thyself' opens three exhortations (vv. 1, 7, 8), each followed by a reason to trust rather than envy.
- **Testimonial appeals.** The speaker twice grounds the argument in personal, lifelong observation rather than abstract proof — 'I have been young, and now am old' (v. 25) and 'I have seen the wicked in great power... yet he passed away' (vv. 35-36) — though v. 25 is best read as one man's reported experience, not an exceptionless promise.
- **A closing recap** (vv. 37-40) that restates the psalm's thesis in miniature: contrasting 'ends' for the upright ('peace') and the wicked ('cut off'), then a summary that salvation belongs to the LORD.

A simple thematic outline: vv. 1-11 (commands not to fret, positive practices of trust); vv. 12-22 (the wicked's violence reverses on themselves); vv. 23-31 (the Lord upholds the righteous, whose mercy and speech mark their way); vv. 32-40 (renewed threat, then final counsel and confession).

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- Trust in the LORD (vv. 3-7) as the alternative to anxious comparison with the wicked's success — an exercise of [[Faith]] expressed through action: doing good, committing one's way, and keeping his way (vv. 3, 27, 34), not passive waiting alone.
- The meek 'inherit the earth' (v. 11), echoed almost word-for-word in the [[Beatitudes]] (Matthew 5:5), linking this psalm's promise to Jesus' teaching on [[Humility]].
- Divine [[Justice and Mercy]]: violence against the poor and needy reverses on its perpetrators (vv. 14-15), 'the LORD loveth judgment, and forsaketh not his saints' (v. 28), while the righteous themselves practice mercy and generosity (vv. 21, 26).
- 'The steps of a good man are ordered by the LORD' (v. 23), paired with 'though he fall, he shall not be utterly cast down' (v. 24) — a promise of preservation through setback and adversity, not exemption from it; the psalm elsewhere names famine, hostile pursuit, and 'the time of trouble' (vv. 19, 32-33, 39) as real experiences of the righteous. Relevant to [[Adversity]] and [[Endurance]], with future language ('the end of that man is peace,' v. 37) touching on [[Final Judgment]] without specifying whether the resolution is this-worldly, eschatological, or both.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV renders the same recurring inheritance phrase as 'inherit the earth' (vv. 9, 11, 22) and 'inherit the land' (vv. 29, 34) within one chapter. The alternation is worth noting, but the English wording alone does not settle whether the underlying scope is the land of Israel specifically or the whole earth; readers should hold any conclusion about geography versus eschatology loosely.

Some lexicons connect the root behind 'wait patiently' (v. 7) to a Hebrew root elsewhere associated with writhing or anguish, which would color 'waiting on the LORD' as strenuous endurance rather than passive calm. This etymology is disputed among scholars and involves unresolved textual questions about the exact form; it should not be treated as settled.

'Delight' works in both directions in the chapter: the faithful 'delight' in the LORD (v. 4), and the LORD 'delighteth' in the good man's way (v. 23) — so the promise of 'the desires of thine heart' (v. 4) sits inside a relationship of reoriented trust, not as an unconditional guarantee.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The psalm draws repeatedly on images of impermanence for the wicked's fate — cut down 'like the grass' and withered 'as the green herb' (v. 2), spreading 'like a green bay tree' yet vanishing without trace (v. 35), consumed 'into smoke' (v. 20) — set against the righteous, who dwell in the land 'for ever' (v. 29) and whose steps are upheld by God's hand (vv. 23-24). The sword that 'enter[s] into their own heart' (v. 15) is the chapter's sharpest image of violence reversing on its author. The imagery does argumentative work a direct claim could not: prosperity that looks solid is shown to be seasonal.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> In Hebrew, Psalm 37 is organized as an alphabetic acrostic, although the pattern is not visible in the English translation. This is strong evidence of deliberate literary organization and may reflect mnemonic or aesthetic design.

> [!evidence] Textual-Criticism *(tentative)*
> Commentators on the Hebrew text of Psalm 37 have long noted at least one irregularity in the otherwise regular acrostic letter sequence, where an expected line is missing, doubled, or displaced. Sources differ on exactly where this occurs and why.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · real but vaguely sourced; no named critical edition

> [!evidence] Canonical-Tension *(tentative)*
> Psalm 37's confident retributive theology - the wicked visibly fail and the righteous visibly prosper - closely resembles the arguments Bildad and Zophar make against Job in Job 18 and Job 20. The book of Job's narrative frame has God reject the friends' theology as an incomplete account of suffering (Job 42:7-8), so the same theological pattern that structures Psalm 37 is elsewhere in the canon treated as a partial truth rather than a universal rule.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.5 · real tension, but equivalence to Job overstated

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does 'inherit the earth/land' name a this-worldly promise (the land of Israel) or a universalized, eschatological one? The chapter's own alternation between 'earth' and 'land' does not resolve this.
- Psalm 37's confidence that the wicked visibly fail and the righteous are visibly upheld resembles the retribution theology of Job's friends (Job 18, Job 20) — a theology the book of Job's frame ultimately treats as an incomplete account of suffering (Job 42:7-8). Should Psalm 37 be read as general pastoral wisdom with acknowledged exceptions (it does name famine, falling, and persecution, vv. 19, 24, 32), or does it sit in real tension with Job's protest?
- Verse 25 reports one man's lifelong observation ('I have not seen the righteous forsaken') — how should that testimony be distinguished from a universal, exceptionless promise?
- In verse 24, does 'fall' mean misfortune, moral failure, or either — and how does that shape what the promise of being upheld actually covers?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Matthew 5:5 — the [[Beatitudes]] quote Psalm 37:11 nearly verbatim in the blessing on the meek.
- [[Psalm 1]] — a companion wisdom psalm contrasting the enduring righteous with the transient wicked, though its specific images (a rooted tree; wind-driven chaff) differ from Psalm 37's grass and smoke.
- [[Psalm 112]] — another Hebrew acrostic wisdom psalm on the security and generosity of the righteous.
- Job 18 and Job 20 — Bildad's and Zophar's speeches argue a similar retribution theology, later complicated within Job itself.
- Proverbs 24:19-20 — a close proverbial parallel warning against fretting over or envying evildoers.
