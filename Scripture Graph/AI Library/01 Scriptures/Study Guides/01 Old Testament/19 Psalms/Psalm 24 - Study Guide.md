# Psalm 24 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 24]] · My notes: [[Psalm 24 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David testifies, The earth is the Lord’s and the fulness thereof, he who has clean hands and a pure heart will ascend unto the hill of the Lord, and the Lord of Hosts is the King of Glory.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The LORD and the world (vv. 1-2):** Universal ownership is grounded in God's founding and establishing work.

2. **Those who seek the LORD (vv. 3-6):** Questions about ascending and standing receive an answer centered on integrity, followed by blessing and a description of the seeking generation. Selah occurs at the end of verse 6.

3. **The entrance of the King (vv. 7-10):** Two closely parallel exchanges summon the gates, announce the King of glory, question his identity, and identify him as the LORD. A second Selah closes the psalm.

This is a strong literary outline; it does not by itself prove a historical entrance ceremony.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The opening strongly supports [[Creation]] and divine ownership: earth, abundance, world, and inhabitants all belong to the LORD. The central stanza connects access to the holy place with both conduct and inward character, making it especially relevant to [[Temples]] and [[Honesty]]. Verse 5 also balances requirement and reception: the worshiper is described as upright, yet blessing and righteousness are things received from the God of [[Salvation]]. The precise sense of "righteousness" may include divine vindication as well as moral rightness, so the verse should not be forced into a later doctrinal formula without further argument.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"Clean hands" and "pure heart" compactly pair conduct with inward character, though purity language need not exclude ritual associations. The precise force of "vanity" in verse 4 is uncertain, so claims specifically about idolatry or ordinary falsehood should remain qualified. In context, "LORD of hosts" joins the martial descriptions "strong and mighty" and "mighty in battle." The KJV wording "that seek thy face, O Jacob" in verse 6 is syntactically difficult and should not be used to settle the identity of the seekers without textual-critical evidence. Selah appears after verses 6 and 10, but its exact function is uncertain.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 7-10 have a dialogue-like pattern: the gates are commanded to rise, the King's arrival is announced, his identity is questioned, and the LORD is named in reply. The exchange then repeats with slight variation. This form is well suited to antiphonal or dramatic performance, but it neither proves multiple speakers nor preserves enough information to reconstruct a particular ceremony. The broader movement from the world, to the holy hill, to the gates also contributes to the psalm's sense of approach and arrival.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Performance And Liturgical Form
> The repeated commands, questions, and answers in verses 7–10 are substantial internal evidence that the conclusion was suited to antiphonal or dramatic performance by more than one voice.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · performance suitability supported; actual performance disputed · [[Performance And Liturgical Form in Psalm 24|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary Structure
> The progression from the LORD's world, to the LORD's holy place, to the entrance of the LORD as King supplies evidence that the three sections have been deliberately arranged as a coherent whole: the universal owner is the holy God whom the righteous seek and the glorious King whom the gates receive.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · coherence supported; compositional history unresolved · [[Literary Structure in Psalm 24|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Proposed Historical-Liturgical Setting *(tentative)*
> The command for gates to admit the LORD as a victorious King is compatible with proposals that the psalm accompanied a procession associated with the ark, especially when read beside the ark's entry into David's city in 2 Samuel 6. Psalm 24 itself, however, never mentions the ark or David.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · compatible comparison but historically underdetermined

> [!evidence] Form-Critical/Comparative Liturgical Reconstruction *(tentative)*
> Form-critical scholarship (following Sigmund Mowinckel and others) has proposed that Psalm 24, especially the gate dialogue of vv.7-10, reflects an actual temple-entrance liturgy, possibly associated with a procession bringing the ark into Jerusalem or the temple (compare the narrative of 2 Samuel 6). This is a reconstructed liturgical setting inferred from the psalm's form, not something the psalm itself states.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · recognized but disputed form-critical reconstruction
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the psalm begin with God's ownership of the whole world before asking who may approach one holy place?

- What dimensions of character are conveyed by reading "clean hands" and "a pure heart" together?

- What might "righteousness" received in verse 5 mean in this context: moral rightness, divine vindication, covenant favor, or some combination?

- Does the dialogue-like form of verses 7-10 require multiple performers, or could one poet or reciter supply every voice?

- How should the difficult phrase "O Jacob" in verse 6 be handled without claiming more textual certainty than the evidence permits?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare Psalm 24:3-6 with [[Psalm 15]], which similarly asks who may approach the LORD's sacred place and answers with a portrait of integrity.

- Compare the hands-heart-nearness pattern with [[Matthew 5]] and especially [[James 4]], without assuming direct literary dependence.

- Study [[1 Corinthians 10]] to see how Paul applies Psalm 24:1 to food and conscience.

- Read [[2 Samuel 6]] as a possible comparison for an ark procession, while remembering that Psalm 24 never names the ark, David, Jerusalem, or that event.
