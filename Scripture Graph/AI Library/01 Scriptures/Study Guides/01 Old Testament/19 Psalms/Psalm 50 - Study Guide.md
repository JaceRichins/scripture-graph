# Psalm 50 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 50]] · My notes: [[Psalm 50 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Asaph speaks of the Second Coming—The Lord accepts the sacrifices of the righteous and will deliver them—Those whose conduct is right will see the salvation of God.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Divine appearance and judgment (vv. 1–6):** God shines from Zion, summons heaven and earth, gathers those covenanting by sacrifice, and is declared judge.
2. **Address to Israel concerning worship (vv. 7–15):** God denies needing Israel's animals as food and calls for thanksgiving, fulfilled vows, calling upon him, and glorification.
3. **Address to the wicked (vv. 16–22):** Covenant speech cannot compensate for rejecting instruction, participating in wrongdoing, deceptive speech, and slander. God's prior silence must not be mistaken for moral approval.
4. **Concluding principle (v. 23):** Praise and rightly ordered conduct are joined with God's showing of salvation.

The transitions at verses 7 and 16 mark two direct addresses. Repeated language concerning covenant, silence, deliverance, and glorifying God creates thematic connections, although a rigid compositional design cannot be proven from those repetitions alone.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Sacrifice]] does not supply a needy God.** God already owns and knows the world's creatures (vv. 9–12), and verse 13 denies that he eats the flesh or drinks the blood of the offered animals.
- **The psalm does not simply abolish ritual worship.** Verse 8 does not present the continual offerings themselves as the immediate charge, while verses 14 and 23 still speak of thanksgiving, praise, and fulfilled vows. Thanksgiving may itself be understood as an offering; the contrast is therefore not merely outward ritual versus inward feeling.
- **Covenant speech must be joined to teachability and conduct.** Those who speak God's statutes and [[Covenants]] while rejecting instruction, joining wrongdoing, deceiving, and slandering are condemned (vv. 16–20).
- **Calling upon God, gratitude, and conduct belong together.** Verses 14–15 join [[Gratitude]], vows, and [[Prayer]] with divine deliverance, while verse 23 joins praise and ordered conduct with [[Salvation]].
- **Divine silence is not approval.** Verse 21 exposes the error of assuming that delayed reproof means God shares the offender's moral outlook.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Psalm 50's heaven-and-earth summons, covenant gathering, identification of God as judge, testimony against Israel, accusations, and announced reproof are consistent with a **covenant-lawsuit** reading. That remains a literary interpretation rather than the psalm's own genre label. A liturgical judgment theophany or prophetic indictment using legal imagery is a substantial alternative.

The repeated silence language creates a notable echo: God "shall not keep silence" when he comes (v. 3), while the wicked had misread his earlier silence (v. 21). Verses 15 and 22 likewise permit a contrast between promised deliverance for those who call upon God and the warning that those who forget him may have no deliverer. These correspondences support thematic coherence without proving a rigid structure or particular compositional history.

For comparison, see [[Deuteronomy 30]], [[Micah 6]], [[Isaiah 1]], and [[Amos 5]], while preserving the differences among their arguments.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary-Form Identification *(tentative)*
> The convergence of a heaven-and-earth summons, covenant assembly, explicit identification of God as judge, testimony against Israel, formal accusations, and announced reproof provides moderately strong internal evidence for the covenant-lawsuit interpretation.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · Internally consistent with disputed genre reading

> [!evidence] Typological/Doctrinal Reading Vs. Historical-Literary Sense *(tentative)*
> The official LDS chapter heading reads the opening theophany (vv.1-6, 'Our God shall come, and shall not keep silence') as a description of the Second Coming of Jesus Christ. Read on the psalm's own terms, the scene most directly depicts God coming to judge and renew covenant specifically with 'his people' Israel (v.4) at 'Zion' (v.2) — a covenant-renewal/judgment theophany, not an explicitly universal or Christological event. The Second-Coming identification is a later typological/doctrinal application rather than something the psalm's plain historical sense establishes on its own.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · Typological distinction sound; historical reconstruction uncertain

> [!evidence] Internal Literary Coherence *(tentative)*
> The paired silence statements, opposed deliverance outcomes, repeated covenant language, and repeated glorification language support the inference that the psalm is carefully organized around contrasts between genuine and merely verbal allegiance.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · Recurring contrasts observed; careful design inferred

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Thematic Parallel *(tentative)*
> Job 35:6-7 (Elihu's speech) articulates a closely parallel theology to Psalm 50:9-13: that God's righteousness or sustenance is not increased by human offerings and is unaffected by human sin, i.e., divine self-sufficiency relative to human moral/ritual acts. This is flagged by the unverified semantic-similarity index (Job 35, score 0.586) and is plausible on thematic grounds, but the connection has not been independently verified here as a direct literary relationship.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · Broad thematic parallel only

> [!evidence] Evidence *(tentative)*
> Psalm 50 uses the form or imagery of a covenant lawsuit: heaven and earth are summoned as witnesses, God acts as judge or prosecutor, and the covenant congregation is addressed as defendant.
> — confidence medium · secondary-claim

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does verse 8 say that Israel's continual offerings are not the immediate basis of reproof before verses 9–13 criticize mistaken assumptions about sacrifice? How do thanksgiving, vows, prayer, and praise redefine the worshiper's relationship to God? What does verse 21 suggest about interpreting delayed judgment? Does the summons of heaven and earth function as formal covenant-witness language, a cosmic audience, or both?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Psalm 50:8–15 with [[Psalm 51]], especially Psalm 51:16–17. Then compare the cosmic summons and ethical critique with [[Micah 6]], the sacrifice-and-conduct argument in [[Isaiah 1]], and the worship-and-justice argument in [[Amos 5]]. These parallels illuminate a shared prophetic concern without requiring every passage to reject sacrifice in precisely the same way.
