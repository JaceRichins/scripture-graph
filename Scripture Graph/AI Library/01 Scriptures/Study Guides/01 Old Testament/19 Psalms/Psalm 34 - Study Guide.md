# Psalm 34 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 34]] · My notes: [[Psalm 34 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David blesses the Lord at all times—He counsels, Keep your tongue from evil; do good and seek peace—He says that not one of the Messiah’s bones will be broken.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Praise and invitation (Psalm 34:1-3):** The speaker resolves to praise continually and invites the humble to join him.

2. **Testimony and exhortation (Psalm 34:4-10):** Seeking and crying are answered by hearing, deliverance, protection, and the invitation to taste the LORD's goodness.

3. **Wisdom lesson (Psalm 34:11-14):** The address to “children” introduces instruction about speech, evil, goodness, and peace.

4. **Two ways and their outcomes (Psalm 34:15-22):** God's eyes, ears, and face distinguish his response to the righteous from his opposition to evildoers; the section ends with redemption for his servants.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Fear of the LORD.** Psalm 34:7-14 associates fear of the LORD with trust, teachability, disciplined speech, departure from evil, good action, and pursuit of peace. This supports connections with [[Faith]], [[Obedience]], and [[Repentance]], while leaving room for fear to include reverential awe as well as practical conduct.

**Divine nearness in affliction.** Verse 18 says that the LORD is near to the brokenhearted and saves those of a contrite spirit. Brokenheartedness is therefore not presented as proof of abandonment. The language resonates with [[Humility]] and [[Adversity]] and parallels Psalm 51:17 and Isaiah 57:15; it need not refer exclusively to repentance.

**The unbroken bones.** Verse 20 speaks within a general discussion of the righteous and does not explicitly identify a future Messiah. John 19:36 applies an unbroken-bone scriptural motif to Jesus' crucifixion, supporting a later Christological reading. Exodus 12:46 and Numbers 9:12 are substantial alternative or complementary sources for John's wording, so Psalm 34 should not be presented as John's certain sole source or as an unambiguous original prediction.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The poem repeatedly moves through bodily vocabulary: mouth and soul (Psalm 34:1-2), faces and sight (Psalm 34:5), taste and sight (Psalm 34:8), tongue and lips (Psalm 34:13), divine eyes and ears (Psalm 34:15), heart and spirit (Psalm 34:18), bones (Psalm 34:20), and soul again (Psalm 34:22). The result is an embodied account of worship and trust: goodness is tasted, cries are heard, faces are transformed, speech is disciplined, and the whole person is preserved.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
In Hebrew, Psalm 34:1-21 is alphabetic from aleph through tav except for a missing waw line; Psalm 34:22 supplies an additional pe-initial redemption line. The acrostic strongly supports deliberate literary composition, but it cannot determine authorship or date.

Repetition provides a second organizing device. The LORD “heard” or “heareth” in Psalm 34:4, 6, and 17; deliverance recurs in Psalm 34:4, 7, 17, and 19; and fear of the LORD links Psalm 34:7, 9, and 11. These repetitions join testimony, invitation, instruction, and assurance.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Reception And Intertextuality
> John 19:36 applies a bone-not-broken scriptural formula to Jesus' crucifixion. Psalm 34:20 is a plausible source, but Exodus 12:46 and Numbers 9:12 concerning the Passover victim are also plausible, and John may evoke more than one text. This is strong evidence for early Christian reception of the motif but not proof that Psalm 34 was originally composed as an exclusively messianic prediction.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Reception certain; exact antecedent disputed · [[Canonical Reception And Intertextuality in Psalm 34|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary Structure
> The Hebrew text shows deliberate alphabetic construction: Psalm 34:1-21 begins successively with letters from aleph through tav, except that waw is omitted; Psalm 34:22 then adds a second pe-initial line as a concluding redemption statement. This is strong evidence of conscious literary shaping, although the pattern is largely invisible in English translation.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Broadly accepted textual and literary observation · [[Literary Structure in Psalm 34|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Typology/Intertextual Application *(tentative)*
> The 'not one of them is broken' language of Psalm 34:20 is applied by many Christian and Latter-day Saint readers (including the official chapter heading for this psalm) to Jesus Christ's unbroken bones at the crucifixion (John 19:33, 36), though the verse's own literary context speaks generally of divine protection of 'the righteous' rather than a single foretold figure.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · Christological reception accepted; precise source disputed

> [!evidence] Comparative Literary/Text-Critical *(tentative)*
> Psalm 34 and Psalm 25 are both widely described as Hebrew acrostics that, per the Masoretic Text, omit a vav-line and append an extra pe-verse after the sequence's closing tav-verse; notably, both extra pe-verses invoke God's redemption of his people/servants 'out of...troubles' (compare Psalm 25:22 and Psalm 34:22), a pattern noted by scholars of Hebrew poetry.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · Structural resemblance plausible; interpretation disputed

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Thematic Parallel *(tentative)*
> The paired idiom 'broken heart'/'contrite spirit' in Psalm 34:18 anticipates similar language in Psalm 51:17 and is later formalized as the required 'sacrifice' in 3 Nephi 9:20 and Doctrine and Covenants 59:8.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Verbal parallel accepted; historical lineage unproven
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should the assurances of no lack and complete deliverance in Psalm 34:9-10, 17-22 be understood alongside the statement that the righteous have many afflictions?

- Does “taste and see” in Psalm 34:8 invite experiential recognition, public worship, covenant participation, or some combination?

- What is gained, and what is risked, when Psalm 34:20 is read first in its general context and then through its Christological reception in [[John 19]]?

- Why does the moral lesson move from restraint of speech to the active pursuit of peace?

- Do the assurances describe temporal rescue, ultimate vindication, wisdom-poetic confidence, or more than one of these?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Psalm 34:8 with [[1 Peter 2]], and Psalm 34:12-16 with [[1 Peter 3]]. The two uses suggest that 1 Peter engages Psalm 34 as more than an isolated proof text. For Psalm 34:18, compare [[Psalm 51]] and [[Isaiah 57]]. For the unbroken-bones motif, study [[John 19]] alongside [[Exodus 12]] and [[Numbers 9]]; this comparison preserves both the Gospel's christological use and the genuine uncertainty about which scriptural source or combination of sources John intended.
