## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds [[Justice and Mercy]] together without making either trivial. Rebellion produces real consequences: plague, defeat, oppression, and scattering. Yet judgment is not the final word. The LORD repeatedly delivers the people, hears their cry, regards their affliction, and remembers his [[Covenants|covenant]] (verses 43-45).

The psalm also treats [[Sin]] as both personal and communal. Verse 6 does not permit the worshipping community to treat ancestral rebellion as someone else's story. This need not mean that every speaker committed every historical act; it does mean that confession rejects self-exemption and acknowledges continuity with a sinful community.

Moses' action in verse 23 illustrates mediation: he stands "in the breach" and turns away threatened destruction. The verse directly presents Moses' role; identifying it as a complete account of later doctrines about mediation would go beyond what this psalm itself establishes.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated contrast between forgetting and remembering is central. The ancestors do not remember the multitude of divine mercies (verse 7), soon forget God's works (verse 13), and forget God their saviour (verse 21). In contrast, God remembers the covenant (verse 45). Within the poem, faithful memory means allowing past deliverance to shape present trust and obedience.

Salvation language also threads through the chapter: “salvation” in verse 4, “saved” in verses 8 and 10, and “saviour” in verse 21. These terms connect the Red Sea narrative, the speaker's present petition, and the community's continuing dependence upon God.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Psalm 106 opens and closes with the identical cry "Praise ye the LORD" (vv.1, 48), forming an inclusio around the whole composition. Its confession in verse 6 ("we have sinned with our fathers") places the present congregation inside the guilt of the historical narrative that follows, rather than treating it as a report about distant ancestors. A striking transition occurs between verses 12 and 13: belief and praise follow deliverance, but forgetting comes "soon" — the abruptness underscores that a momentary religious response is not the same as enduring remembrance and obedience.

The psalm compresses many episodes into brief allusions — Dathan, Abiram, the calf, Phinehas — naming people and acts without explanation, which assumes an audience already familiar with Israel's larger historical traditions.

Verse 31's language that Phinehas's zeal "was counted unto him for righteousness" echoes the covenantal formula applied to Abraham's faith in [[Genesis 15]]:6; the underlying Hebrew uses closely corresponding vocabulary, though what the echo is meant to establish about Phinehas's status remains an open question. The psalm's closing verses (47-48) are closely paralleled in [[1 Chronicles 16]]:34-36, though the Chronicles wording is adapted rather than identical, and the direction of dependence between the two texts is debated. Verse 48 also functions as the doxology closing Book IV of the Psalter (Psalms 90-106).

[[Psalm 105]], [[Psalm 78]], and [[Nehemiah 9]] offer useful comparisons: each retells Israel's history with a different selection and rhetorical purpose — Psalm 105 emphasizing divine grace, Psalm 106 emphasizing repeated rebellion, and Nehemiah 9 combining both within a single communal confession.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Internal Historical Indicator *(tentative)*
> The plea in verse 47 to be gathered 'from among the heathen' implies that the psalm, at least in its final form, speaks to (or on behalf of) an audience already experiencing dispersion among foreign nations, which is commonly taken as evidence of an exilic or post-exilic setting for the psalm's composition or final redaction.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed dating

> [!evidence] Textual Reuse / Redaction Question *(tentative)*
> Psalm 106:47-48 is reproduced almost verbatim in 1 Chronicles 16:35-36, within a psalm the Chronicler attributes to the day David brought the ark to Jerusalem, raising a well-known question in psalm scholarship about the direction of literary dependence between the two texts.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed direction of dependence

> [!evidence] Intertextual Phrase Echo *(tentative)*
> Verse 31's statement that Phinehas's zealous act 'was counted unto him for righteousness' echoes, in the English KJV wording, the covenantal formula used of Abraham's faith in Genesis 15:6 ('he counted it to him for righteousness'), suggesting the psalmist may be deliberately elevating Phinehas's deed to the status of a covenant-establishing act of righteousness.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed interpretive significance

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Genre Pattern *(tentative)*
> The corporate-confession formula of verse 6 ('we have sinned with our fathers') matches a pattern found in other communal confessions of Israel's history, most notably Nehemiah 9, suggesting Psalm 106 belongs to a recognizable genre of national penitential recitation rather than being a unique composition.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Why does the speaker move from “me” in verses 4–5 to “we” in verse 6?
2. What distinguishes remembrance as practiced by God in verse 45 from the people's failures of memory in verses 7, 13, and 21?
3. How are Moses' action in verse 23 and Phinehas's action in verses 30–31 similar, and how are they morally and functionally different?
4. Does verse 15 describe physical wasting, spiritual impoverishment, or both? What can and cannot be concluded from the wording alone?
5. How does the gathering petition in verse 47 affect the way the preceding historical review is read?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
