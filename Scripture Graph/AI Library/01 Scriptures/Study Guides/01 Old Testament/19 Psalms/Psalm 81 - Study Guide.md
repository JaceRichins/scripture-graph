# Psalm 81 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 81]] · My notes: [[Psalm 81 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Israel is commanded to sing praises to God—If the Israelites had walked in the Lord’s ways, they would have triumphed over their enemies.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The psalm can be read in five movements, though the exact seams are debated:

1. **Festival summons and its statute (vv.1-4).** Instruments, new moon, and a solemn feast are commanded as a fixed observance ('a statute for Israel, and a law of the God of Jacob').
2. **Historical grounding (v.5).** The statute is traced to Joseph and the exodus from Egypt, though the identity of 'I' in 'a language that I understood not' is genuinely ambiguous in the KJV.
3. **Remembered deliverance and testing (vv.6-7).** Relief from Egyptian labor, an answered prayer 'in the secret place of thunder,' and testing 'at the waters of Meribah,' closed with 'Selah.'
4. **Call to exclusive allegiance (vv.8-10).** A first-person divine voice — identifiable by its claims to have delivered Israel from Egypt — demands Israel reject 'strange gods.'
5. **Refusal, consequence, and forfeited blessing (vv.11-16).** Israel's refusal leads to being 'given up' to its own counsels (v.12), followed by a wistful counterfactual: obedience would have brought victory and abundance (vv.13-16).

Where exactly the divine voice begins (v.6? v.8?) is not settled by the English text alone.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Covenant language echoing the [[Ten Commandments]].** Vv.9-10 pair 'no strange god... neither shalt thou worship any strange god' with 'I am the LORD thy God, which brought thee out of the land of Egypt' — closely paralleling the Decalogue's preamble and first commandment (Exodus 20:2-3; Deuteronomy 5:6-7). This is strong evidence of shared covenant tradition; it does not by itself prove the psalm quotes a fixed written text rather than a common oral covenant formula.
- **[[Obedience]] and conditional blessing.** Vv.13-16 present subdued enemies and abundant food as what *would have* followed obedience — blessing here is relational and conditional, addressed to Israel as a people, not a guarantee of individual prosperity.
- **[[Agency]] and divine 'giving up.'** V.12 ('I gave them up unto their own hearts' lust') depicts one instance of judgment as God releasing a persistently unresponsive people to its own chosen course; this should not be generalized into a full theory of how divine sovereignty and human agency interact.
- **[[Testimony]].** The festival ordinance is itself called 'a testimony' (v.5), and God says 'I will testify unto thee' (v.8) — worship here doubles as a vehicle for testimony, not only celebration.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
- **Selah** (v.7) marks a pause immediately after the Meribah recollection; its precise musical or liturgical function is unknown, so its placement is suggestive rather than proof of deliberate structure.
- **Timbrel, psaltery** (v.2) are percussion/stringed instruments associated with festival worship.
- **'The time appointed' (v.3)** translates a difficult Hebrew term some render as tied to the full moon rather than a generic 'appointed time' — which could point toward Tabernacles, or possibly Passover/Unleavened Bread, rather than settling on the new-moon Feast of Trumpets alone. The KJV does not resolve which feast(s) are meant.
- **'Strange god' (v.9)** is clarified by its own parallel clause ('neither shalt thou worship any strange god') to mean a deity other than Israel's God, not merely something unfamiliar.
- The divine speaker from v.6 onward is identified by content (claims to have delivered Israel from Egypt) rather than by an introductory formula like 'Thus saith the LORD.'
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Psalm 81 combines festival hymn with prophetic admonition: praise is commanded, then a divine voice interrupts to recall history, restate covenant terms, and indict the worshippers. The sequence (deliverance recalled → exclusive allegiance demanded → disobedience → forfeited blessing) is a recognizable covenant-exhortation pattern. Calling this a formal 'covenant lawsuit,' on the analogy of [[Psalm 95]] or Psalm 50, is a plausible but disputed form-critical label — the psalm lacks explicit courtroom or ratification language, so 'prophetic exhortation within worship' is the safer description.

V.16's 'honey out of the rock' echoes the same phrase in the Song of Moses (Deuteronomy 32:13), though that passage's immediate context describes the land's produce rather than wilderness provision specifically — the verbal link is solid, the shared setting less so.

The ending is unusual: rather than resolving in renewed praise, the psalm closes on an unrealized 'Oh that my people had hearkened' (v.13), leaving obedience's blessings only hypothetical.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Who is speaking in v.5's 'where I heard a language that I understood not' — Israel's collective memory, the psalmist, or someone else? The KJV does not make the subject clear.
- Which festival does v.3 have in view — the new-moon Feast of Trumpets, the full-moon Tabernacles, Passover, or a general festal reference? The underlying term is genuinely ambiguous in translation.
- What does 'the secret place of thunder' (v.7) evoke — Sinai, the pillar of cloud, or a general image of divine self-disclosure? Meribah is explicit; the thunder reference is not similarly specific.
- Does 'the waters of Meribah' point to the episode at Rephidim (Exodus 17) or the later Meribah-Kadesh incident (Numbers 20), or both? The two accounts differ in who is said to test whom.
- Do vv.9-10's near-Decalogue wording reflect direct dependence on a written Exodus/Deuteronomy text, or a shared oral covenant formula that predates any fixed text?
- Some have noted a verbal resemblance between v.12 ('gave them up unto their own hearts' lust') and Romans 1:24 ('gave them up... through the lusts of their own hearts'). Whether this reflects deliberate Pauline allusion or simply a common judgment idiom is unresolved and would need a Hebrew/Greek-level comparison to decide, not an English resemblance alone.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Psalm 81:9-10 with [[Exodus 20]] and [[Deuteronomy 5]] on the shared deliverance-then-allegiance pattern. Compare Psalm 81:7 with both [[Exodus 17]] and Numbers 20 for the two candidate Meribah episodes. A lexical study of Psalm 81:3 against Israel's festival calendar may narrow the feast in view, though certainty should not be overstated.
