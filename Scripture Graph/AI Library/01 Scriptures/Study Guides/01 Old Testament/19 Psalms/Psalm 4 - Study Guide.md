# Psalm 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 4]] · My notes: [[Psalm 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David pleads for mercy—He counsels, Put your trust in the Lord.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Petition based on remembered help (verse 1):** the speaker asks to be heard and recalls relief in distress.
2. **Confrontation and assurance (verses 2-3):** hostile or erring people ('sons of men') are rebuked, while the speaker affirms that the LORD hears the godly.
3. **Counsel (verses 4-5):** avoid [[Sin]], reflect in stillness, offer righteous [[Sacrifice]], and trust the LORD.
4. **Answer to discouragement (verses 6-8):** against the question 'Who will shew us any good?', a petition for divine favor is offered (the text does not explicitly tag this as the psalmist's own voice versus a continuation of the doubters' words); the psalm closes with testimony of inward gladness and peaceful rest.

The two 'Selah' markers (after vv.2 and 4) fall at natural pause points between address to opponents and the turn to counsel, though their precise function is uncertain.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The psalm portrays [[Faith]] as trust expressed amid unresolved pressure, not only after circumstances improve: the confrontation of opponents (vv.2-3) and the counsel to trust (v.5) both occur before the resolution of vv.7-8. Verse 7 places God-given gladness above the joy of an abundant harvest, and verse 8 depicts peace sufficient for sleep — a personal confession, not a blanket promise that the faithful are always spared physical harm. The counsel of verses 4-5 joins inward and outward devotion: self-communing and stillness accompany restraint from [[Sin]], righteous [[Sacrifice]], and trust, without the text ranking one above the other or declaring ritual insufficient on its own.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
'Leasing' in verse 2 is archaic KJV English for falsehood or deception, unrelated to the modern rental sense.

'Stand in awe' in verse 4 should be handled cautiously. [[Ephesians 4]]:26 has the related wording 'Be ye angry, and sin not,' while Psalm 4:4 in the KJV reads 'Stand in awe, and sin not.' The shared warning against sin is clear; whether Paul is echoing this psalm (possibly via the Greek Septuagint) or independently using a common moral phrase is not settled from the English text alone.

The 'light of thy countenance' in verse 6 is an image of favorable divine presence and closely resembles the blessing language of [[Numbers 6]]:25-26 ('The LORD make his face shine upon thee... The LORD lift up his countenance upon thee, and give thee peace'), which together supply both the 'shine' and 'lift up...countenance' language echoed here.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The poem uses contrast throughout: glory and shame, vanity and godliness, public cynicism ('Who will shew us any good?') and personal confidence, agricultural increase and inward gladness, distress and peaceful sleep. This antithetical structure reinforces the psalm's movement toward trust in God over social vindication or material security. The closing testimony of peaceful sleep (v.8) also closely parallels the neighboring [[Psalm 3]]:5 ('I laid me down and slept... for the LORD sustained me'), though this need not imply the two psalms share a common occasion or authorship event.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary Contrast
> The contrast between inward gladness, material abundance, and peaceful sleep strongly supports reading the conclusion as trust grounded in God rather than in favorable harvest conditions.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-hedged internal literary evidence · [[Internal Literary Contrast in Psalm 4|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Text-Critical *(tentative)*
> Ephesians 4:26 opens with "Be ye angry, and sin not," wording that closely parallels the Greek Septuagint rendering of Psalm 4:4 ("be angry and sin not"), which differs from the Hebrew Masoretic sense behind the KJV's "Stand in awe, and sin not." This suggests the New Testament author may be drawing on the Greek Psalter's phrasing of this verse.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · real parallel, but overstated framing

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What specific situation caused the speaker's distress and exposed his 'glory' to shame? The text does not say.
- Who are the 'sons of men' being rebuked in verse 2 — political opponents, social elites, fellow worshippers, or a broader moral audience?
- Does 'sacrifices of righteousness' (v.5) mean properly performed offerings, offerings made by righteous people, a righteous disposition, or some combination?
- In verse 6, is the petition for God's favor voiced by the psalmist in answer to the cynics, or could it continue as the words of 'the many' themselves? The text does not tag the speaker explicitly.
- Is the parallel between Psalm 4:4 and [[Ephesians 4]]:26 ('...and sin not') a deliberate New Testament allusion, possibly via the Septuagint, or an independent use of a common moral phrase?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the 'light of thy countenance' language in verse 6 with the priestly blessing in [[Numbers 6]]:25-26. Readers interested in the New Testament's use of the Psalms may compare the wording of verse 4 with [[Ephesians 4]]:26 ('Be ye angry, and sin not'). The closing image of peaceful sleep (v.8) is also worth reading alongside [[Psalm 3]]:5.
