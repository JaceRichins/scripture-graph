## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David says, The Lord is my light and my salvation—He desires to dwell in the house of the Lord forever—He counsels, Wait on the Lord and be of good courage.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The psalm's clearest pivot comes at verse 7, where confident declaration about the LORD (vv. 1-6) gives way to direct, urgent address -- "Hear, O LORD... answer me." A useful, though not the only defensible, way to read the whole is in three movements: confidence and sanctuary-centered desire (vv. 1-6), direct petition (vv. 7-12), and confessed hope with closing exhortation (vv. 13-14). This should not be pressed as a strict grammatical rule -- the LORD is still referred to in the third person within the petition section (e.g., vv. 10, 13) -- so the shift at verse 7 is best read as a change in dominant rhetorical mode, not a perfectly maintained grammatical division.

Binding the sections together are recurring "enemy" terms (vv. 2, 6, 11, 12) and the keyword "seek," which links the desire to dwell in God's house (v. 4) to the plea to see God's face (v. 8). Verse 14 forms its own small envelope ("Wait on the LORD... wait, I say, on the LORD"), functioning as the psalm's closing summary.

Some scholars propose Psalm 27 originated as two separate compositions later joined by an editor -- a psalm of confidence (vv. 1-6) and a psalm of lament (vv. 7-14) -- pointing to the abrupt shift at verse 7 as evidence. This is a genuinely disputed question; the same shift is equally consistent with a single prayer deliberately moving from rehearsed trust to the harder work of petition, a pattern found elsewhere in the Psalter. The text itself does not resolve it.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The psalm's declared "one thing" (v. 4) makes nearness to God -- dwelling in his house, beholding him, worshiping -- central to the psalmist's desire, not merely a means to safety, connecting the chapter to [[Temples]]. The petitions of verses 7-9 and 11 model [[Prayer]] as bold, repeated asking: for mercy, for God's face, for a "plain path." Verse 10 sets [[Family]] and [[Adversity]] side by side, contrasting even the possibility of parental abandonment with the LORD's reception of the psalmist -- an extreme hypothetical, not a report of actual abandonment. The closing counsel to "wait on the LORD" (v. 14) ties [[Faith]], [[Hope]], and [[Endurance]] together: trust is sustained through waiting, not merely declared once. The LORD is twice named the psalmist's [[Salvation]] (vv. 1, 9).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verse 8 plays on the word "face": in the KJV, God's invitation ("Seek ye my face") is answered by the psalmist reversing the phrase back to him ("Thy face, LORD, will I seek"), enacting in the wording itself the relationship being described. This wordplay is clear at the level of the English translation; whether it reflects a comparable formal structure in the underlying Hebrew has not been verified here.

Verse 13 is a notable case of broken syntax: it opens with "unless" but never states an explicit consequence, so the KJV translators supplied words (traditionally italicized in many printed editions) to complete the English sense. Read this way, the unfinished construction suggests -- though does not prove -- a speaker overtaken by feeling before catching himself in trust; this remains one interpretive reading of an ambiguous ellipsis, not a certainty.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several images build a sense of security: light dispels fear, enemies stumble and fall, the worshipper is hidden in a pavilion and the secret of a tabernacle, and set upon a rock (v. 5); the head lifted up in verse 6 reverses the surrounding pressure of enemies. The psalm's closing refrain, "be of good courage, and he shall strengthen thine heart" (v. 14), closely echoes [[Psalm 31]]'s "Be of good courage, and he shall strengthen your heart" (31:24) -- a striking phrase-level parallel, though the automated overlap check found no verbatim shared verses between the two chapters, so the relationship (shared formula, direct echo, or independent use of stock language) cannot be determined from this alone. Its desire to "dwell in the house of the LORD" (v. 4) likewise echoes [[Psalm 23]]'s wish to "dwell in the house of the LORD for ever" (23:6), a recurring Psalter image for the goal of a faithful life.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why might the psalmist move from bold confidence (vv. 1-6) to urgent pleading (vv. 7-12) rather than staying in one register throughout?
- What does it mean that the psalmist's "one thing" desired (v. 4) is presence in God's house rather than victory over enemies?
- How does verse 10's claim about parental abandonment shape the psalm's picture of God?
- Why might guidance in a "plain path" (v. 11) matter to the psalmist as much as deliverance from enemies?
- What does verse 14 pair with "waiting" that keeps it from meaning passivity?
