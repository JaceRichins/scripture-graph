# Psalm 72 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 72]] · My notes: [[Psalm 72 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David speaks of Solomon, who is made a type of the Messiah—He will have dominion—His name will endure forever—All nations will call him blessed—The whole earth will be filled with the glory of the Lord.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1–4:** Prayer for divinely grounded royal judgment, tested by the treatment of the poor and oppressed.
2. **Verses 5–7:** Enduring peace and flourishing, pictured through rain and agricultural renewal.
3. **Verses 8–11:** Expansive dominion and homage from distant rulers and nations.
4. **Verses 12–14:** The ruler's deliverance of the helpless and redemption of people from deceit and violence. The opening “For” may connect this justice especially with the preceding dominion, though it can also ground the royal portrait more generally.
5. **Verses 15–17:** Tribute, continuing prayer, abundance, an enduring name, and blessing associated with all nations; verse 15 contains shifts of subject that should be interpreted cautiously.
6. **Verses 18–19:** Doxology directing ultimate praise and worldwide glory to God.
7. **Verse 20:** A concluding collection notice concerning the prayers of David.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Psalm 72 closely joins [[Government and Laws]] with [[Justice and Mercy]]. Righteous rule is made concrete through fair judgment, rescue of needy children, opposition to oppression, attention to those without helpers, and protection of lives endangered by violence. Peace is portrayed not merely as the absence of conflict but as flourishing associated with righteousness.

The king receives judgment from God rather than possessing autonomous moral authority. Verses 18–19 reinforce this hierarchy by declaring that God alone does wondrous things and by directing the hope of worldwide glory to him. Within Christian interpretation, the enduring and expansive royal vision can point toward [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] and the [[Kingdom of God]]. This is a canonical and typological application, not proof that the psalm had an exclusively messianic original referent.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Repeated word groups organize the poem. “Judgments,” “judgment,” “righteousness,” and “righteous” shape the opening movement, while “poor” and “needy” recur in verses 2, 4, and 12–14. The widening expressions “ends of the earth,” “all kings,” “all nations,” and “whole earth” create a motif of total scope.

Celestial language—sun, moon, and generations—expresses prolonged or ideal permanence, though not every occurrence is technically a merism. Natural images make the royal vision tangible: rain on cut grass, showers watering the earth, flourishing righteousness, mountain grain, Lebanon-like fruit, and grass-like growth portray beneficial rule as life-giving abundance. “The river” in verse 8 may denote the Euphrates, but the verse alone does not settle that identification.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The placement of verses 12–14 immediately after the claims of dominion and international homage creates a meaningful juxtaposition: extraordinary royal authority is interpreted alongside deliverance of the needy and protection from violence. The word “For” may present such justice as a rationale for the preceding portrait, although it can also introduce a broader elaboration of ideal kingship.

Psalm 72:8 is closely paralleled in [[Zechariah 9]], and its portrait of righteous judgment for the poor resembles [[Isaiah 11]]. Psalm 72:17 also resonates with the worldwide blessing language of the [[Abrahamic Covenant]]. These are useful canonical connections, but they do not by themselves determine literary dependence, the psalm's date, or an exclusively messianic original meaning.

Verses 18–19 form a doxology, and verse 20 functions as a collection-closing notice. This editorial role does not prove that David composed every preceding psalm.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Typological/Canonical Reading Of A Royal Psalm *(tentative)*
> The psalm's language of everlasting dominion, homage from 'all kings' and service from 'all nations,' and a name that endures 'as long as the sun' (v17) exceeds what any historical Israelite king actually achieved. This gap between the language and the historical reality is part of why later Jewish and Christian (including the LDS chapter heading) tradition reads the psalm typologically, with the historical king (traditionally Solomon) prefiguring the Messiah.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the poem interrupt its description of international homage with “For he shall deliver the needy” in verse 12? What does that placement suggest about the moral basis of legitimate authority?

Does “prayer also shall be made for him continually” in verse 15 portray the king as dependent upon the people's prayers even at the height of his glory?

How does the doxology in verses 18–19 prevent the king's worldwide dominion from becoming a claim that the king replaces God?

Which elements could reasonably apply to Solomon or another Davidic ruler, and which stretch beyond ordinary historical kingship? What is gained by retaining both horizons?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Psalm 72:1–4 with [[1 Kings 3]] on Solomon's request for discernment to judge God's people, and with [[Isaiah 11]] on the ideal ruler's righteous judgment of the poor. Compare the dominion formula in Psalm 72:8 with [[Zechariah 9]], noting that close wording does not establish which text depends on the other.

For the psalm's royal setting and enduring throne imagery, study [[2 Samuel 7]]. For the possible worldwide-blessing resonance in verse 17, compare [[Genesis 12]] and [[Genesis 22]]. Within Christian interpretation, compare the enduring Davidic reign in [[Luke 1]], while distinguishing canonical application from proof of the psalm's original authorial intent.

Further study may also examine how verse 20 functions as a collection boundary even though later psalms are again associated with David, and how major translations handle the shifting subjects and pronouns in verse 15.
