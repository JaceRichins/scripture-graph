# Psalm 109 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 109]] · My notes: [[Psalm 109 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David speaks of the cursings due to the wicked and deceitful—He prays that his enemies will be confounded.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1–5 — Complaint:** deceitful and hateful speech surrounds the speaker; love and good are repaid with enmity and evil.
2. **Verses 6–20 — Imprecation:** repeated petitions seek judicial, social, economic, familial, and memorial ruin for a singular offender, followed by a return to plural adversaries.
3. **Verses 21–29 — Plea for deliverance:** the poor, fasting, wounded speaker appeals to God's name and mercy and asks that hostile curses be answered by divine blessing.
4. **Verses 30–31 — Anticipated praise:** the speaker expects to praise publicly because the LORD stands at the poor person's right hand.

A particularly strong structural link joins verses 6 and 31: a condemning figure stands at one right hand, while the LORD stands at the poor person's right hand to save. This judicial reversal helps unify the psalm's movement from accusation to defense.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The psalm presents God as defender of the poor and needy against those who condemn them. Deliverance is requested "for thy name's sake" and "according to thy mercy," making divine character central to the plea. At the same time, the expansive curses resist easy harmonization with a generalized ethic of forgiveness. A responsible reading can treat the psalm as honest lament and appeal for divine judgment without assuming that every requested penalty states a universal rule for Christian conduct. Its moral logic is clearest in verses 16–19: refusal of mercy and love of cursing are answered reciprocally. This contributes to [[Justice and Mercy]], while the petitioner's posture contributes to [[Prayer]] and [[Fasting]].

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Speech governs the chapter. The enemies open deceitful mouths and use lying tongues and words of hatred; the speaker gives himself to prayer; the adversaries speak evil; and the closing vow promises praise "with my mouth." Cursing and blessing are likewise set against one another. The garment imagery intensifies the reciprocal judgment: because the offender clothed himself with cursing, cursing is asked to become his garment, girdle, and an inward substance like water or oil.

Judicial language is also concentrated: an accuser stands at the right hand, the offender is judged and condemned, and the LORD ultimately stands at the poor person's right hand to save him from those who condemn his soul.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The strongest internal literary feature is the right-hand reversal between verses 6 and 31. It transforms the place occupied by a condemning adversary into the place of divine advocacy. Other organizing contrasts include love/hatred, good/evil, mercy/persecution, cursing/blessing, shame/rejoicing, and hostile speech/public praise.

The change from plural enemies to a singular target and back to plural adversaries is conspicuous. It may focalize a chief accuser or representative enemy. A debated alternative is that verses 6–19 quote the enemies' curse against the speaker. Because the text does not explicitly mark a quotation, neither reading should be presented as certain.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Intertextual Reuse
> Acts 1:20 explicitly presents wording from the Psalms and applies Psalm 109:8's instruction that another take the condemned person's office to the replacement of Judas. This is strong evidence for early Christian reuse of the verse in interpreting Judas and apostolic succession, while leaving the original historical referent of Psalm 109 unresolved.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · Broadly accepted canonical intertext · [[Canonical Intertextual Reuse in Psalm 109|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Biblical Intertextuality *(tentative)*
> That the earliest Christian community treated Psalm 109:8 (via the Septuagint, alongside Psalm 69:25) as authoritative warrant for replacing Judas Iscariot (Acts 1:15-26) is evidence that Second Temple/apostolic interpreters read certain individual-lament psalms typologically, applying an anonymous ancient enemy's fate to a specific figure within salvation history.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · Later Christian application accepted; broader generalization disputed

> [!evidence] Internal Literary Structure *(tentative)*
> The opposing right-hand scenes, together with the movement from condemnation to divine defense, are strong internal evidence that verses 6 and 31 function as a deliberate literary contrast within the received form of the psalm.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · Functional contrast strong; deliberate design not demonstrable

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the singular figure in verses 6–19 represent one leading adversary, the adversaries collectively, or the target of words being quoted from the enemies?
- How does the appeal for reciprocal justice in verses 16–19 relate to the petitions affecting innocent or dependent family members in verses 9–15?
- What changes when the two right-hand scenes in verses 6 and 31 are read together?
- Does "Let them curse, but bless thou" imply that divine blessing, rather than control over enemy speech, is the speaker's ultimate hope?
- How should the reuse of verse 8 in [[Acts 1]] be distinguished from a claim that the entire psalm originally referred only to Judas?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Psalm 35]] for false testimony and evil returned for good, and [[Psalm 69]] for undeserved hatred, reproach, and afflicted prayer. Then examine [[Acts 1]], where Psalm 109:8's office-replacement clause is applied to Judas while material associated with Psalm 69 appears in the same citation. This sequence permits careful study of how later scripture reuses an earlier lament without requiring that the earlier psalm's entire original setting be identical to the later application.
