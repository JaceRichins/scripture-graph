# Psalm 31 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 31]] · My notes: [[Psalm 31 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> David trusts in the Lord and rejoices in His mercy—Speaking as the Messiah he says, Into Thine hand I commit my spirit—He counsels, O love the Lord, all ye His Saints, for the Lord preserves the faithful.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves through recurring cycles rather than a simple passage from distress to relief:

1. Trust and petition (31:1–5)
2. Recollection of mercy (31:6–8)
3. Lament over grief, weakness, rejection, and conspiracy (31:9–13)
4. Renewed trust and petition (31:14–18)
5. Praise, retrospective correction, and communal exhortation (31:19–24)

The return from confidence in verses 6–8 to lament in verses 9–13 is significant: [[Faith]] here coexists with continuing [[Adversity]].

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The psalm portrays [[Prayer]] as honest petition rather than denial of suffering: the speaker names fear, grief, social abandonment, personal iniquity, and threats to life, yet continues to entrust himself to God. Verse 22 adds an important qualification — the feeling of being cut off was real ('I am cut off from before thine eyes'), but the speaker recognized in hindsight that God had heard him ('nevertheless thou heardest the voice of my supplications'). The closing counsel links [[Hope]] with courage and a strengthened heart (v.24) rather than promising the immediate removal of danger.

Verses 17-18 also ask God to shame 'the wicked' and put 'lying lips' to silence — a genuine imprecatory element that sits alongside, without being resolved by, the closing call to 'O love the LORD, all ye his saints' (v.23). This can be read as an appeal for judicial vindication against slander rather than simple personal vengeance, but an honest reading shouldn't soften or explain it away.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
A repeated contrast between hands organizes part of the prayer. God's hand receives the speaker's spirit and holds his times (31:5, 15), whereas the enemy's hand represents confinement and danger (31:8, 15). Spatial images develop the same contrast: God is rock, fortress, broad space, hidden presence, pavilion, and strong city, while danger appears as a secret net and hostile enclosure. Hostile words also function as weapons through slander, lying lips, contemptuous speech, and the "strife of tongues."
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Psalm 31:24 closely parallels Psalm 27:14, and Psalm 31:1–3 substantially overlaps Psalm 71:1–3. Psalm 31:13 also resembles Jeremiah 20:10 in its report of hostile speech and "fear on every side." These correspondences justify intertextual study but do not by themselves determine which text borrowed from another, whether a common source was used, or whether the similarities arose from shared worship language.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Literary Reuse
> The reuse of Psalm 31:5 in Luke's crucifixion narrative is strong evidence that this verse was given christological significance in early Christian scripture. It is not, by itself, proof that the original psalmist consciously predicted the crucifixion or that every part of Psalm 31 speaks directly as the Messiah.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · excellent evidentiary calibration · [[Canonical Literary Reuse in Psalm 31|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual Quotation
> Luke 23:46 has Jesus, dying on the cross, say 'Father, into thy hands I commend my spirit' - a close paraphrase of Psalm 31:5, making this one of the clearest instances of a Gospel passion narrative directly echoing the wording of a lament psalm.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted intertextual connection · [[Intertextual Quotation in Psalm 31|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Shared Idiom *(tentative)*
> The shared 'fear on every side' idiom between Psalm 31:13 and Jeremiah's confessions is suggestive of intertextual awareness but is weak evidence of direct, specific dependence, since the same Hebrew phrase (magor missabib) recurs multiple times elsewhere in Jeremiah in varied contexts, indicating it may simply have been a stock idiom of the period.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · appropriately cautious, but idiom recurs elsewhere in Jeremiah
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the psalm's alternation between confidence (vv.6-8) and renewed lament (vv.9-13) challenge assumptions that faith eliminates distress?
- What is gained or lost by reading Psalm 31:5 primarily through its later use in Luke 23:46, given that nothing internal to the psalm identifies its speaker as the Messiah?
- How should v.10's 'mine iniquity' qualify a reading of the whole psalm as direct Messianic speech?
- How should modern readers hold the imprecatory requests of vv.17-18 alongside the call to 'love the LORD, all ye his saints' (v.23) — are these in tension, or two honest sides of the same prayer?
- Does 'My times are in thy hand' (v.15) express passive resignation, active trust, or some combination of the two?
