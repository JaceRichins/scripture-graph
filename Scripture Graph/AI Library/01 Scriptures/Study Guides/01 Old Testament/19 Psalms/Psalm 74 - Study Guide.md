# Psalm 74 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 74]] · My notes: [[Psalm 74 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> O God, remember Thy chosen congregation—The wicked destroy the sanctuary and burn the synagogues—O God, remember them for their deeds, and save Thy people.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
One useful analytical outline has three movements:

1. **Complaint and questions (Psalm 74:1–11):** The congregation asks why God appears to have rejected it, recounts devastation of the sanctuary, and laments its lack of signs and prophetic knowledge.
2. **Confession and remembrance (Psalm 74:12–17):** The declaration, “For God is my King of old,” introduces recollection of ancient salvation, mastery of the sea, and the ordering of [[Creation]].
3. **Renewed petition (Psalm 74:18–23):** Commands to remember and not forget accompany appeals concerning God's name, the oppressed, the covenant, and God's own cause.

This three-part arrangement is interpretive rather than uniquely fixed; verse 11, verse 12, or verse 18 could be treated differently in a more detailed outline. Its chief value is showing how remembered divine action becomes grounds for continued petition without resolving the catastrophe.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Psalm 74 presents lament as compatible with continuing faith. The community questions God candidly while still addressing him as shepherd and King, recalling his saving power, and appealing to the [[Covenants|covenant]]. Its concern with [[Temples]] is concrete: destruction of the sanctuary is portrayed as an assault on the dwelling place of God's name. The repeated language of “thy congregation,” “thy sanctuary,” “thy name,” and “thine own cause” frames the people's injury as involving God's own relationship and reputation. Verses 20–21 place the covenant appeal beside the plight of oppressed, poor, and needy people and their hoped-for praise of God's name. None of this erases the unresolved protest with which the chapter ends.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Possessive language drives the prayer: “thy congregation,” “thine inheritance,” “thy sanctuary,” “thy name,” and “thine own cause.” Commands to “remember” and “forget not” recur in Psalm 74:2 and 18–23, binding the opening appeal to the conclusion.

The KJV also repeats “signs” in verses 4 and 9, juxtaposing enemy ensigns with the community's statement, “We see not our signs.” The English echo is certain, but intentional wordplay in the source language should not be asserted without checking the Hebrew.

Likewise, the KJV's “synagogues of God” in verse 8 should not by itself determine the psalm's date or the institutional identity of the destroyed places. The English text securely reports burned sacred gathering places; a more precise lexical or historical conclusion requires verified Hebrew evidence.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Psalm 74:13–17 gathers several images of divine sovereignty into one recollection: God divides the sea, breaks the heads of dragons and Leviathan, opens fountains, dries rivers, governs day and night, prepares light and sun, fixes the earth's borders, and makes the seasons. The sequence gives the surrounding petition a basis in remembered divine power.

Divided and dried waters may evoke [[Exodus 14]] and related deliverance traditions, while light, sun, seasons, and earthly order invite comparison with [[Genesis 1]]. Leviathan and sea imagery also invite thematic comparison with [[Isaiah 27]] and [[Psalm 89]]. These are conceptual or motif-level connections; the supplied overlap index found no qualifying verse-level overlap with Isaiah 27. The passage may emphasize creation, historical deliverance, conventional sea-combat imagery, or a deliberate combination. It does not establish direct dependence on any particular text.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Textual Ambiguity / Interpretive Dispute
> The phrases 'divide the sea' (v.13) and 'cleave the fountain and the flood... driedst up mighty rivers' (v.15) are worded ambiguously enough to evoke either the primordial ordering of chaotic waters at creation or the historical Exodus/Reed Sea and Jordan crossings; the psalm itself does not specify which is meant.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · Established interpretive ambiguity · [[Textual Ambiguity / Interpretive Dispute in Psalm 74|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Translation / Lexical Note *(tentative)*
> The underlying Hebrew term rendered 'synagogues' in v.8 is widely discussed by commentators as more plausibly meaning local 'meeting places' or shrines rather than the later institution of the synagogue; because that institution is usually associated with the Second Temple period, some scholars treat the KJV wording as a potentially anachronistic translation rather than solid evidence for dating the psalm.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · Prudent conclusion resting on unverified lexical assertions

> [!evidence] Historical Dating Dispute *(tentative)*
> Because the psalm describes a plundered and burned sanctuary but names no specific perpetrator or date, and because the term translated 'synagogues' (v.8) is itself disputed, scholars remain divided on whether Psalm 74 laments the Babylonian destruction of Solomon's Temple (traditionally 586 BCE) or a later crisis such as the Seleucid desecration of the Second Temple under Antiochus IV Epiphanes (2nd century BCE); the text alone does not settle the question.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Historical occasion disputed; proposed balance is insufficiently sourced

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the recollection of God's ancient works begin only after an extended complaint?
- What changes when the community describes its injury as reproach against God's own name and cause?
- Does “there is no more any prophet” describe institutional absence, lack of an accessible oracle, or the worshippers' experience of unanswered uncertainty?
- How do “for ever,” “how long,” “remember,” and “forget not” shape the prayer's emotional movement?
- Do the sea and river images primarily evoke creation, deliverance, or a deliberate fusion of both?
- What does the expression rendered “synagogues of God” denote, and what additional lexical evidence would be needed before using it to date the psalm?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
