# Psalm 118 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 118]] · My notes: [[Psalm 118 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> A messianic psalm—Let all Israel say of the Lord, His mercy endures forever—The Stone that the builders refused is become the headstone of the corner—Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Communal summons** (vv.1-4) — mercy declared generally in v.1, then addressed successively to Israel, "the house of Aaron," and "them that fear the LORD" (vv.2-4).
2. **Individual testimony of deliverance** (vv.5-18) — distress, enemies "compassed about," and rescue, climaxing in "I shall not die, but live" (v.17) and chastening that stopped short of death (v.18).
3. **Entrance and thanksgiving** (vv.19-21) — request for admission through "the gates of righteousness" and praise for being answered.
4. **Reversal and communal exclamation** (vv.22-24) — the stone the builders refused becomes the head of the corner; "the day which the LORD hath made."
5. **Blessing and sacrifice** (vv.25-27) — petition ("Save now"), blessing of the one who comes, and a rite at the altar.
6. **Closing praise** (vv.28-29) — personal praise returning to the opening refrain, closing the inclusio.

The division of speaking parts (who says vv.19-20, 26-27) is not marked in the text and remains a scholarly reconstruction, not a stated fact.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Covenant mercy.** The refrain "his mercy endureth for ever," repeated five times, frames the whole psalm and reflects covenant loyalty (Hebrew *chesed*) as the basis for praise; see [[Justice and Mercy]].

**Priesthood in view.** Verse 3 names "the house of Aaron" as a distinct group called to praise—an explicit textual anchor within Israelite worship, though this verse alone does not establish that the whole psalm originated as Temple liturgy.

**Prayer answered, suffering bounded.** The speaker calls on the LORD in distress and is answered (v.5); v.18 distinguishes severe chastening from being "given over unto death"—suffering under God's hand that stops short of destruction, without the text explaining its purpose. See [[Prayer]] and [[Adversity]].

**Christ as the rejected stone.** Verse 22's image of a stone refused by builders yet made head of the corner is applied by Jesus to himself (Matthew 21 and parallels) and by Peter to identify Jesus before the Sanhedrin (Acts 4)—a central New Testament type, distinct from (and not proof of) what the psalm's original author intended. See [[Salvation]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Repetition supplies much of the psalm’s force: “his mercy endureth for ever” frames and opens the poem; “they compassed me about” intensifies the threat; “the right hand of the LORD” emphasizes divine action; and “in the name of the LORD” accompanies the speaker’s response to surrounding enemies. The shifts among “I,” “we,” and addressed groups create a movement between individual testimony and communal worship, although they do not prove how many speakers performed the psalm.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The psalm builds through repetition rather than narrative development: the mercy refrain frames vv.1-4 and v.29 (with a small variation—"because" in v.1 becomes "for" in v.29); "compassed me about" recurs four times across vv.10-12 with escalating imagery (nations generally, then a bee-simile, then briefly-burning thorn-fire), each clause closing with "in the name of the LORD I will destroy them"; and "the right hand of the LORD" and "I will praise thee... I will exalt thee" are each doubled (vv.15-16, 28). Verse 14 ("The LORD is my strength and song, and is become my salvation") echoes Exodus 15:2 and Isaiah 12:2, though the overlap is a shared phrase rather than a verbatim full-verse match, and the direction of borrowing, if any, isn't recoverable from the wording alone. The rejected-stone image in v.22 works by simple reversal—what builders discard becomes structurally indispensable—echoing the psalm's other reversal, chastening that stops short of death (v.18).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Intertextual Reception
> New Testament writers apply Psalm 118:22’s rejected-stone image to Jesus. This is strong evidence for an early Christian messianic reading of the verse, although it does not by itself settle the metaphor’s original historical referent.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted · [[Canonical Intertextual Reception in Psalm 118|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Canonical Intertextual Reception
> The acclamation from Psalm 118:26 is used in the Gospel accounts in connection with Jesus’ entry into Jerusalem and is also placed on Jesus’ lips in sayings about his future recognition. This establishes important Christian reception of the verse but does not eliminate a prior liturgical sense.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted

> [!evidence] Form-Critical / Genre Analysis *(tentative)*
> Form-critical readings commonly classify Psalm 118 as an individual todah (thanksgiving) psalm, and many scholars suggest the speaking 'I' — surrounded by 'all nations,' delivered from mortal distress, and processing to the temple gates to give thanks — represents a king reporting deliverance from battle on behalf of the whole community, though the speaker's identity is never named in the text itself and remains debated.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, uncited

> [!evidence] Textual Overlap *(tentative)*
> Psalm 118:14 ('The LORD is my strength and song, and is become my salvation') closely echoes the wording of the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15:2: 'the LORD is my strength and song, and he is become my salvation') and is nearly reproduced again in Isaiah's thanksgiving song (Isaiah 12:2). This near-verbatim shared line links Psalm 118's deliverance-thanksgiving to Israel's foundational Exodus deliverance song and to Isaiah's eschatological song of praise.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · accepted observation, disputed significance

> [!evidence] Liturgical-Historical Inference *(tentative)*
> Psalm 118 concludes the 'Egyptian Hallel' (Psalms 113-118), traditionally sung at Passover. Because the Gospels record that Jesus and the Twelve 'sang an hymn' at the close of the Last Supper (a Passover meal) before departing to the Mount of Olives, many scholars and Jewish/Christian liturgical traditions identify that hymn as the Hallel, meaning Psalm 118 may be among the last words Jesus sang before his arrest.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · plausible tradition, not stated in text

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Who is the first-person speaker—an individual king reporting deliverance from battle, a representative of Israel, or a personification of the covenant community? The psalm has no superscription or historical heading to settle this.
- Does the shift from first-person testimony (vv.5-21) to communal exclamation (vv.22-24) indicate a change of speaker (e.g., priests responding to an individual's testimony), or is it the same voice broadening into communal language?
- Who speaks the blessing in v.26, and who is the figure "that cometh in the name of the LORD"?
- What ritual action is intended by binding the sacrifice "unto the horns of the altar" (v.27)? The text is genuinely ambiguous between a bound sacrificial victim and a festal procession with branches.
- If Psalm 118 was part of the Hallel traditionally sung at the Last Supper, how might its imagery have resonated on the night of Jesus' betrayal? The Gospels do not state which hymn was sung.
<!-- SG:END questions -->
