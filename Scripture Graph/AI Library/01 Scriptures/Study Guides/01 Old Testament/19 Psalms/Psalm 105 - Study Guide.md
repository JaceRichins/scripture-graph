# Psalm 105 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 105]] · My notes: [[Psalm 105 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Make the Lord’s doings known among all men—Show His covenant with Abraham and His dealings with Israel—Touch not His anointed, and do His prophets no harm—Israel is to observe His statutes and keep His laws.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Call to worship and remembrance (vv. 1–6):** Give thanks, proclaim, sing, seek, rejoice, and remember.
2. **The patriarchal covenant (vv. 7–15):** The promise of Canaan accompanies a small and vulnerable migrant family.
3. **Joseph and providence (vv. 16–22):** Famine, enslavement, testing, release, and elevation prepare for the family's survival.
4. **Egypt and deliverance (vv. 23–38):** Israel multiplies, hostility arises, Moses and Aaron are sent, and judgments fall on Egypt.
5. **Wilderness provision and land (vv. 39–44):** Cloud, fire, food, water, and inheritance follow because God remembers the promise.
6. **Purpose and praise (v. 45):** Deliverance is directed toward covenant faithfulness.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Abrahamic Covenant]] and [[Exodus and Deliverance]] as one connected story. God's promise to the patriarchs is not forgotten when circumstances turn toward famine, slavery, or oppression. Yet the conclusion prevents covenant grace from being reduced to privilege: the people are delivered and given land so that they may live in [[Obedience]].

The treatment of Joseph also offers a careful theology of providence. Calling Joseph one whom God 'sent' does not erase the human act of selling him; it interprets the outcome as serving a purpose beyond the perpetrators' intent. Likewise, the strong language of divine causation in verse 25 affirms sovereignty but does not itself settle philosophical questions about agency or excuse the oppressors.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The repeated verb **remember** joins worship and covenant: Israel must remember God's works (v. 5) because God remembers his covenant and promise (vv. 8, 42). The long central recital is therefore enclosed by two kinds of faithful memory.

The opening of [[Psalm 105]] closely parallels [[1 Chronicles 16]], showing that this language belonged to Israel's worship traditions. The direction of dependence is uncertain. The plague catalogue is also deliberately selective: it rearranges the Exodus sequence, placing darkness before blood. That poetic ordering cautions against treating the psalm as a replacement for the fuller narrative.

Reading [[Psalm 106]] alongside this chapter gives a fuller theological balance. Psalm 105 foregrounds divine fidelity; Psalm 106 foregrounds Israel's sin and forgetfulness. Each emphasis is real, but neither should be mistaken for the whole biblical account.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Parallel
> Psalm 105:1-15 has a substantial close parallel in 1 Chronicles 16:8-22. This is strong evidence of a literary or liturgical relationship, although the parallel alone cannot determine direction of borrowing, precise date, or authorship.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · verified textual relationship; direction disputed · [[Textual Parallel in Psalm 105|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Reception And Communal Memory
> The psalm is strong evidence that the patriarchal, Joseph, exodus, wilderness, and land traditions functioned together as a covenant-centered account of Israel's identity in the community that used this hymn. As retrospective poetry, it is not independent contemporary documentation of every narrated event.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strong evidence for received communal tradition · [[Reception And Communal Memory in Psalm 105|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Ambiguity *(tentative)*
> The rock-striking episode in v.41 ('He opened the rock, and the waters gushed out') matches details present in two distinct wilderness narratives — Exodus 17:1-7 at Rephidim early in the journey and Numbers 20:2-13 at Kadesh near its end — without the psalm specifying which (or both) is meant.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · referent uncertain; Exodus 17 somewhat likelier

> [!evidence] Source-Critical *(tentative)*
> The direction of literary dependence between Psalm 105 and 1 Chronicles 16 (which text is prior, or whether both draw on a shared earlier liturgical source) is unresolved in scholarship, bearing on how the near-verbatim overlap (c1) should be interpreted historically.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · direction not established by overlap; scholarly distribution unsourced

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the command to remember God's works differ from nostalgia or mere historical recall?
- What changes when Joseph's enslavement is narrated both as a human sale and as divine sending?
- Why does the poem rearrange and omit elements of the plague narrative?
- How should verse 25's strong claim about divine causation be held together with human accountability?
- What does verse 45 imply about the responsibilities attached to covenant gifts?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
