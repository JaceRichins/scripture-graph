# Psalm 107 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 107]] · My notes: [[Psalm 107 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Psalm 107 opens Book V of the Psalter with a call to give thanks for the LORD's enduring mercy (v.1) and closes with an invitation for "whoso is wise" to understand his lovingkindness (v.43), framing everything between as evidence of that mercy. Between these bookends, four scenes portray different kinds of distress — desert wanderers who find no city, prisoners bound in darkness, the gravely afflicted near death's gates, and storm-tossed sailors — each following the same pattern: distress, a cry to the LORD, deliverance, and the refrain "Oh that men would praise the LORD for his goodness, and for his wonderful works to the children of men!" (vv.8, 15, 21, 31). The psalm does not assign the same cause to every distress: rebellion and iniquity are named for the prisoners and the afflicted (vv.11, 17), but no fault is charged against the wanderers or the sailors. A closing meditation (vv.33-43) widens the lens to divine reversals of land and social fortune — fruitful ground turned to wilderness and back, princes brought low and the poor lifted up — inviting reflection on [[Gratitude]], [[Adversity]], and [[Justice and Mercy]] as governing themes of the whole psalm.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious structural outline is:

1. **Call of the gathered redeemed (vv. 1–3):** thanksgiving grounded in the LORD's goodness and mercy.
2. **Wanderers (vv. 4–9):** hunger and homelessness give way to guidance, habitation, and satisfaction.
3. **Prisoners (vv. 10–16):** rebellion and bondage give way to broken bands and opened barriers.
4. **The afflicted (vv. 17–22):** iniquity and near-fatal illness give way to healing and thanksgiving.
5. **Mariners (vv. 23–32):** a divinely raised storm gives way to calm, safe arrival, and public praise.
6. **Providential reversals (vv. 33–42):** fertile and barren lands, rulers and the poor, exchange conditions.
7. **Wisdom conclusion (v. 43):** understanding comes through attentive observation of these patterns.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The psalm's repeated refrain models [[Gratitude]] as the fitting response to deliverance — the redeemed are urged to "say so" (v.2), to "sacrifice the sacrifices of thanksgiving" (v.22), and to praise "in the congregation... and... assembly of the elders" (v.32), making thanksgiving a public and communal act rather than private relief. The fourfold "cried unto the LORD in their trouble" formula (vv.6, 13, 19, 28) models [[Prayer]] as an appeal made from real extremity. [[Adversity]] takes different forms across the four scenes, and the psalm resists a single explanation for suffering: rebellion and iniquity are explicitly named as the cause for the prisoners and the afflicted (vv.11, 17), but the wanderers and sailors are not charged with wrongdoing. The closing verses (33-42), which turn fruitful land to wasteland for wickedness and then wasteland to fruitful land for the hungry poor, and which bring down princes while raising the poor, extend the theme into [[Justice and Mercy]] — judgment and restoration flowing from the same divine hand. Verses 2-3 also touch [[Gathering of Israel]], though the psalm itself does not name Israel or a specific historical gathering.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The opening term "mercy" and the concluding "lovingkindness" form a semantic frame around the rescue narratives in this English text. Repeated vocabulary—trouble, distresses, crying, deliverance, goodness, and wonderful works—binds otherwise different scenes together.

The references to the "shadow of death" and "gates of death" occur within accounts of bondage and illness. In context, they evoke danger and proximity to death rather than offering an explicit teaching about [[Death and the Spirit World]].
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The psalm's central literary device is the fourfold refrain (vv.8, 15, 21, 31) paired with a near-identical "cried unto the LORD in their trouble" formula that resolves each scene (vv.6, 13, 19, 28) — deliberate repetition that binds four otherwise distinct vignettes into one liturgical pattern. Its opening formula, "O give thanks unto the LORD... his mercy endureth for ever," is shared worship vocabulary also found (with minor variation) at the head of [[Psalm 106]], [[Psalm 118]], and [[Psalm 136]], and in the worship texts of [[1 Chronicles 16]], [[2 Chronicles 5]], and [[2 Chronicles 7]] — the psalm draws on common liturgical language rather than coining its own. Within the coda, verses 33-38 pair a reversal (fertile land to wilderness for wickedness, then wilderness to fertile land for the hungry) that shows the same divine power used for both judgment and restoration. The storm scene (vv.23-30) has a well-known thematic parallel in [[Matthew 8]], where Jesus calms a storm endangering a boat's occupants; the resemblance in scene and image is real, but storm-calmed-by-divine-power is also a widespread ancient motif, and no verified textual evidence shows a deliberate literary link between the two passages — worth noting for study, but not to be overstated as intentional allusion.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why are moral causes supplied for the prisoners and afflicted, but not for the wanderers and mariners?
- What forms of public [[Gratitude]] follow deliverance in verses 22 and 32?
- How do the reversals in verses 33–42 complicate assumptions that present prosperity or rank is permanent?
- What does verse 43 mean by observing these things, and how is observation related to wisdom?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
