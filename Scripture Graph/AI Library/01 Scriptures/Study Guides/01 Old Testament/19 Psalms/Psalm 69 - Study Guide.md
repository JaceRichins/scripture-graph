# Psalm 69 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Psalm 69]] · My notes: [[Psalm 69 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> A messianic psalm of David—The zeal of the Lord’s house has eaten Him up—Reproach has broken His heart—He is given gall and vinegar to drink—He is persecuted—He will save Zion.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline of the psalm's movement:

- **Verses 1-4:** drowning imagery (mire, deep waters) and wrongful hostility from enemies.
- **Verses 5-12:** confession of personal sin, concern for fellow worshippers, and reproach tied to devotion, [[Fasting]], and zeal for God's house.
- **Verses 13-21:** renewed petition grounded in mercy and lovingkindness, returning to the mire/water imagery of the opening, culminating in a broken heart and the gall-and-vinegar treatment.
- **Verses 22-28:** imprecations invoked against the persecutors.
- **Verses 29-33:** a transitional vow of praise and thanksgiving, better than an animal offering, with hope extended to the humble and poor.
- **Verses 34-36:** cosmic praise and communal restoration in Zion and Judah.

This outline describes shifts in subject and tone; it does not prove a formal, deliberate compositional scheme.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The prayer combines confession and confidence: God knows the speaker's sins (verse 5), yet the speaker still appeals to divine mercy and salvation (verses 13–18). Verses 30–31 value [[Gratitude]] expressed through song above an animal offering, but the wording is comparative and should not be expanded into a blanket rejection of [[Sacrifice]]. The imprecations place judgment in God's hands, relevant to [[Justice and Mercy]], while their severity remains an ethical problem that should not be minimized.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The water metaphor frames the main plea: the speaker sinks in mire and deep water in verses 1–2, then asks to be taken out of mire and protected from the flood in verses 14–15. Reversal also shapes the poem. A table meant for welfare becomes a trap, public shame is answered by public thanksgiving, and one sufferer's lament expands into hope for a community and its descendants.

The New Testament reception is substantial. [[John 2]] and [[Romans 15]] apply the two halves of verse 9 to Jesus; [[Romans 11]] reuses verses 22–23; [[Acts 1]] adapts verse 25; and the gall-and-vinegar language of verse 21 parallels [[Matthew 27]] and [[John 19]]. This strongly supports a Christian christological or typological reading of portions of the psalm. It does not demonstrate that every verse is direct messianic prediction.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How can the speaker be wrongfully persecuted (v.4) while also confessing sins (v.5)? The simplest reading is relative innocence concerning the present accusation rather than absolute sinlessness.
- Does the New Testament's citation of specific verses (9, 21, 22-23, 25) but never verse 5 reflect deliberate editorial care, or simply that v.5 was not doctrinally useful to apply to Jesus?
- Is the "hated without a cause" language in John 15:25 drawing on Psalm 69:4 or the nearly identical Psalm 35:19 — and does the ambiguity matter for how either psalm's afterlife is read?
- Do verses 22-28 express personal desire for revenge, a judicial appeal, or covenantal curse language? Addressing the requests to God supports a judicial-appeal reading but does not remove their severity.
- Does the communal ending (vv.34-36) arise organically from the individual's anticipated deliverance, or reflect later communal use or editing? The chapter alone cannot settle this.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the wording and surrounding arguments of Psalm 69:9 with [[John 2]] and [[Romans 15]], and Psalm 69:22–25 with [[Romans 11]] and [[Acts 1]]. For verse 21, compare all four passion narratives rather than assuming that every mention of a bitter drink has exactly the same wording or narrative function. Psalm 35:19 should also be considered when evaluating the source of the “without a cause” language in [[John 15]].
