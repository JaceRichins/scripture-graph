# Genesis 35 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Genesis 35]] · My notes: [[Genesis 35 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> God sends Jacob to Bethel, where he builds an altar and the Lord appears to him—God renews the promise that Jacob will be a great nation and that his name will be Israel—Jacob sets up an altar and pours a drink offering—Rachel bears Benjamin, dies in childbirth, and is buried near Bethlehem—Reuben sins with Bilhah—Isaac dies and is buried by Jacob and Esau.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful reading outline is:

1. **Return and preparation (vv. 1–5):** command to go to Beth-el, removal of foreign gods, cleansing, and protected departure.
2. **Worship at Beth-el (vv. 6–8):** altar construction and Deborah's burial.
3. **Covenant renewal (vv. 9–15):** the name Israel, promises of nations and land, and Jacob's pillar and offerings.
4. **Journey, birth, and death (vv. 16–21):** Benjamin's birth and Rachel's death.
5. **Household crisis and genealogy (vv. 22–26):** Reuben's offense and the list of twelve sons.
6. **Generational closure (vv. 27–29):** Jacob reaches Isaac, and Jacob and Esau bury their father.

This progression makes the chapter both a return narrative and a family-transition narrative.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Covenant promise and response:** God renews ancestral promises of fruitfulness, nations, kings, descendants, and land (vv. 9-12). Jacob responds to God's command by returning to Beth-el, directing the removal of foreign gods, building an altar, and marking the encounter with a pillar, drink offering, and oil (vv. 1-7, 14). The narrative therefore places divine promise alongside human response without presenting the promise as earned.
- **Preparation for worship:** The household relinquishes foreign gods, cleanses itself, and changes garments before traveling to Beth-el (vv. 2-4). This sequence supports a reading of preparation and renunciation, though the text does not describe each person's inward state or explain the earrings.
- **Promise amid [[Adversity]]:** Covenant renewal does not remove mortality or family disorder. Rachel's death and Reuben's offense follow the blessing (vv. 16-22), so divine promise and severe family suffering coexist within the narrative.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
- **El-beth-el** (v. 7) is associated by the narrative with the God who appeared to Jacob there during his flight. The verse names the place, and finer claims about whether the compound name emphasizes God over the location remain interpretive.
- **Allon-bachuth** (v. 8) is conventionally understood as "oak of weeping," fitting Deborah's burial notice.
- **Ben-oni** and **Benjamin** (v. 18) preserve Rachel's and Jacob's different names for the child. "Son of my sorrow" and "son of the right hand" are common renderings, but Benjamin has also been associated with "son of the south," and the text does not explain Jacob's motive.
- **Israel** is given to Jacob again in verse 10 after the earlier naming in [[Genesis 32]]. Genesis 35 does not itself explain the name's meaning or the reason for the repetition.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The strongest literary connection is with [[Genesis 28]]. That chapter records Jacob's earlier encounter at Beth-el, an anointed stone, his vow, and his hope of returning in peace. Genesis 35 supplies the return, another divine appearance, an altar, a pillar, oil, and renewed naming of the place. In the received narrative, the scenes form substantial counterparts around Jacob's years away, whether their relationship reflects unified composition, editorial shaping, or inherited traditions.

Two pillars preserve different memories within Genesis 35. The pillar in verse 14 marks the place where God spoke and receives a drink offering and oil; the pillar in verse 20 marks Rachel's grave.

Reuben's offense is placed immediately before the genealogy identifies him as the firstborn. [[Genesis 49]] later recalls the offense when declaring that he will not excel, making the juxtaposition consequential within the larger narrative.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Source Criticism / Doublet *(tentative)*
> The duplicate naming of Jacob as Israel (Genesis 32:28 and 35:10) is frequently cited in source-critical (documentary hypothesis) scholarship as a 'doublet' suggesting the combination of separate underlying traditions in Genesis, though a unified/theological reading treats it as an intentional reaffirmation of the covenant name at a second major theophany.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Textual doublet accepted; source-critical attribution disputed and unsourced

> [!evidence] Literary Structure *(tentative)*
> Genesis 35 exhibits a coherent return-and-transition pattern: command and preparation, arrival and worship at Beth-el, covenant renewal, renewed travel, births and deaths, a complete list of sons, and reunion with and burial of Isaac. This organization supports reading the chapter as a deliberately shaped transition from Jacob's journeys toward the next generation of the family narrative.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · Outline accepted; deliberate chapter-wide shaping remains interpretive
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the household's removal of foreign gods shape the return to Beth-el? What supports reading it as [[Repentance]], and what remains unstated about inward conversion?
- Why does Genesis 35 repeat the name Israel after [[Genesis 32]]? Could the repetition function as covenant reaffirmation in the received narrative, preserve distinct traditions, or do both?
- How did Deborah, identified as Rebekah's nurse, come to be among Jacob's people when the intervening narrative does not explain her presence?
- Why is Reuben's act recorded without an immediate response, although [[Genesis 49]] later gives it serious consequences?
- Does the Padan-aram summary in verse 26 generalize the family's origin, or does it preserve an unresolved tension with Benjamin's birth near Ephrath in verses 16-19?
- How do the deaths of Deborah, Rachel, and Isaac contribute to the chapter's generational transition?
