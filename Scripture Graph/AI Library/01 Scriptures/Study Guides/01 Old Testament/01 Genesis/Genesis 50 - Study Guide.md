# Genesis 50 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jacob’s body is embalmed—Joseph buries him in Canaan—Joseph comforts his brothers—The children of Israel multiply—Joseph promises that God will bring Israel out of Egypt into Canaan—Joseph dies in Egypt and is embalmed.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Jacob mourned and embalmed (vv. 1–3).** Joseph's private grief is followed by Egyptian embalming and seventy days of public mourning.
2. **Journey and burial in Canaan (vv. 4–14).** Joseph asks Pharaoh's household to relay his request; Pharaoh grants it on the basis of Joseph's earlier oath ([[Genesis 47]]). A large company—Egyptian officials, chariots, and horsemen, plus Joseph's household—travels to Canaan, mourns seven days at Atad (naming it Abel-mizraim), and buries Jacob at Machpelah as he instructed in [[Genesis 49]].
3. **Fear and reconciliation (vv. 15–21).** With Jacob dead, the brothers fear retaliation and relay what they say was his dying request for forgiveness — a claim the narrative never independently confirms. Joseph weeps, declines to stand in God's place, and reframes their betrayal: they intended evil, but God meant it for good, to preserve life.
4. **Joseph's final years and death (vv. 22–26).** Joseph sees his great-grandchildren, predicts Israel's departure from Egypt, binds his family by oath to carry his bones to the promised land, and dies at 110, embalmed and coffined in Egypt.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Joseph's response illustrates [[Justice and Mercy]] without erasing moral responsibility. He calls his brothers' purpose evil (Genesis 50:20), yet does not retaliate and instead provides for their households (Genesis 50:21). His statement about God's good intention offers a theology of providence, but the wording should not be expanded into the stronger claim that every evil act is directly caused or morally approved by God.

Joseph's final command is rooted in the [[Abrahamic Covenant]]. His confidence that Israel will leave Egypt links the close of Genesis to [[Exodus and Deliverance]]. [[Hebrews 11]] later interprets this confidence and the command concerning his bones as faith.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two burials frame the chapter but point opposite directions: Jacob dies in Egypt and is carried immediately to the ancestral burial place in Canaan, while Joseph also dies in Egypt but remains there, bound only by an oath about a future removal — a thread resumed in [[Exodus 13]] and reported complete in [[Joshua 24]]. The naming of Abel-mizraim (50:11) is a characteristic Genesis place-name wordplay tied to the Hebrew for mourning, though the underlying event's precise location ('beyond Jordan') is geographically ambiguous and cannot be pinned down from the text alone. The brothers' fearful self-abasement before Joseph also recalls their earlier plot against him in [[Genesis 37]], giving the Joseph story an arc from betrayal toward reconciliation — though the text doesn't supply enough repeated language to call this a formal literary device rather than ordinary narrative resolution.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Genesis 50:16–17 reports the brothers' claim that Jacob commanded Joseph to forgive them, but no earlier verse records Jacob giving that instruction. Was it genuine, embellished, or invented from fear? The text does not decide.
- What does Joseph mean by asking 'am I in the place of God?' (50:19) — that judgment belongs to God, that the past cannot be undone, or both?
- How does Genesis 50:20 hold together human evil intent and divine good purpose without excusing the evil or claiming God caused it?
- Why does Genesis end with Joseph's body still in Egypt rather than with Israel's return to Canaan?
- What does it signify that Machir's children were 'brought up upon Joseph's knees' (50:23)? The idiom could mean formal adoption, or simply close, affectionate reception into the family.
<!-- SG:END questions -->
