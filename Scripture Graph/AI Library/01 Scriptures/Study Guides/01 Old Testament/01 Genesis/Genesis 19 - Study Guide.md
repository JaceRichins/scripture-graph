## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's clearest theological pairing is [[Justice and Mercy]]. Judgment is linked to the city's great cry and iniquity (19:13, 15), while Lot's removal is explicitly linked to divine mercy (19:16) and to God remembering Abraham (19:29). Lot's rescue is not portrayed as a reward for flawless conduct: he offers his daughters to the crowd and later lingers after receiving the warning. The chapter therefore supports a distinction between receiving mercy and having every recorded action endorsed.

The account also treats warning as urgent but not coercively self-executing. The sons in law dismiss Lot's warning, Lot hesitates, and his wife violates the instruction not to look back. The differing outcomes invite study of [[Agency]] and [[Obedience]], while the visitors' intervention prevents Lot's hesitation from being the whole story.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
- **'that we may know them' (19:5)** — the Hebrew yada carries sexual connotation elsewhere in Genesis (e.g., 4:1), supporting the reading that the mob's demand was for sexual violence against the guests.
- **Zoar** — the text supplies its own folk etymology, deriving the name from the city being 'a little one' (19:20, 22); the city was earlier called Bela (cf. Genesis 14:2).
- **Moab / Ben-ammi** — the names given to Lot's grandsons carry etiological force, tied by later tradition to their circumstances of birth ('from father,' 'son of my people'), functioning as an origin story for two nations who become recurring adversaries of Israel.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The strongest canonical narrative parallel is [[Judges 19]]. Both stories feature travelers needing lodging, a host protecting a male guest, townsmen surrounding the house, and women offered to the crowd. Judges 19 ends in the brutal abuse and death of the concubine, whereas Genesis 19 interrupts the attack through angelic action. The resemblance is too extensive to be merely a shared word or isolated motif, but it does not by itself prove direct literary borrowing.

The closing formulas identifying Moab and the children of Ammon give verses 30–38 an etiological function: they explain the origins of neighboring peoples through an account that is profoundly unflattering to their ancestors. Recognizing that function does not settle whether the narrator also intended the episode as history.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> The naming notices for Moab and Ben-ammi give the cave episode an etiological function: the story explains, from the narrator's perspective, the origins of the neighboring Moabite and Ammonite peoples.

> [!evidence] Literary Parallel
> Genesis 19 and Judges 19 share a close narrative cluster: travelers receive lodging, townsmen surround the house and demand the male guest, a host offers women instead, and the threatened violence occurs at the doorway. The outcomes differ sharply, but the parallel is substantial.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-established, appropriately hedged on dependence · [[Literary Parallel in Genesis 19|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Philological/Contextual
> The verb 'know' (Hebrew yada) carries a demonstrable sexual sense elsewhere in Genesis (e.g., 'Adam knew Eve his wife,' Genesis 4:1), and the narrative context here (a threatening mob, Lot's counter-offer of his daughters 'which have not known man') strongly supports reading the mob's demand as intent toward sexual assault of the guests rather than mere interrogation.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · broadly accepted, properly hedged · [[Philological/Contextual in Genesis 19|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Theological Speculation *(tentative)*
> Genesis 19:24 states that 'the LORD rained upon Sodom and upon Gomorrah brimstone and fire from the LORD out of heaven,' a doubled reference to 'the LORD' that some ancient and modern Jewish interpreters have read as evidence for a 'two powers in heaven' concept (one LORD acting on earth, another in heaven).
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · genuine minority tradition, needs sourcing

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical/Literary *(tentative)*
> Many scholars read Genesis 19:30-38 as an Israelite etiological and polemical narrative — its wordplay on 'Moab' (popularly linked to Hebrew for 'from father') and the drunken-incest origin story would have functioned to disparage and delegitimize neighboring rival nations rather than to preserve neutral historical memory.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed, plausible

> [!evidence] Intertextual Tension *(tentative)*
> The New Testament elsewhere calls Lot 'just' and 'righteous,' vexed in his soul by the wickedness around him, which sits in tension with his willingness to surrender his daughters to a violent mob in this chapter; the biblical text nowhere resolves this tension.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · real tension, absolutist framing

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Genesis 19:16 attributes Lot's removal to mercy, while 19:29 says God remembered Abraham. How do these two explanations function together?
- Why does the narrator report Lot's offer of his daughters without an explicit editorial judgment? What prevents narration from being mistaken for endorsement?
- Does the daughters' statement that there was no man “in the earth” reflect literal belief, geographical isolation, trauma after the catastrophe, or narrative exaggeration?
- How do [[Ezekiel 16]], [[Jude 1]], and [[Luke 17]] develop different aspects of the Sodom tradition without making any one later emphasis an exhaustive interpretation of Genesis 19?
