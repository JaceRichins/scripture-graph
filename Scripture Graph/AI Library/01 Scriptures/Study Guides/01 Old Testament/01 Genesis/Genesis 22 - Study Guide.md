# Genesis 22 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Genesis 22]] · My notes: [[Genesis 22 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Genesis 22 contains two connected movements. Verses 1–19 narrate Abraham's test: he is commanded to offer Isaac, proceeds toward the act, is stopped by a heavenly messenger, and offers a ram in Isaac's place. The ensuing oath reiterates the [[Abrahamic Covenant]]. Verses 20–24 introduce Rebekah through Nahor's genealogy, preparing for [[Genesis 24]].

The narrative explicitly demonstrates Abraham's [[Obedience]], but it does not explicitly describe Isaac's consent, emotions, or theological understanding. Claims that “both father and son yield” therefore go beyond the chapter's direct narration unless presented as interpretation.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The test announced (22:1–2):** The narrator explicitly frames the command as a test of Abraham (KJV: "tempt"), and Isaac is described with mounting intensity — "thy son," "thine only son," "whom thou lovest" — before the command to sacrifice him.
2. **Journey and preparation (22:3–8):** Abraham acts immediately and without recorded objection; a three-day journey (22:4) separates the command from arrival, and Isaac's question about the missing lamb ("God will provide himself a lamb," 22:8) goes unanswered in the moment.
3. **Binding and intervention (22:9–12):** Abraham builds the altar, binds Isaac, and reaches for the knife before the angel halts him — the stop command (22:12) comes before any substitute is found.
4. **Substitute and place-name (22:13–14):** A ram, not the lamb Abraham had spoken of, is offered "in the stead of his son" (22:13), and Abraham names the site Jehovah-jireh. The KJV glosses the name only as "In the mount of the LORD it shall be seen" (22:14) — the "the LORD will provide" sense comes from the Hebrew name itself rather than a separate textual statement, so verses 8 and 14 form a suggestive thematic echo, not a formally marked inclusio.
5. **Oath and covenant reaffirmation (22:15–18):** A second angelic address delivers a self-sworn oath ("By myself have I sworn," 22:16) restating and intensifying the promises of posterity and blessing, explicitly grounded in Abraham's obedience (22:18).
6. **Return and genealogical bridge (22:19–24):** Abraham returns to Beer-sheba with the young men — Isaac is not named in this verse, though nothing elsewhere suggests he did not return — and the chapter closes with Nahor's genealogy, whose climactic notice of Rebekah's birth (22:23) points forward to [[Genesis 24]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Faith]], [[Obedience]], and [[Sacrifice]] without collapsing them into one idea: Abraham's conduct is read by the angel as fear of God and a refusal to withhold his son (22:12), and the closing promise explicitly grounds itself in Abraham having "obeyed my voice" (22:18). The reaffirmed [[Abrahamic Covenant]] is sealed with a divine self-oath — "By myself have I sworn" (22:16) — which Hebrews 6:13 explains as the strongest possible form of promise, since God could swear by no greater being than himself. This passage ties that particular blessing to Abraham's tested obedience; it should not be read as converting the entire Abrahamic covenant into a strictly conditional grant, since earlier unconditional promises (Genesis 15, 17) are not renegotiated here.

The test is exceptional and is explicitly halted before Isaac's death; it is not a warrant for human sacrifice, a point reinforced by later biblical law (Deuteronomy 12:31) and prophetic condemnation (Jeremiah 7:31) of child sacrifice.

Latter-day Saint and New Testament writers later read Abraham's willingness to offer his "only son" as illuminating God's gift of Christ. Jacob 4:5 makes this comparison explicit ("a similitude of God and his Only Begotten Son"). Hebrews 11:17-19 and Romans 8:32 supply related but less direct language without themselves asserting the Abraham-God parallel as explicitly as Jacob 4:5 does. This typological reading is a later, inspired interpretive layer; Genesis 22 itself names no Messiah and documents only the testing of one man's faith.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV's 'God did tempt Abraham' (22:1) uses 'tempt' in its older sense of 'test' or 'prove' — consistent with the angel's later statement 'now I know that thou fearest God' (22:12), which frames the episode as a trial of faith rather than an enticement to evil. The repeated phrase 'thine only son' (22:2, 12, 16) cannot mean Abraham's only biological son, since Ishmael had already been born (see [[Genesis 21]]); it is best read as marking Isaac's unique status as the son of promise through Sarah. 'Jehovah-jireh' (22:14) combines the divine name with a verb of seeing/providing, which the text glosses as 'In the mount of the LORD it shall be seen' — the 'the LORD will provide' sense is carried by the name itself rather than stated separately in the verse. Abraham's statement that he and Isaac will 'come again' (22:5) is grammatically a claim that both will return, but Genesis does not explain whether this reflects confidence, concealment, or reassurance; Hebrews 11:17-19 later supplies a resurrection-faith reading, which is an interpretation layered onto the text rather than something 22:5 states outright.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The dialogue in 22:7-8 carries dramatic irony: Isaac asks where the sacrificial lamb is, and Abraham's answer proves true in an unexpected way, since what actually appears and is offered is a ram (22:13), not a lamb. Three restrained repetitions structure the episode: Abraham answers 'here am I' to God, Isaac, and the angel in turn (22:1, 7, 11); Abraham and Isaac go 'both of them together' both before and after Isaac's unanswered question (22:6, 8); and Abraham 'lifted up his eyes' both to see the distant mountain and later to see the ram (22:4, 13) — though this phrase is a common idiom elsewhere in Genesis (e.g., 18:2, 24:63-64), so its recurrence here may reflect ordinary narrative convention as much as deliberate design. Some later readers have also drawn a typological parallel between Isaac carrying the wood for his own sacrifice (22:6) and later sacrificial imagery; this is a retrospective interpretive observation, not something the Genesis narrator comments on directly. The closing genealogy shifts style abruptly, but its climactic naming of Rebekah (22:23) gives it a clear forward-looking function toward [[Genesis 24]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the text call Isaac Abraham's 'only son' (22:2) when Ishmael, born in Genesis 16, was still alive? What might that phrase be doing in context?
- What ethical difficulties does the opening test-frame leave unresolved, even once we know the outcome?
- Why does Abraham say God will provide a lamb (22:8) when the animal that appears is called a ram (22:13)?
- How much can responsibly be inferred from Isaac's silence during the binding — did he consent, resist, or simply not know?
- What is gained by stretching the journey to Moriah over three days (22:4) rather than narrating it as immediate?
- What might Abraham mean in verse 5 when he tells the young men that both he and Isaac will 'come again'?
- How does the angel's statement 'now I know that thou fearest God' (22:12) shape whether we read this as a test for God's benefit, Abraham's, or the reader's?
- Why does the return notice in verse 19 name Abraham and the young men but not Isaac, and how much weight should that silence carry?
- Why might a genealogy of Nahor, and specifically the notice of Rebekah's birth (22:20-24), have been placed immediately after the covenant renewal?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Genesis 21]] for Isaac's covenant status and the departure of Ishmael, [[Genesis 24]] for the narrative purpose of introducing Rebekah, and [[2 Chronicles 3]] for the later association of Moriah with the temple. [[Hebrews 11]] interprets Abraham as reasoning about resurrection, while [[James 2]] uses the offering of Isaac in a discussion of faith and works. These later readings illuminate the chapter's reception but should remain distinguishable from what Genesis 22 itself explicitly states.
