# Genesis 1 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> God creates this earth and its heaven and all forms of life in six days—The creative acts of each day are described—God creates man, both male and female, in His own image—Man is given dominion over all things and is commanded to multiply and fill the earth.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The six days show a widely noted, though imperfect, correspondence between the first three days, which establish domains, and the last three, which populate them:

| Domain established | Domain populated |
|---|---|
| Day 1: light divided from darkness | Day 4: lights to rule day and night |
| Day 2: waters divided by the firmament | Day 5: creatures fill the waters and open sky |
| Day 3: dry land appears **and** brings forth vegetation | Day 6: land animals and humankind; vegetation assigned as food |

Day 3 already contains an act of 'filling' (vegetation), not just 'forming' — the standard complication for reading the two triads as a perfectly clean mirror. Most days follow a recognizable formula — divine command ('And God said...'), fulfillment ('and it was so'), evaluation ('and God saw that it was good'), and a day-count — but the pattern is not uniform: day 2 alone lacks any 'it was good' evaluation, and days 3 and 6 each contain two divine speeches. The sequence culminates not in a separate evaluation of humankind but in God's evaluation of the completed whole as 'very good' (v. 31), a heightening beyond the simple 'good' given to every earlier stage.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Genesis 1 introduces ideas developed at length elsewhere in scripture: humankind, male and female alike, is made 'in the image of God' (v. 27), and the same passage that confers the image also grants 'dominion' over other created life (vv. 26, 28) — their proximity supports reading the image as bound up with a representative role, though the chapter does not define what the image itself consists of or specify the ethical limits of that dominion. Procreation ('be fruitful, and multiply') is given as blessing and commandment at the outset of human existence, connecting to [[Family]]. The plural 'Let us make man in our image' (v. 26) shifts to singular language describing the act in v. 27 and is read differently across traditions; see the language notes below. These threads touch on questions addressed elsewhere under [[The Godhead]] and [[Creation]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Recurring formulas structure the chapter: 'And God said' introduces each creative act, 'and it was so' confirms fulfillment, and the evening-morning formula closes each day. The qualifier 'after his kind' (vv. 11-25) marks a taxonomy of self-reproducing categories that some readers connect to [[Leviticus 11]]'s later classification of clean and unclean animals, though that connection is not developed within Genesis 1 itself. The chapter's most debated grammatical feature is the shift from consistently singular 'God' to the plural 'us'/'our' in v. 26 alone, followed by a return to singular language describing the act itself in v. 27; Genesis 1 does not identify who is addressed, and proposals — a Hebrew idiom of self-address, address to a heavenly council, or (read alongside [[Abraham 4]]'s 'the Gods') multiple divine persons — remain disputed. Readers should also be cautious not to read modern biological classification into archaic English renderings such as 'great whales' (v. 21).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's repeated refrains give it a patterned, formal quality. Creation proceeds through divine speech with no depicted resistance from darkness or the waters — a contrast sometimes drawn with other ancient Near Eastern creation stories, such as the Babylonian Enuma Elish, that stage creation as arising from combat between deities. This comparison is suggestive but should be held loosely: it reflects one disputed scholarly argument, is not established by Genesis 1's own text, and does not by itself indicate literary dependence, date, or authorship. Comparing this chapter to [[Genesis 2]]'s differently ordered, more human-centered creation account raises a genuinely open question addressed below.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Source Criticism / Documentary Hypothesis
> Genesis 1 orders creation as vegetation (day 3), then animals (days 5-6), then humankind last (day 6), whereas Genesis 2:7-19 narrates man formed first, before the garden is planted and before field animals and birds are formed; source-critical scholars (documentary hypothesis) have long cited this difference as evidence of two originally distinct creation traditions, while harmonizing readings treat Genesis 2 as a topical, non-chronological close-up on the events of day six rather than a second, conflicting sequence.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · disputed, responsibly framed · [[Source Criticism / Documentary Hypothesis in Genesis 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Genre And Temporal Language
> The repeated sequence of numbered days, divine speeches, fulfillment formulas, evaluations, and evening-morning closures is strong evidence of a carefully patterned account. Those literary features alone do not decide whether the days are ordinary-length days, extended periods, analogical divine workdays, or elements of a literary framework.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · disputed, well-calibrated · [[Genre And Temporal Language in Genesis 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary Structure
> The six days exhibit substantial literary correspondence: days 1 and 4 concern day, night, and light; days 2 and 5 concern the waters and the open heaven with their creatures; days 3 and 6 concern dry land, its life, and food. This is strong evidence that the account has deliberate literary organization, although the third and sixth days are not exact mirror images.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation · [[Literary Structure in Genesis 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Direct Textual Statement
> Genesis 1:27 explicitly places the creation of male and female within its statement that humankind was created in God's image. This strongly supports the textual conclusion that the divine-image designation includes both, while leaving the precise meaning of that image undefined.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well established · [[Direct Textual Statement in Genesis 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Ancient Near Eastern Comparative Literature *(tentative)*
> Genesis 1's account of creation proceeds entirely by unopposed divine speech ('And God said... and it was so,' repeated across vv. 3, 6, 9, 11, 14, 20, 24, 26) with no depicted resistance from the waters or darkness, in contrast to other ancient Near Eastern cosmologies (e.g., the Babylonian Enuma Elish) that depict creation arising from combat between a god and a chaos monster; many biblical scholars read this contrast as a theological argument for Yahweh's uncontested sovereignty over elements (sun, sea, sky) that neighboring cultures deified.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, oversold and imprecisely sourced

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Which features of the six-day pattern are explicit in the text, and which arise from a reader's own reconstruction of symmetry?
- Does 'day' here mean a literal 24-hour period, an undefined age, or a literary/liturgical unit — the repeated formulas don't by themselves resolve this.
- Who is addressed by 'us' and 'our' in verse 26?
- How does linking the divine image to the grant of dominion (vv. 26, 28) shape what 'the image of God' might mean?
- What ethical limits, if any, does 'subdue' and 'have dominion' imply — the chapter states the grant without specifying its manner?
- How should this chapter's creation order (plants, then animals, then humankind) be read alongside [[Genesis 2]], where man is formed before the garden is planted and before animals are formed — a second sequence, or a topical close-up on day six?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Genesis 2]] for the completion of God's work and its more localized, human-centered retelling of creation. [[Moses 2]] and [[Abraham 4]] provide close restoration-scripture parallels — their differences in wording, especially Abraham 4's consistent use of 'the Gods,' are worth examining rather than assumed away. Psalm 8 develops the link between human honor and dominion over the same categories of creature named here; Genesis 9 repeats the command to be fruitful and multiply after the Flood while broadening the food grant beyond what is given in Genesis 1:29-30.
