## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Abram is commanded to be perfect—He will be a father of many nations—His name is changed to Abraham—The Lord covenants to be a God unto Abraham and his seed forever—Also, the Lord gives Abraham the land of Canaan for an everlasting possession—Circumcision becomes a token of the everlasting covenant between God and Abraham—Sarai’s name is changed to Sarah—She will bear Isaac, with whom the Lord will establish His covenant—Abraham and the men of his house are circumcised.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Divine appearance and summons (vv. 1–3):** God identifies himself, commands Abraham to walk before him, and announces the covenant.
2. **Promises concerning Abraham (vv. 4–8):** a new name, many nations, kings, an enduring divine relationship, and Canaan.
3. **The covenant token (vv. 9–14):** circumcision is commanded for every household male, with inclusion and exclusion clearly defined.
4. **Promises concerning Sarah and the two sons (vv. 15–21):** Sarah is renamed; Isaac is promised as covenant heir; Ishmael receives a separate national blessing.
5. **Immediate implementation (vv. 22–27):** Abraham circumcises himself, Ishmael, and every male of his household on the same day.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Genesis 17 joins divine promise with human obligation. God promises posterity, nations, kings, Canaan, and an enduring relationship with Abraham's descendants; Abraham is commanded to keep the covenant, with circumcision serving as its embodied token. The phrases “to be a God unto thee” and “I will be their God” give the covenant a relational dimension, without making land and posterity merely secondary.

Sarah's role is explicit: she is renamed, blessed, and designated as the mother of the son through whom the covenant line will continue. Ishmael is not ignored or cursed. God hears Abraham's plea, blesses Ishmael, and promises him fruitfulness, twelve princes, and a great nation. The chapter therefore distinguishes Isaac's designated covenant line from the substantial divine blessing extended to Ishmael. Ishmael's circumcision also cautions against describing him as wholly outside every covenantal association.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV commands Abraham to “walk before me, and be thou perfect” (Genesis 17:1). The English wording alone does not settle whether “perfect” should be read as absolute sinlessness, integrity, completeness, or covenant faithfulness, so lexical conclusions require external Hebrew evidence.

“Everlasting covenant” occurs three times: in relation to Abraham and his seed (Genesis 17:7), the covenant embodied in circumcision (Genesis 17:13), and the covenant established with Isaac and his seed (Genesis 17:19). These uses present related dimensions of the covenant rather than necessarily establishing separate covenants. Circumcision is called both a “token” and a covenant “in your flesh,” while failure to receive it is described as breaking the covenant and being “cut off” from the people.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The account repeatedly matches command with fulfillment. The command includes Abraham's descendants, males born in his house, and males bought with money (vv. 10–13); the closing narrative deliberately repeats those categories (vv. 23–27). “In the selfsame day” frames the execution as prompt and comprehensive [[Obedience|obedience]].

The parallel renamings also organize the promises. Abraham is to become father of many nations, and Sarah is to become mother of nations; kings are promised through both (vv. 5–6, 15–16). Abraham's laughter and the naming of Isaac begin a laughter motif developed further in [[Genesis 18]] and [[Genesis 21]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the text call circumcision a “token” while also treating refusal of it as covenant-breaking? What does the inclusion of males born or purchased into Abraham's household suggest about covenant affiliation beyond genealogy? How does the chapter hold together Isaac's distinctive covenant role and Ishmael's genuine divine blessing? What might Abraham's laughter and plea for Ishmael reveal—and what motives remain unstated?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Genesis 12]], [[Genesis 15]], and [[Genesis 17]] sequentially to trace how the promises of posterity, land, blessing, and divine relationship accumulate. Compare Abraham's laughter with Sarah's response in [[Genesis 18]] and the birth account in [[Genesis 21]]. [[Romans 4]] later reasons from the fact that Abraham was counted righteous before receiving circumcision; [[Galatians 3]] supplies another explicitly Christian reception of the Abrahamic promises. These later readings are important interpretations of Genesis, but they should remain distinguishable from what Genesis 17 itself explicitly states.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
