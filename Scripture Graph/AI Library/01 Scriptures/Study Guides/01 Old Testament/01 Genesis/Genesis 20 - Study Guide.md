# Genesis 20 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Genesis 20]] · My notes: [[Genesis 20 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Genesis 20 repeats, with variation, the pattern first seen in [[Genesis 12]]: fearing for his life in unfamiliar territory, Abraham again presents Sarah as his sister, and Abimelech, king of Gerar, takes her. The chapter's interest lies less in the danger itself than in what follows: God warns Abimelech directly in a dream, credits him with 'integrity of heart' even as he requires Sarah's restoration, and Abraham is confronted for his half-truth even as he defends its technical accuracy (Genesis 20:12-13). The episode closes with restitution, the difficult 'covering of the eyes' language of Genesis 20:16, and Abraham's [[Prayer|intercessory prayer]] healing Abimelech's household — grounded explicitly in Abraham's status as [[Prophets|a prophet]] (Genesis 20:7). The narrative resists simple moral sorting: the foreign king is the one vindicated of wrongdoing and the one who rebukes the covenant patriarch.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Danger created (vv. 1–2):** Abraham identifies Sarah as his sister, and Abimelech takes her.
2. **Divine warning (vv. 3–7):** God discloses Sarah's marriage, acknowledges Abimelech's integrity, and commands restoration.
3. **Public confrontation (vv. 8–13):** Abimelech informs his servants and questions Abraham, who explains his fear and the couple's standing arrangement.
4. **Restitution (vv. 14–16):** Sarah is restored; gifts, silver, and freedom to dwell in the land are offered.
5. **Intercession and healing (vv. 17–18):** Abraham prays and Abimelech's household is healed.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays wrongdoing as accountable to God even when the threatened act has not been completed (vv. 3–7). At the same time, intent matters: God accepts Abimelech's claim to integrity and provides warning before judgment. Mercy is linked to concrete restitution rather than merely professed innocence. Abraham's designation as a prophet is tied here to intercessory [[Prayer]], but prophetic status does not erase the narrative's criticism of his conduct.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Abimelech appeals to the "integrity" of his heart and the "innocency" of his hands (v. 5), and God explicitly affirms the first phrase (v. 6). The wording joins inward intention with outward action. God's statement that he withheld Abimelech "from sinning against me" frames the contemplated violation of Sarah's marriage as an offense against God (v. 6). The expression "a covering of the eyes" in verse 16 is difficult, and the chapter alone does not securely determine whether the silver signifies vindication, compensation, reproach, or a combination of these.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The closest literary comparisons are [[Genesis 12]] and [[Genesis 26]]. All three contain a wife-as-sister danger, but their differences matter: Genesis 20 emphasizes Abimelech's dream, innocent intent, Abraham's prophetic intercession, and household infertility. The repetitions invite comparison, but the text itself does not disclose whether they arose from recurring historical events, variant traditions, deliberate thematic composition, or multiple factors.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Abraham repeat the sister strategy that already endangered Sarah once in Egypt ([[Genesis 12]])? Does Genesis 20:13 ('at every place... say of me, He is my brother') suggest this was a standing household policy rather than a one-time decision?
- How should readers weigh Abraham's defense that the sister claim was technically true (20:12) against its function as a deception?
- Why does the narrative preserve Abimelech's pointed rebuke of Abraham ('What hast thou done unto us?' 20:9) rather than shielding the patriarch from criticism?
- How does acknowledged innocent intent (20:5-6) relate to the command for immediate restitution (20:7)?
- What does the closing and reopening of the wombs of Abimelech's house (20:17-18) suggest about how the narrative links Sarah's honor to the household around her — and does the chapter intend to resolve the question of collective consequence, or simply report it?
- How do the similarities and differences among [[Genesis 12]], Genesis 20, and [[Genesis 26]] shape interpretation without predetermining a theory of composition?
