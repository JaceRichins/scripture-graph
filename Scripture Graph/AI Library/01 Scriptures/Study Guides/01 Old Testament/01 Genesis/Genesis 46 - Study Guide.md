## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Departure authorized (46:1-7):** sacrifice, revelation, reassurance, and migration.

2. **The household named (46:8-27):** descendants are grouped through Leah, Zilpah, Rachel, and Bilhah, with stated subtotals and a final total of seventy.

3. **Reunion and settlement planning (46:28-34):** Judah goes ahead, Joseph meets Jacob, and Joseph prepares the family to request Goshen.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The promise "I will go down with thee" places divine presence at the center of the journey (46:4). Egypt will become both a place of danger in later chapters and the place where the promised nation grows (46:3). Within the theology of [[Abrahamic Covenant]], leaving Canaan is therefore not presented as abandonment of the promise: God can preserve and enlarge the covenant family outside the land and later bring it up again.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The paired expressions **go down** and **bring up** first describe geographic movement: Jacob enters Egypt under divine protection and is promised an eventual ascent (46:4). Later passages permit more than one canonical reading—Jacob’s remains return to Canaan, and his descendants are brought up in the Exodus—but Genesis 46 itself does not choose explicitly among an individual, corporate, or layered referent.

The chapter also alternates between **Jacob** and **Israel**. That alternation is observable; readers may hear both the individual father and the ancestor of the emerging people, though deliberate symbolism is not established and ordinary stylistic variation or source history remain possible.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The genealogy divides the travel narrative into distinct movements and presents Jacob’s family through four maternal groupings: Leah, Zilpah, Rachel, and Bilhah. It is not a simple passenger manifest, since Er and Onan are named despite having died in Canaan, while Joseph’s Egypt-born sons are included.

The accounting is not uniformly transparent. Zilpah’s sixteen, Rachel’s fourteen, and Bilhah’s seven can be counted directly. For Leah’s stated thirty-three, excluding Er and Onan leaves thirty-one named male descendants; adding Dinah and Jacob produces thirty-three, but the text does not state that rule. The list therefore supports a genealogical portrait of Jacob’s household more strongly than a modern-style census, while leaving room for inherited or composite counting traditions.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Criticism (Mt Vs. Lxx) *(tentative)*
> The Masoretic Text (reflected in the KJV) gives the total number of Jacob's household entering Egypt as seventy ('threescore and ten,' Genesis 46:27), but Stephen's speech in Acts 7:14 gives the number as seventy-five ('threescore and fifteen souls'), following the Greek Septuagint, whose Genesis 46:20 includes additional grandsons of Joseph/Manasseh not present in the Masoretic Hebrew text.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · central textual divergence accepted; details imprecise

> [!evidence] Internal Literary And Genealogical Structure *(tentative)*
> The maternal organization, explicit subtotals, distinction between sixty-six and seventy, and inclusion of both deceased and Egypt-born relatives strongly support the conclusion that the final text deliberately represents the whole covenant household, rather than offering an unqualified contemporaneous census.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · household portrait supported; design claim overstated
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What fear might “fear not to go down into Egypt” address—danger, departure from the promised land, or both?
- Does “bring thee up again” refer primarily to Jacob’s burial, Israel’s eventual Exodus, or a layered promise?
- Why does the genealogy include deceased and Egypt-born relatives alongside those who traveled, and what might explain the opaque Leah subtotal?
- Does Joseph’s occupational strategy seek primarily suitable land, geographic separation, social separation, or some combination of these?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
