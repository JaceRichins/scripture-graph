# Genesis 21 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Genesis 21]] · My notes: [[Genesis 21 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Sarah bears Isaac—He is circumcised—Hagar and her son are cast out of Abraham’s household—The Lord saves Hagar and Ishmael—Abraham and Abimelech deal honorably with each other.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Promise fulfilled (verses 1–8):** Sarah bears Isaac at the appointed time; his name, circumcision, growth, and weaning are narrated.

2. **Expulsion and rescue (verses 9–21):** concern over inheritance leads to Hagar's removal. Water fails in the wilderness, God hears the child, Hagar sees a well, and the displaced family receives a future.

3. **Treaty and worship (verses 22–34):** Abimelech recognizes God's presence with Abraham. A dispute over water is incorporated into an oath and covenant at Beer-sheba, after which Abraham worships the everlasting God.

The last two movements are joined by water and divine-presence language: a well sustains Hagar's household, another well secures Abraham's position, God is with the lad, and Abimelech says God is with Abraham.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter develops [[Abrahamic Covenant]] through differentiated promises. Isaac is the heir through whom Abraham's seed will be called, but Hagar's son is also Abraham's seed and receives a repeated promise of nationhood. Election therefore does not mean that everyone outside the chosen line is abandoned.

Divine care appears amid morally difficult human circumstances. God does not prevent the expulsion, but hears the endangered child, addresses Hagar, provides access to water, and remains with the lad. Readers should preserve both sides of the account: the narrative's lineage theology and the real suffering it depicts.

Human [[Covenants]] also matter. Abraham and Abimelech address mistrust through direct complaint, gifts, witnesses, and an oath. Their agreement connects faithfulness to God with honest dealings over concrete resources.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Laughter binds the opening conflict together. Isaac's Hebrew name is related to the verb for laughing; Sarah says God has made her laugh; and the description rendered "mocking" in verse 9 comes from the same Hebrew root. The repetition is clear, but the exact behavior in verse 9 is not. Translation choices such as “playing,” “laughing,” and “mocking” carry significantly different implications for Sarah's reaction.

Beer-sheba also carries wordplay. The account juxtaposes seven ewe lambs with swearing an oath; the Hebrew terms for “seven” and “swear” are closely related. The place-name consequently gathers together the well, the seven animals, and the oath.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Genesis 21 places two sons in danger in consecutive chapters with [[Genesis 22]]. Abraham rises early before sending Hagar's son away and again before taking Isaac toward sacrifice. Each son survives through divine intervention and provision, but the narrative roles and human agents differ. The comparison sharpens the cost borne by Abraham's whole family while avoiding the claim that the episodes are identical.

The paired statements “God was with the lad” and “God is with thee” are especially significant. They place divine presence over both the wilderness future of Hagar's son and Abraham's life in the land. The parallel broadens the chapter's portrayal of blessing even while Isaac retains a distinct covenantal role.

The well treaty also anticipates [[Genesis 26]], where Isaac encounters Abimelech and Phichol amid disputes over wells and another oath at Beer-sheba. This recurrence can be read as deliberate generational parallelism, though source-critical explanations remain possible.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What conduct does “mocking” in verse 9 describe, and how much should an uncertain translation control the ethical evaluation of Sarah's demand?

Why does God tell Abraham to heed Sarah while also preserving and blessing the expelled child? Does the narrative distinguish approval of Isaac's lineage from approval of every human action involved in securing it?

How do the parallel statements of divine presence in verses 20 and 22 complicate a simple chosen-versus-rejected reading?

Why are wells central to both Hagar's survival and Abraham's treaty? What do these episodes suggest about the relationship between divine promise and ordinary necessities such as water, land access, and negotiated peace?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the promise and laughter scenes in [[Genesis 17]] and [[Genesis 18]], then trace their fulfillment in this chapter. Read Genesis 21 beside [[Genesis 22]] to examine the two endangered-son narratives and beside [[Genesis 26]] to study the repeated Abimelech, well, oath, and Beer-sheba pattern. [[Galatians 4]] is an important later reception of Sarah's demand, but Paul's stated allegory should be distinguished from the full literary and ethical sense of Genesis 21.
