# Genesis 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Genesis 15]] · My notes: [[Genesis 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Genesis 15 records the Lord's covenant with Abram, addressing two concerns in turn: Abram's lack of an heir and his not yet possessing the promised land. Abram raises the problem that his household steward, Eliezer of Damascus, stands to inherit everything (15:2-3); the Lord promises a son from Abram's own body and points to the stars as an image of the size of his coming posterity (15:4-5). The chapter states plainly that Abram 'believed in the LORD; and he counted it to him for righteousness' (15:6) — a verse later invoked by Paul ([[Romans 4]]:3; [[Galatians 3]]:6) and James ([[James 2]]:23) in their own arguments about faith and righteousness. When Abram asks how he will know he will inherit the land (15:8), the Lord responds with a covenant ceremony: divided animals, a deep sleep and 'horror of great darkness' over Abram, and a smoking furnace and burning lamp that pass between the pieces (15:9-17) — an image recalling the covenant-cutting ritual described later in [[Jeremiah 34]]:18-20. Embedded within the ceremony is a prophecy that Abram's descendants will be afflicted strangers in another land for a time before returning to Canaan, delayed until 'the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet full' (15:13-16). The chapter closes with the terms of the [[Abrahamic Covenant]]'s land grant and a list of ten peoples then occupying it (15:18-21).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Descendant promise (15:1–6):** reassurance, Abram's concern about an heir, the promise of offspring, and the statement about belief and righteousness.

2. **Land promise and preparation (15:7–11):** the Lord identifies himself as the one who brought Abram from Ur; Abram asks how he will know; animals are prepared.

3. **Future affliction and return (15:12–16):** darkness accompanies a prophecy of estrangement, servitude, judgment, deliverance, and delayed return.

4. **Covenant enactment and land grant (15:17–21):** fiery and smoky manifestations pass between the pieces, and the covenant's territorial promise is stated.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Genesis 15:6 joins belief and righteousness in a single, concise statement, and it becomes foundational for later New Testament reflection: [[Romans 4]], [[Galatians 3]], and [[James 2]] all quote it — though each writer puts it to a somewhat different use, and that shared appeal to Abraham should not be read as though every use makes the same point.

The chapter also touches [[Justice and Mercy]]: the delay in Israel's return to Canaan is tied explicitly to divine patience, since judgment on the Amorites waits until their 'iniquity... is not yet full' (15:16).

In the covenant ceremony, only the theophanic 'smoking furnace' and 'burning lamp' are described passing between the divided animals, while Abram is not said to do so (15:17). Some readers take this as signaling a unilateral, self-binding divine oath in which God alone assumes the covenant's obligation — though the text does not state this explicitly, and Abram's absence may simply reflect that he had fallen into a deep sleep (15:12).

[[Covenants]] in this chapter also extend beyond the lifetime of the person who receives them: Abram is promised both descendants and land, yet is told he will die before his descendants return to possess it — covenant assurance coexists with waiting and delay.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The word “seed” can function collectively for descendants in this chapter, as the comparison with innumerable stars makes clear. Later Christian interpretation can give the Abrahamic promise further christological significance, but that later use should not be mistaken for proof that every occurrence of “seed” in Genesis 15 refers exclusively to one individual.

The land statement “have I given” appears alongside future events and delayed possession. At minimum, the wording presents the grant as certain; conclusions about the underlying Hebrew tense or the exact legal force of the expression require linguistic evidence beyond the English wording alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter opens with an explicit vision formula and reassurance ('Fear not,' 15:1) before moving, by nightfall, into deep sleep, 'horror of great darkness,' and a burning lamp — a progression from light toward darkness that gives the covenant scene an unusual solemnity (15:1, 12, 17).

The animal-division ritual of 15:9-11, 17 — dividing three animals and passing a fiery, smoking form between the pieces — corresponds to a covenant-ratification image also used in Jeremiah's rebuke of covenant-breakers who 'cut the calf in twain, and passed between the parts thereof' ([[Jeremiah 34]]:18-20), suggesting Genesis 15 draws on a recognizable, if only partly explained, covenant-cutting practice.

God's self-identification by a past act ('I am the LORD that brought thee out of Ur of the Chaldees,' 15:7) before stating the land's terms has also been compared to the [[Ten Commandments]]' prologue (Exodus 20:2), though the two passages serve different purposes and the parallel is only structural.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does the 'four hundred years' of affliction (15:13) relate to 'the fourth generation' that returns (15:16), and to the 'four hundred and thirty years' given for the sojourn in Exodus 12:40?
- What distinction, if any, does the narrative draw between trusting a promise and still asking how it will be fulfilled, given that Abram does both?
- Why are the birds left undivided while the larger animals are split, and does Abram's driving away the birds of prey (15:11) carry symbolic weight or simply describe a realistic vigil?
- What exactly do the smoking furnace and burning lamp represent, and is Abram's absence from passing between the pieces (15:17) theologically significant or simply a consequence of his being asleep (15:12)?
- Why does the chapter present affliction as part of the descendants' promised future rather than immediate possession and prosperity?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Genesis 15:9–18 with [[Jeremiah 34]], especially Jeremiah 34:18–20, for another biblical description of passing between divided animal parts in a covenant setting. Compare Genesis 15:13–14 with [[Exodus 1]] and [[Exodus 12]] to trace the canonical pattern of servitude, judgment, and departure with possessions. For major later interpretations of Genesis 15:6, study [[Romans 4]], [[Galatians 3]], and [[James 2]], noting both their shared quotation and their different argumentative settings.
