# Genesis 12 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Genesis 12]] · My notes: [[Genesis 12 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Abram will become a great nation—He and his seed will bless all the families of the earth—He travels from Haran to the land of Canaan—Because of famine, he goes down into Egypt—Abram and Sarai are tested in Pharaoh’s court.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Call and promises (12:1-3):** Abram is commanded to leave, and the LORD promises nationhood, blessing, a great name, reciprocal blessing or curse, and worldwide benefit.

2. **Journey and worship in Canaan (12:4-9):** Abram travels from Haran through Sichem toward the south, receives the land promise, builds altars, and calls upon the LORD.

3. **Famine and danger in Egypt (12:10-16):** Material crisis leads to migration; fear leads to the wife-sister strategy; Sarai enters Pharaoh's house while Abram prospers materially.

4. **Divine intervention and expulsion (12:17-20):** Plagues expose the crisis, Pharaoh rebukes Abram, Sarai is returned, and the household is sent away.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The promises of 12:1-3 and 12:7 form an early foundation for the [[Abrahamic Covenant]], although the word “covenant” is not used in this chapter. Blessing is simultaneously personal, familial, national, and universal: Abram is blessed in order to become a blessing.

Abram's departure illustrates [[Faith]] and [[Obedience]], especially when read with [[Hebrews 11]]. Yet the famine and Egypt episode resist a prosperity-only reading of divine blessing. A divine promise does not remove adversity, and a called individual is not portrayed as morally infallible.

The danger to Sarai also makes [[Marriage]] central to the episode. The text reports divine protection of the marriage, but it does not give Sarai's perspective or explicitly approve Abram's method.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Forms of “bless” dominate 12:2-3, moving from what God will do for Abram to what Abram will become for others. The scope expands until it reaches “all families of the earth.” The corresponding curse statement is narrower in its wording, while blessing supplies the passage's controlling emphasis.

The command moves outward through “country,” “kindred,” and “father's house,” emphasizing the social cost of Abram's departure. Conversely, the promised effects move outward from Abram to a nation and finally to every family of the earth.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Three tensions organize the chapter: Sarai's barrenness stands behind the promise of a great nation; Canaanite occupancy stands beside the promise of land to Abram's seed; and famine follows arrival in the promised land. Fulfillment is therefore announced before its obstacles are removed.

The contrast with [[Genesis 11]] is suggestive: Babel's builders seek to make themselves a name, whereas God promises to make Abram's name great. The Egypt episode also resembles a small-scale [[Exodus and Deliverance]] pattern—descent into Egypt, peril under Pharaoh, plagues, release, and departure with goods—but intentional foreshadowing should be treated as a literary interpretation rather than a demonstrated historical conclusion.

The wife-sister episode belongs to a recurring Genesis pattern developed again in [[Genesis 20]] and [[Genesis 26]]. The repetitions invite comparison of character, danger, divine intervention, and the vulnerability of the promised family.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Why does the narrative place barrenness, Canaanite occupancy, and famine so near promises of descendants, land, and blessing?

2. What does the narrator explicitly say—and leave unsaid—about the morality of Abram's wife-sister strategy?

3. How does Abram function both as a recipient of blessing and as a potential source of blessing to others?

4. What is gained, and what might be overstated, by reading the Egypt episode as anticipating the later exodus?

5. How do [[Galatians 3]] and [[Hebrews 11]] extend the meaning of Genesis 12 beyond what the chapter itself explicitly states?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the chapter with [[Abraham 2]] for a substantially expanded Latter-day Saint account of the call, promises, journey, and Egypt episode. Compare [[Acts 7]] for Stephen's placement of the call before Abraham's residence in Haran, [[Hebrews 11]] for the faith interpretation of his departure, and [[Galatians 3]] for the Christian interpretation of the universal-blessing promise. The related narratives in [[Genesis 20]] and [[Genesis 26]] permit close study of the repeated wife-sister motif.
