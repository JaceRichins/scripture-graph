# Genesis 48 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Genesis 48]] · My notes: [[Genesis 48 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jacob tells of the appearance of God to him in Luz—He adopts Ephraim and Manasseh as his own children—Jacob blesses Joseph—He puts Ephraim before Manasseh—The seed of Ephraim will become a multitude of nations—The children of Israel will come again into the land of their fathers.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Covenant and inheritance (48:1-7):** Jacob recalls the land-and-descendants promise and adopts Joseph's two Egyptian-born sons.
2. **Recognition and presentation (48:8-12):** Jacob embraces the sons, expresses gratitude for seeing Joseph's descendants, and Joseph formally presents them.
3. **Blessing and deliberate reversal (48:13-20):** Jacob crosses his hands, resists Joseph's correction, and places Ephraim before Manasseh.
4. **Death, return, and portion (48:21-22):** Jacob anticipates his death, promises divine accompaniment and return, and grants Joseph an additional portion.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**[[Abrahamic Covenant]]**: Jacob frames his blessing of Joseph's sons as a continuation of the covenant of posterity and land he received at Luz, itself inherited from Abraham and Isaac (Genesis 48:3-4, 15).

**[[Patriarchal Blessings]]**: The chapter is a scriptural example of a father pronouncing an individualized blessing over his descendants, assigning each a distinct future (compare [[Genesis 49]]). This narrative episode predates, and should not be conflated with, the modern priesthood ordinance of the same name.

**[[Prophecy]]**: Jacob's insistence on blessing Ephraim above Manasseh is presented as informed knowledge rather than mere preference — "I know it, my son, I know it" (Genesis 48:19) — though the chapter does not itself describe this as a revelation.

**[[Gathering of Israel]]**: Genesis 48:21 has Jacob promise that God will "bring you again unto the land of your fathers." Later Latter-day Saint tradition connects Ephraim's promised multiplication (v.19) to a future gathering role, but that connection is later reception, not something this chapter states outright.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The narration says Jacob "blessed Joseph" (v.15), yet the spoken blessing itself addresses "the lads" (v.16) — Joseph's blessing is mediated through his sons. The chapter alternates the names Jacob and Israel without comment. Verse 22's "one portion" is often connected by commentators to the Hebrew *shekem*, also the place name Shechem — a plausible wordplay this chapter's English text cannot itself confirm. Ephraim's promised future, that his "seed shall become a multitude of nations" (v.19), echoes the covenant language given to Jacob himself ("a multitude of people," v.4), extending the grandfather's promise to the grandson. The blessing's closing line, "The Angel which redeemed me from all evil" (v.16), stands in close parallel with the preceding description of God, but the text does not itself clarify the Angel's identity or relationship to God.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's central device is the crossed hands (vv.13-14): Jacob's right hand rests deliberately on the younger Ephraim rather than the firstborn Manasseh, and the text underscores the intent twice — in narration ("guiding his hands wittingly") and in Jacob's own words ("I know it, my son, I know it," v.19). This invites comparison with [[Genesis 27]]: both chapters open with a patriarch whose eyes are dim with age, and both end with a younger son gaining precedence. The decisive difference is that Isaac in Genesis 27 blesses unknowingly, deceived by disguise, while Jacob here acts with declared, repeated knowledge. Whether this reflects a deliberate echo of Genesis 27 or simply a recurring "dim-eyed patriarch" narrative pattern (compare Eli in 1 Samuel 3:2) cannot be settled from the text alone. The Angel/God parallelism in the blessing (vv.15-16) is likewise better treated as an open question than as evidence of a distinct divine figure.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Jacob recall Rachel's death (v.7) right after adopting her grandsons? The text gives no stated reason.
- Is Jacob's crossed-hand blessing better read as a conscious counterpoint to the deceptive blessing in [[Genesis 27]], or as an instance of a broader "dim-eyed patriarch" narrative pattern?
- Does "the Angel which redeemed me" (v.16) name a being distinct from "God" earlier in the same verse, or is this Hebrew poetic parallelism? What would settle the question either way?
- What is the "one portion" Jacob gives Joseph (v.22), and what event does "my sword and my bow" refer to — a claim that sits in tension with Genesis 34, where Simeon and Levi, not Jacob, take Shechem by violence Jacob elsewhere condemns?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
