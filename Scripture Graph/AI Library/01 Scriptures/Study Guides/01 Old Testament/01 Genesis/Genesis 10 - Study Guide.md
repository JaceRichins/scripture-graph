## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The descendants of Noah are Japheth, whose descendants are Gentiles; Ham, whose descendants include the Canaanites; and Shem, of whom came Peleg (in whose days the earth was divided).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **Japheth (10:2–5):** descendants conclude with a summary of lands, tongues, families, and nations.

- **Ham (10:6–20):** the longest geographic and political notices concern Nimrod's kingdom and the boundaries and spread of Canaan.

- **Shem (10:21–31):** the line is narrowed through Eber, Peleg, and Joktan, with a final territorial notice.

- **Summary (10:32):** the three branches are gathered into one statement about the nations divided upon the earth after the flood.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents the diversity of nations within the unity of Noah's family. Its genealogical-geographic scheme is not a sound basis for imposing modern racial classifications or moral rankings on peoples. Genesis 10 itself does not pronounce all of Ham's descendants cursed.

The chapter also bridges two larger narrative movements. Its population and territorial spread follow the command in [[Genesis 9]] to multiply, while its families of the earth prepare for the universal reach of Abram's calling in [[Genesis 12]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase “before the LORD” in Genesis 10:9 places Nimrod's might in relation to the Lord's presence or sight, but by itself does not determine whether the description is praise or condemnation. Likewise, Genesis 10:25 connects Peleg with a division of the earth without specifying exactly what was divided. The nearby Babel account makes division of peoples and languages a plausible reading, but the identification should not be presented as explicit.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Genesis 10 names multiple tongues before [[Genesis 11]] opens with one language and narrates its confusion. The final sequence can therefore be read as panoramic result followed by an explanatory episode rather than as strict chronology at every point. Whether that ordering reflects deliberate retrospective narration or the combination of traditions is not settled by the text alone.

The extended notices about Nimrod, cities and kingdoms, Canaanite borders, and Joktan's dwelling distinguish this chapter from a bare list of births. Together with the recurring references to families, tongues, lands, and nations, they make the genealogy function as a conceptual map of peoples and territories.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the account give substantially more geographic detail for Nimrod and Canaan than for most other descendants?

- Does “the earth divided” in Peleg's days anticipate Babel, refer to territorial separation, or intentionally permit both associations?

- How does reading [[Genesis 10]] before [[Genesis 12]] affect the meaning of the promise to bless all families of the earth?

- Which claims commonly made about Nimrod actually appear in the chapter, and which come from inference or later tradition?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

Then read [[Genesis 9]], [[Genesis 10]], [[Genesis 11]], and [[Genesis 12]] as a sequence: renewed multiplication, the spread of nations, Babel's scattering, and the calling of Abram for the blessing of all families.
