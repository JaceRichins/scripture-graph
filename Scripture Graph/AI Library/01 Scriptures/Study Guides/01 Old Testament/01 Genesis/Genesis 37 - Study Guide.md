# Genesis 37 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Genesis 37]] · My notes: [[Genesis 37 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jacob loves and favors Joseph, who is hated by his brothers—Joseph dreams that his parents and brothers make obeisance to him—His brothers sell him into Egypt.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Household conflict (vv. 1–4):** Joseph's age, report, Jacob's favoritism, the coat, and the brothers' hatred.
2. **Two dreams and escalating hostility (vv. 5–11):** the imagery expands from sheaves to the heavenly family, while Jacob's guarded attention contrasts with the brothers' envy.
3. **Joseph sent to his brothers (vv. 12–17):** a journey from Hebron through Shechem to Dothan.
4. **Conspiracy, pit, and sale (vv. 18–28):** the plan moves from murder, to Reuben's attempted rescue, to Judah's proposal of sale.
5. **Deception and grief (vv. 29–35):** the coat becomes fabricated evidence that leads Jacob to conclude Joseph is dead.
6. **Transition to Egypt (v. 36):** Joseph is sold to Potiphar, preparing the setting of [[Genesis 39]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter is especially useful for distinguishing human intention from providential outcome. The brothers choose envy, conspiracy, violence, sale, and deception; Genesis 37 never excuses those choices. Only later does Joseph say that God sent him ahead to preserve life (Genesis 45:5–8) and that God intended good through what the brothers intended for evil (Genesis 50:20). The larger story therefore does not require calling evil good. It portrays culpable human [[Agency]] operating within a redemptive outcome that the offenders neither intended nor understood.

The dreams are naturally connected with [[Revelation]] and [[Prophecy]], but the chapter itself does not explicitly state that God gave them. Their later correspondence with events is strong internal narrative evidence; a theological conclusion about their divine source comes from reading the Joseph cycle as a whole.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repetition gives the chapter its emotional trajectory: the brothers hate Joseph in verse 4, hate him more in verse 5, and hate him still more in verse 8. The paired dreams likewise expand from the brothers' sheaves to celestial imagery interpreted by Jacob as the wider family.

The coat is the chapter's central object. It embodies favoritism, is stripped away, stained with a goat's blood, presented for recognition, and used to construct a false account. The deception recalls [[Genesis 27]], where garments and a kid help mislead Isaac. The combined motifs support a literary comparison, but Genesis 37 does not say that Jacob's suffering is divine repayment.

The brothers' declaration, “we shall see what will become of his dreams” (Genesis 37:20), creates narrative irony: their effort to eliminate Joseph sends him toward the circumstances in which they later bow before him in [[Genesis 42]] and [[Genesis 43]]. Hatred and envy remain the conspiracy's repeatedly stated motives, so the line should not be treated as its only purpose.

The alternating references to Ishmeelites and Midianites in verses 25–36 create a genuine textual difficulty, intensified by ambiguous pronouns in verse 28. Proposed explanations include overlapping ethnonyms, a mixed caravan, ordinary narrative compression, and the joining of distinct traditions. Judges 8:24 supports possible overlap between the names but does not decide the question.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Recurrent Literary Motif
> Genesis 27 and Genesis 37 share a distinctive cluster of family-deception motifs: a favored child's garment, a kid of the goats, and a father induced to reach a false conclusion from disguised evidence. The recurrence strongly supports a literary connection in the received narrative, although identifying it as deliberate retributive justice remains interpretive.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · literary parallel accepted; retribution reading disputed · [[Recurrent Literary Motif in Genesis 37|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Fulfillment
> Within Genesis as a finished narrative, the brothers' later bowing before Joseph in Egypt strongly supports reading the sheaf dream as deliberate foreshadowing. It does not by itself prove that the dream was a supernatural revelation, because a narrative can be composed or shaped around its eventual outcome.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · narrative correspondence accepted; supernatural inference disputed · [[Internal Narrative Fulfillment in Genesis 37|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual And Narrative Complexity
> The account refers to an Ishmeelite caravan, Midianite merchantmen at the pit, a sale to the Ishmeelites, and a final sale by the Midianites. This variation is genuine evidence of textual complexity, but it is not sufficient on its own to establish one particular source-critical reconstruction.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 1.0 · textual complexity accepted; explanation disputed · [[Textual And Narrative Complexity in Genesis 37|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Philological/Comparative *(tentative)*
> The Hebrew phrase behind 'coat of many colours' (ketonet passim) recurs in the Old Testament only here and in the description of Tamar's royal garment in 2 Samuel 13, which the text says was the standard dress of unmarried royal daughters — suggesting the coat marked Joseph's favored/heir-like status rather than merely being decoratively colorful, and some translators (following the root's likely sense of 'extremities') render it 'a long robe with sleeves' rather than 'many colours.'

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Joseph brings an “evil report,” but the text does not say whether the report was accurate, exaggerated, or malicious. How much should readers infer about his motives?
- Jacob interprets the moon as Joseph's mother (Genesis 37:10), although Rachel's death has already been narrated in Genesis 35:19. Is the dream symbolic of the household as a whole, does “mother” refer to another maternal figure, or does the passage preserve a different narrative chronology?
- Where is Reuben during the sale, and why does the text leave his absence unexplained?
- How should the alternating Ishmeelite and Midianite designations be understood: overlapping identities, cooperating merchants, ambiguous syntax, or joined traditions?
- Why do the brothers say “thy son's coat” rather than directly naming Joseph when speaking to Jacob (Genesis 37:32)? Does this wording distance them from both Joseph and their deed?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the garment-and-goat deception with [[Genesis 27]], the brothers' acts of obeisance with [[Genesis 42]] and [[Genesis 43]], Joseph's disclosure and providential interpretation with [[Genesis 45]], and his final distinction between human evil and divine good with [[Genesis 50]]. Psalm 105:16–22 and Acts 7:9–10 show how later biblical writers summarize the episode with different but complementary emphases.
