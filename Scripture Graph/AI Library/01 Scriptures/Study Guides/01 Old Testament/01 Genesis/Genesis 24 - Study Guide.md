## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Commission and oath (vv. 1–9):** Abraham defines the mission by the promises of land and seed.
2. **Prayer and encounter at the well (vv. 10–27):** Rebekah fulfills the servant's requested sign, after which he verifies her identity and worships.
3. **Report and family decision (vv. 28–53):** The servant retells the mission as evidence of divine guidance, and the family agrees.
4. **Rebekah's decision and departure (vv. 54–61):** Her family asks her directly, and she chooses to go.
5. **Meeting and marriage (vv. 62–67):** Isaac receives Rebekah, loves her, and is comforted after Sarah's death.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Prayer]] alongside careful action. The servant asks for guidance, observes Rebekah's conduct, asks factual questions, explains his mission, and seeks a decision from her household. This can support a reading in which trust and practical discernment complement one another, but it does not establish a universal method for seeking signs.

The narrative also gives meaningful attention to [[Agency]]: Abraham anticipates that the woman may refuse, and Rebekah is eventually asked whether she will go. Her answer should not be converted into a claim of modern social equality or wholly unconstrained consent, since the decision occurs within an ancient patriarchal household after negotiations and gift exchange.

Isaac's marriage continues the family line associated with the [[Abrahamic Covenant]]. Abraham invokes the promised land in verses 6-8, and the blessing in verse 60 closely resembles the seed-and-enemy-gate language of [[Genesis 22]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verses 27 and 49 form a thematic echo in this translation. The servant praises the Lord for showing Abraham "mercy" and "truth," then asks the family to deal "kindly and truly" with Abraham. This links divine faithfulness with the faithful human response the servant requests. Because the English expressions are not identical, any claim that they repeat the same underlying lexical pair should be confirmed from the Hebrew before being stated as a philological fact.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The servant's long speech retells much of the preceding narrative. The repetition is not merely inert: he refuses food until he has explained his errand, organizes the events into an appeal for a decision, and receives the family's answer that the matter "proceedeth from the LORD." This supports a persuasive-function reading, though oral convention, messenger-report convention, and compositional history remain possible contributing explanations.

The well encounter also resembles [[Genesis 29]] in its combination of water, kinship discovery, running home, and marriage negotiation. Genesis 24 differs significantly because the encounter is conducted by a servant acting for the future bridegroom. The resemblance may reflect a conventional scene or shared social realities; it does not determine authorship, dependence, fictionality, or historicity.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the servant continue watching and asking questions after Rebekah performs the requested actions? What might this suggest—and what does it not prove—about the relationship between prayer and practical discernment?

Which characters attribute the mission's outcome to the Lord, and what reported events lead them to that conclusion?

Why is the jewelry presented before the servant learns Rebekah's lineage? Does the sequence communicate confidence, create narrative tension, or preserve a compositional difficulty?

How do Abraham's acknowledgment that the woman may refuse, the household negotiations, and Rebekah's answer each contribute to the narrative's portrayal of choice? What limits prevent equating that portrayal with modern consent?
