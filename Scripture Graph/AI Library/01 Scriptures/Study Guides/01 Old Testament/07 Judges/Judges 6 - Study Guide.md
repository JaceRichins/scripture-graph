<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
Judges 6 moves through six clear movements:

1. **Crisis** (6:1-6) — Midianite/Amalekite oppression and Israel's cry.
2. **Prophetic indictment** (6:7-10) — an unnamed prophet recalls the Exodus and charges Israel with disobedience.
3. **Call of Gideon** (6:11-24) — angelic appearance, protest, reassurance, and a fire-sign; altar 'Jehovah-shalom' built.
4. **Reform at home** (6:25-32) — destruction of Baal's altar and Asherah; Joash's defense; the naming Jerubbaal.
5. **Mustering** (6:33-35) — the Spirit comes upon Gideon; tribes are summoned.
6. **Fleece signs** (6:36-40) — two further confirming signs requested and granted.

Notably, the chapter stacks two separate messenger scenes — a human prophet (vv.7-10) and the angel of the LORD (vv.11ff) — before the deliverer is commissioned, a doubling not present in every judges narrative.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Angels]]**: the angel of the LORD both speaks for God and, at points, is addressed and responds as though he *were* the LORD (compare 6:12 and 6:14) — a fluidity typical of many 'angel of the LORD' passages. The text itself does not resolve the figure's identity.
- **[[Obedience]]**: Gideon's destruction of the Baal altar (6:27) is obedient but explicitly fearful and carried out secretly by night — a deliverer who obeys while still afraid, not courage preceding obedience.
- **[[Apostasy]]**: Israel's national crisis is paired with a concrete, local instance of the same problem — an altar of Baal standing in Gideon's own father's household (6:25) — tying national covenant failure to household practice.
- **[[Faith]]** and sign-seeking: Gideon asks for confirming signs more than once (the fire sign, then two fleece tests) even after direct reassurance and the Spirit's empowerment (6:34). The fleece requests explicitly reconfirm a promise already given (6:36, "as thou hast said") rather than originate his call; the text records no rebuke for either request, though it does not explicitly commend the practice either.
- **[[Sacrifice]]**: both the fire-consumed offering (6:19-21) and the burnt offering on the new altar (6:26) tie personal encounter with God to sacrificial action.
- **[[Family]]**: Gideon's call unfolds inside domestic tension — his self-description as least in his father's house (6:15), and Joash's public defense of his son (6:29-31).

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Gideon's commissioning (6:11-24) follows a recognizable biblical call-narrative pattern — encounter, protest of inadequacy, reassurance, and a confirming sign — closely paralleling Moses's call in [[Exodus 3]] (compare Gideon's "wherewith shall I save Israel?" in 6:15 with Moses's objection in Exodus 3:11). The angel's greeting, "thou mighty man of valour" (6:12), creates a striking contrast with a Gideon found threshing grain in hiding; many readers hear irony or a prospective naming of what Gideon will become, though the underlying Hebrew idiom (gibbor chayil) is also used elsewhere in the Old Testament for social standing or capability rather than martial courage specifically, so the irony reading is one plausible option rather than the settled meaning.

The fire-from-the-rock theophany (6:21) is one of several biblical scenes where fire authenticates a divine encounter — compare the closely similar offering-and-fire scene with Manoah in [[Judges 13]] (shared elements: an offering near a rock, miraculous fire, the angel's departure, and fear after recognizing the encounter). A more distant, thematic-only echo appears in the later Baal-versus-the-LORD fire contest at Mount Carmel (1 Kings 18); this is a shared motif, not a demonstrated literary dependence. The chapter is also rich in etiological naming: the altar 'Jehovah-shalom' (6:24) and the byname 'Jerubbaal' (6:32) both explain a name through the events just narrated, a common feature of Hebrew narrative art.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Evidence
> Within the narrative, fire consuming Gideon's food offering functions as evidence to Gideon that his visitor is an angel of the LORD. Gideon's ensuing fear, divine reassurance, and construction of an altar show that he understands the event as a genuine divine encounter.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · correctly scoped narrative-function claim · [[Internal Narrative Evidence in Judges 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Contextual Limitation
> The chapter provides weak support for treating 'putting out a fleece' as a universal method of decision-making. Gideon has already received a direct commission, a promise, a miraculous sign, and the Spirit before requesting the fleece signs; the immediate issue is reassurance about an announced mission.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-hedged contextual limitation · [[Contextual Limitation in Judges 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Theological Interpretation *(tentative)*
> Some Latter-day Saint and other Christian readers identify 'the angel of the LORD' who appears to Gideon as a premortal appearance of Jesus Christ (a christophany). This identification is not made explicit within Judges 6 itself; it is imported from broader doctrinal frameworks elsewhere in scripture. Mainstream biblical scholarship generally treats 'the angel of the LORD' as a literary/theological device representing God's own presence or agency, without settling on a specific personal identification, and notes the figure is sometimes indistinguishable from the LORD himself in the narrative (compare v.14, 'the LORD looked upon him,' immediately after 'the angel of the LORD appeared unto him' in v.12).
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · disputed, honestly labeled

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean that the angel calls Gideon "a mighty man of valour" (6:12) before Gideon has done anything to earn that title — is this ironic, prospective, or simply a stock idiom of standing rather than courage?
- Why does the LORD answer Israel's cry first with a prophet's indictment (6:7-10) rather than immediately with military relief?
- Joash alone — not the town — argues that Baal should be left to defend himself (6:31-32). What does his argument assume about the nature of true versus false gods, and what might have changed his own stance toward Baal worship?
- The text records Gideon's fear at each stage (concealing the wheat, obeying by night, fearing death after seeing the angel) alongside genuine obedience. What does this suggest about the relationship between fear and faith in a divine call?
- Gideon's fleece requests explicitly ask God to reconfirm a promise already given (6:36, "as thou hast said"), after he had already received a sign and the Spirit's empowerment. Does this change how the double fleece test (6:36-40) should be read, compared to treating it as a general model for seeking guidance under uncertainty?
