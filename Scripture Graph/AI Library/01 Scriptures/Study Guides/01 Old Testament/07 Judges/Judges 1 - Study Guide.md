# Judges 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Judges 1]] · My notes: [[Judges 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Judah, Simeon, and Joseph continue to conquer the Canaanites—Remnants of the Canaanites remain in the lands of Judah, Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Asher, Naphtali, and Dan.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Leadership and cooperation after Joshua (vv. 1–3):** Israel inquires of the LORD; Judah is selected and invites Simeon into a reciprocal campaign.
2. **Judah's campaigns (vv. 4–20):** The account includes victories at Bezek and several southern cities, Adoni-bezek's requital speech, the Caleb-Othniel-Achsah episode, the Kenite migration, and Judah's inability to expel the valley inhabitants with iron chariots.
3. **Jerusalem and Beth-el (vv. 21–26):** Benjamin does not expel Jerusalem's Jebusites, while the house of Joseph captures Beth-el through information supplied by a man who is spared with his family.
4. **Incomplete occupation (vv. 27–36):** The remaining notices describe varied outcomes among Manasseh through Dan: continued residence, tribute, Israelites dwelling among Canaanites, and Dan's confinement to the hill country.

The chapter broadly moves toward increasingly prominent incomplete occupation, but it is not a clean victory-then-failure sequence: Judah's limitation appears in verse 19, and the house of Joseph succeeds at Beth-el after verse 21.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Requital and moral attribution (1:6–7).** Adoni-bezek interprets his mutilation as measure-for-measure requital: “as I have done, so God hath requited me.” This is the defeated king's explanation as reported by the narrator, not a separate divine pronouncement or command approving the mutilation.

**Divine presence and military limitation (1:19).** The same sentence says that the LORD was with Judah and that Judah could not expel the valley inhabitants because they had iron chariots. Grammatically, Judah is the unsuccessful actor; the verse identifies the immediate military obstacle without explaining the full relationship between divine presence and the failed campaign.

**The covenantal sequel.** Judges 1 mainly reports the uneven occupation. [[Judges 2]] more explicitly connects the continuing presence of the inhabitants with Israel's covenant disobedience and prohibited agreements. This later theological frame need not erase the immediate military, political, and economic circumstances reported in Judges 1.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The recurring vocabulary of “drive out,” “dwell,” and “became tributaries” traces different balances of power. Some Canaanite populations dwell among Israelites; Asher and Naphtali are instead described as dwelling among Canaanites; Dan is forced into the mountain. This shift is meaningful, though tribute notices and the renewed strength of the house of Joseph prevent the sequence from being a simple, uninterrupted decline.

The alternate place-name notices—Hebron/Kirjath-arba, Debir/Kirjath-sepher, Zephath/Hormah, and Beth-el/Luz—preserve geographical memory within the narrative. The formulas “unto this day” in verses 21 and 26 indicate retrospective narration, but they cannot establish a precise date or a single stage of composition.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
**Accumulating notices of incomplete possession.** From verse 21 onward, related but varied formulas catalogue tribes that do not fully control their allotted territory. The sequence culminates in Dan being confined by the Amorites, although tribute and the strength of the house of Joseph complicate any reading of a steadily worsening decline.

**Parallels with Joshua.** Judges 1 shares close material with [[Joshua 15]], [[Joshua 16]], and [[Joshua 17]]. The Caleb-Othniel-Achsah account is the clearest extended parallel; the Manasseh and Ephraim notices also correspond closely to territorial reports in Joshua. These parallels strongly establish a literary or traditional relationship, but not its direction, date, or precise compositional history.

**The Bethel mercy bargain.** The reconnaissance, local assistance, promised mercy, preservation of a household, and fall of the city resemble the Rahab narrative in Joshua. The resemblance is worth studying, but wording alone does not prove direct borrowing.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Internal Chronological Indicator
> The expressions stating that the Jebusites remained in Jerusalem and that the second Luz retained its name 'unto this day' present the narrator as looking back from a time later than the narrated events. They provide evidence of temporal distance but no precise date.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 1.0 · accepted chronological observation; precise dating disputed · [[Internal Chronological Indicator in Judges 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Textual Consistency *(tentative)*
> The Jerusalem tension in Judges 1:8 vs 1:21 (and its consistency with Jebusite Jerusalem persisting until David's capture of the city in 2 Samuel 5:6-9) is a textual feature that supports the reading that verse 8 describes a raid/partial destruction of the city rather than a lasting conquest and occupation, but does not by itself establish which account is more historically primary or resolve why two seemingly conflicting notices sit in the same chapter.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should Judah's capture and burning of Jerusalem in verse 8 be related to Benjamin's failure to expel the Jebusites in verse 21? Do temporary capture, partial control, later reoccupation, or differing traditions best explain the notices?

- Why does [[Joshua 15]] attribute failure to expel Jerusalem's Jebusites to Judah, while Judges 1:21 attributes it to Benjamin? Does Jerusalem's position near their boundary help explain the difference?

- How should divine presence, Judah's military limitation, and the iron chariots in verse 19 be related without claiming more than the text states?

- When a strong Israel imposes tribute rather than expelling inhabitants, does this suggest economic preference, political accommodation, incomplete obedience, or some combination?

- What can Achsah's direct request for springs establish about property negotiation in this narrative, and what remains uncertain because of verse 14's compressed grammar?

- What changes when Judges 1's descriptive catalogue is read alongside the explicit covenantal rebuke in [[Judges 2]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the Caleb, Othniel, and Achsah account with [[Joshua 15]]; the Gezer notice with [[Joshua 16]]; and the Manasseh towns with [[Joshua 17]]. These comparisons demonstrate close literary or traditional relationships without determining which text is earlier. Then read [[Judges 2]] to distinguish Judges 1's account of uneven occupation from the sequel's explicit covenantal interpretation. [[Judges 3]] further develops the consequences of Israel dwelling among the remaining peoples and introduces Othniel as a judge.
