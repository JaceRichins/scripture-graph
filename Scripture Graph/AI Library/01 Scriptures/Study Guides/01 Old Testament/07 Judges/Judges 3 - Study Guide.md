## Overview

> The children of Israel intermarry with the Canaanites, worship false gods, and are cursed—Othniel judges the Israelites—They serve Moab and are delivered by Ehud, who slays Eglon.

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **3:1-4** — Rationale for the remaining nations: training in war and a test of obedience to the commandments given through Moses
- **3:5-7** — Intermarriage and apostasy: the setup for the first cycle
- **3:8-11** — Cycle 1: Chushan-rishathaim's oppression (8 years) → Othniel raised up → 40 years' rest
- **3:12-14** — Renewed apostasy and Eglon of Moab's oppression (18 years), aided by Ammon and Amalek
- **3:15-26** — Ehud's mission: the gift to Eglon, the concealed dagger, the private audience, the killing, and the escape
- **3:27-30** — Israel's mobilization and rout of Moab at the fords of Jordan → 80 years' rest
- **3:31** — Shamgar, a third deliverer, in a single unadorned verse

The repeated sequence — sin, servitude, crying out, a raised-up deliverer, rest — first announced in [[Judges 2]], organizes this chapter and recurs through the rest of the book of Judges.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's clearest doctrinal thread ties covenant fidelity to the test of the remaining nations (3:4): Israel's continued exposure to rival peoples is framed as a live test of whether they would keep "the commandments of the LORD, which he commanded their fathers by the hand of Moses" ([[Law of Moses]]). Intermarriage with peoples outside the covenant becomes the occasion for [[Apostasy]] (3:6-7) — the text draws a direct narrative line from marriage choices to idol worship to divine judgment, though it does not analyze every social cause involved.

Both deliverance accounts turn on the same hinge: Israel's crying out to the LORD (3:9, 15) precedes the raising up of a deliverer. The text records distress and a cry, not an explicit confession or turning away from sin, so this is better read as a national pattern of appeal-then-relief than equated with [[Repentance and Forgiveness]]. The chapter also coordinates divine and human [[Agency]]: the LORD leaves nations, strengthens an oppressor, and raises deliverers, while Othniel and Ehud themselves judge, plan, and fight. This national-scale theology of sin-and-deliverance should not be read as a claim that every individual's suffering is caused by personal sin.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
A few terms reward attention in translation:
- **'Lefthanded' (3:15)** — the underlying Hebrew idiom describes someone 'bound/restricted in his right hand,' i.e., someone who could not use the right hand as expected — the same idiom used of the 700 Benjamite slingers in Judges 20:16.
- **'Chushan-rishathaim' (3:8, 10)** — the second element resembles a Hebrew word for wickedness in an intensified or dual form, which many read as a mocking epithet layered onto (or replacing) the king's actual name, though this is debated.
- **'Covereth his feet' (3:24)** — a standard Hebrew euphemism for relieving oneself, used here with dramatic irony since the reader already knows the true, fatal reason for the locked door.
- **'Eglon' (3:17)** — possibly evoking the Hebrew word for a young bull or fattened calf, which if intentional would make the king's name itself foreshadow the description of him as 'a very fat man,' though the name's etymology is not settled.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The Othniel and Ehud accounts share the same cycle formula but differ sharply in narrative texture: Othniel's victory is summarized in a few verses, while Ehud's is told through concealed weaponry, private space, misdirection, delay, and battlefield strategy.

Ehud's left-handedness (3:15) is repeatedly paired with a dagger worn on his right thigh (3:16) and drawn with his left hand (3:21), which plausibly helped him retrieve the weapon undetected — though the text never mentions a weapons search, so any account of guards checking the "usual" side is a modern reconstruction, not something the narrative states. His approach to Eglon layers deception in stages — a public tribute, a dismissal of attendants, a claimed "secret errand," and finally "a message from God" (3:19-20) — each phrase technically true while concealing his intent; the phrase invites an ironic reading given what follows, though the narrator never flags it as wordplay.

The killing itself (3:21-22) is described with unusual physical detail: the blade disappears as "the fat closed upon the blade," and something (rendered "the dirt" in the KJV) escapes — a phrase that is textually difficult and rendered variously across translations. Together with the servants' assumption that the locked door meant Eglon was relieving himself (3:24), many readers see deliberate dark humor humiliating the oppressor in death. This reading is common in literary-critical scholarship but remains an interpretive judgment about tone, not a textual certainty; a more conservative reading treats the same details as vivid, forensic narration.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary-Pattern Evidence
> The repeated ordering of apostasy, oppression, appeal, deliverance, and rest is strong internal evidence that the narrator presents Israel's political fortunes through a theological framework of disobedience and deliverance.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted (Deuteronomistic judges-cycle pattern) · [[Literary-Pattern Evidence in Judges 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Philological/Literary *(tentative)*
> The oppressor's name 'Chushan-rishathaim king of Mesopotamia' is unusually elaborate compared to other named oppressors in Judges, and the second element 'rishathaim' resembles the Hebrew word for wickedness in a dual/intensified form, leading many commentators to read the name as a derisive epithet ('Cushan of double wickedness') rather than, or in addition to, a straightforward historical name.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, under-cited

> [!evidence] Philological *(tentative)*
> Some Hebraists note that the king's name 'Eglon' shares its consonantal root with the Hebrew word for a young bull or fattened calf ('egel'), which would make 'a very fat man' named 'Eglon' a fitting wordplay reinforced by the description of his death; this etymological connection is plausible but not certain, since 'Eglon' most directly derives from a root meaning 'circle/round,' also plausibly consistent with rotundity.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · speculative, uncited

> [!evidence] Literary-Critical *(tentative)*
> Many literary readings of the Ehud narrative treat its graphic and scatological details — Eglon's fat swallowing the blade, the escaping bodily contents, and the servants concluding their king was merely 'covering his feet' (a euphemism for relieving himself) while he lay dead — as intentional dark comedy that humiliates the oppressor king in death, a technique attested elsewhere in ancient Near Eastern and biblical satire of enemies.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed literary reading

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Judges 2:20-23 already gives a divine-testing rationale for the remaining nations, so is the fuller version in 3:1-4 (adding the war-training purpose) a complementary layer, or does it sit in tension with the incomplete-conquest account in Judges 1?
- What does it mean that Israel's cry for help (3:9, 15), not an explicit confession of sin, is what triggers deliverance in both cycles?
- The narrator calls Ehud a deliverer raised by the LORD, and Ehud calls his approach "a message from God" (3:20) — but no divine speech in the chapter explicitly commands the deception or the killing. Does being a divinely raised deliverer imply divine endorsement of every tactic used to accomplish it?
- Why is Ehud's story narrated in such graphic, extended detail while Othniel's and Shamgar's are compressed into a few verses or one?
- Does the arguably comic detail in the Ehud account (3:21-24) serve a theological purpose, a political one (delegitimizing Moab's king), or simply vivid storytelling?
