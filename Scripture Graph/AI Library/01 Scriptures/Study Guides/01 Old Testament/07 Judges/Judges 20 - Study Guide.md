# Judges 20 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Judges 20]] · My notes: [[Judges 20 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Judges 20 narrates a national civil war triggered by the atrocity at Gibeah recounted in [[Judges 19]]. All Israel gathers 'as one man' at Mizpeh to hear the Levite's account of the crime — though his retelling omits his own decision to surrender the concubine to the mob. When Benjamin refuses to hand over the guilty men of Gibeah, the tribes go to war against their own brethren. Despite consulting the LORD before each battle, Israel loses the first two engagements at a combined cost of forty thousand men; only after fasting and offering sacrifice does a third inquiry bring a promise of victory, won through an ambush that all but annihilates the tribe of Benjamin. The narrator credits the LORD with the battlefield victory (20:35) but does not explicitly command the wholesale slaughter of Benjamite cities, people, and animals that follows in verse 48 — a gap the chapter leaves unresolved rather than smoothed over.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Assembly and testimony (vv. 1–11):** Israel gathers “as one man,” hears the Levite’s selective account, and resolves to act.
2. **Demand and mobilization (vv. 12–17):** Israel demands the Gibeah offenders; Benjamin refuses and prepares for war.
3. **Two defeats (vv. 18–25):** Israel consults the LORD but loses forty thousand sword-bearing men across two days.
4. **Fasting, sacrifice, and a third inquiry (vv. 26–28):** The people ask whether to continue or cease and receive a promise of victory.
5. **Ambush and collapse (vv. 29–47):** A feigned retreat and burning-city signal reverse the battle; six hundred Benjaminites escape.
6. **Destruction of the cities (v. 48):** Israel extends the violence to city populations, animals, and settlements.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter is relevant to [[Revelation]], [[Fasting]], [[Sacrifice]], [[Justice and Mercy]], and [[War and Peace]], but it resists simple moral formulas. Divine guidance is followed first by loss rather than immediate success. Later, the narrator attributes victory to the LORD, yet does not explicitly attribute every action of the victors to divine command. Careful reading should therefore distinguish God’s reported answer from Israel’s subsequent choices.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Judges 20 reuses earlier biblical patterns in increasingly disturbing settings. Its opening inquiry echoes [[Judges 1]], where Judah is appointed to lead against the Canaanites; here Judah leads against Benjamin. Its final ambush echoes [[Joshua 8]], but the conquest tactic is now directed against an Israelite city. The repeated “as one man” language initially presents national unity, yet that unity helps produce catastrophic civil violence. The chapter also lets the Levite tell the initiating story without correcting his omission in the speech itself; comparison with [[Judges 19]] is essential for evaluating his testimony.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Narrative Theological Attribution
> The text strongly supports the limited claim that the narrator attributes Benjamin’s third-day defeat to the LORD. It provides substantially less support for claiming that God approved every Israelite decision, especially the later killing of city populations and animals, because no divine command for that broader destruction is reported.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strongest, most careful claim in the set · [[Narrative Theological Attribution in Judges 20|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Text-Critical/Demographic *(tentative)*
> The chapter reports troop figures (400,000 Israelite swordsmen, 26,700 Benjamites, tens of thousands of casualties per battle) that are strikingly large for Iron Age Israelite population estimates, a tension long noted in biblical scholarship.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the first inquiry ask who should go first rather than whether Israel should begin the war?
- What changes between Israel’s first consultation and its fasting, sacrifices, and willingness to ask whether it should cease?
- Does the promise of victory in verse 28 authorize the actions in verse 48, or does the absence of an explicit command matter?
- How should the differing Benjaminite totals in verses 15, 35, and 44–47 be understood?
- What does the reuse of the Ai strategy communicate when Israelites employ it against Israelites?
