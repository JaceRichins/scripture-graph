## Overview

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **National crisis and family setting (13:1–2):** Philistine oppression and the barrenness of Manoah's wife.
2. **First announcement (13:3–7):** The woman receives instructions and learns the child's calling.
3. **Prayer and confirmation (13:8–14):** Manoah asks for instruction; God answers through a second appearance to the woman.
4. **Offering and recognition (13:15–23):** Hospitality becomes [[Sacrifice]], the angel ascends in the flame, and the couple interpret what they have witnessed.
5. **Birth and transition (13:24–25):** Samson is born, blessed, and first moved by the Spirit of the LORD.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The narrative connects [[Prayer]] with divine response: God hearkens to Manoah's petition, although the answer comes by appearing again to the wife first (13:9), preserving her role as the original recipient of [[Revelation]]. The chapter also directs worship toward the LORD rather than the messenger: the angel refuses Manoah's food and insists any burnt offering be made "unto the LORD" (13:16), and Manoah's [[Sacrifice]] follows this instruction.

The Nazarite restrictions given here (13:4-5,7,14) — no wine or strong drink, no unclean food for the mother during pregnancy, no razor for the child — draw on the law of the Nazarite in [[Numbers 6]], though Samson's case is unusual: the consecration is announced before birth, appears to be lifelong, and the dietary restrictions are placed on the mother rather than expressed as a vow spoken by Samson himself. This is a distinct category from the modern [[Word of Wisdom]]; both involve abstention from wine and strong drink, but the Nazarite vow is an individually-imposed consecration under the Law of Moses, not a general health law for a covenant people, and the two should not be treated as equivalent practices.

The promise that Samson will only "begin" Israel's deliverance (13:5) sets a restrained expectation from the outset: divine calling and empowerment are real in this chapter, but they do not by themselves guarantee that every later act of Samson will be exemplary.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"Nazarite" denotes a person specially separated or consecrated to God; [[Numbers 6]] is the primary legal comparison. Judges 13 modifies the ordinary presentation by locating the consecration "from the womb" and imposing prenatal restrictions on the mother.

The KJV renders the angel's response about his name as "secret" (13:18) and the following action as "wondrously" (13:19). The underlying Hebrew words share the same root (associated with wonder/extraordinariness), producing a wordplay in which the messenger declines an ordinary name and is instead disclosed through a wondrous act. This wordplay exists only at the Hebrew level and is not visible in the KJV's English wording — a reader working from the English text alone will not see the connection between "secret" and "wondrously." It suggests disclosure through action but does not by itself identify the angel's name or settle his metaphysical identity.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The unnamed woman is the chapter's most reliable human interpreter. She receives the announcement, accurately conveys its essential content (with the notable addition of "to the day of his death," 13:7, not present in the angel's recorded words to her in 13:5), immediately fetches Manoah when the messenger returns, and counters his fear of death with an argument grounded in what the LORD has accepted, shown, and promised (13:23). This narrative prominence is observable, though broader claims about the author's view of gender would require evidence beyond this chapter.

By contrast, Manoah is repeatedly marked as slow to perceive: the narrator states plainly that "Manoah knew not that he was an angel of the LORD" (13:16) and only "knew" after the angel ascended in the flame (13:21). When he asks the angel's name, he is told only that it is "secret" (13:18) — a refusal of self-disclosure that recalls, without necessarily depending on, the similar refusal of the divine being who wrestles with Jacob in [[Genesis 32]].

The encounter also echoes [[Judges 6]], where Gideon prepares food for an initially unrecognized messenger, witnesses a fire-associated sign, recognizes the angel of the LORD, and fears death at having seen him — a substantial literary parallel, though it cannot by itself determine whether one account directly borrowed from the other.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Legal And Intertextual Context
> Numbers 6 supplies the clearest legal context for the word 'Nazarite': both passages associate consecration with avoiding products of the vine and leaving the hair uncut. Judges 13 nevertheless adapts the pattern by making the consecration prenatal and divinely announced and by extending restrictions to the mother.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted, well-hedged · [[Legal And Intertextual Context in Judges 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary/Narrative Parallel
> Judges 13:19-20 (a kid offered on a rock, the angel of the LORD ascending in the flame from the altar) closely parallels Judges 6:19-21 (Gideon's offering on a rock consumed by fire, after which the angel of the LORD departs), and both accounts are followed immediately by the human witness's terrified realization that they have seen a divine being (Judges 13:22; Judges 6:22-23).
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · broadly accepted, properly hedged · [[Literary/Narrative Parallel in Judges 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intrabook Literary Parallel
> The encounter resembles Gideon's commissioning in Judges 6: an initially unrecognized divine messenger, preparation of food or an offering, a miraculous fire-associated sign, recognition of the messenger, and fear connected with seeing the angel of the LORD. The repetitions form a credible literary parallel within Judges.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · affirmed, properly hedged · [[Intrabook Literary Parallel in Judges 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Lexical/Etymological Connection *(tentative)*
> When asked his name, the angel replies 'Why askest thou thus after my name, seeing it is secret?' (Hebrew pil'i); some scholars connect this root to the word pele ('wonderful') used of the messianic figure in Isaiah 9:6, though this proposed intertextual echo is speculative rather than demonstrated.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.3 · speculative, unsourced

> [!evidence] Etymological/Onomastic *(tentative)*
> The name Samson (Hebrew Shimshon) is sometimes linked by scholars to shemesh ('sun'), with some proposing solar imagery connected to the nearby town of Beth-shemesh; this etymology and any associated solar-cult reading remain a minority, speculative position rather than an established conclusion.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why is Manoah's wife left unnamed throughout the chapter while her husband is named, and what (if anything) does that signal about the narrative's interests?
- Why does the text say Samson will only "begin" Israel's deliverance?
- Why does the messenger answer Manoah's question about raising the child by repeating instructions directed chiefly to the mother?
- Did the woman receive additional information about lifelong consecration, or does "to the day of his death" (13:7) represent her inference or the narrator's compressed reporting?
- What distinction does the narrative maintain — or leave unresolved — between seeing "the angel of the LORD" and Manoah's claim to have "seen God" (13:22)?
- How should the LORD's blessing and the Spirit's activity in verses 24-25 affect, or not affect, evaluation of Samson's later conduct?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the Nazarite regulations in [[Numbers 6]], the shared no-razor clause and barrenness-to-promised-son pattern in [[1 Samuel 1]], and the angelic birth announcement (including abstention from wine and strong drink) in [[Luke 1]]. For the closest encounter pattern within Judges, compare the offering, sign, recognition, and fear in [[Judges 6]]; for the motif of a divine being declining to give its name, compare [[Genesis 32]]. These comparisons illuminate recurring motifs, but similarities alone do not demonstrate direct literary dependence.
