# 2 Chronicles 26 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Uzziah reigns and prospers as long as he keeps the commandments—He transgresses, attempts to burn incense upon the altar, and is cursed with leprosy.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Accession and governing criterion (verses 1–5):** Uzziah becomes king, acts rightly, and prospers while seeking the Lord.
2. **Expansion and strength (verses 6–15):** Victories, fame, fortifications, agriculture, administration, arms, and defensive technology accumulate.
3. **The hinge (verse 16):** “When he was strong,” pride and temple trespass begin his reversal.
4. **Confrontation and judgment (verses 17–20):** Azariah and eighty priests resist the king; his anger is followed by visible affliction and removal.
5. **Separation, record, and succession (verses 21–23):** Jotham governs, Isaiah is named as a recorder, and Uzziah receives a qualified royal burial.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The narrative sharply limits royal authority. Uzziah can command armies and strengthen a kingdom, but he cannot appropriate an ordinance assigned to Aaron's consecrated descendants. This makes the chapter relevant to [[Aaronic Priesthood]], [[Priesthood]], and [[Temples]]: zeal, status, and power do not substitute for authorization.

The account also offers a sustained warning about [[Pride]]. Divine help is acknowledged repeatedly, yet strength becomes dangerous when Uzziah treats it as permission to exceed his office. His earlier prosperity should not be generalized into a promise that every obedient person will enjoy material or military success; it is the narrator's interpretation of this particular reign.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The most consequential verbal sequence is “till he was strong” (verse 15) followed by “when he was strong” (verse 16). Strength itself is not condemned—the preceding achievements are repeatedly associated with divine help—but it becomes the setting in which Uzziah's heart is lifted up.

The KJV's “leprosy” should not automatically be read as a modern medical diagnosis of Hansen's disease. The underlying biblical category covers conditions evaluated in terms of visible symptoms and ritual status. The text permits the observations that the condition appeared on Uzziah's forehead, resulted in separation, and continued until death; it does not provide enough clinical information for a secure modern diagnosis.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's rise-and-reversal design is unusually concentrated. “His name spread abroad” appears during expansion (verse 8), “his name spread far abroad” crowns the achievement catalogue (verse 15), and the next sentence overturns that public greatness through private exaltation of heart (verse 16). The same strength that completes the success section opens the destruction section. This repetition makes the moral interpretation integral to the narrative's construction rather than an incidental comment appended to a list of royal acts.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrator say “as long as” Uzziah sought the Lord rather than simply describing him as faithful?
- Which strengths in verses 6–15 later become possible occasions for self-exaltation?
- What enables the priests to confront a king, and why are they described as valiant?
- How does Jotham's administrative role complicate the distinction between holding the royal title and exercising day-to-day rule?
- What can responsibly be inferred from Isaiah's source notice, and what remains unknown about that record?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the condensed regnal account in [[2 Kings 15]] with the expanded explanation in this chapter. [[Exodus 30]] and [[Numbers 18]] clarify why the priests regard incense service as restricted. [[Numbers 16]] supplies a more distant parallel involving unauthorized incense and priestly boundaries. [[Isaiah 6]] dates Isaiah's temple vision to the year of Uzziah's death and thus places prophetic calling beside a major royal transition.
