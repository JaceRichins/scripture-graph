# 2 Chronicles 25 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Chronicles 25]] · My notes: [[2 Chronicles 25 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Amaziah reigns, smites the Edomites, and worships false gods—A prophet foretells Amaziah’s destruction—Judah is defeated by Israel, and Amaziah is slain in a conspiracy.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Qualified accession and lawful retribution (vv. 1–4):** Amaziah receives a mixed evaluation and limits punishment to the guilty.
2. **Army, warning, and costly compliance (vv. 5–10):** He hires Israelite troops but dismisses them at the word of a man of God.
3. **Victory and its violent aftermath (vv. 11–13):** Judah defeats Seir and kills captives, while the dismissed soldiers raid Judah.
4. **Idolatry and rejected correction (vv. 14–16):** Amaziah worships the gods of Seir and silences a prophet.
5. **Pride, war, and humiliation (vv. 17–24):** Joash warns him through a parable, but Amaziah persists and Judah falls.
6. **Closing record and assassination (vv. 25–28):** The account cites another royal record and connects the conspiracy with Amaziah's departure from the LORD.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects [[Obedience]] with willingness to act despite financial loss: Amaziah dismisses troops for whom he has already paid one hundred talents (vv. 6–10). Yet their retaliatory raid (v. 13) complicates any claim that obedience always prevents immediate suffering.

The written [[Law of Moses]] limits royal vengeance by protecting the children of guilty people from execution for their parents' offense (vv. 3–4). That judicial rule should not be expanded without qualification into a claim that families never experience consequences from another person's actions.

The chapter also portrays [[Prophets]] as confronting both military policy and idolatry. Amaziah first listens, then threatens a prophet, and finally refuses Joash's warning. His decline therefore concerns his response to correction as well as his formal worship.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The narrative uses sharp irony: gods unable to save Seir become the gods Amaziah expects to worship (vv. 14–15). It also uses Joash's compact thistle-and-cedar parable to depict disproportionate ambition and foreshadow defeat (vv. 18–23). The sequence from victory to [[Pride]] to humiliation supplies the governing reversal.

Comparison with [[2 Kings 14]] is especially useful. Both accounts contain the legal restraint, royal challenge, parable, defeat, plunder, and assassination, while Chronicles greatly expands the material about the mercenaries, prophets, and Edomite gods. Those additions make divided allegiance and rejected counsel much more prominent in the Chronicler's presentation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why did Amaziah adopt the gods of a people those gods had failed to deliver? The prophet exposes the contradiction, but the narrative does not state Amaziah's motive.
- How should verse 20's assertion of divine purpose be read alongside Amaziah's repeated decisions and culpability?
- What ethical judgment should readers make about the killing of the captives in verse 12, given that the narrator reports it without an explicit command or approval?
- How does the opening phrase about an imperfect heart guide the reading of Amaziah's early acts of obedience and later [[Apostasy]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
