# 2 Chronicles 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Chronicles 6]] · My notes: [[2 Chronicles 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Solomon blesses Israel and presents the completed temple as fulfillment of God's promise to David. His dedicatory prayer does not portray the building as containing God: heaven remains God's dwelling, while the temple is the place of God's name and the earthly focus of Israel's [[Prayer]]. Solomon then considers concrete future crises—legal accusation, defeat, drought, famine, disease, war, and exile—and repeatedly asks God to hear, judge, forgive, and restore. The prayer also makes room for a foreign worshipper whose appeal may lead all peoples to know God.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has four principal movements:

1. **Temple and fulfilled promise (vv. 1-11):** Solomon blesses the congregation, recalls the promise to David, and identifies the ark with the covenant.
2. **Invocation (vv. 12-21):** Solomon kneels, praises the covenant-keeping God, invokes the Davidic promise, and acknowledges that the temple cannot contain God.
3. **Seven case petitions (vv. 22-39):** oath, defeat, drought, calamity, the foreigner, war, and captivity. Variations of “hear thou from heaven” unify the cases.
4. **Concluding appeal (vv. 40-42):** Solomon asks for divine attention and invokes language closely related to [[Psalm 132]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Temples]] with divine transcendence. The house matters because God has chosen the city and placed his name there, but God's effective presence is not confined to its walls. The repeated movement from sin through confession or return to forgiveness gives the prayer a covenantal account of [[Repentance and Forgiveness]]. At the same time, forgiveness is not presented as indifference to conduct: God judges, knows hearts, and renders according to each person's ways, holding together [[Justice and Mercy]].

The foreigner's petition widens the temple's horizon beyond Israel. Its stated purpose is that all peoples may know God's name. This supports a universal dimension to Israel's worship, though it does not by itself erase distinctions between Israel and the stranger.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three recurring expressions organize the prayer. **“For my name”** describes the temple's consecrated association with God. **“Hear thou from heaven”** locates God's dwelling beyond the temple even while prayers are made in or toward it. **“Turn”** and **“return”** describe repentance as reorientation toward God, not merely regret. These expressions prevent a simplistic reading in which the building mechanically guarantees divine favor.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The prayer closely parallels [[1 Kings 8]] in content, structure, and theology — the same praise of covenant-keeping, the same admission that heaven cannot contain God, and the same case-by-case petitions — though the wording is not verbatim identical throughout (e.g., 2 Chronicles 6:18 conveys the same point as 1 Kings 8:27 in different phrasing). This points to a shared literary or liturgical tradition rather than a word-for-word transcript; the direction of dependence cannot be settled from the parallel alone. The closing appeal (vv.41-42) adapts [[Psalm 132]] rather than reproducing it word for word: 'rest' becomes 'resting place,' and the description of worshippers shifts from 'clothed with righteousness'/'shout for joy' (Psalm 132:9) to 'clothed with salvation'/'rejoice in goodness' (2 Chronicles 6:41) — a real substitution, not just phrasing variance. These differences caution against calling either relationship an exact quotation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Solomon direct prayer toward the temple while repeatedly identifying heaven as God's dwelling place?
- Which petitions explicitly mention repentance, and which ask for judgment or aid without doing so?
- What does the prayer for the stranger include, and what conclusions about covenant membership would go beyond its actual wording?
- How does the condition attached to Davidic succession in verse 16 qualify the surrounding language of promise and fulfillment?
- Does the captivity petition function only as a hypothetical warning, or does its placement in Chronicles also invite later readers to remember Israel's exile?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare this chapter closely with its parallel in [[1 Kings 8]] to see where Chronicles adds, omits, or reshapes material — most notably the brasen scaffold detail (v.13, unique to Chronicles) and the closing appeal that echoes [[Psalm 132]] rather than the ending preserved in 1 Kings 8:54-61. Reading [[2 Chronicles 7]] alongside this chapter shows how God's fiery answer and subsequent oracle respond to some of the scenarios Solomon raises here, including drought and pestilence — though not every case, such as captivity, is explicitly answered there.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
