## Overview

> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The faithful Israelites overthrow false worship among them—The people pay tithes and offerings—The Levites administer in temporal matters—Hezekiah serves faithfully.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Removal of rival worship (v. 1):** Participants extend reform beyond Jerusalem and then return home.

2. **Restoration and support of worship (vv. 2–4):** Courses are appointed, the king supplies his portion, and the people are commanded to support sanctuary personnel.

3. **Abundant response (vv. 5–10):** Firstfruits, tithes, and consecrated things accumulate from the third through the seventh months.

4. **Stewardship and distribution (vv. 11–19):** Chambers, supervisors, records, courses, and local officers ensure that resources reach eligible ministers and households.

5. **Narrative evaluation (vv. 20–21):** Hezekiah is commended for goodness, truth, wholeheartedness, and seeking God.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter treats [[Tithing]] as part of a larger ecology of worship. Gifts sustain those assigned to the [[Temples]], but the account also stresses lawful use, faithful administration, and equitable distribution. Hezekiah's personal contribution precedes his command to the people, presenting leadership as both example and governance.

The text associates wholehearted [[Obedience]] with prosperity, but its narrative conclusion should not be converted into a universal formula promising material wealth to every obedient person. Azariah's claim that God blessed the people is the chapter's theological interpretation of this particular surplus.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several distinctions deserve preservation. Verse 5 names both **firstfruits** and **the tithe of all things**; verses 6 and 12 also speak of holy or dedicated things. These expressions overlap in supporting worship but are not simply interchangeable.

The repeated language of **courses**, **charges**, **genealogy**, and persons **expressed by name** gives the second half of the chapter an administrative register. The phrase “good and right and truth” in verse 20 functions as the narrator's moral evaluation of the whole program.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
One useful way to read the chapter's movement is from removal to ordered provision: rival worship is dismantled in verse 1, while authorized worship is then staffed, supplied, stored, and administered. The visible heaps signify abundant communal response, but verses 11–19 shift attention from abundance to stewardship and distribution. This progression supports a literary emphasis on zeal joined with order, though it does not by itself prove the exact historical sequence of implementation. The closing evaluation in verses 20–21 is the narrator's theological judgment of Hezekiah's conduct, not an independent measurement of outcomes.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrative place the destruction of rival worship immediately before the organization and financing of temple worship?

- What does the king's own contribution add to the meaning of his later command to the people?

- Why might the Chronicler preserve so many names, offices, age thresholds, and distribution procedures?

- How does verse 18 broaden the social meaning of support beyond the individual priest or Levite?

- Which parts of the chapter describe events, and which parts explicitly interpret those events as divine blessing or prosperity?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the offering calendar in 2 Chronicles 31:3 with [[Numbers 28]] and [[Numbers 29]], and the support of priests and Levites with [[Numbers 18]]. Compare the priestly and Levitical courses with [[1 Chronicles 23]] and [[1 Chronicles 24]], and the later collection and storage system with [[Nehemiah 10]]. Reading [[2 Chronicles 29]], [[2 Chronicles 30]], and [[2 Chronicles 31]] together shows the Chronicler's narrative sequence of temple purification, communal Passover, removal of rival worship, and institutional stabilization. These comparisons establish literary and legal context; they do not by themselves settle the date or historical origin of every administrative detail.
