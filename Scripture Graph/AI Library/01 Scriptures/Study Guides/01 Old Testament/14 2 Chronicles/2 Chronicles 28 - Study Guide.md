# 2 Chronicles 28 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Chronicles 28]] · My notes: [[2 Chronicles 28 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter portrays Judah's collapse through Ahaz's sustained [[Apostasy]], but its moral judgment is not one-sided. Judah is punished for forsaking the LORD, while victorious Israel is condemned for rage and intended enslavement. Oded's warning is followed by the captives' release and concrete care, showing [[Justice and Mercy]] in action. The narrative then returns to Ahaz: Assyrian aid fails, and his appeal to foreign gods deepens rather than relieves his ruin.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Ahaz's initial apostasy (vv. 1–4):** the reign opens with a concentrated indictment.
2. **Defeat and captivity (vv. 5–8):** Syria and Israel inflict severe losses on Judah.
3. **Oded's warning and restoration (vv. 9–15):** Israel's excess is condemned, and the captives are released, cared for, and returned.
4. **Further invasions and failed Assyrian help (vv. 16–21):** Judah is brought low, and Ahaz's payment obtains no effective aid.
5. **Intensified apostasy (vv. 22–25):** Ahaz turns further to foreign gods, damages temple vessels, closes the temple, and multiplies rival altars.
6. **Death and succession (vv. 26–27):** Ahaz is excluded from the royal sepulchres—probably implying diminished honor—and Hezekiah succeeds him.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds [[Justice and Mercy]] together. Judah's guilt does not authorize Israel's cruelty, and being permitted to prevail does not exempt the victors from moral accountability. The leaders acknowledge Israel's existing guilt and reverse a harmful course: the captives are released, the spoil surrendered, immediate needs supplied, and vulnerable people returned home. This conduct gives [[Repentance]] a practical dimension, although the text does not reveal whether fear, compassion, or mixed motives prompted it. Ahaz provides the opposite pattern: in [[Adversity]] he trespasses further rather than changing course.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several contrasts and reversals shape the chapter. The returning army relinquishes captives and spoil; the spoil is then used to clothe and restore the captives. Ephraimite leaders acknowledge guilt and reverse course, whereas Ahaz responds to distress by trespassing further. Repeated references to failed “help” create narrative irony: Assyria does not help Ahaz, and the gods he expects to help become ruinous. These patterns support deliberate literary contrast, though chronological arrangement and the Chronicler's broader theology may also account for them. [[2 Kings 16]] provides the principal parallel account.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does Oded's warning challenge the assumption that military victory proves moral or divine approval?
- Why does Oded emphasize both Israel's existing sins and the captives' identity as brethren?
- Which actions in verse 15 constitute restitution, and which go beyond it into [[Service]]?
- What contrast, if any, does the chapter create between the leaders' response to warning and Ahaz's response to distress?
- How do the failures of Assyria and the gods of Damascus expose the weakness in Ahaz's search for help?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
