# Ezekiel 13 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 13]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 13 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Ezekiel reproves false prophets, both male and female, who speak lies, to whom God has not spoken.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Commission and accusation (13:1–7):** Ezekiel is told to confront prophets whose visions originate in themselves.
2. **Judgment and failed protection (13:8–9):** The prophets face divine opposition and exclusion.
3. **Wall and storm image (13:10–16):** False peace is exposed when the wall collapses under judgment.
4. **Oracle against the daughters of the people (13:17–21):** Their objects are torn away and those they hunt are released.
5. **Moral consequences and judgment (13:22–23):** Their lies grieve the righteous, reinforce wickedness, and obstruct [[Repentance]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter frames [[Prophecy]] and [[Revelation]] as something that must be *sent*, not merely *felt*: the condemned prophets are indicted not simply for insincerity but for lacking genuine commission (13:2-3, 6-7) — invoking the LORD's name does not make a message divine. Verse 22 grounds this in real moral consequence: false reassurance "made the heart of the righteous sad, whom I have not made sad," and "strengthened the hands of the wicked... by promising him life," obstructing [[Repentance]]. This shows lying prophecy causes concrete harm; it should not be over-read as a complete, transferable test for identifying false prophets in every case.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The central image of a wall coated with "untempered mortar" (13:10-15) pictures an inadequate coating papering over a structurally unsound wall — reassurance that cannot survive real testing; the exact composition of the coating is debated among translators and should not be pinned to a single technical gloss. The Hebrew terms behind "pillows" (13:18, 20) and "kerchiefs" (13:18, 21) are rare and disputed — proposals range from magic bands or amulets to veils used in divination — and the chapter itself does not explain their form or function, so any specific reconstruction should be held loosely.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly turns claimed protection into exposure: the prophets fail to repair Israel's defenses (13:5), disguise an unstable wall (13:10-16), and ensnare rather than protect people (13:18-21). The recognition formula "ye shall know that I am the LORD" recurs four times (13:9, 14, 21, 23), but this refrain is common throughout Ezekiel and is best read as characteristic diction rather than a structural device unique to this chapter. The closest internal parallel is [[Ezekiel 22]], especially its cluster of untempered mortar, vain visions, and lying divination (22:28); [[Jeremiah 23]] independently develops the same charge that prophets speak "a vision of their own heart" rather than the LORD's word.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual-Theological Argument *(tentative)*
> Verse 22 grounds the condemnation of false prophecy in its real-world moral effects rather than only its false claim of divine source: the false prophets/diviners 'made the heart of the righteous sad, whom I have not made sad,' and 'strengthened the hands of the wicked... by promising him life.' This frames false prophecy as harmful because it inverts the intended moral effects of true revelation (comfort withheld from the righteous, false comfort given to the unrepentant wicked), supporting a doctrine that prophetic legitimacy is tested partly by consequence, not merely by the speaker's sincerity or popularity.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · moral harm in v22 solid; 'test of legitimacy' overreaches

> [!evidence] Internal Literary Pattern *(tentative)*
> The repetition of prophesying from one's own heart, the declaration that the LORD is against the practitioners, and the concluding recognition formulas constitute internal evidence that verses 2–16 and 17–23 are presented as coordinated indictments. This establishes literary correspondence in the received chapter more securely than it establishes any particular theory of composition.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · refrain is book-wide diction, not bespoke chapter design

> [!evidence] Historical-Contextual Inference *(tentative)*
> Ezekiel prophesied among the Babylonian exiles in the years surrounding Jerusalem's fall (traditionally dated to the reigns spanning roughly 593-571 BC by the book's internal regnal-year datings). Read against that setting, this chapter's polemic against prophets promising 'peace' plausibly targets an optimistic nationalist prophetic party insisting Jerusalem and the exile would be short-lived, a conflict paralleled in Jeremiah's disputes with prophets in Jerusalem and among the exiles.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · plausible historical background; faction claim unsupported
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What specifically were the "pillows" and "kerchiefs," and how did they function in the divination the women practiced?
- Does the chapter offer any positive criteria for recognizing a true prophet, beyond negatively describing what a false one does?
- How does the "peace, and there is no peace" charge relate to the political situation facing Jerusalem before its fall?
- Why does Ezekiel address women prophets in a separate oracle rather than folding them into 13:1-16 — does this reflect a distinct social role or practice?
