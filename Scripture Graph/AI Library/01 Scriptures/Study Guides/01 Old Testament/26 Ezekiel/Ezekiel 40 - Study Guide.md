## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The tour moves outward-to-inward, gate by gate, and can be read in five stages:
1. **Vision and commission (vv.1-4)** — the date is fixed, Ezekiel is transported to Israel, and commanded to observe and report everything to the house of Israel.
2. **East outer gate (vv.5-16)** — the measuring reed is calibrated (a cubit plus a handbreadth), then the gate's chambers, porch, posts, windows, and palm trees are measured in full detail, establishing the pattern repeated at every later gate.
3. **Outer court and its north/south gates (vv.17-27)** — the pavement and thirty chambers of the outer court, then the north and south outer gates, each matching the east gate's dimensions (fifty by twenty-five cubits) and reached by seven steps.
4. **Inner court gates and sacrificial facilities (vv.28-43)** — the south, east, and north inner gates repeat the same fifty-by-twenty-five-cubit plan but are reached by eight steps; nearby are the washing area and eight tables for slaying burnt, sin, and trespass offerings.
5. **Priestly chambers, court, and porch (vv.44-49)** — chambers are assigned by duty (general priestly service vs. altar service reserved to 'the sons of Zadok,' v.46), the inner court is measured as a 100-cubit square with the altar before the house, and the tour reaches the porch of the house itself, which [[Ezekiel 41]] will enter.

The repeated 50x25-cubit gate module and the outer/inner step difference (seven vs. eight) give the complex a symmetrical, ordered plan; the text itself does not explain why the step count differs.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter contributes most directly to the biblical presentation of [[Temples]], [[Sacrifice]], [[Priesthood]], and [[Revelation]]. Sacred service is not portrayed as spatially or institutionally undifferentiated: gates regulate approach, ascending stairs mark progression, particular locations serve particular offerings, and designated priests keep the charges of the house and altar. These are secure observations about the visionary complex. Applying them to later temple systems requires a separate interpretive argument.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated formulas—especially descriptions made “according to these measures” and lists of chambers, posts, arches, windows, and palm trees—give the chapter the cadence of a measured survey. “Cubit and an hand breadth” defines the unit used by the reed but does not supply a universally certain modern conversion. Several KJV architectural terms, including “arches,” “posts,” and “little chambers,” should therefore be handled cautiously when reconstructing a floor plan.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Three features stand out. First, the vision supplies its own measuring standard before any measuring begins: the reed is fixed at six cubits, each a common cubit plus a handbreadth (v.5), so the account does not assume a shared unit with the reader. Second, the narrative functions as both report and guided tour — motion verbs carry Ezekiel progressively inward while repeated, formulaic measurements (chambers, posts, arches, windows, palm trees, 'according to these measures') slow the pace and invite comparison between gates. Third, the recurring image of a guide measuring a sacred space with a reed — and, elsewhere in this same vision, a line ([[Ezekiel 47]]) — has parallels in other prophetic and apocalyptic texts (Zechariah 2; Revelation 11; Revelation 21) that put a heavenly figure to work surveying a holy city or temple. It is a recognizable motif, but its precise significance varies by context and should not be flattened into one fixed meaning.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical Comparison *(tentative)*
> The temple plan measured in Ezekiel 40-42 was not constructed to these specifications by the returning exiles, whose Second Temple (per Ezra 3) was comparatively modest and did not match this design; this gap is commonly read as evidence that the vision functions as an idealized, symbolic, or eschatological pattern of divine order rather than a literal, immediately executable architectural blueprint.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Was this temple plan ever meant to be built as described, or is it a symbolic/idealized pattern — and if literal, is its fulfillment past, unrealized, or still future?
- Why do the outer gates have seven steps while the inner gates have eight (vv.22, 26, 31, 34, 37)? The pattern is clear; the text offers no stated reason.
- Is the bronze-appearing measuring man (v.3) an angel, or some other kind of visionary figure? Ezekiel calls him only 'a man' and never states his nature.
- What is signified by assigning one priestly chamber to general 'keepers of the charge of the house' and a separate chamber specifically to 'the sons of Zadok' as 'keepers of the charge of the altar' (vv.45-46), and how do the singers' chambers of v.44 relate to this pair?
- How much confidence should a modern floor plan place in KJV terms like 'arches,' 'posts,' and the converted length of the 'cubit and an hand breadth' (v.5), given that the underlying architectural vocabulary is not fully certain?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the tabernacle's divinely-given measurements ([[Exodus 26]]) and the Solomonic temple's dimensions and ornamentation ([[1 Kings 7]]; [[2 Chronicles 3]]) as earlier instances of measured sanctuaries. Continue the vision itself into [[Ezekiel 41]]-[[Ezekiel 43]] as the tour reaches the house and the glory of the LORD returns; the line of flax introduced but unused in this chapter (v.3) is finally put to work measuring the river in [[Ezekiel 47]]. Read ahead to [[Ezekiel 44]] for the fuller statement on Zadokite priesthood only briefly signaled here (v.46), and to [[Ezekiel 46]] for how these gates and courts are actually used. For a much later reworking of the measuring-a-holy-space motif — with an explicit and important difference — compare [[Revelation 21]], where the visionary city is measured but 'no temple' is found in it.
