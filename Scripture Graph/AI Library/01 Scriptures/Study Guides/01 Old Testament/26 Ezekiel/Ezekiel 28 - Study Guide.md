# Ezekiel 28 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 28]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 28 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Tyre and Sidon will fall and be destroyed—The Lord will gather the people of Israel to their own land—They will then dwell safely.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The prince's divine claim and mortal end (28:1–10).** Wisdom and commerce produce riches; riches lift the heart; foreign swords expose the claimant as human.
2. **Lament over the king of Tyrus (28:11–19).** Eden, precious stones, the covering cherub, God's mountain, corruption, expulsion, and fire portray extraordinary privilege followed by public ruin.
3. **Oracle against Zidon (28:20–24).** Pestilence and the sword accompany the repeated declaration that the people will know the Lord; hostile neighbors will cease to afflict Israel like thorns.
4. **Israel gathered and settled (28:25–26).** Dispersion gives way to land, houses, vineyards, safety, and confidence.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Pride]] and mortality.** The prince claims divine status, but the Lord answers that he is human and can be killed (28:2, 8–9).
- **Prosperity does not excuse self-exaltation.** Wisdom and commerce bring riches, but judgment follows when riches lift the ruler's heart toward divine pretension (28:4–6). The text condemns this response to prosperity rather than wisdom or wealth in isolation.
- **Judgment reveals divine sovereignty.** One expressly stated result of judgment on Zidon is that people will know the Lord, who will be glorified and sanctified among them (28:22–24).
- **[[Gathering of Israel]].** The chapter closes with Israel gathered from dispersion to the land given to Jacob, where the people build houses and plant vineyards in safety (28:25–26).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated phrase **“heart is lifted up”** links the Tyre units (28:2, 5, 17). In the first, the prince sets his heart as the heart of God; in the lament, beauty lifts the king's heart and brightness corrupts his wisdom. Other contrasts reinforce the movement from exaltation to ruin: God and man, height and pit, holy mountain and ground, splendor and ashes.

The Zidon oracle repeats **“they shall know that I am the LORD”** three times (28:22–24). Related language continues in verses 25–26, where the Lord is sanctified publicly in gathered Israel and recognized after judgment on hostile neighbors.

The KJV calls the lamented figure **“the anointed cherub that covereth”** (28:14). Reports that the underlying Hebrew permits materially different translations are relevant but need textual and scholarly documentation before one rendering is preferred.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The lament's imagery operates first within an oracle addressed to the king of Tyrus. The KJV poetically portrays that king through Eden, precious stones, a covering cherub, God's holy mountain, stones of fire, initial perfection, corruption, expulsion, and destruction. These images need not constitute a literal human biography.

Later readers may see in this pattern an association with Satan's fall through pride. That typological reading is meaningful but disputed: the chapter never names Satan, while its explicit royal address and repeated references to merchandise and traffic preserve the Tyrian setting. The chapter alone therefore does not establish Satan as the direct or exclusive referent.

[[Isaiah 14]] offers a useful comparison because it also applies elevated fall imagery to a proud foreign ruler. [[Genesis 3]] and [[Exodus 28]] provide possible comparisons for Eden, cherubic guardianship, and precious stones, but those parallels do not by themselves prove direct dependence, priestly symbolism, or a shared referent.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary And Referential Evidence
> The Edenic fall, cherub, beauty, and pride imagery can support a typological association with Satan's fall, but the passage explicitly presents itself as a lamentation over the king of Tyrus and repeatedly relates his corruption to merchandise and traffic. The chapter alone therefore does not establish that Satan is its direct or exclusive referent.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Textual limits accepted; typological association disputed · [[Literary And Referential Evidence in Ezekiel 28|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Typological/Theological Interpretation *(tentative)*
> The Eden/cherub/holy-mountain imagery applied to the king of Tyrus (28:12-19) exceeds anything plausible for a literal human biography, which has led many Christian and Latter-day Saint readers to interpret the lament typologically (or, in some readings, as a description with a second referent) as depicting the premortal rebellion and fall of Lucifer/Satan, comparing it to Isaiah 14:12's taunt against the king of Babylon ('How art thou fallen from heaven, O Lucifer, son of the morning'). This reading is disputed: the passage's own addressee throughout is 'the king of Tyrus' (28:12), never named as Satan or Lucifer, and comparable Eden/paradise-of-god and cosmic-mountain imagery is attested in other ancient Near Eastern royal ideology describing human kings, which supports reading the passage as hyperbolic court rhetoric about a mortal ruler rather than a literal statement about a heavenly being.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · Traditional typology; direct referent disputed

> [!evidence] Onomastic/Textual Ambiguity *(tentative)*
> The identity of the 'Daniel' invoked in 28:3 as a wisdom benchmark is not certain. The traditional reading (standard in LDS and most Christian contexts) takes this as the biblical prophet Daniel, a contemporary of Ezekiel already renowned for wisdom in exile. Some scholars instead propose an allusion to an older legendary sage ('Danel'), noting that Ezekiel elsewhere pairs a figure by this name with Noah and Job as ancient non-Israelite paragons of righteousness (Ezekiel 14:14, 20), and that a young contemporary would be an unusual proverbial benchmark for long-established wisdom.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Scholarly dispute; insufficient sourcing supplied

> [!evidence] Textual/Typological Correspondence *(tentative)*
> The nine precious stones listed as covering the king of Tyrus (28:13: sardius, topaz, diamond, beryl, onyx, jasper, sapphire, emerald, carbuncle) are all found among the twelve stones listed for the high priest's breastplate (Exodus 28:17-20), with only three breastplate stones (ligure, agate, amethyst) absent from Ezekiel's list. Some commentators take this overlap to suggest the figure in Eden is being characterized with priestly/Adamic royal-priest imagery; this significance is disputed, since the lists differ in order and count and Hebrew vocabulary for precious stones was limited, making the overlap plausibly coincidental or simply conventional royal-splendor imagery.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · KJV overlap credible; intended significance disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the oracle answer the ruler's claim to sit in the seas with death in the seas (28:2, 8)?
- How do wisdom and economic success become morally dangerous in verses 4–6 without being condemned in themselves?
- Which details in verses 12–19 fit a Tyrian king, which belong to symbolic sacred-space imagery, and which support later typological readings?
- Why is the Lord said to be sanctified both in judged Zidon and in gathered Israel (28:22, 25)?
- What does the concrete language of houses and vineyards add to the promise of restoration (28:26)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Read [[Ezekiel 26]]–[[Ezekiel 28]] together to trace the treatment of Tyre's city, commerce, and rulers.
- Compare [[Ezekiel 29]] for another oracle confronting an exalted foreign ruler and connecting judgment with recognition of the Lord.
- Compare [[Isaiah 14]] for elevated fall imagery applied to a proud foreign king, while keeping each passage's stated addressee in view.
- Compare [[Genesis 3]] for Eden and cherubic guardianship. Treat this as a thematic comparison rather than a demonstrated textual echo.
- Compare [[Exodus 28]] with Ezekiel 28:13 at the level of the KJV gemstone lists. Any claim of deliberate priestly symbolism should remain tentative pending comparison of the Hebrew terms and ancient textual witnesses.
