# Ezekiel 2 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 2]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 2 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Ezekiel is called to take the word of the Lord to Israel—He sees a book in which lamentations and mourning are written.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Raised to hear (vv.1-2):** Ezekiel is commanded to stand, and the spirit sets him upon his feet.
2. **Sent to rebellious Israel (vv.3-5):** God identifies the audience, authorizes the message, and anticipates either hearing or refusal.
3. **Commanded not to fear (vv.6-7):** Opposition is pictured vividly, but Ezekiel must continue speaking God's words.
4. **Warned against rebellion (v.8):** Ezekiel must hear and open his mouth to receive what God gives.
5. **Shown the scroll (vv.9-10):** A hand presents a scroll inscribed within and without with lamentations, mourning, and woe.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- [[Prophets]] and [[Prophecy]]: Ezekiel is sent with an authorized divine message and must speak despite anticipated resistance. The text emphasizes fidelity to the commission without formally defining prophetic "success."
- [[Obedience]]: Ezekiel is warned not to become rebellious like his audience; he must hear and receive what God gives him.
- Divine enabling: Ezekiel is commanded to stand, yet the spirit sets him upright. This can suggest enabled service, though the episode does not by itself establish a complete doctrine of grace and agency.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"Son of man" occurs four times (vv.1, 3, 6, 8), renewing the address to Ezekiel at successive stages of the commission. In this chapter the expression addresses the human prophet; the text does not itself connect it with the distinct Son of Man usage in Daniel 7 or the Gospels.

Forms of "rebel" occur seven times in vv.3-8. This conspicuous repetition makes Israel's resistance a dominant description of the audience and culminates in the warning that Ezekiel must not become like them. No comparative corpus evidence is supplied to establish that the concentration is unusual across scripture.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repetition creates much of the chapter's pressure. Israel is repeatedly described as rebellious, and "whether they will hear, or whether they will forbear" appears in vv.5 and 7. Verse 8 then turns rebellion into a warning to the messenger himself.

The final sequence moves from hand, to scroll, to writing on both sides, to "lamentations, and mourning, and woe." Writing within and without may suggest fullness or abundance, but the chapter does not explain its significance. Ezekiel offers no recorded protest in this chapter, although that silence cannot establish what he felt or whether the narrative omits a response.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Repetition And Contrast
> The repeated descriptions of rebellion, the duplicated hear-or-forbear formula, and the direct contrast between the rebellious house and the prophet provide strong textual evidence that faithful reception and delivery of God's word under anticipated rejection are central concerns of the chapter.

> [!evidence] Form-Critical / Genre Convention *(tentative)*
> The 'Thus saith the Lord GOD' formula in v.4 matches the ancient Near Eastern messenger-speech pattern, in which an envoy delivers a superior's message in first person as if the sender were speaking directly.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · Standard-looking comparison but unsourced here

> [!evidence] Material-Culture Inference *(tentative)*
> The scroll is described as 'written within and without,' i.e. inscribed on both sides, which is unusual since scrolls were customarily written on one side; this may signify that the message of judgment was so complete that no space (or hope) remained.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.5 · Surface description affirmed; material inference speculative

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does the movement from the command to stand to the spirit setting Ezekiel upright suggest—and what does it not prove—about divine enabling?
- How do the repeated alternatives of hearing and forbearing shape Ezekiel's responsibility as a messenger?
- Why is Ezekiel warned not to become rebellious before he is commanded to eat what God gives him?
- What possible functions could the writing within and without serve, given that the chapter does not explain it?
- When and how might the people come to know that a prophet had been among them?
- What, if anything, should be inferred from the absence of a recorded protest by Ezekiel?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Continue with [[Ezekiel 3]], especially Ezekiel 3:1-3, where Ezekiel eats the scroll and the commission proceeds. Compare [[Jeremiah 1]], especially Jeremiah 1:7-9, for another call narrative involving commanded speech and reassurance against fear. Jeremiah 15:16 uses eating imagery for receiving God's words, while [[Revelation 10]] depicts John receiving and eating a little book before further prophecy. Revelation 5:1 offers a more limited comparison: a book written within and on the backside, serving a different role in its vision.
