# Ezekiel 37 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 37]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 37 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Ezekiel is shown the valley of dry bones—Israel will inherit the land in the Resurrection—The stick of Judah (the Bible) and the stick of Joseph (the Book of Mormon) will become one in the Lord’s hand—The children of Israel will be gathered and cleansed—David (the Messiah) will reign over them—They will receive the everlasting gospel covenant.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The dead nation revived (vv.1-14).** Ezekiel is set in a valley of very many, very dry bones (v.2) and asked whether they can live (v.3); he defers to God's own knowledge. He prophesies twice — first to the bones themselves, bringing sinews, flesh, and skin but no breath (vv.4-8), then to "the wind"/breath, which enters and gives life (vv.9-10). The vision interprets itself: the bones are "the whole house of Israel" in exilic despair (v.11), and the promised restoration is God opening graves and bringing His people back to "the land of Israel" (vv.12-14).
2. **The divided nation reunited (vv.15-23).** A new oracle ("the word of the LORD came again," v.15) commands Ezekiel to inscribe and join two sticks representing Judah and Joseph/Ephraim (vv.16-17), held up "before their eyes" (v.20). The LORD's own explanation (vv.19-22) ties the sign to political reunification, followed by promises of gathering, saving, and cleansing (vv.21-23).
3. **One ruler, covenant, and sanctuary (vv.24-28).** David is named one shepherd-king (vv.24-25), and the oracle climaxes in an everlasting covenant of peace and a sanctuary/tabernacle set permanently among the people (vv.26-28).
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Restoration and resurrection imagery**: the vision's own stated meaning (v.11-14) is Israel's corporate return from captivity, expressed through resurrection imagery; later tradition (rabbinic, Christian, and LDS) has read it more directly as teaching literal bodily resurrection, a legitimate but secondary application beyond the text's own stated referent. See [[Resurrection]].
- **Gathering as more than geography**: the promised gathering includes deliverance from sin, cleansing from idolatry, and a renewed covenant relationship, not just return to the land (vv.21-24,27). See [[Gathering of Israel]] and [[Scattering of Israel]].
- **Reunification**: Israel and Judah, divided since the kingdom split, become "one nation" under "one king" (vv.15-22).
- **Everlasting covenant and temple**: an everlasting covenant of peace and a sanctuary "in the midst of them for evermore" (vv.26-28) tie covenant renewal to God's abiding presence. See [[Covenants]] and [[Temples]].
- **Davidic king/shepherd**: "David my servant shall be king... their prince for ever" (vv.24-25) is most naturally read as an idealized future ruler rather than the historical David returning to life; the chapter alone does not settle whether this means a future descendant, a symbolic title, or (in later Christian and LDS reading) the Messiah. See [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV distributes a single recurring idea across three English words — "spirit" (v.1,14), "breath" (vv.5,6,8,10), and "wind" (v.9) — that in Hebrew are generally renderings of one word, ruach (the verb "breathe" in v.9 is a related but distinct form). Whether or not a reader can check the Hebrew directly, the pattern is visible in English: the same animating force carries Ezekiel in vision, revives the dry bones, and is summoned as "wind" from the four corners. Verse 23's formula, "so shall they be my people, and I will be their God," is a recognized covenant refrain that recurs elsewhere in Ezekiel's restoration oracles (compare Ezekiel 36:28) and in other prophetic new-covenant promises (compare Jeremiah 31:33).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter develops a repeated theme of "oneness" in its second half — one stick, one nation, one king, one shepherd (vv.17,22,24) — dramatizing reunification after division. The two-stick sign-act (vv.16-20) fits Ezekiel's broader pattern of physical, enacted prophecy; read on its own plain sense, it suggestively parallels [[Zechariah 11]]'s enacted sign of two staffs, though there the staffs are broken to dramatize covenant and brotherhood breaking apart — an inverse image to Ezekiel's joining of two sticks into one, and the two passages are not demonstrably linked beyond this thematic contrast. Likewise, the dry bones' two-stage restoration — body formed first, breath given after (vv.5-10) — echoes the structural pattern of [[Genesis 2]]'s creation account, where the man's form is fashioned before the breath of life enters him; the resemblance is suggestive of shared ancient imagery about what distinguishes a living body from a lifeless one, rather than a demonstrated instance of direct literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual And Contextual
> The chapter supports an interpretation of the two sticks as symbols of Judah and Joseph/Ephraim becoming one people. Although both sticks are written upon, the chapter itself never identifies them as the Bible and the Book of Mormon. That identification is a later theological interpretation rather than an explicit statement of Ezekiel 37.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation, needs careful framing (see A:c10) · [[Textual And Contextual in Ezekiel 37|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual-Historical Interpretation
> The vision's own stated referent (v.11-14) is national restoration — God opening graves and bringing the exiled house of Israel back into 'the land of Israel' — using resurrection imagery as a metaphor for return from captivity, rather than the passage being framed as direct teaching about individual post-mortem bodily resurrection.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed larger application · [[Textual-Historical Interpretation in Ezekiel 37|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Contextual Interpretation
> The movement from exposed bones to living, standing persons and the language of opened graves provide substantial resurrection imagery. Within this chapter, however, those images are explicitly used to promise Israel renewed life and restoration to its land, so they do not by themselves establish that the passage is a direct prediction of universal bodily resurrection.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed larger application · [[Contextual Interpretation in Ezekiel 37|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- The text explicitly interprets the dry bones as "the whole house of Israel" in exile (v.11). What is gained, and what is at risk, in also applying the vision to individual bodily resurrection beyond its stated original referent?
- The LORD's own explanation of the two sticks (vv.19-22) frames them as a sign of political and national reunification. How does the LDS reading of the sticks as the Bible and Book of Mormon relate to — and go beyond — that stated meaning?
- Verses 24-25 name "David my servant" as the future king, though David had died centuries earlier. What does this suggest about how Ezekiel's audience may have understood Davidic hope — a resurrected David, a future descendant, or an idealized title?
- How does the promise of a permanent sanctuary "in the midst of them" (vv.26-28) anticipate Ezekiel's later, more detailed temple vision (chs. 40-48)?
- Why does the prophecy make cleansing and obedience, not just relocation, essential parts of the promised gathering (vv.23-24)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
