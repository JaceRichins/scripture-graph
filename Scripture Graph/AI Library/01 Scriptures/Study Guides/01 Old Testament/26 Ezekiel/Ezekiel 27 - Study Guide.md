# Ezekiel 27 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 27]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 27 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Ezekiel 27 extends the doom pronounced in [[Ezekiel 26]] by casting Tyre as a magnificent ship built and crewed by nations across the Mediterranean and Near East, then wrecked by 'the east wind' (v.26) and mourned by her trading partners. The chapter is framed from the outset as a lamentation (v.2) sung for a city whose ruin is treated as prophetically certain. Verses 12-24 form an unusually detailed catalogue of Tyre's trading partners and goods — including, notably, Judah and Israel themselves (v.17) — that reads almost like a trade ledger. The chapter is not a blanket condemnation of commerce: its explicit target is Tyre's self-declared 'perfect beauty' (v.3) and the way glory, once perfected, collapses entirely (v.27).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv.1-3** — Commission: the LORD commands Ezekiel to 'take up a lamentation for Tyrus' (v.2); Tyre's self-declaration, 'I am of perfect beauty' (v.3).
- **vv.4-11** — Tyre pictured as a ship under construction: planks, mast, oars, benches, sail, and covering supplied by Senir, Lebanon, Bashan, Egypt, and Elishah (vv.4-7); mariners, pilots, and calkers from Zidon, Arvad, and Gebal (vv.8-9). Verses 10-11 shift to garrison imagery — army, shields, walls, towers — rather than continuing the ship's parts.
- **vv.12-24** — Catalogue of Tyre's trading partners and goods: Tarshish; Javan, Tubal, and Meshech; Togarmah; Dedan; Syria; Judah and Israel; Damascus; Arabia and Kedar; Sheba and Raamah; and the Syro-Mesopotamian centers of Haran, Canneh, Eden, Asshur, and Chilmad.
- **vv.25-27** — The laden ship is wrecked: 'the east wind hath broken thee in the midst of the seas' (v.26); riches, mariners, pilots, calkers, merchants, and soldiers fall together into the sea (v.27).
- **vv.28-36** — International mourning: mariners perform mourning rites on shore (vv.28-31), a lament is quoted in their voice beginning 'What city is like Tyrus...' (v.32), and the nations react with astonishment and hissing (vv.33-36), closing with 'thou shalt be a terror, and never shalt be any more' (v.36).

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Tyre's self-description as 'of perfect beauty' (v.3), echoed by the refrain that her builders and allies 'perfected'/'made perfect' her beauty (vv.4, 11), sets up a study in [[Pride]]: confidence grounded in wealth, craftsmanship, and international alliance collapses entirely (v.27, v.36). The chapter dramatizes this through narrative reversal rather than direct accusation — it does not itself say Tyre trusted wealth 'rather than God'; that sharper indictment belongs to [[Ezekiel 28]]'s oracle against the king of Tyre. Nor is this a blanket condemnation of trade: Judah and Israel are themselves named among Tyre's trading partners (v.17), so ordinary economic exchange and prophetic judgment on Tyre coexist in the text without the chapter resolving the tension explicitly.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter blends nautical and civic language: Tyre's 'borders are in the midst of the seas' (v.4), yet she also has boards, mast, oars, and sail (vv.5-7); vv.10-11, however, describe an army, shields, walls, and towers — city/garrison imagery rather than ship's parts — so the ship metaphor is not maintained with total consistency. Many of the geographic names in vv.6-23 (Chittim, Elishah, Tarshish, Togarmah, Chilmad) are disputed among scholars, so precise historical-geography claims drawn from this chapter should be held loosely. 'Eden' in v.23 is grouped with Haran, Canneh, Asshur, and Chilmad — all Syro-Mesopotamian trading centers — so it most plausibly names a trading city rather than the paradisal Eden of Genesis; the chapter's automatic place-index, which tags this verse as Garden of Eden, should be read with that caveat. The 'east wind' that breaks Tyre (v.26) is not explicitly decoded within the chapter; reading it as either a judgment motif (compare Psalm 48:7) or a veiled reference to an eastern invader goes beyond what the text itself states.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Three features stand out. (1) Extended allegory: Tyre is portrayed as a ship under construction (vv.4-9) before the image is carried through to a wreck (vv.25-27), though vv.10-11 momentarily shift to city/garrison imagery. (2) Catalogue form: vv.12-24 read like a trade ledger, naming roughly two dozen partners and goods, making the vessel feel progressively fuller before its sudden, compact destruction in vv.26-27. (3) A lament within a lament: the prophet's own lamentation (v.2) frames a second lament placed in the mouths of Tyre's mourning mariners, opening 'What city is like Tyrus...' (v.32). This catalogue-then-sudden-collapse pattern, and the mourning of merchants who once profited from the city, closely resemble [[Revelation 18]]'s lament over Babylon's fall — a suggestive parallel, though not proof of direct literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does naming Judah and Israel as Tyre's trading partners (v.17) suggest about how ordinary economic ties could coexist with prophetic judgment on Tyre?
- Why might Ezekiel choose an extended ship allegory, rather than direct address, to announce Tyre's judgment?
- Does the detailed, almost admiring description of Tyre's construction and trade (vv.3-24) function as praise, irony, or both?
- Does 'east wind' in v.26 function only as nautical/storm imagery, or also as a veiled reference to an invader from the east?
- What should readers make of the flat mention of trading 'the persons of men' (v.13) without further comment from the text?
- How does the embedded lament of the mariners (vv.32-36) change the reader's relationship to Tyre's fall compared to a direct prophetic pronouncement?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
For the fuller Tyre oracle cycle, see [[Ezekiel 26]] (the initial doom oracle) and [[Ezekiel 28]] (judgment on the king/prince of Tyre, with its Eden/cherub imagery). Isaiah 23 is an earlier oracle against Tyre with similar maritime-commerce themes. Revelation 18's lament over Babylon's fall closely parallels this chapter's catalogue-of-goods-then-sudden-ruin structure and its mourning merchants. Nehemiah 13:16 shows Tyrian trade with Judah continuing generations later; Joshua 13:5 supplies background on Gebal (Byblos) as a longstanding neighbor of Israel; Psalm 45:12 offers a contrasting courtly image of 'the daughter of Tyre' bringing tribute. The Tyre-Israel commercial relationship recalls the earlier Solomon-Hiram alliance (1 Kings 5).
