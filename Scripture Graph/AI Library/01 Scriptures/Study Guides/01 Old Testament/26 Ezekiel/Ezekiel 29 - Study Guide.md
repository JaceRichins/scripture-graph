# Ezekiel 29 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 29]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 29 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Ezekiel 29 contains two dated oracles. The first condemns Pharaoh's boastful claim over the river and Egypt's failure as Israel's political support. Judgment includes desolation and dispersion, followed by a limited restoration in which Egypt remains a diminished kingdom. The later oracle assigns Egypt to Nebuchadrezzar as compensation for his army's unrewarded service against Tyrus. The chapter closes with an enigmatic promise concerning Israel's horn and Ezekiel's opened mouth.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Ezekiel 29:1-7 — Pharaoh and the river:** Pharaoh is pictured as a great dragon in the rivers, then as a failed reed staff for Israel.
2. **Ezekiel 29:8-12 — Judgment and dispersion:** Sword, desolation, and scattering are announced.
3. **Ezekiel 29:13-16 — Limited restoration:** Egyptians return to Pathros, but Egypt remains politically weak and ceases to be Israel's confidence.
4. **Ezekiel 29:17-20 — Egypt as Babylon's wages:** Nebuchadrezzar receives Egypt after unrewarded service against Tyrus.
5. **Ezekiel 29:21 — Concluding signs:** Israel's horn buds, Ezekiel's mouth opens, and recognition of the Lord follows.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter challenges both royal self-sufficiency and misplaced national trust. Pharaoh's assertion concerning the river is answered by God's declaration of control over Pharaoh, Egypt, and Babylon's campaigns. Israel is also implicated: Egypt's removal as a dependable ally recalls Israel's iniquity when it looks to Egypt for confidence (Ezekiel 29:16). The chapter therefore joins [[Pride]] with a warning against substituting political power for covenantal trust.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two image systems govern the first oracle. Pharaoh is first a river creature whose apparent mastery ends when God hooks and removes him (Ezekiel 29:3-5). Egypt is then a reed staff that collapses under Israel's weight and injures the one leaning on it (Ezekiel 29:6-7). The images make different points: the first overturns Pharaoh's boast, while the second exposes Egypt's unreliability as an ally.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Recognition formulas recur after judgments and promises: inhabitants of Egypt, Israel, and the final audience are told that they will know that the speaker is the Lord (Ezekiel 29:6, 9, 16, 21). The repeated formula frames political reversals as disclosures of divine identity. Related Egypt oracles continue in [[Ezekiel 30]], while [[Ezekiel 26]] supplies the earlier Tyre setting assumed in Ezekiel 29:18.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Textual Evidence
> The chapter supplies strong textual evidence that this oracle presents Nebuchadrezzar's campaign as service used and recompensed by God, even though the military action is carried out by Babylon.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted, carefully bounded · [[Internal Textual Evidence in Ezekiel 29|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Corroboration *(tentative)*
> Ezekiel 29:18's description of Nebuchadnezzar's army serving a long, costly campaign against Tyre 'for the service that he had served against it' yet receiving 'no wages' corresponds to an extra-biblical tradition of a roughly thirteen-year Babylonian siege of Tyre that did not yield the plunder a successful sack would have.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed interpretation of extra-biblical corroboration

> [!evidence] Prophecy Vs. Historical Record *(tentative)*
> The oracle's specific predictions about Egypt — total forty-year depopulation and desolation 'from the tower of Syene even unto the border of Ethiopia,' dispersal of Egyptians among the nations, and permanent reduction to 'the basest of the kingdoms' that would never again rule over others — are not corroborated by the historical record of Egypt, which remained an inhabited, functioning state (and later a significant regional power under the Saite, Persian, and Ptolemaic periods) rather than undergoing a documented forty-year total desolation.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · disputed — important honest fulfillment problem
