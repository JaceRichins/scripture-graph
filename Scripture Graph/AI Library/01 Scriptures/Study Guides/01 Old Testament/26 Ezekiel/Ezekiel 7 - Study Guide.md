## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
One useful outline is:

1. **The end announced (7:1–9).** Repeated declarations of arrival surround two closely parallel judgment units. Both connect recompense with the people's ways and conclude with recognition of the LORD.
2. **The day disrupts ordinary life (7:10–18).** Pride and violence mature into judgment; transactions, military response, city life, and rural life are overtaken by disaster, while survivors flee in mourning.
3. **Wealth, ornament, and sacred space (7:19–22).** Silver and gold cannot deliver, ornament is associated with idolatrous images, and a place belonging to God is polluted and plundered. Its precise identification should remain qualified.
4. **Social helplessness and final verdict (7:23–27).** Violence fills land and city; houses and holy places are defiled; peace cannot be found; prophetic, priestly, elder, royal, and popular figures are caught in the disaster. The conclusion returns to judgment according to conduct and recognition of the LORD.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents judgment as moral and reciprocal: the LORD repeatedly judges the people “according to” their ways and recompenses their abominations. This describes the explanation supplied by this particular oracle; it should not be generalized into a claim that every instance of suffering is direct punishment for individual sin.

Material wealth cannot provide salvation from the announced catastrophe. Silver and gold are unable to deliver and are also associated with iniquity, while ornament has been used for idolatrous images. The passage therefore challenges confidence in possessions, status, and sacred privilege when they have become entangled with [[Pride]] and [[Sin]].

The refusal to spare or pity makes this a stark text for studying [[Justice and Mercy]], but it is not the whole scriptural account of that relationship. Likewise, the survivors' mourning “every one for his iniquity” may acknowledge guilt, yet the chapter does not explicitly portray completed [[Repentance]], forgiveness, or covenant restoration.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Arrival language dominates the opening half: “the end is come,” “it is come,” “the time is come,” and “the day draweth near.” Together with the repeated formulas about recompense, refusal to spare, and knowing the LORD, this gives the oracle a forceful, patterned rhythm. Such repetition demonstrates rhetorical patterning, though it does not by itself prove a single-stage composition or an elaborate formal design.

Verse 7's phrase “not the sounding again of the mountains” and the KJV terms rendered “morning” in verses 7 and 10 are difficult. Interpretations that depend heavily on those particular English expressions should therefore be treated cautiously.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repetition is the chapter's chief literary force. Variations on “come” and “near” compress the threatened future into an urgent present, while the parallel language of verses 3–4 and 8–9 reinforces judgment according to conduct and recognition of the LORD.

The growth imagery of 7:10–11 is ominously inverted: the rod blossoms, pride buds, and violence rises into a rod of wickedness. Images normally associated with vitality instead portray wickedness reaching maturity for judgment.

The chapter also employs reversals: buying and selling lose their normal emotional significance, a trumpet produces no battle response, wealth becomes useless, ornament is implicated in idolatry, the strong lose their pomp, and guidance is sought amid cascading disaster. These reversals portray the undoing of ordinary securities.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does “the four corners of the land” (v.2) contribute to the oracle's portrayal of the judgment's scope?
- How do the close parallels between verses 3–4 and 8–9 shape the chapter's message?
- What does verse 14's contrast between the trumpet call and the absence of fighters communicate about the coming disaster?
- How does “every one for his iniquity” (v.16) individualize mourning without removing the chapter's collective frame?
- Why do people seek prophetic vision, priestly law, and elders' counsel only amid cascading disaster (v.26), and does the verse explicitly describe each source as unavailable?
- How should recognition of the LORD under judgment be distinguished from repentance, forgiveness, or willing covenant renewal?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the refusal-to-spare and recompense formulas with [[Ezekiel 9]], and the weakening of hands and knees with [[Ezekiel 21]]. [[Zephaniah 1]] provides a close parallel to verse 19 on silver and gold being unable to deliver in the day of wrath; the similarity does not determine literary dependence.

[[Ezekiel 8]] begins the nearby temple vision and helps illuminate the chapter's language of images, abominations, and pollution, while the broader glory-departure sequence continues beyond that chapter. [[Jeremiah 18]] contains a comparable priest-law, counselor, and prophet triad, though both texts may draw on conventional institutional language. The possible connection between verse 13 and the jubilee legislation in [[Leviticus 25]] is weaker and should remain tentative. [[2 Kings 25]] supplies useful historical-scriptural context for the siege, famine, plunder, and sanctuary-defilement imagery, without proving the oracle's exact date or exclusive historical referent.
