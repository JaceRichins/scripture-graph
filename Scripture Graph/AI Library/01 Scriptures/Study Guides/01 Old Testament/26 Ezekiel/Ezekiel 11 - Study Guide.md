## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Ezekiel 11:1-13 — Judgment at the east gate.** The Spirit brings Ezekiel to twenty-five leaders, exposes their counsel, reverses their caldron slogan, and announces removal and sword judgment. Pelatiah's death prompts Ezekiel's plea for the remnant.

2. **Ezekiel 11:14-21 — Hope for the displaced.** God answers the crisis by addressing Ezekiel's scattered kindred. He promises to be a sanctuary among them, gather them, cleanse the land, transform their hearts, and renew the covenant relationship. Verse 21 preserves a warning for persistent allegiance to abominations.

3. **Ezekiel 11:22-25 — Glory departs and the prophet reports.** The glory moves east of Jerusalem; Ezekiel returns in vision to Chaldea and tells the captives what he has seen.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter associates covenant restoration with both divine initiative and transformed conduct. God gathers, gives a new spirit, and replaces the stony heart; the stated purpose is that the people will walk in his statutes and actually do his ordinances. Renewal is therefore inward, ethical, and communal rather than merely geographic.

The promise of a little sanctuary also shows that divine presence is not depicted as inaccessible outside Jerusalem. This does not make the temple irrelevant: its force comes precisely from addressing people deprived of access to it. Rather, it offers hope that exile cannot place the covenant community beyond God's reach.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly uses spatial language: the east gate, removal from the city's midst, judgment at Israel's border, scattering among countries, return to the land, the mountain east of the city, and Chaldea. This geography carries theological force. Those inside Jerusalem are not thereby secure, while those far away are not thereby abandoned.

The recurring language of the heart also creates a moral contrast. God knows what enters the leaders' minds in verse 5; he promises a heart of flesh in verse 19; and verse 21 describes hearts that continue to follow detestable things. Inner orientation and outward conduct remain connected throughout.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
- **Caldron reversal:** The leaders call Jerusalem a caldron and themselves its flesh (v. 3). The oracle reuses their vocabulary but identifies the slain as the flesh and announces that the addressed leaders will be brought out for judgment (vv. 7-11). A claim of security or privileged status is a plausible reading of the original slogan, but its precise nuance is disputed.
- **Reversal of distance:** Jerusalem's inhabitants tell the displaced Israelites to remain far from the LORD, yet God promises to be a sanctuary to them in the countries of dispersion (vv. 15-16). Physical proximity to Jerusalem is therefore not presented as an automatic guarantee of divine favor.
- **Departure and return:** The glory's movement continues the scene in [[Ezekiel 10]], passing eastward beyond the city. [[Ezekiel 43]] later provides a canonical counterpoint by depicting the glory returning from the east.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What assumptions about physical location, institutional status, or inherited privilege are exposed by the reversal of the caldron image?

- Why does the promise of gathering include removal of abominations and transformation of the heart rather than return to the land alone?

- How does Ezekiel's alarm at Pelatiah's death shape the transition from judgment to the promise for the remnant?

- What is preserved, and what is challenged, by describing God as a sanctuary among people who lack access to Jerusalem's temple?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

For the movement of divine glory, read [[Ezekiel 10]], Ezekiel 11:22-23, and [[Ezekiel 43]] as a connected departure-and-return arc.
