## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **East-gate access and calendrical offerings (46:1–8):** Sabbath and new-moon worship, offerings, and the prince's route.
2. **Festival movement and voluntary offerings (46:9–12):** worshippers leave through the opposite gate, the prince moves among them, and the east gate opens for voluntary sacrifice.
3. **The continual morning offering (46:13–15):** a lamb, grain, and oil are prepared every morning.
4. **Inheritance and limits on dispossession (46:16–18):** permanent gifts to sons, temporary grants to servants, and protection of the people's holdings.
5. **Sacrificial preparation areas (46:19–24):** rooms beside the priestly chambers and four corner cooking courts.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter brings [[Sabbath Day]], [[Sacrifice]], and [[Temples]] together with specific limits on princely authority. The prince participates prominently in worship, while verse 2 distinguishes his immediate role from the priests' preparation of offerings. He is also forbidden to take the people's inheritance through oppression (Ezekiel 46:18). These provisions support study of ritual responsibility and lawful restraint, but they do not establish a comprehensive political theory or show that the envisioned regulations were historically enforced.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV expression "meat offering" denotes a grain or food offering here; the chapter associates it with flour and oil. The phrase "as his hand shall attain unto" describes provision according to ability. The "year of liberty" in verse 17 invites comparison with [[Leviticus 25]] because both contexts concern liberty and the return of hereditary possession, though Ezekiel does not explicitly name Jubilee. The purpose clause "to sanctify the people" in verse 20 supplies a holiness rationale for restricting where certain offerings are prepared, but its precise mechanism and consequence are not explained.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Movement recurs throughout the chapter: the east gate opens and closes; the prince enters, worships, and departs; festival worshippers continue to the gate opposite their entrance; and Ezekiel is led from the priestly chambers through the outer court. Repeated routes, measurements, and offering ratios give the vision a strongly ordered character. The prescribed movements are explicit, but their purposes are not: practical circulation, ritual boundaries, and symbolic readings remain possible explanations.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Comparative Textual Evidence
> Ezekiel's Sabbath and new-moon quantities differ materially from those in Numbers 28: Ezekiel prescribes six lambs and a ram for the Sabbath and one bullock, six lambs, and a ram for the new moon, whereas Numbers prescribes two lambs for the Sabbath and two bullocks, one ram, and seven lambs for the new moon. This is strong evidence that Ezekiel 46 is not merely a verbatim repetition of that Pentateuchal schedule.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does this chapter specify a morning continual offering while [[Numbers 28]] describes morning and evening lambs?
- Why must festival worshippers depart through the gate opposite their entrance: ritual symbolism, practical circulation, or both?
- What is implied by the prince being "in the midst" of the people while retaining distinct access rules?
- Do the differing Sabbath and new-moon quantities reflect adaptation, a distinct ritual tradition, or the vision's particular setting?
- Does the "year of liberty" refer specifically to Jubilee or to a related release institution?
- How should these visionary regulations be related to historical practice or later expectations?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the sacrificial calendar with [[Numbers 28]], the "year of liberty" with [[Leviticus 25]], and the protection of inherited land with [[Ezekiel 45]]. [[Ezekiel 44]] provides further context for the east gate and the prince's access, while [[Ezekiel 42]] helps illuminate the priestly chambers and separation of sacred spaces. Numerical differences between Ezekiel and Numbers should be examined rather than harmonized prematurely.
