## Overview

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Temple and most holy place (vv. 1–4):** The guide measures the main room and the twenty-by-twenty inner room.
2. **Surrounding chambers (vv. 5–11):** Three levels of side chambers encircle the house and broaden upward.
3. **Western building and larger dimensions (vv. 12–15):** The separate place, rear building, galleries, and several hundred-cubit spans are measured.
4. **Interior finish and imagery (vv. 16–21):** Woodwork, windows, cherubim, palm trees, and posts are described.
5. **Furnishing and entrances (vv. 22–26):** A wooden altar or table, divided doors, carved ornament, windows, and porch details complete the description.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents sacred space through differentiated architecture: the inner room is explicitly named the most holy place, distinct from the main temple room (vv. 1–4), and the wooden table stands "before the LORD" (v. 22). This supports a reading of the sanctuary as ordered around holiness and divine presence. The chapter itself, however, does not explain access rules, rites, or the symbolic meaning of each measurement and ornament. These limits should govern its use in studying [[Temples]].

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verse 1 says that the guide brought Ezekiel to the temple, while verse 3 says that "he" went inward. The shift may indicate that only the guide entered the most holy place, but Ezekiel's position is not explicitly stated.

Verse 22 uses both "altar" and "table" for the wooden furnishing before the LORD. The double designation is secure, while its exact ritual identity remains uncertain.

Repeated forms of "measure" organize much of the chapter, and "round about" repeatedly emphasizes enclosure. These patterns support—but do not independently prove—a literary portrayal of ordered, bounded space.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter combines technical measurement with a coordinated visual program. Palm trees alternate with two-faced cherubim, and related imagery continues from the walls to the doors (vv. 18–20, 25).

Specific correspondences with [[1 Kings 6]] include surrounding chambers, a twenty-by-twenty inner room, cherub-and-palm ornamentation, and divided doors. These parallels strongly support continuity with an earlier Israelite temple tradition, but they do not determine whether the relationship arose through shared architectural conventions, cultural memory, literary dependence, later shaping, or some combination.

The two-faced carvings differ from the four-faced creatures in [[Ezekiel 1]] and [[Ezekiel 10]]. Their orientation toward neighboring palms fits the needs of a wall composition, but the text does not state that artistic medium caused the difference. The variation therefore should not be forced into either a contradiction or a single fixed anatomy.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the grammatical shift in verses 1–3 indicate that Ezekiel remained outside the most holy place, or does it merely preserve the guide as the acting subject?
- Why does verse 22 call the wooden furnishing both an altar and a table?
- Do the repeated hundred-cubit measurements serve practical planning, literary symmetry, symbolic meaning, or some combination?
- Why do the temple cherubim have two faces, unlike the four-faced creatures in [[Ezekiel 1]] and [[Ezekiel 10]]?
- What function was intended for the western building in verses 12–15?
- How does the measured structure prepare for the later return of glory in [[Ezekiel 43]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Ezekiel 40]], [[Ezekiel 41]], and [[Ezekiel 42]] as a continuous architectural survey, then follow the return of glory and the sacrificial altar in [[Ezekiel 43]]. Compare the architecture with [[1 Kings 6]] and, more narrowly, [[2 Chronicles 3]]. For the varying appearance of cherubim, compare [[Ezekiel 1]] and [[Ezekiel 10]]. Keep the large sacrificial altar in Ezekiel 43 distinct from the wooden altar or table in Ezekiel 41:22.
