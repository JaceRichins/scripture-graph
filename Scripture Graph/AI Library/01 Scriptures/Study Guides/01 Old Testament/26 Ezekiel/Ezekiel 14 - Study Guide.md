# Ezekiel 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 14]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Ezekiel 14 contains two related oracles. Verses 1–11 confront people who seek prophetic guidance while maintaining idols in their hearts. The Lord commands [[Repentance]], assigns responsibility to both corrupt inquirers and compromised [[Prophets]], and states a restorative covenant goal: that Israel may again be his people. Verses 12–23 insist that even Noah, Daniel, and Job could save only themselves when a land's guilt had reached the point of judgment. The four judgments are then applied explicitly to Jerusalem. A remnant survives, but its observed conduct confirms that the judgment was not causeless.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The elders and inward idolatry (vv. 1–5):** The elders sit before Ezekiel, while the oracle exposes idols in the heart.

2. **Call to turn and warning to inquirers (vv. 6–11):** Israel and resident strangers are commanded to turn. The prophet and the inquirer are both accountable, with restoration of the covenant relationship stated as the intended end.

3. **Four test cases (vv. 12–20):** Famine, harmful beasts, sword, and pestilence are considered separately. Each case repeats the conclusion that Noah, Daniel, and Job could deliver only themselves.

4. **Application to Jerusalem (vv. 21–23):** The phrase “How much more” gathers all four judgments against Jerusalem. The conduct of those brought out explains why the judgment was warranted.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
1. **Idolatry of the heart.** Sin need not be visible; the refrain "idols in their heart" (vv.3-4,7) locates the problem in inward allegiance rather than physical cult objects alone — even an outward approach to inquire of the Lord can mask an unrepentant heart.
2. **Prophetic accountability.** A prophet consulted by a divided-hearted inquirer shares responsibility: "the punishment of the prophet shall be even as the punishment of him that seeketh unto him" (v.10). Verse 9's statement that the Lord himself "deceived that prophet" is not resolved within this chapter; readings that flatten it into simple permission, or into approval of falsehood, both go beyond what the text establishes.
3. **[[Repentance]] as the remedy.** The command is concrete: "repent, and turn yourselves from your idols" (v.6), aimed at the restorative goal "that they may be my people, and I may be their God" (v.11).
4. **Limits on vicarious deliverance.** Within this specific judgment scenario, even Noah, Daniel, and Job — paradigms of righteousness — "deliver but their own souls" (v.14) and cannot rescue their children or an unrepentant land from the four judgments. This is a bounded statement about temporal judgment on a condemned land, not a general doctrine about intercession or final salvation.
5. **A remnant, not necessarily a righteous one.** A remnant is preserved (vv.22-23), but the text credits their survival with proving the judgment "was not without cause" — evidence vindicating God's justice, not a clear statement that the remnant itself was righteous or that mercy overrode judgment.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Idols in their heart” shifts attention from merely visible cult objects to an inwardly established allegiance. The accompanying phrases “stumblingblock of their iniquity,” “estranged from me,” and “separateth himself from me” describe a settled moral and relational condition.

The statement “I the LORD have deceived that prophet” is one of the chapter's most difficult expressions. The text plainly attributes the deception to the Lord while also punishing the prophet; it does not explain the precise mechanism or remove the prophet's accountability. Interpretations that reduce the wording either to simple divine approval of falsehood or to no divine involvement go beyond what this chapter alone establishes.

In verses 22–23, the remnant's “ways” and “doings” are evidentiary: seeing them leads the earlier exiles to understand that Jerusalem's calamity had cause. The text does not explicitly characterize this remnant as righteous.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 13–20 use a fourfold repetitive pattern. Each judgment is introduced, Noah, Daniel, and Job are invoked, and the limits of their deliverance are restated. Verse 21 then turns the pattern into an argument from lesser to greater: if any one judgment would defeat reliance upon exemplary righteous persons, “how much more” when all four come upon Jerusalem. This repetition is rhetorically forceful even though it does not, by itself, resolve questions about the passage's compositional history.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean to set up "idols in the heart" today, when literal idol worship is rare?
- Why does the oracle target inward allegiance rather than only visible religious practice?
- How can the chapter affirm personal deliverance by righteousness (vv.14-20) while still depicting judgment on an entire land?
- What does verse 9 mean by the Lord "deceiving" a prophet, and which readings keep both its causal language and the prophet's own accountability (v.10) intact?
- Why does the conduct of Jerusalem's survivors comfort the exiles (vv.22-23) — does it commend the remnant, or mainly justify the judgment?
- Who is the "Daniel" of verse 14 — the biblical exile-era prophet, or an older figure known from ancient Near Eastern tradition — and does the answer matter doctrinally?
