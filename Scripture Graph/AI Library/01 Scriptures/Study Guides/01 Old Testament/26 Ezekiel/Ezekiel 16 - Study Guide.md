# Ezekiel 16 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 16]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 16 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jerusalem has become as a harlot, reveling in her idols and worshipping false gods—She has partaken of all the sins of Egypt and the nations round about, and she is rejected—Yet in the last days, the Lord will again establish His covenant with her.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter unfolds in five movements:

1. **Origin and covenant (16:1-14)** — Jerusalem's disgraceful birth (16:3-5), rescue and growth (16:6-7), marriage covenant (16:8), and royal adornment (16:9-14).
2. **Escalating unfaithfulness (16:15-34)** — idolatry with images made from the Lord's own gold and silver (16:17), child sacrifice (16:20-21), and harlotry named specifically with Egypt, Assyria, and Chaldea (16:26, 28-29). Philistia (16:27) appears differently — as a neighbor to whom Jerusalem is delivered in shame, not as a named lover. The section climaxes in the "reverse harlot" charge that she pays her lovers rather than being paid (16:31-34).
3. **Judgment oracle (16:35-43)** — a formal sentence of exposure, stoning, and burning, closing with the stated principle, "I also will recompense thy way upon thine head" (16:43).
4. **Comparison to two sisters (16:44-52)** — Samaria (elder) and Sodom (younger) are named, and Jerusalem is declared worse than both (16:47-51).
5. **Restoration and everlasting covenant (16:53-63)** — the Lord promises to "bring again their captivity" for Sodom, Samaria, and Jerusalem together (16:53, 55), and to establish "an everlasting covenant" despite Jerusalem's breach of the original one (16:59-62).
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Covenant as marriage.** The Lord's covenant with Jerusalem is cast in the language of betrothal and marriage (16:8), framing [[Covenants]] through the lens of [[Marriage]] — and covenant-breaking through the lens of [[Chastity]] violated.
- **Idolatry as unfaithfulness.** Worship of other gods is framed as marital betrayal rather than a neutral category error, casting [[Sin]] as a rupture of relationship, not merely rule-breaking.
- **Justice and mercy held together.** Jerusalem's covenant breach carries real consequences — loss of the very gifts she treated as her own (16:9-14, 39) — yet the chapter closes with the Lord's promise to remember his covenant and establish an "everlasting covenant" (16:60). The clause "not by thy covenant" (16:61) is grammatically ambiguous — it may describe the new status given her restored sisters rather than announce that Jerusalem's own faithfulness is irrelevant to the renewal — but the initiative throughout is plainly the Lord's. Compare [[Justice and Mercy]] and [[Repentance and Forgiveness]].
- **Sodom's iniquity, as named here.** Ezekiel 16:49 identifies pride, abundance, idleness, and failure to help the poor and needy as Sodom's iniquity, alongside "abomination" (16:50). This is what this passage foregrounds when using Sodom as a rhetorical foil against Jerusalem; it is not necessarily an exhaustive account of Genesis 19.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter sustains its central metaphor through concrete, physical vocabulary — nakedness, blood, adornment, "high places" — rather than abstract theological terms, which is part of what makes the indictment land as visceral rather than merely propositional. The recurring divine speech-formula "saith the Lord GOD" (and, once, "saith the LORD," 16:58) repeatedly re-anchors the extended metaphor to prophetic authority.

This rhetoric is intentionally severe, using a woman's sexual exposure and punishment to depict a city's guilt. Readers should recognize its corporate prophetic target — Jerusalem as a covenant community — and take care not to transfer its gendered violence to individual women, abuse survivors, or read it as divine sanction for such treatment. Naming that ethical difficulty does not require softening what the text actually says.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Ezekiel 16 is built on verbal motifs that recur at structurally significant points:

- **"Days of thy youth" / remembering.** Jerusalem's failure to remember her helpless youth is charged twice — "thou hast not remembered the days of thy youth" (16:22, repeated 16:43) — before the Lord reverses the verb: "I will remember my covenant with thee in the days of thy youth" (16:60). Jerusalem is then told she will herself remember, and be ashamed (16:61). This shift from human forgetting to divine remembering, followed by human remembering-with-shame, is the chapter's clearest structuring device.
- **Nakedness.** Jerusalem is "naked and bare" as an abandoned infant (16:7), is charged with forgetting that condition (16:22), and is stripped "naked and bare" again in judgment (16:39) — undoing the clothing the Lord gave her (16:9-13).
- **Gifts given, misused, and removed.** Garments, gold, silver, oil, and food that the Lord gave Jerusalem (16:9-13) are turned into instruments of idolatry (16:16-19) and are finally stripped away in judgment (16:39).
- **Blood.** Introduced at birth ("polluted in thine own blood," 16:6), it recurs at her cleansing (16:9), in the blood of her sacrificed children (16:36), and in the sentence itself ("I will give thee blood in fury and jealousy," 16:38).
- **High places.** What Jerusalem built for idolatry ("high place in every street," 16:24-25, 31) is what her judges tear down (16:39).

Several of these reversals converge in a measure-for-measure judgment — the chapter states the underlying principle outright, "I also will recompense thy way upon thine head" (16:43) — though not every motif maps precisely onto a sin later punished in kind (her infancy nakedness and birth-blood, for instance, describe helplessness rather than an offense), so this is best read as a pervasive literary tendency rather than a fully engineered scheme.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Is the promised restoration of Sodom (16:53, 55) meant literally, or rhetorically — measuring how far Jerusalem has fallen? The text does not resolve this.
- How should Ezekiel's naming of Sodom's sin as "pride, fulness of bread, and abundance of idleness…neither did she strengthen the hand of the poor and needy" (16:49) be weighed against the sexual-violence narrative of Genesis 19 — redefinition, supplement, or simply a different rhetorical emphasis in this passage?
- Which details in the chapter identify idolatry explicitly (16:17-19), and which might additionally suggest political dependence on foreign powers (16:26, 28-29)?
- Why does the chapter repeat Jerusalem's failure to remember her youth (16:22, 43) before the Lord promises that he will remember his covenant (16:60)?
- What is the effect of addressing an entire city-nation in second-person singular, as a single woman, across sixty-three verses — and how can this chapter's violent, gendered rhetoric be studied honestly without transferring its accusations to people outside its corporate prophetic target?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
