## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The roll received (3:1–3):** Ezekiel eats the roll, which tastes sweet in his mouth.

2. **The commission renewed (3:4–11):** He is sent to Israel rather than to foreign-language peoples and is strengthened against anticipated resistance.

3. **Transport to the exiles (3:12–15):** Amid the sound of wings and wheels, the Spirit carries him to Tel-abib, where he sits astonished for seven days.

4. **The watchman charge (3:16–21):** Four cases distinguish Ezekiel's duty to warn from each hearer's response and outcome.

5. **Glory, confinement, and regulated speech (3:22–27):** The glory appears in the plain, and future confinement, binding, muteness, and divinely opened speech are announced.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Within Ezekiel's particular commission, the watchman oracle differentiates the responsibility of messenger and hearer. Ezekiel must transmit the warning entrusted to him; failure to warn brings blood accountability, while faithful warning releases him from that liability even if the warning is refused. The hearer's response still matters.

The four cases also show that prior moral status does not make later conduct irrelevant: a wicked person may turn from wickedness, and a righteous person may turn to iniquity. These observations support connections with [[Sin]], [[Repentance]], and, by interpretation, [[Agency]]. The passage does not establish that every believer bears Ezekiel's specific watchman office, nor does it provide a complete account of [[Final Judgment]] or [[Salvation]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter builds around organs of reception and communication: mouth, belly, bowels, heart, ears, face, forehead, tongue, and mouth again. God's word must enter the prophet before it comes from him. At the end, even his capacity to reprove is subject to divine restraint. This embodied vocabulary makes prophetic speech an enacted vocation rather than the mere transmission of information.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly uses bodily language—mouth, belly, bowels, heart, ears, face, forehead, tongue, and mouth again—to portray prophetic reception, endurance, and speech. God's word enters Ezekiel before it comes from him, and his later capacity to reprove is placed under divine restraint.

The roll's sweetness in verse 3 and Ezekiel's bitterness and heated spirit in verse 14 create a possible literary contrast, but the text does not explain the bitterness. It may reflect anger, indignation, distress, reluctance, or another response rather than simply the burden of preaching. The repeated phrase “a rebellious house” in verses 9, 26, and 27 connects Israel's resistance with both Ezekiel's strengthening and the restriction of his speech.

[[Revelation 10]] offers a close parallel in which an eaten book is sweet in the mouth and bitter in the belly. The resemblance is strong, but Revelation's immediate bodily bitterness should not be equated without qualification with Ezekiel's later bitterness of spirit.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What emotion does “bitterness” and “the heat of my spirit” describe in verse 14, and does the text connect it to the sweet roll, Israel's rebellion, divine compulsion, or something else?

- What does Ezekiel's seven days among the exiles contribute to the watchman commission? Does the scene imply solidarity, mourning, stunned observation, or more than one of these?

- How should the watchman charge be read alongside Ezekiel's announced muteness? What is the difference between faithful warning and speaking on one's own initiative?

- In verse 20, how should God's placement of a stumblingblock be understood alongside the righteous person's action and Ezekiel's duty to warn?

- Do verses 18–21 principally concern temporal death, covenant judgment, wider spiritual consequences, or language the chapter leaves deliberately undefined?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the commissioning scene with [[Ezekiel 2]], the renewed watchman language in [[Ezekiel 33]], and the eaten-book episode in [[Revelation 10]]. [[Jeremiah 1]] provides another commission involving opposition and divine strengthening, while [[Jeremiah 15]] uses the related metaphor of eating God's words. [[Job 2]] may offer a suggestive comparison for seven days of overwhelmed sitting, but Ezekiel does not explicitly identify his interval as mourning.
