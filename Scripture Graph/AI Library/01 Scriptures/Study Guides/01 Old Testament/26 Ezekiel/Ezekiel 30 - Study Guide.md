## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1–3 — Announcement:** Ezekiel is commanded to prophesy concerning the near day of the LORD.
2. **Verses 4–9 — Egypt and its supporters:** Sword, fear, fire, and desolation extend through Egypt's network of allies.
3. **Verses 10–12 — Named conqueror:** Nebuchadrezzar and his people are identified as the agents who devastate the land.
4. **Verses 13–19 — Geographic survey:** Judgment is applied to Egypt's idols, ruler, cities, military strength, and population; the unit ends with the recognition formula.
5. **Verses 20–26 — Broken and strengthened arms:** A dated oracle contrasts Pharaoh's inability to wield the sword with Babylon's divinely strengthened arms. The repeated prediction of Egyptian dispersion frames the conclusion.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's theological center is the recognition formula, 'they shall know that I am the LORD' (vv.8, 19, 25, 26). Egypt's fall is presented as an act of divine judgment in which the Babylonian king and his army — while acting as historical, human agents of conquest — are also said to have their strength given by the LORD (vv.24–25, 'I will strengthen the arms of the king of Babylon... and put my sword in his hand'). This reflects a broader biblical pattern of the LORD's sovereignty over history and nations, not Israel alone. See also [[Government and Laws]] and [[Prophecy]].

This should be read with some care: the text portrays Babylon as an instrument of judgment in this oracle, but it does not by itself offer a general warrant for nations to identify their own conquests with God's will, nor does the recognition formula necessarily imply willing conversion — in context it may describe recognition compelled by catastrophic defeat rather than repentance.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The dominant metaphor in verses 21–25 is the **arm** as military strength. Pharaoh's broken arm cannot be healed sufficiently “to hold the sword”; then both of his arms are broken, while the arms of Babylon's king are strengthened. The sword consequently falls from Pharaoh's hand and is placed in Babylon's hand.

Day, cloud, darkness, and fire form another cluster of images. The announced “cloudy day” in verse 3 anticipates the darkening and covering cloud at Tehaphnehes in verse 18. In context, these images communicate approaching disaster, though the text does not require every image to be reduced to a single literal phenomenon.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter uses several conventional prophetic devices: the 'day of the LORD' formula (vv.2–3), the recognition formula as a refrain closing both major units (vv.8, 19, 25–26), a catalog/list form for naming allied peoples (v.5) and doomed cities (vv.13–18), and an extended body-part metaphor — the 'arm' as military strength (vv.21–25) — that pairs Pharaoh's broken arm against Babylon's strengthened one. Verse 6 also names 'the tower of Syene' as the place where Egypt's power falls, a phrase textually close to Ezekiel 29:10's use of Syene as a boundary marker in the KJV; some modern translations render the underlying geography differently (e.g., 'from Migdol to Syene'), so the exact relationship between the two verses' wording is best treated as a KJV-level observation rather than a settled point of shared authorial vocabulary.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical *(tentative)*
> Ezekiel's dated prediction that Nebuchadnezzar would move against Egypt corresponds to an actual, independently attested Babylonian campaign into Egypt during Nebuchadnezzar's reign (a fragmentary Babylonian chronicle records a Babylonian move against Egypt around 568/567 BC), which lends historical plausibility to the setting of this oracle.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does “the time of the heathen” in verse 3 denote judgment upon the nations generally, a particular period of foreign domination, or something narrower in this oracle?
- What is meant by “the day of Egypt” in verse 9, and is it identical with the “day of the LORD” in verse 3?
- Does “there shall be no more a prince” in verse 13 claim a permanent end to native Egyptian rule, or use prophetic totalization for a historically bounded collapse?
- How should readers distinguish the text's attribution of events to divine agency from moral approval of every act committed by the conquering army?
- How do the surrounding Egypt oracles in [[Ezekiel 29]], [[Ezekiel 31]], and [[Ezekiel 32]] qualify or expand this chapter?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare with the closely related oracles in [[Ezekiel 29]] (dating formula, Syene, Nebuchadrezzar) and [[Ezekiel 32]] (Egypt's downfall), part of the wider Egypt-judgment cycle running through [[Ezekiel 29]]–[[Ezekiel 32]]. Jeremiah 46 offers an independent prophetic oracle against Egypt that also names Nebuchadrezzar; the topical overlap is worth studying, but it is a semantic match rather than a verified textual parallel for this chapter, so treat any claim of direct literary dependence cautiously. When studying under [[Prophecy]] or [[War and Peace]], keep the text's own attribution of Babylon's victory to the LORD distinct from historical verification of the oracle's full scope — for example, how completely Nebuchadnezzar's actual campaign against Egypt (attested only in a fragmentary and damaged Babylonian source) matched the totalizing language of 'no more a prince' and universal desolation.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
