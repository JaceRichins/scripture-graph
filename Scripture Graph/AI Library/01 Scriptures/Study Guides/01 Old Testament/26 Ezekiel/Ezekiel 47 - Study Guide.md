## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **47:1–5 — The deepening river:** Water emerges beneath the sanctuary threshold and rises at four successive thousand-cubit measurements.
2. **47:6–12 — Life, healing, and an exception:** The river supports trees and fish and heals the sea, while the marshes remain salt.
3. **47:13–21 — Borders and tribal inheritance:** The vision turns to the boundaries and division of the land.
4. **47:22–23 — Inheritance for resident strangers:** Qualifying strangers receive inheritance in the tribe where they live.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter associates the sanctuary with life, healing, and abundance extending into the surrounding land. Interpreters differ over whether the river and renewed landscape describe literal future conditions, symbolic covenant blessing, or both. The text itself does not settle that question.

Verses 22–23 make a more explicit social provision: resident strangers who have children among Israel are treated as native-born for purposes of receiving land in their tribe of residence. This makes the passage relevant to [[Temples]], the [[Gathering of Israel]], and [[Jews and Gentiles]], while not establishing equality in every legal or religious institution.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Four repeated thousand-cubit measurements organize the river scene and create a clear escalation from ankle depth to an uncrossable river. The question, “Son of man, hast thou seen this?” (Ezekiel 47:6), marks the transition from Ezekiel's passage through the water to the description of its effects.

The combination of a sacredly sourced river, recurring fruit, and healing leaves forms a close canonical parallel with [[Revelation 22]]. [[Genesis 2]], [[Joel 3]], and [[Zechariah 14]] offer additional sacred-river comparisons. These relationships establish shared imagery; by themselves they do not prove a single direction of literary dependence or identical meaning.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why are the miry places and marshes left unhealed and assigned to salt (Ezekiel 47:11)?
- Should the rapidly deepening river be understood literally, symbolically, or as a vision capable of working at both levels?
- What does the grant of tribal land to resident strangers add to earlier commands concerning strangers?
- How, if at all, are the environmental renewal of verses 1–12 and the territorial and social order of verses 13–23 meant to interpret one another?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the river imagery with [[Genesis 2]], [[Joel 3]], [[Zechariah 14]], and [[Revelation 22]]. Read [[Ezekiel 40]]–[[Ezekiel 46]] for the larger temple vision and [[Ezekiel 48]] for the completed tribal allotments. Leviticus 19:34 provides a useful comparison for the instruction to regard a resident stranger as native-born, while Ezekiel 47 applies that language specifically to land inheritance.
