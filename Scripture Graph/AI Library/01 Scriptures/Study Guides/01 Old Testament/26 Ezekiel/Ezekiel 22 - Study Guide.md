# Ezekiel 22 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 22]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 22 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Ezekiel catalogs the sins of the people of Judah in Jerusalem—They will be scattered and destroyed for their iniquities.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
Three oracle-units, each triggered by 'the word of the LORD came unto me':

1. **22:1-16** — the bloody city: charge (2-4), catalog of sins (6-12), and sentence of scattering (13-16), closing with the recognition formula ('thou shalt know that I am the LORD,' v.16).
2. **22:17-22** — Israel as dross: an extended smelting metaphor, also closing with the recognition formula (v.22).
3. **22:23-31** — the land and its leaders: four groups indicted in turn (prophets v.25 and again v.28, priests v.26, princes v.27, the people of the land v.29), the search for an intercessor (v.30), and a summary of judgment (v.31) — without the recognition formula that closed the first two units.

The third unit's missing recognition formula and its report of judgment already carried out may suggest a bleaker close than the first two — though Ezekiel does not attach the formula to every judgment oracle in the book, so this reading should be held loosely rather than as an established pattern.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Sin as covenant violation** — the catalog in 22:3-12 tracks [[Law of Moses]] categories: sexual purity, [[Sabbath Day]] observance, honest dealing, and protection of the stranger, fatherless, and widow. Guilt is defined relationally, against covenant obligations, not abstractly.
- **Institutional accountability** — 22:25-29 assigns distinct blame to [[Prophets]], priests, and princes in turn, plus the general population, rather than treating the nation as an undifferentiated mass. Priests are condemned specifically for failing their defining cultic duty to distinguish holy from common and clean from unclean (compare Leviticus 10:10).
- **[[Justice and Mercy]] and intercession** — 22:30's search for 'a man... to stand in the gap' frames judgment as something a single righteous figure might, in principle, have averted. The text does not specify whether that figure is envisioned as an intercessor, a reformer, or a defender — the hedge-and-breach image supports more than one reading.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two images carry the chapter's argument:

- **Dross and the furnace (22:17-22).** Silver-refining separates dross from precious metal by melting; elsewhere in scripture (e.g., Isaiah's 'I will purge away thy dross') that process yields a purified remainder. Ezekiel's version stops short of that: the text says only that God will 'leave you there, and melt you' (v.20) and that the result is recognition of his fury (v.22). Whether a purified remnant is assumed elsewhere in Ezekiel's theology, or simply left unaddressed here, is not something this passage settles.
- **'Stand in the gap' (22:30).** The image is of a breached wall or hedge that a defender must occupy to keep out an enemy. Applied to a person pleading for the land, it could picture prayerful intercession, active reform, or physical defense of the community — the verse itself does not specify which.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's most distinctive structural feature is the sequence of indictments in 22:25-29: prophets, priests, princes, and the people of the land are each charged in turn (prophets are named twice, in v.25 and again in v.28), using parallel accusatory patterns — prophets like 'a roaring lion,' princes 'like wolves ravening the prey.' This sweep through every social tier assigns comprehensive blame without exempting any group.

Verse 28's language of prophets who 'daubed... with untempered mortar' reuses the same distinctive construction imagery developed at length in [[Ezekiel 13]], where prophets cover a flimsy wall with whitewash instead of reinforcing it. Read together, the two chapters suggest this was a recurring idiom in Ezekiel's own repertoire for false, self-generated prophecy — evidence of a recurring motif, not necessarily a deliberate cross-reference meant for a reader to notice.

The epithet 'the bloody city' (22:2) recurs later in the book for Jerusalem itself, in [[Ezekiel 24]] — a closer parallel than the similar language applied to Nineveh in Nahum. Within Ezekiel's own oracles, 'bloody city' appears to function as the prophet's standing epithet for Jerusalem's own guilt, rather than a borrowing from oracles against foreign nations.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does 'consume thy filthiness' (22:15) imply any restorative outcome, or does judgment in this chapter remain purely punitive throughout?
- Is 'a man... to stand in the gap' (22:30) primarily an intercessor, a reforming leader, or a defender — or does the image deliberately combine these roles?
- Why does the third oracle (23-31) omit the recognition formula that closes the first two units — deliberate rhetorical bleakness, or ordinary variation, given that Ezekiel does not use the formula after every oracle in the book?
- How does the priests' failure to 'put no difference between the holy and profane' (22:26) relate to the priestly duty stated in Leviticus 10:10?
- What does the chapter gain rhetorically by indicting prophets, priests, princes, and people in succession rather than addressing the city as a whole?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
