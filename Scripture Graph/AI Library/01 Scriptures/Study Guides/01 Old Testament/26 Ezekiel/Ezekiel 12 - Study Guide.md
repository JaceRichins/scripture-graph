<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter divides into five units, each opened by a 'word of the LORD' formula (vv. 1, 8, 17, 21, 26):

- **vv. 1-7** — Ezekiel enacts the sign: packing for exile, digging through a wall, carrying his goods at twilight with a covered face, and the report that he did as commanded (v. 7).
- **vv. 8-16** — The LORD interprets the sign: it concerns 'the prince in Jerusalem' (v. 10), who will attempt the same escape, be captured, and be exiled to Babylon; his followers are scattered, and a remnant is preserved to testify among the nations (v. 16).
- **vv. 17-20** — A second commanded sign: eating bread and drinking water 'with quaking' and 'trembling,' picturing the fear accompanying Jerusalem's coming desolation.
- **vv. 21-25** — An oracle answering the proverb 'the days are prolonged, and every vision faileth.'
- **vv. 26-28** — A second oracle answering the related claim that Ezekiel's visions concern only 'times that are far off.'

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents judgment as a response to rebellion and violence, not an unexplained disaster (vv. 2, 19), and its repeated outcome is recognition of the LORD (vv. 15-16, 20) — central to the chapter's picture of [[Prophecy]] as something that will certainly come to pass ('the word which I have spoken shall be done,' v. 28). The people's failure to perceive, despite having 'eyes to see' and 'ears to hear' (v. 2), is set against their description as 'a rebellious house'; the text itself, however, does not settle whether this blindness is self-caused defiance or a hardening permitted by God, a live ambiguity elsewhere in prophetic literature. The few survivors of v. 16 are not favored escapees — they are preserved specifically to declare Israel's 'abominations' among the nations, joining accountability, testimony, and divine self-disclosure without describing a full national restoration.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated phrase “in their sight” makes the first sign deliberately public (12:3–7). This emphasis answers the opening diagnosis of people who have eyes but do not see (12:2): the action is visible, yet visibility does not guarantee understanding. The two delay sayings also differ subtly. Verse 22 treats elapsed time as evidence that visions fail, whereas verse 27 does not deny fulfillment but safely relocates it to the distant future.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The enacted sign is followed by the question 'What doest thou?' and then its interpretation (vv. 7-11), turning Ezekiel's behavior into a public riddle whose meaning must be supplied by the prophetic word. The recognition formula ('they/ye shall know that I am the LORD') occurs three times in this short chapter alone (vv. 15, 16, 20), one of Ezekiel's most characteristic refrains. The 'eyes...ears' idiom (v. 2) belongs to a fixed biblical phrase for failed perception, echoed almost verbatim in Jeremiah 5:21 and later applied by Jesus to his hearers in Matthew 13:13. The prediction that the prince would be taken to Babylon yet 'not see it, though he shall die there' (v. 13) is especially striking alongside Zedekiah's capture, blinding, and deportation in [[2 Kings 25]] and [[Jeremiah 39]] — a close correspondence that strongly supports identifying the unnamed prince as Zedekiah, though it does not by itself establish when the oracle reached its present written form.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Intertextual-Historical Correspondence
> The prince's departure, capture, transport to Babylon, and inability to see Babylon in Ezekiel 12:12-13 closely correspond to accounts of Zedekiah's nighttime flight, capture, blinding, and deportation in 2 Kings 25:4-7 and Jeremiah 39:4-7. This strongly supports identifying the unnamed prince with Zedekiah and reading the oracle in relation to his fate.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean for a prophet's *actions*, not just his words, to function as a sign (vv. 6, 11)? The phrase 'in their sight' recurs seven times across vv. 3-7 alone — why does the sign need to be so insistently public for an audience already diagnosed as having eyes that do not see (v. 2)?
- Why are a few survivors preserved (v. 16), and what does their assigned role — declaring Israel's 'abominations' among the nations — suggest about the purpose of survival?
- What different evasions are represented by 'every vision faileth' (v. 22, treating delay as proof of failure) versus 'times that are far off' (v. 27, conceding fulfillment but deferring it)? What modern equivalents serve the same function toward warnings we would rather not act on?
- Why does Ezekiel call Judah's ruler 'prince' rather than 'king' (vv. 10, 12)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the predicted fate of 'the prince' (Ezekiel 12:10-13) with the historical account of Zedekiah's flight, capture, and deportation in [[2 Kings 25]] and [[Jeremiah 39]], and his blinding and eventual death in Babylon per [[Jeremiah 52]]. The correspondence — night flight, capture, transport to Babylon, and loss of sight — makes the identification of the unnamed prince with Zedekiah nearly universal among interpreters, though it does not by itself resolve whether the oracle in its present wording predates or was shaped after the events of 586 BC.
