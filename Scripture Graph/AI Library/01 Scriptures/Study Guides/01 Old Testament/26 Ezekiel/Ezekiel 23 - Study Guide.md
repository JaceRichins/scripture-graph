# Ezekiel 23 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 23]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 23 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Two sisters, Samaria and Jerusalem, committed whoredoms by worshipping idols—Both are destroyed for their lewdness.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The sisters identified (vv. 1-4):** Aholah is Samaria; Aholibah is Jerusalem.
2. **Aholah's course and judgment (vv. 5-10):** desire for Assyria, idolatrous defilement, and destruction by Assyria.
3. **Aholibah repeats and exceeds her sister (vv. 11-21):** Jerusalem pursues Assyria and Chaldea despite seeing Samaria's fate.
4. **Jerusalem's approaching judgment (vv. 22-35):** former lovers become attackers, and Jerusalem drinks Samaria's cup.
5. **Formal indictment and sentence (vv. 36-49):** adultery is joined to bloodshed, child sacrifice, profaned worship, and national destruction.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's controlling concern is broken allegiance. It presents [[Apostasy]] as a pattern involving desire, worship, political trust, and violence rather than a merely private error. Verse 35 locates the root in forgetting God and casting him behind one's back. Verses 37-39 connect corrupt worship with harm to children and profanation of [[Temples]] and the [[Sabbath Day]]. The chapter's account of [[Justice and Mercy]] is heavily weighted toward judgment; restoration and mercy must be studied in the wider book rather than imported into this oracle.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The prophecy repeatedly turns attraction into alienation: Aholibah desires the Babylonians, becomes alienated from them, and is then delivered into the hands of those she hates. That reversal makes the former lovers instruments of judgment. The recurring language of seeing, desiring, sending, defilement, exposure, and stripping gives the oracle a grim internal coherence. Its explicit sexual and anatomical language is part of the canonical text and should be acknowledged rather than softened.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The two-sister design creates comparison and escalation. Aholah's course and destruction are narrated first; Aholibah then follows the same way and is called more corrupt (23:11, 31). This sequence can present Samaria's fate as an unheeded warning, although the text does not specify exactly what Jerusalem witnessed or knew. The "sister's cup" in verses 31-34 condenses the comparison into an image of shared judgment and desolation. The foreign powers Jerusalem desired later become her attackers and judges (23:22-29). Like [[Ezekiel 16]] and [[Jeremiah 3]], the chapter uses sexual imagery for national disloyalty, but Ezekiel 23 is unusually graphic. Its portrayal of female bodies through sexual humiliation and violence creates a serious moral and pastoral difficulty that should remain visible in interpretation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What specific actions does the chapter itself identify behind the sexual metaphor?
- How does Jerusalem's knowledge of Samaria's fall affect the accusation against her?
- What is gained, and what moral or pastoral dangers arise, from portraying nations as women subjected to sexualized punishment?
- How should readers distinguish the oracle's denunciation of national apostasy from harmful claims about actual women or victims of violence?
- Where does the wider book of Ezekiel place hope and restoration alongside the severe judgment emphasized here?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Ezekiel 16]] for another extended allegory of Jerusalem, [[Ezekiel 20]] for the themes of Egyptian idolatry, sabbaths, and child sacrifice, and [[Jeremiah 3]] for the unfaithful-sisters comparison. Read [[2 Kings 17]] for the Assyrian fall of Samaria and [[2 Kings 24]] for the Babylonian pressure on Jerusalem. These comparisons clarify the historical and literary setting without requiring every image in Ezekiel 23 to correspond to one discrete event.
