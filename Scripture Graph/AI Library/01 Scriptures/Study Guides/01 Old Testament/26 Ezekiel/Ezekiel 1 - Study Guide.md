# Ezekiel 1 — Study Guide

## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Setting and prophetic encounter** (1:1-3) -- dates, exile setting, Ezekiel's identity, and the word and hand of the LORD.
2. **Storm and living creatures** (1:4-14) -- cloud, fire, four composite creatures, and rapid, spirit-directed movement.
3. **Wheels** (1:15-21) -- wheel imagery beside the creatures, eye-covered rings, and complete coordination with the creatures' motion.
4. **Firmament and voice** (1:22-25) -- an expanse above the creatures, overwhelming wing-sounds, and a voice from above.
5. **Throne and glory** (1:26-28) -- a sapphire-like throne, a humanlike fiery appearance, rainbow-like brightness, and Ezekiel's prostration.

The description moves in a roughly bottom-to-top sweep -- from the storm and creatures at ground level, up through the wheels and firmament, to the throne above -- but this reflects the vision's own unfolding rather than a tightly engineered literary scheme.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The vision insists, through its repeated hedging language ("likeness," "appearance," "as it were"), that what Ezekiel witnessed exceeds ordinary description -- worth taking seriously rather than resolving into a tidy diagram. Because the word, hand, and visions of the LORD reach Ezekiel in exile rather than at the Jerusalem temple, the chapter supports (without stating outright) the inference that [[Revelation]] is not geographically confined to Jerusalem. The wheels move only "whither the spirit was to go" (1:12, 20-21) and rise and stand together with the creatures beneath the throne -- a coordinated apparatus that later chapters (Ezekiel 10; Ezekiel 43) explicitly revisit as the same vision returning. Read across the book, Ezekiel 1 functions as the first statement of a theme about where and how God's presence can be found; the vision's arrangement (creatures and wheels below, firmament and throne above) also visually sets the enthroned glory apart from what accompanies it, though the chapter itself does not spell out a doctrine of rank.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The controlling vocabulary is analogical: “likeness,” “appearance,” “like,” “as,” and “as it were.” These expressions allow Ezekiel to report what he perceived while repeatedly marking the limits of ordinary language. Readers should therefore distinguish what the text describes from later symbolic assignments. The chapter itself does not explain what each face, eye, color, or direction signifies.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
[[Ezekiel 10]] is the most important internal interpretive parallel: it revisits the same visionary complex and explicitly identifies the living creatures as cherubim (Ezekiel 10:20). [[Ezekiel 43]] later compares another vision directly with "the vision that I saw by the river Chebar" (43:3). [[Revelation 4]] draws on related throne-room vocabulary -- four living creatures, abundant eyes -- but rearranges the details rather than reproducing Ezekiel's scene exactly: Ezekiel places the eyes on the wheel-rings (1:18), Revelation on the creatures themselves. The four-faced, multi-winged composite creatures also resemble, in a general way, the winged hybrid guardian figures known from Mesopotamian art; this is a suggestive but unconfirmed comparison, worth noting as a tentative observation about the visual world available to an exile in Babylonia rather than a demonstrated source.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Intratextual Identification
> Ezekiel 10 explicitly identifies the living creatures seen by the river Chebar as cherubim. Within the book's final form, this is strong intratextual evidence that the four living creatures of Ezekiel 1 are intended to be understood as cherubim.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strongest connection claim in the set · [[Intratextual Identification in Ezekiel 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical/Archaeological *(tentative)*
> 'The river of Chebar' is generally identified by scholars with a major irrigation canal in the Nippur region of Babylonia (sometimes rendered nar/naru kabari in later cuneiform administrative archives from that area), consistent with the practice of settling deportee communities along Babylonian canal systems for agricultural labor and taxation.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.3 · disputed identification, specific archaeological detail unverified

> [!evidence] Literary/Intertextual *(tentative)*
> Daniel's throne vision describes a divine throne whose 'wheels' are 'as burning fire' (Daniel 7:9), a striking image-level parallel to Ezekiel's wheels of fire-like appearance beneath the divine throne. This supports the idea that throne-chariot imagery involving fiery wheels was a shared element of exilic/post-exilic Judean visionary vocabulary, independent of whether one text used the other directly.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.3 · parallel exists but is misdescribed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What is gained by the steady upward movement of the description, from storm, creatures, and wheels to firmament and throne?
- Why does the narrator use so many layers of resemblance before naming "the glory of the LORD"?
- What does it mean that this revelation reaches Ezekiel among captives in Chaldea rather than at the Jerusalem temple?
- Does Ezekiel already understand the living creatures as cherubim here, or is that identification only made explicit later, in Ezekiel 10?
- Which interpretations arise from the chapter itself, and which depend on Ezekiel 10, Ezekiel 43, or other later texts?
