# Ezekiel 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 5]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Ezekiel 5 completes the sign-act begun in [[Ezekiel 4]]: the prophet shaves his head and beard, weighs the hair, and divides it into three portions — burned in the city, struck with a knife, and scattered to the wind, with a sword drawn out after it (vv.1-4) — while a few strands are bound in his garment and then partly burned as well. Verse 5 supplies the sign's referent: "This is Jerusalem." The oracle that follows (vv.6-17) explains why: Jerusalem "changed my judgments into wickedness more than the nations" and defiled God's sanctuary (vv.6-7, 11). The threefold hair-division is restated almost verbatim as the literal fate of the city's people — pestilence and famine, the sword, and scattering to every wind (v.12) — and the judgment is repeatedly described as unfolding publicly, so that Jerusalem becomes "a reproach and a taunt... unto the nations that are round about thee" (vv.8, 14-15). A closing catalogue of famine, evil beasts, pestilence, and sword (vv.16-17) restates the judgment once more, each block sealed by "I the LORD have spoken it" (vv.13, 15, 17).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- vv.1-4 — Sign-act: hair is shaved, weighed, and divided into three (burned / struck / scattered); a small remnant is bound in the prophet's skirts, and some of even that remnant is burned.
- v.5 — Interpretive key: "This is Jerusalem."
- vv.6-7 — Indictment: Jerusalem's rebellion is described as exceeding that of the surrounding nations.
- vv.8-11 — Sentence announced: God declares himself "against" the city, grounding judgment in defiled sanctuary and unmatched abominations.
- v.12 — The three portions of hair restated as the three fates of the population: pestilence/famine, sword, scattering.
- vv.13-15 — Consequence: Jerusalem becomes a reproach and astonishment to the nations, closed with "I the LORD have spoken it."
- vv.16-17 — A further judgment list (famine, evil beasts, pestilence, sword), closed again with the same formula.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter grounds Jerusalem's judgment in stated causes — refusal of God's "judgments and statutes" (vv.6-7) and defilement of the sanctuary (v.11) — framing the disaster as covenant consequence rather than an unexplained military defeat, and tying it to [[Law of Moses]], [[Covenants]], and [[Temples]]. The comparison with surrounding nations (v.6) suggests, as interpretation rather than stated doctrine, that greater covenant knowledge carries greater accountability. The chapter's own language is nearly all justice, not mercy: "neither shall mine eye spare, neither will I have any pity" (v.11). Readers should be cautious about importing a mercy counterpoint the text itself does not supply here.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated fractions link action and explanation: three portions of hair in verse 2 become three portions of the population in verse 12. Repeated divine-speech formulas—especially “Thus saith the Lord GOD” and “I the LORD have spoken it”—frame the oracle as claimed revelation and emphasize its asserted authority. References to the nations “round about” Jerusalem recur throughout the chapter, making the international audience part of the composition rather than a passing detail.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter moves from symbolic action to explicit interpretation. This reduces uncertainty about the sign's main referent, although not every minor detail is decoded. The few hairs bound in the skirts create a limited-preservation motif, but verse 4 complicates any simple equation of that group with a completely protected remnant.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Is the threefold hair-division a literal demographic prediction, or a schematic way of saying no group is wholly spared?
- What is preserved, and what remains at risk, in the hairs first bound in the skirts and then partly burned (vv.3-4)?
- In what sense does Jerusalem's downfall become an "instruction" to the surrounding nations (v.15), and does the text say anything about whether that lesson is received?
- How does the fourfold list in vv.16-17 (famine, beasts, pestilence, sword) relate to the threefold structure of vv.1-4 and v.12 — the same judgment recast, or a separate, cumulative catalogue?
- Does Ezekiel's identity as a priest (see [[Ezekiel 1]]) add significance to his being commanded to shave his head and beard, given priestly restrictions found elsewhere in the Law of Moses?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Ezekiel 4]] for the preceding siege sign, [[Ezekiel 12]] for another pairing of scattering and pursuing sword, and [[Ezekiel 14]] for the fourfold judgment of sword, famine, harmful beasts, and pestilence. Compare Ezekiel 5:10 with [[Leviticus 26]] and [[Deuteronomy 28]], where siege cannibalism appears among covenant curses.
