# Ezekiel 43 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ezekiel 43]] · My notes: [[Ezekiel 43 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The glory of God fills the temple—His throne is there, and He promises to dwell in the midst of Israel forever—Ezekiel sees the altar and the ordinances of the altar.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Return of divine glory (1-5):** glory approaches from the east, enters the house, and fills it.

2. **Divine dwelling and the end of defilement (6-9):** the house is identified as God's throne-place, and Israel is commanded to remove defiling practices.

3. **The pattern as instruction (10-12):** Israel is to see and measure the house, be ashamed, and keep its laws and ordinances; the whole precinct is most holy.

4. **Dimensions of the altar (13-17):** the altar's levels, horns, square form, border, and east-facing stairs are specified.

5. **Purification and continuing worship (18-27):** Zadokite priests conduct a seven-day dedication; regular offerings begin on the eighth day with the promise of divine acceptance.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Temples]], [[Repentance]], and [[Obedience]] rather than treating sacred architecture as sufficient by itself. Israel must put away what defiles God's name (verses 7-9), and the revealed pattern is meant to produce shame for iniquity and observance of divine ordinances (verses 10-11). The final promise, “I will accept you” (verse 27), places acceptance at the culmination of purification and authorized [[Sacrifice]]. Care is needed when applying these rites beyond their own priestly and visionary setting; the chapter itself describes an altar cult using categories of the [[Law of Moses]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's most significant literary feature is the reversal of direction: earlier in Ezekiel, the divine glory departs the temple eastward, pausing at the east gate before standing on the mountain east of the city ([[Ezekiel 10]]; [[Ezekiel 11]]); here it approaches from the east and fills the restored house. This is a deliberate narrative counterpart to the earlier departure, though it describes a literary correspondence rather than settling how literally the vision's architecture is meant to be realized. Verses 3-6 also explicitly recall Ezekiel's earlier visionary experience — falling on his face, being addressed by a voice from within the house — naming both the Chebar vision and the vision of the city's destruction as points of comparison, so the prophet deliberately situates this scene alongside his own earlier revelations rather than presenting it as unconnected.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary And Spatial Correspondence
> The east-to-temple movement of divine glory in Ezekiel 43 forms a strong literary counterpart to the earlier departure of glory toward the east. This supports reading the scene as a reversal of abandonment and a portrayal of restored divine presence.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · broadly accepted literary observation · [[Internal Literary And Spatial Correspondence in Ezekiel 43|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Comparison *(tentative)*
> The extraordinarily precise temple and altar dimensions given in Ezekiel 40-43 were not, so far as the historical/archaeological record indicates, followed in constructing the post-exilic Second Temple (Zerubbabel's, later Herod's), which raises the question of whether the vision was intended as a literal building blueprint.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What exactly does 'the carcases of their kings' (43:7, 9) refer to — literal royal tombs near the temple, idolatrous cult objects, or something else? The text does not say.
- Why is priestly service restricted here to 'the seed of Zadok' (43:19) when the law of Moses speaks more broadly of Aaron's sons? ([[Ezekiel 44]] addresses this further.)
- Is the temple of chapters 40-43 meant as a literal construction plan, a symbolic ideal, or a description of a future temple? The text itself does not settle the question.
- Does 'I will dwell in the midst of them for ever' (43:7, 9) function as an unconditional promise, a covenant promise joined to Israel putting away defilement, or both?
- Why is showing and measuring the house expected to make Israel 'ashamed of their iniquities' (43:10-11)?
- How should the difficult wording 'when I came to destroy the city' (43:3) be understood, given that elsewhere in the vision judgment is carried out by divine agents rather than by Ezekiel himself?
