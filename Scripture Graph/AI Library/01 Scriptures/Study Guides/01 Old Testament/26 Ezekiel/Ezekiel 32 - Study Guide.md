## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Pharaoh captured and exposed (vv. 1–6):** The ruler figured as a lion and aquatic monster is netted, cast onto the land, and made food for animals.
2. **Cosmic and international repercussions (vv. 7–10):** Darkness accompanies Egypt's fall, while nations and kings respond with amazement and fear.
3. **Babylonian destruction and desolation (vv. 11–16):** Babylon's sword destroys Egypt's multitude and pomp; the unit ends by returning to lamentation.
4. **Egypt summoned to the pit (vv. 17–21):** A second oracle commands a wail and announces Egypt's placement among those slain by the sword.
5. **Catalogue of fallen powers (vv. 22–30):** Asshur, Elam, Meshech, Tubal, Edom, northern princes, and Zidonians occupy the realm of the defeated.
6. **Pharaoh joins their company (vv. 31–32):** His only consolation is to see that other terrifying powers share his fate.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter emphasizes the LORD's sovereignty over nations. Egypt's military power, prestige, population, and resources do not exempt it from judgment, and the recognition formula in verse 15 connects that judgment with knowing that the speaker is the LORD.

The fall of powers that once spread terror offers a severe meditation on [[Pride]] and [[War and Peace]]. Their weapons and former might cannot prevent their descent among the slain.

The pit imagery is relevant to [[Death and the Spirit World]], but it is a poetic judgment scene rather than a complete map or systematic doctrine of the afterlife. Its details should not be pressed beyond what the lament itself establishes.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly uses the vocabulary of descent: "nether parts of the earth," "hell," and "the pit." In the canonical English text these expressions build a vertical contrast between former prominence in "the land of the living" and present humiliation below it.

"Slain by the sword," "uncircumcised," "graves," "terror," and "shame" recur through verses 20–30. The accumulation matters more than any isolated occurrence: every new nation added to the list reinforces the shared fate of seemingly exceptional powers.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The first oracle is organized by reversal. Pharaoh begins as a disruptive lion and aquatic creature but becomes captured prey, an exposed carcass, and a source of blood and pollution. The darkening of the heavenly lights enlarges Egypt's political collapse into cosmic-scale judgment imagery, without settling whether a literal celestial event is intended.

The second oracle is a descent scene structured as a catalogue of fallen powers. Repeated references to the sword, graves, the pit, uncircumcised status, terror, and shame give the section a deliberate rhetorical unity, though they do not prove ritual use or a particular compositional history.

[[Ezekiel 31]] is the closest contextual parallel because it shares substantial descent language. [[Isaiah 14]] offers a further genre comparison: both passages place a fallen imperial ruler among the dead to expose the mortality of political greatness. Resemblance does not establish direct borrowing.

A final irony concerns terror. Nations that had caused terror in the land of the living now lie powerless, while the LORD claims the decisive terror in verse 32.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What is the effect of portraying Pharaoh first as both a lion and an aquatic creature, then as captured prey?
- How does the cosmic-darkening imagery intensify the portrayal of Egypt's fall? What does the text establish, and what remains uncertain about literal fulfillment?
- Why does the poem place Egypt alongside Asshur, Elam, Edom, and other fallen powers rather than treating Egypt's judgment as unique?
- How do the repeated phrases about the sword, graves, shame, and the pit shape the second oracle?
- What might Pharaoh's "comfort" in verse 31 mean, and which explanations go beyond what the verse states?
- How does comparison with [[Ezekiel 31]] clarify the imagery without requiring that the chapters were originally composed as one unit?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the descent language in verses 17-32 with [[Ezekiel 31]], especially its references to the nether parts, the uncircumcised, and those slain by the sword. Compare the underworld humiliation scene with [[Isaiah 14]], while considering both shared convention and possible literary relationship.

For other uses of descent-to-the-pit imagery in Ezekiel, examine [[Ezekiel 26]] and [[Ezekiel 28]]. For the wider collection of judgments against Egypt, read [[Ezekiel 29]] and [[Ezekiel 30]]. Ezekiel 29:18-20 supplies the full compensation account connecting Nebuchadnezzar's unrewarded labor against Tyre with Egypt's spoil.

Compare the darkening of the heavenly lights in verses 7-8 with [[Isaiah 13]]. These parallels illuminate recurring prophetic imagery but do not by themselves prove direct borrowing or identical fulfillment.
