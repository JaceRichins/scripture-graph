# Ezekiel 26 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Because she rejoiced in the sorrows and fall of Jerusalem, Tyre will be destroyed.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Verses 1-6 — Cause and sentence:** Tyre celebrates Jerusalem's breach; many nations are compared to waves; the city is reduced to bare rock and fishing ground.
2. **Verses 7-14 — Siege and dismantling:** Nebuchadrezzar and his forces attack the dependent settlements and defenses. The description moves from conquest to removal of buildings and commercial wealth, then to silence.
3. **Verses 15-18 — International lament:** Sea-princes leave their thrones and ceremonial clothing, sit on the ground, and mourn the renowned maritime city.
4. **Verses 19-21 — Descent into death:** The sea imagery becomes cosmic: the deep covers Tyre, which descends into the pit among peoples of old.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects divine judgment with [[Pride]] expressed as opportunistic delight in another community's ruin. Its logic challenges the assumption that another person's catastrophe is merely one's own opportunity.

The recognition formula in verse 6 presents judgment as revelation of divine sovereignty. Even so, the chapter does not authorize readers to identify every historical disaster as a transparent divine punishment; that conclusion would go beyond what this particular oracle claims.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three linked image systems govern the chapter. First, nations arrive like sea waves, and the deep ultimately covers the maritime city. Second, public sound is reversed into silence: horses, wheels, slaughter, songs, and harps disappear. Third, status becomes descent: rulers come down from thrones and sit on the ground before Tyre itself goes down into the pit.

The shift from singular 'he' in the Nebuchadrezzar section to plural 'they' in verse 12 deserves attention. It permits more than one reading but does not, without additional evidence, identify Alexander or divide the passage conclusively between Babylonian and Macedonian campaigns.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The lament deliberately magnifies Tyre before announcing its disappearance: it is inhabited by seafaring people, renowned, strong in the sea, and feared by those around it. This heightening makes the reversal sharper. Royal sea-princes enact Tyre's humiliation symbolically by surrendering thrones and embroidered garments.

The bare-rock and fishing-net image forms an inclusion around the siege account in verses 4-5 and 14. The repetition emphasizes loss of constructed urban identity rather than merely a change of ruler.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical Counterevidence
> Tyre's substantial later history and its continued existence as an inhabited place make an exhaustive literal reading of 'built no more' and 'never be found again' historically difficult. This is strong counterevidence to a maximal interpretation in which no settlement could ever again exist at Tyre, though it is not decisive against interpretations limited to the ancient city's political form, former commercial supremacy, or the conventional totality language of prophetic judgment.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong historical counterevidence to maximal literalism · [[Historical Counterevidence in Ezekiel 26|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Correspondence *(tentative)*
> Ancient historical traditions report a prolonged Babylonian siege of Tyre under Nebuchadnezzar and, centuries later, Alexander's successful siege of the island city by constructing a causeway. These events provide moderate circumstantial support for a cumulative reading of the many nations, Babylonian assault, and material cast into the water. The correspondence is meaningful but incomplete and does not prove that Alexander was consciously predicted in verse 12.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.8 · Historical events accepted; prophetic linkage disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does 'many nations' describe Nebuchadrezzar's multinational army, successive historical conquerors, or both?
- Does 'built no more' refer to all later habitation, the ancient island city's form, or Tyre's former imperial-commercial status?
- How should [[Ezekiel 29]]'s statement that Babylon received no wages from Tyre constrain readings of this chapter?
- Which features belong to ordinary prophetic hyperbole, and which were intended as narrowly testable historical predictions?
- Why does the lament focus on the fear of maritime rulers rather than sympathy for Tyre's slain inhabitants?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Ezekiel 27]] for the commercial world associated with Tyre, [[Ezekiel 28]] for the accompanying material concerning Tyrian rule and pride, and [[Ezekiel 29]] for the later statement that Nebuchadrezzar received no wages from Tyre. Compare [[Isaiah 23]] and [[Zechariah 9]] for other prophetic treatments of the city. [[Revelation 18]] offers a later literary parallel in its lament by maritime and commercial beneficiaries.

For a responsible study of [[Prophecy]], keep several questions distinct: what Ezekiel 26 itself says; what limited evidence survives for the Babylonian siege; what better-attested sources say about Alexander's later siege; what Tyre's subsequent habitation means for absolute language; and how ancient judgment poetry uses totalizing expressions. Similarity to a later event can be significant without proving that event was the passage's original or exclusive referent.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
