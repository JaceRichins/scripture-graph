## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **Micah 3:1-4 — Rulers:** Those expected to know judgment instead hate good, love evil, and prey upon the people; their later cries will not be heard.

- **Micah 3:5-8 — Prophets:** Materially conditioned messages lead to darkness and the loss of divine answers, while Micah claims power by the Spirit to declare [[Sin]].

- **Micah 3:9-12 — The leadership establishment:** Heads, priests, and prophets profit from their offices and presume that the Lord's presence guarantees safety. The answer is a prediction of devastation for Zion, Jerusalem, and the mountain of the house.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Justice and Mercy]] without reducing either to sentiment. The leaders' treatment of vulnerable people matters to God, and religious claims do not cancel accountability. Micah 3:4 should not be generalized into a claim that every unanswered [[Prayer]] results from personal sin; within this passage, the refusal to hear is a judicial response to the conduct of the oppressors being addressed.

Micah 3:5-8 also distinguishes claimed religious authority from truthful [[Revelation]]. The corrupt prophets' access to visions ends, while Micah identifies the Spirit of the Lord, judgment, and moral courage as enabling him to name transgression openly.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 1 and 9 both open with the summons 'Hear' addressed to the heads and princes of Jacob and Israel ([[Micah 3]]:1, 9), framing the chapter's two main indictments around a distinct prophet-oracle (3:5-8) that sits between them. Verses 2-3 use an extended, visceral image of butchery and cannibalism — rulers who 'eat the flesh of my people' and 'chop them in pieces, as for the pot' — to depict predatory, dehumanizing treatment of the people; the chapter does not specify which particular abuses this figuratively represents. Verses 6-7 answer with an image of darkness: night, no vision, a sun gone down over the prophets, and 'no answer of God' — before verse 8's 'But truly' marks Micah's own contrasting claim to power by the Spirit of the LORD. The chapter's close reverses the trajectory of sacred space: the mountain of the house — commonly understood as the temple mount, though the verse itself only says 'the house' — is pictured not as a cultivated, built center but 'as the high places of the forest.' Outside the chapter, Jeremiah 26:18 preserves a close verbal parallel to Micah 3:12 and explicitly names 'Micah the Morasthite' as its source — a rare case in the Old Testament of a named earlier prophet's words being directly cited as precedent by a later generation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Intertextual Citation
> Micah 3:12 is quoted essentially verbatim roughly a century later in Jeremiah 26, where elders invoke 'Micah the Morasthite' and this specific oracle against Zion as precedent that a prophet who pronounces coming judgment on Jerusalem should not be put to death for it. This is one of the few places in the Hebrew Bible where one named prophet's oracle is explicitly cited by a later generation as authoritative precedent.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What specific forms of exploitation lie behind the butchery imagery in verses 2-3? The chapter does not identify them precisely.

- Does "bite with their teeth" in verse 5 describe prophets consuming supplied food, attacking people, or deliberately combine both ideas?

- Why do the leaders treat the Lord's presence as protection while disregarding judgment and equity?

- How does the report in [[Jeremiah 26]] affect the reading of Micah 3:12 as warning, prediction, or both?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
[[Jeremiah 26]] is the clearest canonical reception of this chapter. Jeremiah 26:18 closely repeats Micah 3:12 and attributes it to Micah the Morasthite. Jeremiah 26:19 then recalls Hezekiah's fear of the Lord and appeal for mercy. That narrative treats Micah's severe announcement as capable of producing repentance; however, this conditional dimension comes from Jeremiah's retrospective account rather than from an explicit condition stated in Micah 3:12 itself.
