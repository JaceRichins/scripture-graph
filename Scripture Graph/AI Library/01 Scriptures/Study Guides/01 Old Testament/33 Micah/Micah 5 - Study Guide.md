# Micah 5 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Messiah will be born in Bethlehem—In the last days, the remnant of Jacob will triumph gloriously over the Gentiles.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Siege and humiliation (verse 1):** Israel's judge is struck while the people face siege.

2. **The ruler and deliverance (verses 2-6):** A ruler comes from Beth-lehem Ephratah, shepherds through the LORD's power, and answers the Assyrian threat.

3. **The remnant among the peoples (verses 7-9):** Jacob's remnant is compared first to dew and then to a lion.

4. **Purification and judgment (verses 10-15):** Repeated declarations of "I will cut off" remove military power, fortifications, occult practices, and idols; vengeance then falls upon unheeding nations.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins divine deliverance with divine purification. The LORD does not merely defeat external adversaries; he also removes horses, chariots, strongholds, witchcrafts, and handmade objects of worship from among the addressed people. Its vision of [[War and Peace]] therefore resists a simple triumphalist reading: covenant restoration includes the loss of corrupt worship and objects of human reliance.

Christian interpretation identifies the ruler from Bethlehem with [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]. That identification is explicit in [[Matthew 2]], though Matthew's citation there conflates Micah 5:2 with 2 Samuel 5:2 and should be distinguished from claims about what Micah's own eighth-century audience understood.

The remnant-of-Jacob oracle in vv.7-8 becomes, in [[3 Nephi 20]] and [[3 Nephi 21]], a direct textual anchor for the Lord's latter-day covenant with scattered Israel — the strongest, most concretely verified [[Gathering of Israel]] connection in this chapter.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The expression 'from of old, from everlasting' (v.2) gives the ruler an extraordinary antiquity, but the verse does not itself resolve what kind of antiquity is meant. Readings include ancient lineage or appointment tied to the Davidic house, or, in Christian and Latter-day Saint tradition, personal premortal existence. The Hebrew wording alone does not decide between them.

'This man shall be the peace' (v.5) is stronger than 'he will negotiate peace,' but in context that peace is demonstrated through deliverance during invasion (vv.5-6), not an abstract, unconditional peace running through the whole chapter.

The 'seven... eight' formula in v.5 matches a recognized Hebrew numerical-parallelism pattern (compare Proverbs 6:16, Amos 1:3) signaling ample, sufficient leadership rather than a literal roster of fifteen named men; the text gives no basis for identifying specific historical figures.

A more speculative crux: the verb behind 'gather thyself in troops' (v.1) shares a root elsewhere associated with self-laceration in mourning (compare Deuteronomy 14:1) as well as military mustering. This is worth flagging as an open lexical question rather than a settled point.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two contrasts organize the chapter. Little Beth-lehem Ephratah produces a ruler who becomes great to the ends of the earth (vv.2,4). And the remnant of Jacob is compared first to gentle, life-giving dew and then to a lion 'among the flocks of sheep' (vv.7-8) — assigning the remnant both a beneficent and a judicial role, with neither image erasing the other.

A shepherd/flock vocabulary also runs through verses 4-8: the ruler 'shall stand and feed' his people (v.4), deliverers are called 'shepherds' (v.5), and the remnant is pictured among 'flocks of sheep' (v.8) — the same pastoral image doing double duty for protection and for predation.

Verses 10-14 are bound together by the repeated formula 'I will cut off,' moving from instruments of war and fortified places to occult practices and images — pairing the dismantling of military self-reliance with the removal of false worship.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Scriptural Reception
> Matthew 2 provides strong evidence that Micah 5:2 was received within early Christianity as a prophecy concerning the Messiah's association with Bethlehem.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · solid but originally overscored evidence · [[Explicit Scriptural Reception in Micah 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Interpretive
> The phrase 'whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting' (Micah 5:2) is read two distinct ways: (a) as an assertion of the Messiah's premortal, eternal existence, the traditional Christian/LDS reading; or (b) as Hebrew idiom pointing to the antiquity of the Davidic royal line rooted in Bethlehem (David's ancestral town), without implying literal personal eternity.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · disputed interpretation, both readings live · [[Textual/Interpretive in Micah 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Extended Intertextual Reuse
> The sustained verbal correspondence establishes that 3 Nephi 20-21 presents Micah 5's remnant and judgment language within Jesus's covenant discourse concerning the remnant of Jacob and the Gentiles.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · well-bounded, does not overclaim historicity · [[Extended Intertextual Reuse in Micah 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Typological Reading Vs. Historical Referent *(tentative)*
> Micah 5:1's image of the 'judge of Israel' being smitten 'with a rod upon the cheek' most immediately depicts the humiliation of a reigning king of Judah during siege; later Christian readers have sometimes seen it as foreshadowing the mocking and striking of Jesus at his trial, but this typological extension goes beyond the chapter's own historical referent.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does 'whose goings forth have been from of old, from everlasting' (v.2) require personal premortal existence, or is it sufficiently explained by the antiquity of the Davidic royal line and promise?
- Who is the woman in travail in v.3, and how directly is she tied to the Bethlehem ruler?
- Who, historically, are the 'seven shepherds, and eight principal men' (v.5) — identifiable figures, or purely idiomatic language for ample leadership?
- Are Assyria and 'the land of Nimrod' confined to Micah's own historical horizon, or do later readings legitimately extend them typologically?
- When [[3 Nephi 20]] and [[3 Nephi 21]] reapply Micah 5:8-14 to a latter-day remnant, does that preserve Micah's original address to ancient Israel, or substantially redirect it?
- Is the 'judge of Israel' struck in v.1 meant to evoke the later mocking of Jesus, or is that association read back into the text rather than found in it?
