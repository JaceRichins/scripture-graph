# Micah 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Micah 6]] · My notes: [[Micah 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> In spite of all His goodness to them, the people have not served the Lord in spirit and in truth—They must act righteously, love mercy, and walk humbly before Him.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Summons and controversy (vv. 1-2):** The mountains and foundations of the earth are called to hear the LORD's case.
2. **The LORD's testimony (vv. 3-5):** He challenges the people to name his offense and recalls the exodus, appointed leaders, and the Balak-Balaam episode.
3. **Questions and answer (vv. 6-8):** Proposals of increasingly costly sacrifice lead to the threefold requirement of justice, mercy, and humble life with God.
4. **Charges against the city (vv. 9-12):** The text condemns dishonest trade, violence, lying, and deceit.
5. **Sentence (vv. 13-16):** Sickness, frustrated labor, military loss, desolation, and reproach are announced, with Omri and Ahab cited as corrupt precedents.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Micah 6:8 states the chapter's ethical center: 'to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God' (see [[Justice and Mercy]] and [[Humility]]). It directly answers the escalating hypothetical offerings of verses 6-7, establishing that no volume of ritual [[Sacrifice]] substitutes for justice, mercy, and humility. This should not be read as abolishing the sacrificial system elsewhere required under the [[Law of Moses]]; the verse emphasizes priority and inward disposition rather than making an explicit claim that the sacrificial system itself is illegitimate. The same emphasis on covenant fidelity over ritual performance is voiced by Hosea ('I desired mercy, and not sacrifice,' Hosea 6:6) and later echoed when Jesus quotes that verse against religious formalism (Matthew 9:13; 12:7) — a recurring theme in prophetic and later Christian reading, though this should be read as reception and application rather than proof of Micah's own intent. The recollection of Israel's deliverance ([[Exodus and Deliverance]]) in verses 3-5 also grounds the LORD's requirement in prior grace: accountability follows gift, not the reverse.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's coherence rests on sustained legal imagery: a cosmic audience is summoned (vv.1-2), remembered benefits function as testimony (vv.3-5), an unnamed voice raises escalating hypothetical offerings (vv.6-7) that culminate in the shock of offering one's firstborn, verse 8 redirects the argument from cost to covenant conduct, specific social charges follow (vv.9-12), and the chapter closes with futility curses (vv.13-15) and an indictment naming Omri and Ahab (v.16). The repeated reversals in verses 14-15 — eating without being satisfied, sowing without reaping, treading olives without anointing oneself — draw on the same covenant-curse vocabulary found in Deuteronomy 28:38-40, framing the coming judgment as covenant sanction rather than arbitrary punishment. Note that the speaker of verses 6-7 is not identified in the text; reading it as Israel's or a representative worshiper's voice is a reasonable but interpretive choice.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How do the memories of Egypt, Balak, Balaam, Shittim, and Gilgal function as testimony in the LORD's case?
- Does verse 8 reject sacrifice itself, or the attempt to substitute costly sacrifice for justice, mercy, and humility?
- Why are commercial fraud, violence, and deceit the specific offenses named after the broad requirement of verse 8?
- What might the statutes of Omri and the works of Ahab's house specifically refer to, and why would Micah, prophesying primarily to Judah, invoke Northern Kingdom rulers as a warning?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Micah 6:6-8 with Hosea 6:6, 1 Samuel 15:22, Amos 5:21-24, and Isaiah 1:11-17 for the broader prophetic critique of ritual without ethics. Compare the covenant-lawsuit form with Psalm 50 and Jeremiah 25:31 (near-identical 'controversy... plead' language to Micah 6:2). For the covenant-curse language of vv.13-15, see Deuteronomy 28:38-40 and Leviticus 26. For Omri and Ahab's legacy, see 1 Kings 16:23-33 and 21.
