# Zechariah 12 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Zechariah 12]] · My notes: [[Zechariah 12 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Zechariah 12 opens a three-chapter unit (12–14) built around a repeated "in that day" refrain: an oracle of Jerusalem's deliverance from a siege by "all the people of the earth" (vv1-9), followed by a dramatic pivot at verse 10, where military triumph gives way to an outpoured "spirit of grace and of supplications" and mourning for one "whom they have pierced." The verse contains a genuine grammatical crux in the received text — the LORD speaks of being pierced in the first person ("me"), yet the following clause shifts to third person ("him") — which the chapter does not itself resolve. The official chapter heading reflects the historic Christian reading, developed explicitly in [[John 19]] and echoed in Revelation 1:7, that identifies the pierced figure with the crucified [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)|Christ]]; Doctrine and Covenants 45 further elaborates a scene of the Jews recognizing the resurrected Christ by his wounds at his [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ|Second Coming]]. This is a well-attested New Testament and latter-day reception of the verse, but it is an interpretation applied to Zechariah's wording rather than something the chapter states outright — other interpretive traditions read the pierced figure differently (see Uncertainties). The chapter closes (vv11-14) with mourning organized clan by clan — David, Nathan, Levi, Shimei, and "all the families that remain" — each mourning "apart," a structure immediately followed in [[Zechariah 13]] by the opening of a cleansing fountain for the house of David and Jerusalem.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **v1 — Basis of the oracle:** the LORD is introduced as the one who stretches out the heavens, founds the earth, and forms the human spirit — the ground of the authority exercised in what follows.
- **vv2-9 — Siege and deliverance:** Jerusalem as a "cup of trembling" (v2) and "burdensome stone" (v3); the LORD strikes besieging horses and riders (v4) while defending Judah's governors and Jerusalem's inhabitants (vv5-8), even saving "the tents of Judah first" so that David's house and Jerusalem do not exalt themselves over Judah (v7); the LORD purposes to destroy the attacking nations (v9).
- **v10 — The pivot:** deliverance gives way to an outpoured "spirit of grace and of supplications" and mourning for one who has been "pierced."
- **vv11-14 — Nationwide mourning:** compared to the mourning of Hadadrimmon (v11), then itemized family by family — David, Nathan, Levi, Shimei, and all remaining families, each with their wives mourning "apart" (vv12-14).

The recurring phrase "in that day" (vv3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 11) links these movements without supplying dates or requiring that every image describes a single moment.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's central doctrinal crux is verse 10: the LORD says "they shall look upon **me** whom they have pierced," yet the next clause shifts to mourning for "**him**." This shift is a genuine feature of the received text and should not be smoothed over. [[John 19]] applies the saying directly to the crucifixion of [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)|Christ]] ("They shall look on him whom they pierced," John 19:37), and Revelation 1:7 combines the same piercing language with mourning at Christ's coming. Doctrine and Covenants 45:51-53 elaborates this reading further, describing the Jews at the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]] asking the resurrected Christ about the wounds in his hands and feet. This Christian and latter-day reception is well-documented, but it is an application of Zechariah's words rather than something the chapter states explicitly — Jewish interpretive tradition reads the pierced figure differently (see Uncertainties and Counterarguments). The mourning that follows may be read, in light of the immediately following [[Zechariah 13]] 13:1 ("a fountain opened... for sin and for uncleanness"), as moving toward repentance and cleansing, though grief and repentance are not identical and Zechariah 12 itself does not name repentance. Verse 8's comparison of the house of David to "God, as the angel of the LORD" is best read, in its immediate defensive context, as exalted language for divinely-backed strength and deliverance rather than a settled statement about human divine status.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verse 1's opening "burden" (Hebrew *massa*) is the standard term for a weighty prophetic oracle. The refrain "in that day" (vv3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 11) binds the chapter's separate scenes together. The pronoun shift in verse 10 — first-person "me" pierced, then third-person "him" mourned — is a genuine feature of the text; the KJV of John 19:37 itself renders the object as "him," reflecting one way later tradition handled the shift. Readers should not treat the pronouns as simply uniform or already resolved.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter builds through escalating, vivid metaphors — Jerusalem as an intoxicating "cup of trembling" (v2) and a "burdensome stone" that wounds those who try to lift it (v3) — before pivoting at verse 10 from martial imagery to intimate grief ("as one mourneth for his only son"). Verse 7 checks any triumphalism along the way, having the tents of Judah saved first so that the house of David and Jerusalem's inhabitants "do not magnify themselves against Judah." The closing catalog of mourning families (vv12-14), each named and each mourning "apart," reads almost like a solemn litany, individualizing what began as a description of national-scale warfare.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Prophetic Description
> Zechariah 12:2-9 strongly supports a prophetic scenario of broad opposition to Jerusalem followed by divine defense. Because the passage supplies neither a date nor identifiable modern combatants, it supplies weak evidence for any detailed current-events timetable.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · sound methodological caution · [[Internal Prophetic Description in Zechariah 12|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Canonical Intertextual Reception
> John 19:37 uses Zechariah's looking-and-piercing language in the account of Jesus's crucifixion, changing the wording represented in the KJV from looking upon "me" to looking on "him." Revelation 1:7 also combines the pierced figure with widespread mourning. These passages provide strong evidence for an early Christian christological reception of Zechariah 12:10.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · well established · [[Canonical Intertextual Reception in Zechariah 12|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Onomastic Identification *(tentative)*
> "The family of Shimei" (12:13), paired with "the house of Levi" in the same verse, is plausibly a Levitical clan (a Shimei is listed among the sons of Gershon the Levite), which would make verse 13 name two priestly/Levitical groups rather than a mix of priestly and unrelated lines; however, the name Shimei is common in the Hebrew Bible and the identification is not certain from the text alone.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.3 · minority interpretation, uncertain

> [!evidence] Philological / History-Of-Religions *(tentative)*
> An alternative scholarly reading takes "Hadad-Rimmon" as a compound name drawn from the Canaanite storm-deities Hadad and Rimmon, suggesting the verse evokes the imagery of ritual mourning rites for a dying god (compare the mourning for Tammuz in Ezekiel 8:14) rather than, or in addition to, mourning for Josiah.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.3 · minority/speculative

> [!evidence] Textual/Theological Crux *(tentative)*
> Zechariah 12:10 contains a well-known grammatical crux: the LORD speaks in the first person ("they shall look upon me whom they have pierced"), yet the following clause shifts to third person ("they shall mourn for him"), so that the pierced figure is grammatically identified with both the speaking LORD and a separately mourned "him."
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · disputed, imprecisely sourced
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Who is the figure "pierced" in verse 10, and how should the shift from "me" to "him" be understood?
- Why does the chapter move from military victory (vv1-9) into mourning (v10) rather than celebration — and is that mourning grief, repentance, or both?
- What event or tradition lies behind "the mourning of Hadadrimmon in the valley of Megiddon" (v11)? Is Josiah's death at Megiddo the intended comparison?
- What is signified by naming the houses of David, Nathan, Levi, and Shimei specifically as mourning "apart" (vv12-13)?
- How much chronological detail can legitimately be drawn from the repeated "in that day," and how does this chapter relate to the similar siege scenario in [[Zechariah 14]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
