# Zechariah 13 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Zechariah 13]] · My notes: [[Zechariah 13 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Jews will gain forgiveness at the Second Coming—They will ask the Lord, What are these wounds in Thine hands?—The remnant, tried and refined, will be His people.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. **Cleansing opened (v. 1):** a fountain for sin and uncleanness.
2. **Religious corruption removed (vv. 2–6):** idols disappear, lying prophets are rejected, and deceptive prophets attempt to conceal their identity.
3. **Shepherd struck and people tested (vv. 7–9):** the sheep scatter, severe loss follows, and a third is refined into a people who call upon the Lord.

The last movement does not present refinement as comfortable preservation. The remnant passes through fire before the reciprocal declarations “It is my people” and “The LORD is my God.”
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The fountain in verse 1 naturally supports study of [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], but the verse does not specify how the cleansing is accomplished. Christian readers may connect it with the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], while recognizing that this is a theological interpretation rather than an explicit identification in the verse.

Verse 9 joins refining trial with prayer and covenant recognition. The Lord hears the remnant when they call, and both parties voice the restored relationship. This makes purification relational and covenantal, not merely punitive.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“In that day” functions as the chapter’s temporal refrain, appearing in verses 1, 2, and 4. Within this chapter alone, however, the expression does not provide a precise chronology.

The sequence in verses 4–6 deserves close attention. A prophet becomes ashamed, abandons the rough garment used to deceive, denies being a prophet, and is then questioned about wounds. This continuity makes the false-prophet context important when evaluating the traditional Christological reading of verse 6.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The rough garment mentioned in verse 4 was the traditional mark of a prophet, but the text specifies it was worn "to deceive" — so its abandonment is best read as exposed false prophets dropping a deceptive costume, not legitimate prophets erasing genuine signs of a real calling. Verses 5–6 build dramatic irony: a figure denies being a prophet, claims humble agrarian origins, and yet is questioned about wounds in his hands — a terse question-and-answer exchange that compresses a theologically loaded moment into two short lines. Because the wounded speaker in verse 6 follows directly from the prophet-denier of verse 5, the immediate context favors reading him as continuous with that figure; identifying him instead with the resurrected Christ (as the chapter heading and [[D&C 45]] do) is a later, canonically significant reapplication rather than something verse 6 states on its own. The shepherd-and-sheep imagery of verse 7 draws on pastoral metaphor used elsewhere in the book, so that a single blow to the shepherd immediately becomes a fate shared by "the little ones" as well.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Canonical Reuse
> Matthew 26:31 and Mark 14:27 explicitly apply the struck-shepherd saying of Zechariah 13:7 to Jesus and the impending scattering of his disciples. This is strong evidence for a Christian canonical reading of the shepherd as Christ.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted, textually verified · [[Explicit Canonical Reuse in Zechariah 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Prophecy-Fulfillment / Intertextual Citation *(tentative)*
> The New Testament's direct citation of Zechariah 13:7 by Jesus as a description of his own betrayal and the disciples' scattering is evidence that early Christians (including, per the Gospel accounts, Jesus himself) read this oracle as messianic prophecy fulfilled in the events of Gethsemane and the arrest.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Does “the prophets” in verse 2 mean that prophecy itself ceases, or does the description of lies and deception in verses 3–4 narrow the target to false prophets?
2. Is verse 6 continuous with the evasive speaker of verse 5, or does it introduce a new figure? What textual signal would establish a change of speaker?
3. What is meant by the Lord turning his hand “upon the little ones” in verse 7—further judgment, protective attention, or something else?
4. Is the division into two parts and a third intended as a literal proportion, a rhetorical depiction of severe loss, or both?
5. Which elements of the official Second Coming interpretation arise directly from this chapter, and which depend on its wider canonical reception?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
