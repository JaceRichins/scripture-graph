# Joshua 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Joshua 3]] · My notes: [[Joshua 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Joshua leads Israel to the Jordan—The Lord cuts off the water of the Jordan; it stands up as a heap, and Israel passes over on dry ground.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. Approach and three-day wait at the Jordan (3:1-2).
2. Instructions for following the ark at a distance, and the call to sanctify (3:3-6).
3. The LORD's word to Joshua establishing his standing before the people, and Joshua's public speech naming the seven nations and introducing twelve tribal representatives whose task is not yet explained (3:7-13).
4. The crossing itself: the priests enter the overflowing river, the water stands in a heap upstream, and the people cross on dry ground opposite Jericho (3:14-17).

The twelve men named in 3:12 are left unexplained within this chapter; their role in the memorial stones is supplied in [[Joshua 4]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter ties personal sanctification to participation in a divine act (3:5), though the text does not specify what sanctification practically involved. The LORD's promise to magnify Joshua "as I was with Moses" (3:7) presents the crossing as evidence of continuing divine presence with Israel's new leader, though the text speaks of demonstrated backing rather than a formal transfer of Moses' office. The ark, repeatedly called "the Lord of all the earth" (3:11, 13), is tied throughout to [[Covenants]]. Joshua frames the sign itself as evidentiary — "hereby ye shall know that the living God is among you" (3:10) — and links that assurance to the promised displacement of the seven named nations, a [[Miracles|miracle]] bound to a land-promise that also raises real moral questions about conquest and displacement which should not be glossed over.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The sequence follows a command-prediction-fulfillment pattern: instructions regarding the ark and priests (3:3-8), a specific prediction that the waters will be cut off (3:13), and a fulfillment narrated in matching detail (3:15-17). The language of water standing "upon an heap" (3:13, 16) echoes the poetic description of the Red Sea in Exodus 15:8, and the LORD's explicit comparison of Joshua to Moses (3:7) anchors this echo in the text itself rather than requiring outside pattern-matching — a deliberate "new exodus" framing for Israel's entrance into the land. This supports a literary reading of renewed deliverance but does not by itself establish when or how the account was composed. The twelve tribal representatives introduced without explanation in 3:12 are a narrative thread left open at the chapter boundary, resolved in [[Joshua 4]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary And Intertextual
> The divided waters, passage on dry ground, water standing as a heap, and explicit comparison of Joshua with Moses provide strong textual evidence that the Jordan account is meant to recall Israel's earlier deliverance through the sea.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted, well-hedged · [[Literary And Intertextual in Joshua 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical-Geological *(tentative)*
> The place-name 'Adam...beside Zaretan' given for the upstream location of the water's damming (Joshua 3:16) is generally associated by geographers with the vicinity of Tell ed-Damiye near the Jabbok's confluence with the Jordan — a stretch of river where earthquake-triggered landslides of the soft marl banks have historically dammed the Jordan's flow for hours to days, with recorded instances in 1267, 1906, and 1927 CE.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, uncited specifics

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the text emphasize that Israel had "not passed this way heretofore" (3:4) — given that most of this generation was born after the prior generation's death in the wilderness (Numbers 14; 26), is this underscoring an inexperienced new generation rather than the nation's own history at the Red Sea?
- Why are seven specific nations named (3:10) rather than a generic reference to "the Canaanites"?
- What does "sanctify yourselves" (3:5) require in this context, since the chapter never specifies a ritual procedure?
- Why are the twelve tribal representatives introduced in 3:12 before their purpose is explained?
- How should readers hold together the chapter's message of deliverance with its promise of displacing the land's existing peoples?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
For the erection of the twelve memorial stones, see [[Joshua 4]]. For the earlier dry-ground crossing this account echoes, compare Exodus 14:21-22 and the poetic account in Exodus 15:8. Deuteronomy 31:7-8 records Moses' earlier public commissioning of Joshua and provides context for 3:7. [[Joshua 6]] shows the ark and priests again taking a leading role at Jericho. The wording shared with 1 Chronicles 15:12 ("sanctify yourselves") is common consecration language rather than a proven ark-specific formula, and the identification of "Adam" and "Zaretan" (3:16) with particular modern sites remains a matter of ongoing scholarly discussion rather than settled fact.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
