## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter is framed by a simple departure-and-return frame: Joshua sends the two spies out in verse 1, and they return to report 'all things that befell them' in verses 23-24. Within that frame, the action moves through concealment, pursuit, and escape — the spies hidden on the roof, then let down by a cord through the window, then hidden again on the mountain for three days before recrossing the Jordan to Joshua's camp. The center of the chapter is occupied not by action but by dialogue: Rahab's confession (2:9-11) and the negotiated oath (2:12-21), which together occupy more verses than the surrounding narrative of concealment and escape.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Rahab's episode raises the question of who may be included among the people of God. She confesses the LORD's sovereignty before any Israelite in the chapter does, and acts on that confession at risk to her own life by concealing the spies and misdirecting the king's men — though the text also gives her clear motive of self-preservation amid Jericho's terror (see [[Faith]]). The mutual exchange between Rahab and the spies (2:12-21) also illustrates covenant-like dealing: a request for [[Covenants|kindness]], reciprocal promises, explicit conditions, and a physical sign meant to be visible and unambiguous at the moment of crisis.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Rahab's request in verse 12 that the spies 'shew kindness' and her citing that she has already 'shewed you kindness' reflects the Hebrew concept of chesed — loyal, covenant-keeping kindness — used here between people rather than only between God and Israel. Some commentators also note that the Hebrew word behind 'line' in verse 18 (tiqvah) shares its root with the word for 'hope,' so that the visible scarlet cord may carry a double sense of physical marker and expectant hope (see claim c15); this reading is suggestive rather than certain, since wordplay is not marked explicitly in the Hebrew text.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The scarlet cord bound in Rahab's window (2:18, 21) is frequently read alongside the blood applied to Israelite doorposts at the first Passover (Exodus 12): in both cases, a visible sign marks a household to be spared when judgment sweeps through a city. The chapter is also built on dramatic irony — the reader already knows, from Rahab's own mouth, that Jericho's fall is regarded as certain by its own inhabitants, well before Joshua's army ever appears at its walls.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrative give an outsider — a Canaanite woman with no standing in Israel — the clearest statement of faith in the chapter, rather than putting comparable words in the mouths of the spies or Joshua?
- What does the specificity of Rahab's conditions (silence, staying inside, the visible sign) suggest about how ancient covenants or oaths were expected to function?
- How does Rahab's report of Jericho's fear (2:9-11) change the reader's expectations for the battle narrative that follows in later chapters?
