# Joshua 22 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Joshua 22]] · My notes: [[Joshua 22 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Joshua 22 closes the conquest narrative. Joshua commends the Reubenites, Gadites, and half-tribe of Manasseh for their obedience and for not abandoning their brethren during the campaign — fulfilling the arrangement set out in [[Numbers 32]] — then blesses and dismisses them to their eastern possessions, instructing them to share the war spoil with those who stayed behind. Before settling in, they build a large, conspicuous altar at the Jordan. The western tribes interpret this as apostasy — a rival altar that would violate Israel's single legitimate place of [[Sacrifice]] before the tabernacle — and assemble for war at Shiloh. Before fighting, they send the priest Phinehas and ten tribal princes to investigate. The eastern tribes explain that the altar is not for sacrifice but a [[Testimony|witness]] (they name it *Ed*), meant to preserve their descendants' recognized place in Israel's worship across the Jordan divide. The explanation satisfies the delegation, war is averted, and the chapter ends with the altar's naming.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter moves in six clear movements:
1. **Dismissal and blessing** (vv.1–6) — Joshua commends the 2½ tribes' obedience and sends them home.
2. **Manasseh's divided inheritance and spoil** (vv.7–9) — a note on Manasseh's two portions (Bashan under Moses, the western portion under Joshua), plus the instruction to share war spoil.
3. **The altar and the alarm** (vv.10–12) — a great altar is built at the Jordan; Israel hears of it and gathers at Shiloh, prepared for war.
4. **Confrontation** (vv.13–20) — Phinehas and ten princes travel to Gilead and deliver a formal accusation, citing Peor and Achan as precedent for the danger of corporate guilt.
5. **Defense and clarification** (vv.21–29) — the eastern tribes deny any sacrificial intent and explain the altar as an intergenerational witness.
6. **Resolution and naming** (vv.30–34) — the delegation is satisfied, war is called off, and the altar is named Ed.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Joshua's opening charge presents [[Obedience]] as wholehearted attachment to God rather than bare rule-keeping: Israel is to love, walk, keep, cleave, and serve (22:5). The altar episode then distinguishes [[Testimony]] from [[Sacrifice]] — within the narrative, Ed is accepted because it witnesses to shared worship without becoming a rival sacrificial altar. The delegation's appeal to Peor and Achan reflects a strong concern for communal responsibility for [[Sin]], though this is best read as the delegation's stated fear in this specific episode rather than expanded into a settled doctrine of collective punishment.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two literary features stand out. First, the repeated denial of sacrificial intent — 'not for burnt offering, nor for sacrifice' and its variants — recurs across vv.23, 26, 28, and 29; the specific offerings named shift each time, but the cumulative effect is an insistent, almost legal disclaimer. Second, the doubled divine title in v.22, 'The LORD God of gods, the LORD God of gods,' opens the tribes' defense with unusual solemnity, invoking God himself as witness to their innocence. The chapter closes with an etiological naming — Ed, 'witness' — tying the altar's name directly to the narrative's resolution, a device also used for the memorial stones in [[Joshua 4]]. The Jordan itself carries a double meaning throughout: it is both the political border separating the tribes' territories and the site chosen for a monument meant to unite their descendants across that same border.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What evidence did the western tribes possess before mobilizing, and what did they only learn through direct conversation?

- Why do the eastern tribes fear exclusion by future generations rather than by their present companions?

- Does the delegation's offer of western land in 22:19 soften its accusation, or does it still assume that eastern territory might be religiously inferior?

- What safeguards distinguish a legitimate witness from an unauthorized center of worship in this narrative?

- Which parts of the reconciliation are directly narrated, and which modern lessons about conflict resolution are applications rather than claims made explicitly by the text?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
