# Joshua 7 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Joshua 7]] · My notes: [[Joshua 7 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Israel is defeated by the people of Ai—Joshua complains to the Lord—Achan and his household are destroyed because he disobeyed the Lord by taking the spoils of Jericho.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Cause disclosed to the reader, not yet to Joshua** (7:1)
2. **Reconnaissance, defeat, and fear at Ai** (7:2-5)
3. **Joshua's lament before the ark** (7:6-9)
4. **Divine diagnosis: covenant violation, not military weakness** (7:10-15)
5. **Progressive identification: tribe → family → household → man** (7:16-18)
6. **Confession and recovery of the goods** (7:19-23)
7. **Judgment and the naming of the valley of Achor** (7:24-26)

The text never says how "the LORD taketh" was determined at each stage (7:14) — tradition often assumes some form of sacred lot, but no mechanism is named in this chapter. The movement from unexplained national crisis to a specific, named individual gives the chapter a documentary, case-building feel, though the narrative itself never calls the process "legal" in any formal sense.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Agency]] and [[Sin]]**: Achan's confession locates the sin in a sequence of his own choices — he saw, coveted, and took (7:21) — rather than blaming circumstance.
- **[[Covenants]] and corporate consequence**: the LORD tells Joshua that Israel "could not stand before their enemies... because they were accursed" (7:12), and repeatedly speaks of "Israel" sinning even though one man acted (7:1, 11-13). This chapter is strong evidence for how *this* narrative frames the offense corporately; it should not be over-read as a general doctrine that every defeat or unanswered [[Prayer]] signals someone's hidden sin — within the story, the diagnosis comes from direct revelation to Joshua, a warrant readers don't otherwise have.
- **[[Honesty]]**: Joshua's charge, "give... glory to the LORD God of Israel, and make confession unto him... hide it not from me" (7:19), frames truthful acknowledgment as honoring God — though note that Achan's confession comes only after the lot process has already identified him; it is a prompted admission, not a spontaneous one.
- **[[Prayer]] and [[Revelation]]**: Joshua's extended lament (7:6-9) is met not with comfort but with a brisk redirection — "Get thee up; wherefore liest thou thus upon thy face?" (7:10) — followed by the actual diagnosis and instructions (7:11-15). The exchange models bringing real grief and complaint to God, while showing that God's answer moves Joshua toward required action rather than resolving his grief on its own terms.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verse 1 opens with "the anger of the LORD was kindled against the children of Israel" — a stock phrase for decisive divine anger that recurs in various forms elsewhere in the Torah and Former Prophets (e.g., Exodus 4:14, Numbers 25:3, 2 Samuel 6:7). Its placement here, before the battle narrative rather than after as a reaction to the defeat, tips the reader off from the first sentence that this is fundamentally a covenant story — though the idiom itself is common enough elsewhere in scripture that it shouldn't be read as a signal exclusive to covenant-breaking.

The chapter's clearest wordplay is built into its ending: Joshua's question to Achan, "Why hast thou **troubled** us? the LORD shall **trouble** thee this day" (7:25), is immediately followed by naming the execution site "The valley of **Achor**" (7:26) — Achor being the Hebrew word for "trouble." 1 Chronicles 2:7 records the man's name itself as "Achar," not "Achan," explicitly calling him "the troubler of Israel" — a variant spelling that makes the pun visible in the name's own consonants, though it doesn't establish which spelling came first.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The reader learns Achan's identity and act in the opening verse; Joshua and Israel learn it only after the defeat and a lengthy identification process (7:18) — a dramatic-irony structure that shifts the reader's attention from "what went wrong" to "how will it be discovered." The image of hearts "melted, and became as water" after the loss (7:5) reverses the earlier description of Canaanite hearts melting before Israel (Joshua 2:11; Joshua 5:1): Israel briefly occupies the position of fear it had previously inflicted on others, underscoring that its standing in the land depends on covenant fidelity rather than automatic military strength.

Achan's ancestry — "the son of Carmi, the son of Zabdi, the son of Zerah" — is given in full twice (7:1, 18), lending the narrative a documentary feel as it builds toward naming him, though the text never frames this as a formal legal proceeding. Joshua's closing wordplay on "trouble" and "Achor" (see Language, above) is the chapter's most deliberate literary device, turning a single act of theft into a permanent geographic memorial "unto this day" (7:26).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Corroboration
> Within the narrative, the messengers' recovery of the goods from Achan's tent, including the reported placement of the silver underneath, independently verifies the specific details of his confession. This is internal narrative corroboration, not external historical corroboration.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.8 · accepted observation, evidentiary framing downgraded

> [!evidence] Textual Ambiguity
> Whether Achan's sons and daughters were executed along with him is genuinely ambiguous in the received text. Verse 24 lists his sons, daughters, animals, and tent among what was 'brought... unto the valley of Achor,' but verse 25 shifts pronoun number — 'stoned him' (singular) followed by 'burned them with fire' (plural) — leaving it unclear whether the plural refers to Achan plus his possessions/animals, or to Achan's family as well. This sits in tension with the principle later stated in Deuteronomy 24:16 that children should not be put to death for a parent's sin.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed but the ambiguity itself is real and important · [[Textual Ambiguity in Joshua 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Incidental Material-Culture Detail *(tentative)*
> Achan describes the stolen items as 'a goodly Babylonish garment, and two hundred shekels of silver, and a wedge of gold of fifty shekels weight' among the spoils of Jericho. This single narrative detail is sometimes cited as incidental support for the presence of long-distance luxury trade goods (a Mesopotamian-style textile) reaching Canaanite cities in this period, but as a lone literary detail within a theological narrative it cannot independently corroborate historical trade patterns or the chapter's date of composition.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.3 · speculative, appropriately hedged

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Were Achan's sons and daughters executed with him, or only present as witnesses? Verse 24 lists them among those brought to Achor, and verse 25 shifts between singular ("stoned **him**") and plural ("burned **them**") without resolving the ambiguity — a question with real weight given Deuteronomy 24:16's statement that children should not die for a parent's sin. The grammar leans toward their inclusion, but the text does not say so outright.
- What physical means determined which "tribe... family... household... man" the LORD "taketh" (7:14-18)? The chapter describes the stages but never names the mechanism.
- How should this chapter's own revealed explanation for defeat — a hidden sin, diagnosed by direct word from God — be distinguished from unwarranted attempts to explain modern unexplained suffering or setbacks as punishment for someone's concealed sin?
- Why does God's first answer to Joshua's lament (7:10) sound impatient rather than comforting, and what does that suggest about the relationship between lament and required action?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- **[[Joshua 6]]** — the herem command over Jericho's spoil, including silver and gold reserved for the Lord's treasury (6:17-19), that Achan's act specifically violates.
- **[[Joshua 8]]** — the renewed, successful campaign against Ai immediately after the accursed thing is removed.
- **[[Joshua 22]]** — later invokes Achan by name as a warning that one person's trespass brought wrath on the whole congregation (22:20).
- **1 Chronicles 2:7** — records the man's name as "Achar," explicitly "the troubler of Israel," clarifying the wordplay built into Joshua 7:26.
- **Hosea 2:15** and **Isaiah 65:10** — both later reuse "the valley of Achor" as a symbol of restored hope and pasture, worth reading as a reversal of its original meaning here.
