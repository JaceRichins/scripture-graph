# Joshua 23 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Joshua 23]] · My notes: [[Joshua 23 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Joshua 23 is a farewell exhortation grounded in remembered deliverance and directed toward Israel's future. Joshua argues from experience: the LORD has fought for Israel and fulfilled every good promise, so Israel should also trust the warnings attached to covenant violation. The chapter holds together divine gift and human responsibility. The land is given by God, while continued possession is tied to [[Obedience]] and exclusive covenant loyalty.

A notable tension gives the chapter nuance: Israel has received rest, yet nations remain and complete possession still lies ahead. The speech therefore celebrates decisive success without depicting Israel's territorial situation as entirely finished.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Setting and retrospective (vv. 1–3):** Joshua is old; Israel has rest; the LORD has fought for them.
2. **Inheritance and unfinished possession (vv. 4–5):** Land has been allotted, but nations remain to be expelled.
3. **Positive exhortation (vv. 6–11):** Keep the written [[Law of Moses]], reject other gods, cleave to the LORD, and love him.
4. **Conditional warning (vv. 12–13):** Reversal, attachment, and marriage with the remaining nations will produce entanglement and loss.
5. **Promise-and-warning conclusion (vv. 14–16):** Fulfilled good promises become the warrant for believing the threatened covenant judgments.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Joshua 23 presents [[Covenants]] as joining divine faithfulness with accountable human response. The LORD has fought for Israel, given the land, and fulfilled his good promises; Joshua therefore calls Israel to [[Obedience]], love, and exclusive loyalty. The gift of land does not make continued possession unconditional: covenant transgression and service to other gods are tied to dispossession (Joshua 23:15–16).

The warning about [[Marriage]] with the remaining nations belongs to a larger cluster of concerns involving association, covenant identity, idolatry, land, and communal survival (Joshua 23:7, 12–16). Its religious setting is explicit, but the text does not fully separate religious, ethnic, social, and political boundary concerns. It should therefore not be converted uncritically into a modern rule of racial exclusion.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The key lexical contrast is between cleaving to the LORD (v. 8) and cleaving to the remaining nations (v. 12). One verb describes two incompatible attachments. The movement from "go back" to "cleave," marry, and associate portrays apostasy as progressive rather than instantaneous.

The "evil things" in verse 15 are the opposite of the promised "good things" and are explained by destruction and loss of the land. In this context, "evil" denotes calamity or adverse judgment, not moral wrongdoing by God.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Joshua 23 uses repetition and cumulative imagery for rhetorical force. The doubled assertion that "not one thing hath failed" emphasizes the reliability of the LORD's promises (Joshua 23:14), while snares, traps, scourges, and thorns accumulate into a vivid warning (Joshua 23:13). The repeated verb "cleave" contrasts attachment to the LORD with attachment to the remaining nations (Joshua 23:8, 12).

Joshua 23:6 closely reprises Joshua's opening commission in [[Joshua 1]] through its language of courage, observance of the Mosaic law, and refusal to turn right or left. This can be read as a book-level leadership frame, though reuse of established Deuteronomic covenant language is also a serious explanation. Parallels with [[Deuteronomy 4]], [[Deuteronomy 7]], and [[Deuteronomy 11]] support a meaningful covenant-tradition relationship but do not determine authorship, date, editorial history, or whether the speech is a verbatim transcript.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should "rest" in verse 1 be understood when verses 4–5 still anticipate the expulsion of remaining nations?
- Does "not one thing hath failed" in verse 14 summarize God's faithfulness to a corporate stage of the promise rather than claim that every aspect of possession was already complete?
- What does the chapter's portrayal of divinely authorized dispossession require readers to confront ethically, beyond merely explaining its literary function?
- How does the contrast between the two objects of "cleave" clarify the chapter's understanding of covenant loyalty?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Joshua's initial commission in [[Joshua 1]] with his farewell here, especially the shared charge to be courageous, keep the law, and avoid turning aside. Then read [[Joshua 24]] as the following covenant-renewal scene. For the chapter's marriage, idolatry, and land-loss warnings, compare [[Deuteronomy 4]], [[Deuteronomy 7]], and [[Deuteronomy 11]]. Compare the affliction imagery of Joshua 23:13 with [[Numbers 33]], and the declaration that no good promise failed with [[Joshua 21]] and [[1 Kings 8]].
