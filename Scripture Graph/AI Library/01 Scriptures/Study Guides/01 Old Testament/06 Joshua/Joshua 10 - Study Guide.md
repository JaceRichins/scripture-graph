# Joshua 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Joshua 10]] · My notes: [[Joshua 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Joshua 10 continues directly from [[Joshua 9]]: Gibeon's treaty with Israel provokes a five-king Amorite coalition (Jerusalem, Hebron, Jarmuth, Lachish, Eglon) to attack the city for defecting (10:1-5). Joshua honors Gibeon's appeal, marches all night from Gilgal, and the coalition is routed; the narrative credits both Israel's action and the LORD's intervention (hailstones, and the sun and moon 'standing still') for the victory (10:6-15). The five kings are captured, humiliated, and executed at Makkedah (10:16-27), and the chapter closes with a formulaic summary of a southern campaign (Makkedah, Libnah, Lachish, Eglon, Hebron, Debir) that repeatedly measures each city's fate against the earlier destruction of Jericho (see [[Joshua 6]]). The chapter's controlling claim is that 'the LORD fought for Israel' (10:14, 42) — a theological interpretation offered by the narrator, not an independent verification. Its herem language ('left none remaining,' 'utterly destroyed all that breathed,' 10:40) is severe and should not be softened in study; it also sits in tension with 10:20's note that some survivors escaped to fortified cities, a wrinkle worth sitting with rather than resolving too quickly.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Coalition and crisis (10:1-5):** Five kings mobilize against Gibeon over its peace with Israel.
2. **Appeal and response (10:6-9):** Gibeon calls for help; Joshua receives divine assurance and marches all night from Gilgal.
3. **Battle and signs (10:10-15):** The LORD discomfits the coalition, hailstones kill more than the sword, and the sun and moon are said to stand still.
4. **The five kings (10:16-27):** Pursuit continues while the kings hide in a cave; they are later brought out, ritually subdued, executed, and buried the same day.
5. **Southern campaign (10:28-39):** A tight refrain — 'smote it with the edge of the sword...left none remaining...as he had done to [city]' — is applied to Makkedah, Libnah, Lachish, Eglon, Hebron, and Debir.
6. **Summary and return (10:40-43):** A territorial summary and theological conclusion, then Israel returns to Gilgal.

Note: verses 15 and 43 both report Joshua's return to Gilgal in nearly identical wording, yet vv16-42 (the kings still in the cave, the whole southern campaign) must occur first. This is most simply read as v15 being a proleptic summary of where the story is heading before the narrator backs up to fill in the details — a recognized device in Hebrew narrative — though some note it as a possible scribal anticipation of v43.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins promise and effort: Joshua is told the coalition is already 'delivered into thine hand' (10:8), yet still executes a forced night march, surprise attack, and sustained pursuit (10:9-19) — reliance on God does not replace disciplined action. Israel's defense of Gibeon also touches [[Covenants]]: though the treaty in [[Joshua 9]] was obtained by deception, Israel treats the oath as binding, at real military cost. The hailstones and the 'long day' are framed as [[Miracles]], and Joshua's address to the LORD (10:12) plus the narrator's comment that 'the LORD hearkened unto the voice of a man' (10:14) connect the episode to [[Prayer]] — without the text specifying any physical mechanism for what happened. The southern campaign's destruction is repeatedly framed as done 'as the LORD God of Israel commanded' (10:40), a strong claim of [[Obedience]] that should be held alongside the chapter's difficult herem language, not used to foreclose ethical reflection on it. Joshua's charge to his captains — 'Fear not, nor be dismayed, be strong and of good courage' (10:25) — echoes the LORD's own charge to Joshua at the outset of the conquest (Joshua 1:9), so the leader passes on an assurance he once received himself.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The formula 'delivered into thine/your hand' recurs at 10:8, 19, 30, and 32, interpreting military outcomes theologically rather than simply reporting them. The city-conquest notices (10:28-39) repeat a tight formula — taken, struck with the sword, none remaining, compared to an earlier city — giving the second half of the chapter the compressed feel of a campaign summary rather than a battle narrative. 'The book of Jasher' (10:13; also cited at 2 Samuel 1:18) names an external, non-extant source; the elevated, parallel phrasing of Joshua's address to the sun and moon (10:12) is consistent with, though not certain proof of, a quoted or adapted poetic fragment. The Hebrew verb behind 'stand still' (dom) has a semantic range that includes 'be silent' or 'cease,' which is part of why translators differ on what exactly the text describes — but this lexical possibility alone doesn't settle the question, especially given v13's added statement that the sun 'hasted not to go down about a whole day.' 'At one time' (10:42) most plausibly means one unified campaign, not one calendar day, since Lachish itself falls 'on the second day' (10:32).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Joshua's parallel address to the sun ('stand thou still upon Gibeon') and moon ('in the valley of Ajalon,' 10:12) is a compact poetic couplet, and the following citation of the book of Jasher (10:13) suggests the prose narrative may be incorporating or citing older poetry — though what exactly was quoted, and how much, can't be recovered from what survives. The chapter's two halves differ sharply in pace: the Gibeon relief is told through speech, movement, weather, and a celestial sign, while the southern campaign (10:28-39) collapses into a repeated refrain across six cities. That repetition ('smote it with the edge of the sword...as he had done to [city]') also ties the whole southern campaign back to the destruction of Jericho in [[Joshua 6]], the conquest's opening battle. [[Joshua 11]]'s northern campaign mirrors this chapter's structure — coalition, divine assurance, victory, formulaic destruction summary — making the two chapters a matched literary pair.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual-Criticism *(tentative)*
> Textual critics have long noted that Joshua 10:15 is absent from principal Septuagint witnesses to Joshua and is widely regarded as a secondary insertion anticipating verse 43, since its content interrupts the cave-and-campaign narrative that follows it in the Masoretic Text.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · unverifiable as stated

> [!evidence] Historicity/Archaeology *(tentative)*
> Joshua 10:40's summary claim that Joshua 'left none remaining, but utterly destroyed all that breathed' in the southern campaign is in tension with later biblical notices of surviving Canaanite/Anakite populations in that same region (e.g., Hebron and the hill country in Joshua 11:21-22 and Judges 1), and with the absence of a uniform Late Bronze Age destruction horizon at every named site in the archaeological record; many scholars therefore read the totalizing conquest language as ancient Near Eastern military-rhetorical convention rather than literal, exhaustive extermination.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed

> [!evidence] Translation/Interpretive-Crux *(tentative)*
> The text marks the 'long day' as unique ('there was no day like that before it or after it'), but what actually happened is genuinely disputed among translators and commentators: a literal cessation of the sun and moon's apparent motion, a poetic quotation from an ancient victory hymn (the lost Book of Jasher) using hyperbolic battle imagery, or a Hebrew idiom (the root behind 'stand still,' dom, can mean 'be silent/cease [glaring]') describing the sun's heat being muted rather than its motion halted.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Was the 'long day' (10:12-14) a literal suspension of the sun and moon's motion, a citation of poetic hyperbole from the lost book of Jasher, or something else the text doesn't fully specify? Is Joshua 10:15 a deliberate proleptic summary or a later scribal anticipation of v43? How should the herem language in vv28-40 ('left none remaining,' 'utterly destroyed all that breathed') be read against v20's note that some survivors reached fortified cities, and against later notices of continued Canaanite presence in this region (compare [[Joshua 11]] and Judges 1) — as literal and complete, as ancient conquest-rhetoric convention, or as some mix the text itself doesn't sort out for us? What does Israel's costly defense of a treaty obtained through deception (see [[Joshua 9]]) suggest about the weight of an oath? And however this chapter is read historically, what does it mean that a chapter this violent is placed in scripture at all — is the object lesson at Makkedah (10:24-25) purely military encouragement, or does it also expose something troubling about ancient warfare's treatment of the defeated?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Joshua 9]] first for why Gibeon calls on Israel. Compare [[Joshua 11]] for the parallel northern campaign and its matching formulas. [[Joshua 6]] and [[Joshua 1]] supply, respectively, the Jericho benchmark repeatedly invoked in 10:28-39 and the 'be strong and of good courage' charge reused at 10:25. Deuteronomy 21:22-23 clarifies the same-day removal of the hanged kings (10:26-27); Deuteronomy 20:16-18 gives the legal basis for herem warfare invoked in vv28-40. 2 Samuel 1:18 is the only other biblical citation of 'the book of Jasher.' Habakkuk 3:11 offers a poetic comparison for a stationary sun and moon. Judges 1 raises real, unresolved questions about later control of Jerusalem, Hebron, and Debir worth sitting with rather than harmonizing away.
