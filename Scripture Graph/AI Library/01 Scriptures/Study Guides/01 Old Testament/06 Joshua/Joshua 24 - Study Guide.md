# Joshua 24 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Joshua 24]] · My notes: [[Joshua 24 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Joshua recites how the Lord has blessed and led Israel—Joshua and all the people covenant to choose the Lord and serve Him only—Joshua and Eleazar die—The bones of Joseph, taken from Egypt, are buried in Shechem.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has five main movements:

1. **Assembly and divine historical recital (24:1-13).** Israel gathers before God, and the LORD is presented as the principal actor who called, gave, sent, delivered, and granted land.
2. **Call to exclusive service (24:14-15).** The historical recital leads to the demand: "choose you this day whom ye will serve."
3. **Profession, warning, and renewed commitment (24:16-24).** The people pledge loyalty; Joshua stresses the holiness of God, the danger of apostasy, and the need to put away strange gods.
4. **Covenant, record, and witnesses (24:25-28).** Joshua establishes a covenant and ordinance, writes the words, and erects a stone as witness.
5. **Closing notices (24:29-33).** The deaths or burials of Joshua, Joseph's bones, and Eleazar conclude the book.

These features clearly form a covenant-renewal sequence. Comparisons with ancient treaty forms may be informative, but the structure alone cannot establish a particular treaty template, date, author, or historical setting.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Agency]] and accountability:** The command to choose concerns competing loyalties, not religious neutrality. Israel must deliberately own whom it will serve and accept the consequences of that allegiance.
- **[[Covenants]] and [[Obedience]]:** The demand to serve follows the recital of [[Exodus and Deliverance]] and the gift of land. Obligation is presented as a response to prior divine action, while the written covenant and witnesses emphasize continuing accountability.
- **[[Apostasy]]:** Joshua warns that abandoning the LORD for strange gods repudiates the covenant. His statement that the people "cannot serve the LORD" is a severe warning whose precise rhetorical force remains open; the passage should not be turned into a comprehensive claim that repentance can never be forgiven.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
- **"Serve":** The repeated verb describes allegiance expressed in practice. It applies both to service of the LORD and to service of ancestral, Egyptian, or Amorite gods.
- **"The other side of the flood" (24:2-3, 14-15):** In this context the KJV wording is geographical, representing "beyond the River," conventionally the Euphrates region. It is not a reference to Noah's flood.
- **"The hornet" (24:12):** The expression recalls Exodus 23:28. Its precise referent is uncertain; proposals include literal hornets and imagery for divinely caused terror or displacement. A specific foreign military identification is substantially more speculative.
- **"Witness":** The people accept status as witnesses against themselves, and the stone is personified as having heard the covenant words and is appointed as a continuing witness.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The divine recital repeatedly gives the LORD the initiating role, and the people's answer in 24:16-18 echoes that recital. Shared memory supplies the stated reason for their pledge.

Witness imagery develops through the scene: the people become witnesses against themselves, Joshua writes the covenant words, and the stone is personified as having heard them. The monument gives a durable form to a public commitment.

Joshua 24:28-31 closely parallels [[Judges 2]], where the notice about Joshua and the elders is followed by a contrasting generation that did not know the LORD's works. Joseph's burial also completes the narrative sequence begun by his command in [[Genesis 50]] and continued when Moses carried his bones in [[Exodus 13]]. The three closing burial notices may together emphasize rest in the inherited land, though they could also preserve separate traditions gathered at the book's conclusion.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary And Covenantal Structure
> Joshua 24 presents a coherent covenant-renewal sequence: a representative assembly before God, a recital of the LORD's prior acts, a demand for exclusive loyalty, repeated public declarations, self-witness, formal covenant and ordinance, a written record, and a monumental witness. This strongly supports reading the chapter as deliberately shaped covenant-renewal literature.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Covenant sequence accepted; external template unnecessary · [[Literary And Covenantal Structure in Joshua 24|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Reception History / Intertextual *(tentative)*
> Verse 2's statement that Terah 'and they served other gods' is the primary explicit biblical basis for later Jewish and Christian tradition (e.g., rabbinic midrash, and popular retellings) that Abraham's own household worshiped idols before his call — the earlier Genesis narrative (Genesis 11-12) does not itself state this directly.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · Biblical observation sound; reception claim unverified

> [!evidence] Form-Critical / Comparative Ane Literature *(tentative)*
> The chapter's arrangement — historical prologue (24:2-13), stipulation/exhortation (24:14-15,23), the people's response, and a witness clause with a deposited document (24:25-27) — resembles the structure scholars have identified in ancient Near Eastern (e.g., Hittite) suzerain-vassal treaties, and is frequently cited as evidence that covenant renewal in Israel drew on that ANE legal genre.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Recognized but disputed form-critical comparison

> [!evidence] Textual/Comparative Ambiguity *(tentative)*
> Verse 12 credits Israel's victories to 'the hornet' sent before them rather than to Israelite weapons, a term that also appears in Exodus 23:28 and Deuteronomy 7:20 describing the same conquest tradition. The referent of 'hornet' is disputed among interpreters: it has been read as a literal insect plague, a metaphor for panic/terror sent upon the inhabitants, or a figure for a foreign military power (e.g., Egyptian campaigns) that weakened Canaanite resistance beforehand.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · Term and parallels verified; referent disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does Joshua answer the people's first profession with “Ye cannot serve the LORD” rather than immediately accepting it? What does the command to remove gods “among you” reveal about the difference between public profession and actual practice? How do human testimony, written record, and the stone witness reinforce one another? Why does the narrator connect continued faithfulness specifically with the generation that had known the LORD's works?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare Joshua's earlier farewell in [[Joshua 23]] with the covenant ceremony here.
- Read [[Judges 2]] alongside Joshua 24:28-31, noting both the repeated notice and the later-generation contrast.
- Compare [[Deuteronomy 6]] with Joshua 24:13-20 on unearned inheritance, exclusive loyalty, and divine jealousy.
- Compare the promised hornet in [[Exodus 23]] with Joshua 24:12 while leaving its precise referent unresolved.
- Trace Joseph's bones from [[Genesis 50]] through [[Exodus 13]] to their burial in Joshua 24:32.
- Compare the explicit covenant-witness stones in [[Genesis 31]] with the memorial, rather than legal-witness, stones in [[Joshua 4]].
- Read [[1 Samuel 12]] as a broader comparison involving historical recital, public witness, service, and warning.
