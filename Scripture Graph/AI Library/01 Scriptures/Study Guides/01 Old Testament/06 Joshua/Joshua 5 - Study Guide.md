# Joshua 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Joshua 5]] · My notes: [[Joshua 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Joshua 5 forms a transition between the Jordan crossing (chapters 3-4) and the assault on Jericho (chapter 6). Canaan's kings lose courage on hearing of the crossing (v.1), yet Israel does not immediately attack. Instead, the wilderness-born generation is circumcised, Israel observes [[Passover]] at Gilgal in the plains of Jericho, and manna ceases as the people begin eating the produce of Canaan. The chapter closes with Joshua's encounter, near Jericho, with a figure who identifies himself as captain of the LORD's host and declares the ground holy. See [[Joshua 2]] for Rahab's earlier report of Canaanite fear that this chapter's opening verse echoes.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Canaan's kings lose courage (v. 1):** news of the Jordan crossing further demoralizes the Amorite and Canaanite kings, echoing Rahab's report in [[Joshua 2]].
2. **Circumcision and recovery (vv. 2-9):** the generation born in the wilderness — never circumcised — is circumcised at Gibeath-haaraloth, and the camp recovers while Gilgal is named for the LORD having 'rolled away' the reproach of Egypt.
3. **Passover and the end of manna (vv. 10-12):** Israel keeps [[Passover]] at Gilgal, eats the land's produce, and the manna ceases.
4. **Joshua's encounter (vv. 13-15):** an armed figure identifies himself as captain of the LORD's host; Joshua is told to remove his shoe because the ground is holy.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter binds divine gift to human response: the LORD who gave manna in the wilderness now gives the land itself, but Israel must still respond through [[Obedience]] and covenant [[Ordinances]]. Circumcision reasserts the [[Abrahamic Covenant]] sign for a generation that had never received it, and [[Passover]] re-enacts the Exodus deliverance on the very soil it was promised toward. This connects [[Covenants]] to entry into the land without implying that the rites themselves accomplished the conquest — the text credits none of what follows to the ceremonies performed here. Joshua's response to the captain of the LORD's host — falling down and calling himself a servant — raises real questions about divine presence and holiness, though the chapter never states the figure's precise identity, so any claim about [[Angels]] or a christophany here is interpretation, not text.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verses 1 and 6 briefly shift from third-person narration ('the children of Israel', 'their fathers') to first-person plural ('until we were passed over'; 'that he would give us') — a pronoun shift whose cause (narratorial identification with Israel, a textual variant, or something else) the text does not explain. The naming of Gilgal (v. 9) plays on the Hebrew root commonly associated with 'to roll' (galal), tied in the text itself to the LORD having 'rolled away' Israel's reproach — an etiological wordplay embedded in the narrative rather than a demonstrated historical derivation of the name.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The kings' terror in v. 1 ('heart melted...neither was there spirit in them any more') echoes Rahab's report of her people's fear in [[Joshua 2]] ('our hearts did melt, neither did there remain any more courage'). Similar terror language recurs elsewhere in the conquest narrative, so the echo may reflect a recurring idiom as much as a deliberate one-off cross-reference — though its reappearance here still confirms and extends Rahab's earlier report. More distinctive is the captain's command in v. 15 ('the place whereon thou standest is holy'), which closely parallels the LORD's words to Moses at the burning bush in [[Exodus 3]] ('the place whereon thou standest is holy ground'). The resemblance is close enough to support a deliberate literary association between Joshua's encounter and Moses's call, without by itself proving common authorship or settling the captain's identity.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Intertextual Parallel
> The command for Joshua to remove his shoe because he stands on holy ground closely parallels the command to Moses at the burning bush. This is strong evidence of a literary association between Joshua's encounter and Moses's commissioning encounter, although claims about the author's precise intention remain interpretive.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted parallel / disputed intent · [[Canonical Intertextual Parallel in Joshua 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary/Textual Parallel
> The captain's instruction, 'Loose thy shoe from off thy foot; for the place whereon thou standest is holy,' closely parallels the LORD's words to Moses at the burning bush, 'put off thy shoes from off thy feet, for the place whereon thou standest is holy ground' (Exodus 3:5), a wording pattern this study did not find repeated elsewhere in the text, which supports (without proving) a deliberate literary echo casting Joshua's commission in parallel to Moses's.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · accepted parallel / disputed intent · [[Literary/Textual Parallel in Joshua 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary/Textual Parallel *(tentative)*
> The description of the Canaanite kings' fear in Joshua 5:1 echoes Rahab's earlier report of Israel's effect on the land's inhabitants in Joshua 2:11, both using 'heart(s)...melted' plus a loss of courage/spirit, which some readers take as a deliberate inclusio bracketing the Jordan-crossing narrative (Joshua 2-5) with matching fear language, now inverted onto the Canaanites.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · disputed — idiom vs. deliberate echo

> [!evidence] Theological/Interpretive *(tentative)*
> The identity of the 'captain of the host of the LORD' is disputed: interpreters variously read the figure as a theophany of the LORD or the premortal Christ (supported by his acceptance of worship and the 'holy ground' language), or as a created angelic commander (e.g., sometimes identified with Michael) who is granted deputized authority and prostration in an ancient Near Eastern court sense.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · disputed, unresolved by the text

> [!evidence] Interpretive/Textual *(tentative)*
> The precise meaning of 'the reproach of Egypt' that the LORD says he has 'rolled away' (5:9) is disputed among interpreters: it may refer to the shame of uncircumcision now removed, to a taunt Egypt or the nations could raise that Israel had perished in the wilderness, or more broadly to the lingering stigma of Egyptian slavery.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, appropriately hedged

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does 'the reproach of Egypt' (v. 9) refer to — uncircumcision, the taunt that Israel might not reach the land, the stigma of slavery, or some combination? The text does not say.
- Who is the 'captain of the host of the LORD,' and why does Joshua's prostration before him go unrebuked — is this a theophany, or a created being acting with delegated authority?
- Does the captain's answer 'Nay' (v. 14) reject Joshua's for-us/for-them framing outright, or chiefly redirect attention to divine authority?
- Why does the narrative place circumcision and Passover — leaving the newly circumcised vulnerable — immediately before the Jericho campaign rather than after?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
