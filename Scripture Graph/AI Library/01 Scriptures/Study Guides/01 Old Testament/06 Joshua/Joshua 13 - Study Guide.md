# Joshua 13 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Joshua 13]] · My notes: [[Joshua 13 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> There remain some lands yet to be possessed—Some inhabitants are not expelled—The inheritances of Reuben, Gad, and one half of Manasseh are confirmed.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Unpossessed territories and Joshua's commission (vv. 1-7):** remaining regions are catalogued, and Joshua is told to allot them to nine and one-half tribes.
2. **Summary of the eastern inheritance (vv. 8-14):** Moses's victories over Sihon and Og are recalled; surviving Geshurites and Maachathites are acknowledged; Levi's exception is stated.
3. **Reuben (vv. 15-23):** boundaries, towns, and the defeat of Sihon, Midian's princes, and Balaam.
4. **Gad (vv. 24-28):** territory in Gilead and the Jordan valley.
5. **Half Manasseh (vv. 29-31):** Bashan, Jair's towns, and territory associated with Machir.
6. **Conclusion (vv. 32-33):** the setting in the plains of Moab is recalled, followed by a second statement of Levi's inheritance.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Joshua 13 distinguishes assigned inheritance from completed occupation: territory may be allotted while possession remains unfinished (vv. 1, 6-7). It also presents two forms of inheritance within this chapter. Reuben, Gad, and half Manasseh receive bounded territories, while Levi receives no territorial grant; offerings are called Levi's inheritance in verse 14, and the LORD God of Israel is called Levi's inheritance in verse 33. This is directly relevant to [[Sacrifice]], but the chapter does not state a general doctrine that material inheritance is inferior to sacred service.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV word **coast** repeatedly means a boundary or territorial border, not necessarily a seashore. The recurring phrases **inheritance**, **according to their families**, and **the cities and their villages** give the tribal accounts an administrative rhythm. The expression **until this day** in verse 13 signals a narrative viewpoint later than the event described, but supplies no precise date.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter places divine promise beside incomplete occupation. The LORD says that remaining inhabitants will be driven out and commands Joshua to allot the land (vv. 6-7), while verse 13 preserves a case in which expulsion did not occur. The eastern register is patterned by recurring grant formulas, boundaries, towns, and family-based conclusions. The Levi notices at verses 14 and 33 stand around the detailed register and may function as a literary frame, although administrative organization, inherited source arrangement, or editorial shaping could also explain their placement.

Joshua 13:21-22 also connects Balaam's death with five princes of Midian called 'dukes of Sihon.' [[Numbers 31]] calls the same five men kings of Midian. The shared names and Balaam notice establish a strong connection, while the differing titles remain a genuine textual question rather than something the evidence presently resolves.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Temporal Marker
> The phrase "until this day" presents the continued presence of the Geshurites and Maachathites from a narrative standpoint later than the initial settlement event. It is genuine evidence of temporal distance or later editorial perspective, but it cannot by itself determine how much time had elapsed.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.9 · Temporal viewpoint accepted; dating significance weak · [[Internal Temporal Marker in Joshua 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary And Administrative Structure
> Repeated opening and closing formulas, boundary descriptions, and town lists provide strong internal evidence that Joshua 13:15-31 is arranged as a deliberate territorial register rather than as an unstructured collection of place-names.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Strong internal structural evidence · [[Literary And Administrative Structure in Joshua 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical-Geographical / Textual-Critical *(tentative)*
> Joshua 13:3 names a fully organized Philistine pentapolis (the five lords of Gaza, Ashdod, Ashkelon, Gath, and Ekron). Historians generally date the Philistines' settlement of this coastal strip to the Sea Peoples migrations of c. 1175 BC, later than the traditional Late Bronze Age setting of the conquest narrative (c. 1400s BC). This gap is commonly cited as evidence that place-name material in Joshua was updated or glossed by a later hand rather than transmitted unchanged from the conquest generation.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · Textual observation secure; historical dating disputed and unsourced

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why is Joshua commanded to allot territory before Israel possesses it fully?
- How does verse 13 qualify the preceding language of victory and divine promise?
- What complementary ideas are conveyed by calling both sacrifices and the LORD Levi's inheritance?
- Why does the Reubenite list preserve the retrospective notice about Balaam?
- Which boundaries rely on natural landmarks, and which rely on cities or neighboring peoples?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the Transjordan request and grant in [[Numbers 32]] and [[Deuteronomy 3]] with its confirmation here, noting that Reuben and Gad make the initial request while half Manasseh is included later. Read [[Numbers 21]] and [[Joshua 12]] for the victories over Sihon and Og. Compare Balaam and the five Midianite rulers in [[Numbers 31]], including the differing ruler titles. Then read [[Joshua 18]] and [[Joshua 19]] for the later western allotment, and [[Judges 3]] for the closely parallel list of peoples remaining in the land.
