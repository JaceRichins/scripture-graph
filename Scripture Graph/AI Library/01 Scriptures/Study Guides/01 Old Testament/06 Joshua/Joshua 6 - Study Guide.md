## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Jericho closed and victory announced (vv. 1–5):** The LORD gives Joshua detailed instructions and predicts the wall's fall.
2. **Instructions enacted for six days (vv. 6–14):** Priests, armed men, the rearward, and the ark move in an ordered procession while the people remain silent.
3. **The seventh day (vv. 15–20):** Seven circuits culminate in trumpets, Joshua's command, the people's shout, and the wall's collapse.
4. **Ban, destruction, and rescue (vv. 17–25):** Property is assigned either to destruction or the LORD's treasury; Jericho's inhabitants are killed, while Rahab's household is removed according to the spies' oath in [[Joshua 2]].
5. **Curse and concluding notice (vv. 26–27):** Joshua curses Jericho's future rebuilder, and the narrator reports Joshua's growing fame.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects divine promise with exacting obedience, but it does not present obedience as a mechanical technique for obtaining miracles: the LORD declares the city given before the procession begins. Its treatment of property illustrates [[Consecration]] in a severe conquest setting—Israel's soldiers may not convert the designated valuables into private spoil. Rahab's rescue highlights the binding force of an oath and is later read through [[Faith]] in [[Hebrews 11]] and active faith in [[James 2]].

The destruction in verse 21 must remain visible in theological study. The narrator frames it as divinely authorized judgment, but that framing does not make the modern moral questions disappear or license readers to imitate conquest violence.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated vocabulary of circling, trumpets, priests, the ark, and seven creates a cumulative rhythm. A particularly tight verbal correspondence joins prediction and performance: verse 5 anticipates the trumpet, shout, fallen wall, and straight ascent that verse 20 narrates. The KJV also places "accursed" and "consecrated" near one another: Jericho is removed from ordinary human use, while specified metals are transferred to the LORD's treasury.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter delays its decisive action through repetition. Six days of one circuit establish restraint and expectation; on the seventh day the pattern accelerates to seven circuits before silence gives way to trumpets and a communal shout. Priests, the ark, armed men, and the rearward give the movement both sacred-processional and military features. The narrative presents victory as dependent on the LORD's prior promise, though that theological presentation does not independently establish the event's historical mechanism.

The chapter also prepares later narrative developments. The warning against taking the accursed property anticipates Achan's offense in [[Joshua 7]], and Joshua's curse receives an explicit fulfillment notice in [[1 Kings 16]]. These are strong literary connections, not independent proof of supernatural prediction.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should readers hold together the narrator's divine-command framework and the explicit killing of men, women, children, and animals? What does Rahab's rescue reveal about allegiance and covenant obligation, and what does it not resolve about the fate of the rest of Jericho? Why does the account devote more space to ritual order than to combat? Does the repeated number seven reflect historical ceremony, literary shaping, or both? What can—and cannot—the phrase "even unto this day" tell us about the chapter's composition?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Joshua 2]] for the oath behind Rahab's rescue and [[Joshua 7]] for the consequences of violating the ban. Compare Joshua 6:26 with [[1 Kings 16]], where Hiel's rebuilding is explicitly linked to Joshua's word. For later theological reception, compare [[Hebrews 11]] and [[James 2]]. Deuteronomy 7 and 20 provide broader literary context for the conquest commands, though that context should be studied alongside, not used to suppress, the ethical difficulty of Joshua 6:21.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
