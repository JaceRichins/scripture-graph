# Numbers 21 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Numbers 21]] · My notes: [[Numbers 21 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The children of Israel destroy those Canaanites who fight against them—The Israelites are plagued with fiery serpents—Moses lifts up a serpent of brass to save those who look thereon—Israel defeats the Amorites, destroys the people of Bashan, and occupies their lands.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Hormah (vv. 1–3):** Israel vows to destroy the cities of an attacking Canaanite force and receives victory.
2. **Fiery serpents (vv. 4–9):** discouragement becomes complaint; punishment is followed by confession, intercession, and a divinely appointed remedy.
3. **Journey and songs (vv. 10–20):** itinerary notices are interwoven with a cited book, a geographical fragment, and the well song.
4. **Sihon (vv. 21–32):** a peaceful transit request is refused; Israel defeats Sihon and occupies the territory he had taken from Moab.
5. **Og (vv. 33–35):** divine assurance precedes victory over Bashan.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The serpent account depicts a compact movement from [[Sin]] to [[Repentance]], [[Prayer]], and deliverance. Moses acts as an intercessory [[Prophets|prophet]], while each bitten person must respond to the appointed remedy. Numbers says that looking brought life; later passages connect that response more explicitly with [[Faith]] and [[Obedience]]. In [[John 3]], [[Alma 33]], and [[Helaman 8]], the lifted serpent becomes a type of the saving work of [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]. That later interpretation is canonically explicit, even though Numbers itself does not name Christ or explain the gaze in those terms.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The naming of Hormah follows Israel's vow to place the enemy cities under total destruction. In Hebrew, the verb describing this destruction and the name Hormah share the same root, so the place-name functions as a wordplay on the narrated event. The KJV partly preserves the relationship through the juxtaposition of “utterly destroyed” and “Hormah,” although the shared root is clearer in Hebrew.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly allows other voices to enter the prose narrative. It names “the book of the wars of the LORD” (vv. 14–15), records Israel's well song (vv. 17–18), and quotes proverb-speakers concerning Heshbon (vv. 27–30). These introductions make the chapter valuable for studying [[Records and Sacred Writings]] and the preservation or adaptation of traditional material. The Heshbon poem also creates narrative irony: the city that once projected Sihon's power over Moab becomes part of the territory lost to Israel.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Reception And Typology
> The explicit reuse of this episode in John and the Book of Mormon is strong evidence for its importance in later canonical interpretation as a pattern of salvation through responding to a divinely provided means.

> [!evidence] Literary And Compositional Evidence

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Typological *(tentative)*

> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · Typology explicit; speaker attribution and rarity disputed

> [!evidence] Philological/Text-Critical *(tentative)*

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the Lord answer the request to remove the serpents by providing a remedy instead of explicitly removing them? What, if anything, does Numbers itself imply about the disposition required when a bitten person looks? How should readers distinguish the narrative's claims about ancient warfare from moral authorization for warfare in other settings? Why are a source citation and two songs embedded within this itinerary?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the serpent narrative with [[John 3]], [[1 Nephi 17]], [[Alma 33]], and [[Helaman 8]], noting both their close correspondences and the interpretive elements added by the later texts. [[2 Kings 18]] traces the object's problematic later reception. For the campaigns, compare [[Deuteronomy 2]], [[Deuteronomy 3]], [[Judges 11]], and [[Joshua 12]]. These passages show how the Sihon and Og traditions function in retrospective history, territorial argument, and worship.
