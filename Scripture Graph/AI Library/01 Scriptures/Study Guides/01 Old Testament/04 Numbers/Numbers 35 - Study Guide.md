# Numbers 35 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Numbers 35]] · My notes: [[Numbers 35 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Levites are to possess their own cities—Cities of refuge are established for those guilty of manslaughter—Murderers are to be executed by the revenger of blood.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Levitical cities and surrounding land (vv. 1–8):** forty-eight cities, including six refuges, are supplied proportionately by the tribes. Verses 4–5 give measurements of one thousand and two thousand cubits whose precise relationship is not explained.

2. **Purpose and accessibility of refuge (vv. 9–15):** refuge protects a slayer from the avenger pending judgment and is available to Israelite, stranger, and sojourner.

3. **Cases of murder and unintentional killing (vv. 16–23):** the law considers lethal instruments, hatred, ambush, enmity, sudden action, and accidental circumstances.

4. **Judgment and bounded refuge (vv. 24–29):** the congregation decides whether the slayer receives protection; residence continues until the high priest's death, and leaving early removes that protection.

5. **Witnesses, satisfaction, and land defilement (vv. 30–34):** one witness cannot secure execution, satisfaction cannot replace the prescribed outcomes, and bloodshed defiles the land.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
As an interpretive application, the refuge law can be studied through [[Justice and Mercy]], provided its severity is not softened. The system protects an accused slayer until judgment and protects one judged unintentional within defined borders, but it also requires displacement, retains the blood-avenger, and prescribes death for murder.

The chapter's explicit theology is that bloodshed defiles the land and must not remain unresolved because the Lord dwells among Israel (vv. 33–34). This connects homicide law with the sacred character of the land under the [[Law of Moses]]. The text itself does not identify the high priest's death as a symbol of Christ's atonement or explain why that death ends confinement.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly contrasts terms of intention: “hatred,” “laying of wait,” “enmity,” and seeking another's “harm” are set against acting “suddenly,” “without enmity,” “without laying of wait,” and while “seeing him not” (vv. 20–23). This vocabulary makes motive and circumstance central to the classification of the death.

The KJV alternates among “manslayer,” “slayer,” “murderer,” “avenger,” and “revenger of blood.” These are functional legal categories in the passage, not interchangeable labels: the congregation's task is precisely to decide whether a slayer is a murderer or is entitled to refuge.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The homicide law proceeds through concrete cases. Verses 16–21 accumulate circumstances classified as murder; verses 22–23 contrast cases marked by suddenness, absence of enmity or ambush, and failure to see the victim. Verses 24–28 then move from hypothetical acts to congregational judgment and the spatial and temporal limits of refuge.

The repeated verdict, “he is a murderer: the murderer shall surely be put to death,” gives the section a formulaic legal rhythm. Earlier, verses 4–5 juxtapose one-thousand- and two-thousand-cubit measurements. A two-zone arrangement is possible, but the text does not explicitly explain their geometric relationship.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why is the congregation assigned to judge between the slayer and the blood-avenger rather than leaving the matter to the avenger alone?

What purposes might required residence in a refuge city serve—protection, restraint of retaliation, acknowledgment of the death, community peace, or some combination?

Why does the high priest's death end the confinement, and why does the chapter leave that rationale unstated?

How does the multiple-witness rule limit irreversible punishment within a legal system that still authorizes capital punishment?

How might the one-thousand- and two-thousand-cubit measurements in verses 4–5 relate, and what remains uncertain in each proposed explanation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Exodus 21]] for a shorter law distinguishing presumptuous killing from killing without lying in wait and providing a place of flight. Compare [[Deuteronomy 19]], which supplies additional refuge-city procedures and an accident example. Then read [[Joshua 20]], which presents the appointment and naming of the cities commanded through Moses. These passages form a strong canonical comparison while retaining differences in wording and detail; they do not by themselves settle questions of dating, literary dependence, or historical implementation.
