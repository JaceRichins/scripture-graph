# Numbers 27 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Numbers 27]] · My notes: [[Numbers 27 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The law of inheritances to sons, daughters, and kinsmen is explained—Moses will see but not enter the promised land—Joshua is called and set apart to lead Israel.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **A named petition (27:1–4):** Zelophehad's daughters identify the family problem and request land.
2. **Judgment and statute (27:5–11):** Moses refers the case to the LORD; the favorable ruling is generalized into an inheritance order.
3. **Moses's approaching death (27:12–14):** he may see the land but may not enter because of Meribah.
4. **Prayer and succession (27:15–23):** Moses requests a shepherd for Israel, and Joshua is selected and commissioned before Eleazar and the congregation.

The transition is meaningful: a section about transmission of family inheritance is followed by a section about transmission of public leadership.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter portrays [[Revelation]] as relevant to a concrete legal question: Moses acknowledges that the case requires a ruling and brings it before the LORD (27:5). The answer vindicates the petitioners and produces a continuing statute (27:7–11).

It also combines mercy toward the congregation with accountability for its leaders. Moses remains the LORD's servant and commissions his successor obediently, but the Meribah judgment is not canceled (27:12–14, 22–23). Joshua's appointment further depicts leadership as both spiritually qualified and publicly accountable: he is described as possessing spirit, receives a public charge, and must coordinate with Eleazar's priestly inquiry (27:18–21).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The daughters' concern that their father's "name" might be "done away" shows that inheritance is presented as more than private wealth; land also sustains a household's recognized continuity (27:4). This should not be expanded into a claim of fully equal inheritance, since daughters inherit here when there is no son and the statute remains organized around the paternal family.

The paired expressions "go out" and "come in," followed by "lead them out" and "bring them in," portray comprehensive public leadership (27:17). Verse 21 repeats the going-out/coming-in language while placing Joshua's decisions in relation to Eleazar's inquiry by the Urim.

The Noah of 27:1 is a daughter of Zelophehad. Readers and indexes should not confuse her with the earlier male patriarch of the same English name.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two carefully narrated public scenes frame the chapter. In the first, five named women stand before Moses, Eleazar, Israel's princes, and the congregation (27:1–2). In the second, Joshua is set before Eleazar and the congregation and publicly charged (27:18–23). Both scenes concern legitimate continuity and require a visible decision before the community.

The chapter also places failure beside obedience. Moses's exclusion recalls his rebellion at Meribah (27:14), yet the closing report twice stresses that he performed Joshua's commissioning as commanded (27:22–23). The contrast permits a nuanced portrait: prior failure has consequences, but it does not erase subsequent faithful service.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Public Succession Ritual
> The repeated emphasis on selection, public presentation, witnessed charging, transferred honor, and laying on of hands is strong textual evidence that the commissioning scene is designed to legitimate Joshua's leadership and provide visible continuity before Israel.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · Strong literary inference with adequate limitations · [[Public Succession Ritual in Numbers 27|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual Comparison *(tentative)*
> The chapter marks a procedural difference in how Joshua receives divine guidance compared to Moses: Joshua's direction comes through Eleazar consulting the Urim (v. 21), whereas Moses is elsewhere described as receiving revelation directly and 'face to face' (Numbers 12:6-8; Deuteronomy 34:10). This textual contrast supports, but does not prove, a reading in which Moses' successor's access to revelation is institutionally mediated through the priesthood rather than personally direct in the same way Moses' was.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why do the daughters explicitly distinguish Zelophehad from Korah's company (27:3)? What legal or moral assumption are they answering?
- What does the transformation of one family's petition into a general statute suggest about the narrative's understanding of just law (27:5–11)?
- Why does Moses answer news of his death by requesting a leader for the congregation (27:15–17)?
- Which aspects of Moses's role pass to Joshua, and which remain associated with Eleazar and the Urim (27:18–21)?
- How do [[Numbers 36]] and [[Joshua 17]] qualify and complete the inheritance narrative?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Numbers 20]] for the Meribah event behind verses 12–14, [[Numbers 36]] for the later tribal-marriage qualification, and [[Joshua 17]] for the reported allotment to Zelophehad's daughters. Compare Joshua's commissioning here with [[Deuteronomy 31]] and [[Deuteronomy 34]]. These comparisons clarify both continuity and limits: Joshua receives public authority, but the text does not simply describe him as another Moses in every respect.
