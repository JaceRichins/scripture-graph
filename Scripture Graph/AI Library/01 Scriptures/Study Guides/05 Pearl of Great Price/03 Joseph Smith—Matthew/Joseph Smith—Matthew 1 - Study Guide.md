# Joseph Smith—Matthew 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Joseph Smith—Matthew 1]] · My notes: [[Joseph Smith—Matthew 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Joseph Smith—Matthew 1 is Joseph Smith's inspired revision (JST) of the Olivet Discourse recorded in [[Matthew 24]] (paralleled in [[Mark 13]] and [[Luke 21]]). Prompted by Jesus's prediction that the temple would be thrown down, the disciples ask a two-part question: when will the temple and Jerusalem be destroyed, and what is the sign of Christ's coming and of the end of the world? Verses 12–20 give locally framed instructions concerning Jerusalem and Judea; verse 21 then explicitly marks a transition ('these things I have spoken unto you concerning the Jews; and again...') to a broader treatment of deception, worldwide upheaval, gospel preaching, cosmic signs, the gathering of the elect, and the coming of the Son of Man. The chapter reads less as a fixed timetable than as sustained counsel to watch: treasure Christ's word, remain steadfast, and continue faithful service, since 'of that day, and hour, no one knoweth... but my Father only' (v.40). It is thus a discourse about both [[Signs of the Times]] and [[Endurance]] as much as about the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]] itself.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Temple prediction and the disciples' questions (vv.1–4).** Jesus predicts the temple's destruction; the disciples ask privately about its timing, the fate of the Jews, and the sign of his coming and of the end of the world.

2. **Warnings concerning Jerusalem and the Jews (vv.5–20).** Deception, persecution, apostasy, and flight from Judea dominate this section, closing with the 'abomination of desolation' explicitly tied to 'the destruction of Jerusalem' (v.12).

3. **Transition and broader latter-day material (vv.21–37).** Verse 21 explicitly marks the preceding material as 'concerning the Jews' before turning to renewed warnings of deception, worldwide upheaval, gospel preaching, a second, unqualified reference to the abomination of desolation (v.32), cosmic signs, and the gathering of the elect.

4. **Uncertain timing and required vigilance (vv.38–48).** The fig tree parable permits recognizing nearness from the signs, but the day and hour remain known only to the Father (v.40); Noah and the thief illustrate unexpected arrival.

5. **Faithful and evil servants (vv.49–55).** The discourse closes by converting expectation into an ethical test: the servant is judged by what the returning lord finds him doing.

The recurring refrain ('iniquity shall abound... love shall wax cold... he that... is not overcome, the same shall be saved,' vv.10–11 and 30) links the earlier and later material, though whether this marks two cleanly parallel prophecies or a single discourse reusing shared language across a blended near/far horizon is itself disputed (see Questions).
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The text defines the elect in covenant terms (vv.20, 22) and depicts divine concern for their preservation and [[Gathering of Israel|gathering]] (vv.23, 27, 37). Yet election does not remove the demand for vigilance: the elect remain possible targets of unusually persuasive deception. [[Salvation]] is repeatedly connected with not being overcome (vv.11, 30).

The chapter also distinguishes knowledge of general nearness from knowledge of the precise time. Signs may prompt readiness, but verse 40 reserves the exact day and hour to the Father — not even the [[Angels|angels]] of heaven know it. The sound doctrinal use of the signs is therefore watchfulness and faithful service, not confident date-setting; Joseph Smith later prayed to learn the time of the Second Coming, and D&C 130:14–17 records that inquiry, connecting thematically (though not by direct quotation) to verse 40.

Verse 31 connects [[Missionary Work]] with the end: the gospel is to be preached throughout the world as a witness to all nations. The verse states a sequence, but it does not supply a measurable threshold by which readers can calculate when that witness is complete.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The expression “end of the world” is interpreted within the chapter as “the destruction of the wicked” (verses 4 and 31). Verse 55 then says that “the end of the earth is not yet,” distinguishing the judgment of the wicked from the earth's later end. This internal gloss should govern interpretation more securely than assumptions imported into the phrase.

The repeated command to resist deception frames the discourse. False claims are contrasted with a coming described as earth-covering light and as a manifestation seen by all tribes. Verse 37 supplies the chapter's stated safeguard: treasuring Christ's word.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repetition links the two broad movements of the discourse. False prophets and deception appear in verses 5–9 and again in verses 21–25; abounding iniquity, cooling love, and salvation through steadfastness appear in verses 10–11 and again in verse 30. These recurrences may show that similar spiritual dangers operate in more than one crisis, but they do not, by themselves, prove that every repeated detail refers to the same event.

The chapter also repeats its reference to the 'abomination of desolation' (Daniel): once explicitly tied to Jerusalem's destruction (v.12) and once without that qualifier (v.32). Some readers take this doubling as marking a second, future fulfillment, though the text itself does not specify what that fulfillment is.

The closing [[Parables of Jesus]] move from recognition to conduct: the fig tree concerns discernment, Noah and the thief concern unexpectedness, and the servants concern behavior during apparent delay.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does verse 21 affect the division between the Jerusalem material and the later discussion of Christ's coming — two distinct prophecies, or one discourse reusing shared language across near and far horizons?

- Verse 34 says 'this generation, in which these things shall be shown forth, shall not pass away until all I have told you shall be fulfilled.' Does 'this generation' mean Jesus's contemporaries, or — as the added clause 'in which these things shall be shown forth' may suggest — the generation that witnesses the signs described earlier in the chapter? The added wording tilts toward the latter reading but does not settle the question outright.

- Why does the text provide signs of nearness (the fig tree, vv.38–39) while withholding the day and hour (v.40)?

- What actions in verses 37 and 46–50 define readiness more concretely than speculation about chronology?

- What does the chapter's own definition of 'the end of the world' (vv.4, 31, 55) clarify, and what chronological questions does it leave unresolved?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the ordering and wording of Joseph Smith—Matthew 1 with [[Matthew 24]], [[Mark 13]], and [[Luke 21]]. D&C 45 is especially relevant for examining how a related 1831 revelation treats the Jerusalem discourse, the elect, and signs of Christ's coming. D&C 130:14–17 records Joseph Smith's own later prayer to learn the time of the Second Coming and is worth reading alongside verse 40's statement that only the Father knows the day and hour. Moses 7 offers a Restoration-scripture parallel for the flood/Noah imagery in verses 41–43; its later verses on gathering are a closer match to the gathering imagery in verses 27 and 37 than the flood verses themselves. Useful questions while studying include which transitions in the text are explicit (like v.21) and which connections are inferred, and whether a proposed interpretation preserves the chapter's distinction between signs of nearness and knowledge of the exact time.
