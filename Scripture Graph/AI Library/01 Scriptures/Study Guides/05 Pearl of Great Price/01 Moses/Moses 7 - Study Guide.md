# Moses 7 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Moses 7]] · My notes: [[Moses 7 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Moses 7 records a single extended theophany granted to Enoch. Called from 'the place Mahujah' up 'the mount Simeon,' Enoch is shown 'the world for the space of many generations' (v.4). The vision moves through Enoch's own generation and the rise and translation of his City of [[Zion]] (vv.5-24); a sweeping view of 'all the nations of the earth' that provokes God himself to weep over human wickedness (vv.22-41); the coming Flood and Noah's ark (vv.42-44); and a forward-looking sequence culminating in Christ's mortal coming, crucifixion, and eventual [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ|return]] to establish a [[Millennium|thousand-year]] Zion, a New Jerusalem (vv.45-67). The chapter's emotional center is not prediction alone: God, the heavens, Enoch, and even the personified earth all weep over sin and suffering, and the vision repeatedly asks how holiness, judgment, [[Agency]], and compassion fit together. The chapter closes with Enoch's own people being received into heaven, echoing the earlier translation of Zion (vv.68-69).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. Call and theophany on Mount Simeon (vv.1-4)
2. Vision of Shum and Canaan; the curse on Canaan's land (vv.5-8)
3. Commission to preach and baptize; Enoch's power over nature (vv.9-17)
4. Founding of the City of [[Zion]], later taken up into heaven (vv.18-21)
5. Cosmic vision of the wicked residue of Adam's posterity; God and the heavens weep (vv.22-41)
6. Noah and the Flood foreseen; Enoch's grief and prayer for Noah's seed (vv.42-52)
7. Christ's coming, crucifixion, and the spirits in prison (vv.53-57)
8. The Second Coming, [[Millennium]], and New Jerusalem (vv.58-67)
9. Coda: 365 years of Zion, and the translation of Enoch's people, echoing vv.18-21 (vv.68-69)

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Zion** is explicitly defined in v.18: a people 'of one heart and one mind,' living in righteousness, 'and there was no poor among them.' The text pairs spiritual unity with the concrete absence of poverty; it does not describe an economic system, so calling this 'consecration' in its later developed sense goes beyond what the verse itself states, though the connection to [[Consecration]] is a natural one within LDS scripture.

**A weeping God.** vv.28-40 depict God weeping over the residue of humanity, joined by the heavens, Enoch, and (v.48) the personified earth. God explains that he gave mankind knowledge and [[Agency]] (v.32, in the Garden of Eden), commanded love, and grieves because they instead 'hate their own blood' (v.33). The passage holds divine holiness, [[Justice and Mercy]], and grief together without resolving whether God's weeping here is literal or accommodated language for Enoch's benefit.

**Intercession and the spirit world.** In v.39 an unnamed 'Chosen' pleads before God and suffers for the sins of a doomed generation so that they may repent, while they remain 'in torment' until the Chosen's return. Read with vv.47, 50, and 55-57 (the Lamb slain, prayer 'in the name of...Jesus Christ,' Christ on the cross, spirits in prison coming forth), this figure is naturally identified as Christ, and the passage anticipates, without fully spelling out, later doctrines of the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] and [[Death and the Spirit World]].

**Second Coming and Millennium.** vv.60-67 prophesy latter-day tribulation, the gathering of the elect to a New Jerusalem, and Christ's [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ|return]] to reign for a thousand years ([[Millennium]]).

**The 'blackness' passages (vv.8, 22).** The text describes a curse and 'blackness' upon the children of Canaan (v.8) and separately says 'the seed of Cain were black, and had not place among' the residue of Adam's descendants shown to Enoch (v.22). The chapter does not itself explain the nature of this blackness or assert a genealogical link between Canaan and Cain — that connection is an inference readers bring to the text. These verses matter for another reason: together with related Book of Abraham language, they were part of the reasoning LDS leaders historically invoked to justify the Church's pre-1978 restriction on priesthood ordination and temple ordinances for members of Black African descent. In 2013 the Church's Gospel Topics essay 'Race and the Priesthood' formally disavowed any teaching that dark skin reflects divine curse, disfavor, or unworthiness. A responsible reading holds both facts together: what the verses actually say, which is limited and ambiguous, and how they were historically used, which the Church has since repudiated.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The vision is threaded together by the formula 'And it came to pass' and advanced scene by scene through the Lord's repeated command 'Look.' A recurring dialogic pattern structures its emotional core: Enoch asks an anguished question ('How is it...', 'When shall...', 'Wilt thou not...') and the Lord answers (vv.28-29, 45-47, 49-53, 54-57, 58-65). God's self-naming in v.35 ('Man of Holiness... Man of Counsel... Endless and Eternal') is distinctive phrasing with no close biblical parallel. Weeping is the chapter's dominant image — God, the heavens, Enoch, and the personified earth (v.48, 'the mother of men') all weep — and after Enoch learns why God weeps, his own heart 'swelled wide as eternity' (v.41), suggesting that in this text prophetic knowledge includes learning to feel compassion. The refrain 'ZION IS FLED' (v.69) closes the account as a proverb-like tagline.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Formally, Moses 7 belongs to the apocalyptic-panoramic-vision genre: a seer ascends, is shown successive scenes of history culminating in the Messiah's ministry and the end of the world, and reacts emotionally to what he sees. This pattern is broadly comparable to other visionary scripture such as [[1 Nephi 11]], though the specific wording is largely independent, so the comparison should be read as a shared genre convention rather than demonstrated literary dependence. The repeated command 'Look' structures the vision's expanding scope, from nearby peoples to future generations, the Flood, Christ, and the end of the world. Within the chapter, the 365-year lifespan of the city of Zion (v.68) invites comparison with the 365 years attributed to Enoch's own life in Genesis 5:23 — an intriguing numerical echo, though it may be coincidental, since both figures were available to the nineteenth-century producer of the text.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Is 'that which I have chosen' / 'my Chosen' in v.39 the same figure as the Lamb of v.47 and the Jesus Christ named in v.50? The identification is the standard and most natural reading but is not stated at v.39 itself.
- What do vv.8 and 22 actually assert about 'blackness,' and what do they leave unstated? Given the historical use of these verses to help justify the Church's pre-1978 priesthood and temple restriction, and their disavowal in the Church's 2013 'Race and the Priesthood' essay, how should a modern reader hold the text's limited claims and its reception history together?
- What does it mean for God to weep (vv.28-31) — literal divine emotion, accommodated language for Enoch's sake, or something else — and how does Enoch's own transformation (v.41) inform that question?
- Is the 365-year figure for Zion's duration (v.68) a deliberate echo of Enoch's 365-year lifespan in Genesis 5:23, or coincidental?
- In vv.48-65, what does the earth's requested 'rest' include — cleansing, relief from wickedness, Christ's presence, or all of these?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
This chapter's strongest verified textual overlap is with [[1 Nephi 11]] (10 shared verses) — compare Nephi's own panoramic, Christ-centered vision. The baptismal commission of v.11 overlaps with the Father/Son/Holy Ghost formula in [[D&C 20]] and Matthew 28:19. The latter-days material in vv.60-67 overlaps with Joseph Smith—Matthew 1 and Matthew 24, and more lightly with D&C 130, D&C 68, D&C 63, and D&C 45 — which explicitly revisits Enoch's Zion and its translation. Also worth comparing: Genesis 5:24, where Enoch himself (rather than his city) 'walked with God' and 'was not, for God took him,' close in wording to Moses 7:69; and Genesis 9:11, the flood covenant matching the promise Enoch receives in v.51. These are meaningful textual and thematic parallels, not proof of direct literary dependence in either direction.
