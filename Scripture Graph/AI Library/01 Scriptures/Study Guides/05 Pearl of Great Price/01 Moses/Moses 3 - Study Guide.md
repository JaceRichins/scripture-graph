# Moses 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Moses 3]] · My notes: [[Moses 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Moses 3 moves from the sanctification of the seventh day to a focused account of embodied life in Eden. It describes spiritual creation preceding natural manifestation, Adam's formation from dust and divine breath, the garden and its rivers, Adam's work and moral choice, and the creation of woman. Its closing movement presents [[Marriage]] as the answer to the first condition called “not good”: human solitude.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Completion and sacred rest (vv. 1–3):** God finishes the work, rests, and sanctifies the seventh day.

2. **Spiritual and natural creation (vv. 4–7):** the account distinguishes prior spiritual creation from subsequent embodied existence and narrates Adam's formation.

3. **Eden as provisioned place (vv. 8–14):** trees, the two central trees, and the four rivers define the garden.

4. **Vocation and command (vv. 15–17):** Adam is assigned to dress and keep the garden and receives a consequential choice.

5. **From solitude to union (vv. 18–25):** animal naming demonstrates the absence of a suitable counterpart; the woman is then created, recognized as kin, and joined with the man as one flesh.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins several themes without collapsing them into one another. [[Creation]] is purposeful and ordered; [[Sabbath Day]] holiness is grounded in God's completed work and rest; work belongs to Eden before the Fall (Moses 3:15); and [[Agency]] operates within a real command and announced consequence rather than outside moral obligation (Moses 3:17). The marriage passage emphasizes kinship, cleaving, unity, and the absence of shame (Moses 3:23–25), while making no explicit statement here about every later question concerning marital roles or eternal duration.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Living soul” has unusually broad scope within the chapter. Adam becomes a living soul through dust and divine breath (Moses 3:7), but a tree and the animals also receive that description (Moses 3:9, 19). Interpretations should therefore avoid assuming that the expression is exclusively human in this text.

The wording “thou mayest choose for thyself” occurs alongside “I forbid it” (Moses 3:17). The juxtaposition distinguishes the existence of choice from divine endorsement of every available option.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly contrasts provision with a remaining human need. God completes and provides the created setting, but then declares Adam's solitude “not good” (Moses 3:18). The animal-naming episode demonstrates that no suitable counterpart is found, after which the woman is formed and recognized through the balanced kinship language “bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh” (Moses 3:23).

The word *good* also recurs in different voices: God sees the completed work as good (Moses 3:2), Adam sees the trees as good for food (Moses 3:9), and God calls solitude not good (Moses 3:18). This creates a possible verbal progression, though the shift in speaking subject cautions against treating it as a formally demonstrated literary structure.

The repeated first-person formulas “I, God” and “I, the Lord God” give the chapter a distinctive divine narrative voice. Adam's first recorded speech in this sequence is recognition of relationship rather than the naming of another creature.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Comparative Textual Difference
> Moses 3 contains explicit material not present in the corresponding Genesis verses, notably the explanation of spiritual creation and the statement that Adam may choose for himself. This establishes that Moses 3 is not merely an exact reproduction of Genesis 2 at these points, while leaving the origin and authority of the expansions open.

> [!evidence] Textual Relationship
> Close correspondences in the formation of man, planting of Eden, garden vocation, command concerning the tree, creation of the woman, and one-flesh saying provide strong evidence of a direct literary relationship between Moses 3 and Genesis 2. The correspondences alone do not determine the direction or mechanism of that relationship.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What range of meanings could “spiritually” bear in Moses 3:5–9, and which meanings are actually required by the wording?

- How does Moses 3:17 hold command, freedom, warning, and accountability together?

- What does the chapter's broad use of “living soul” contribute to its view of created life?

- Why does the narrative place Adam's work in the garden before the Fall?

- What can legitimately be inferred from the woman being described as Adam's bone and flesh, and what later claims go beyond the text?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Moses 3]], [[Genesis 2]], and [[Abraham 5]] side by side, marking shared wording as well as substantive differences. Give particular attention to spiritual creation (Moses 3:5, 7, 9), moral choice (Moses 3:17), God's address to the Only Begotten (Moses 3:18), and the description of trees and animals as living souls (Moses 3:9, 19).

The extensive parallels establish close textual relationships, but verbal comparison alone cannot determine whether particular differences are restorations, inspired revisions, modern expansions, adaptations, or products of another mechanism. Those conclusions require independent historical and theological evidence. Continue into [[Moses 4]] to observe how the trees, command, choice, and warning become active elements in the account of the [[Fall of Adam and Eve]].
