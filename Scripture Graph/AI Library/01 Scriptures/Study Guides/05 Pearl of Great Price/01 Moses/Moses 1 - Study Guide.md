# Moses 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Moses 1]] · My notes: [[Moses 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Moses 1 presents revelation as both elevating and destabilizing. Moses is sustained by divine glory, sees creation, and then collapses when that glory withdraws. His resulting humility does not erase his revealed identity: when Satan calls him a "son of man," Moses answers that he is a son of God. After resisting the counterfeit claimant through remembered revelation and [[Prayer]], Moses receives a broader vision of [[Creation]] and a commission connected with [[Exodus and Deliverance]] and [[Records and Sacred Writings]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has three clear movements:

1. **Encounter and descent (vv. 1-11):** Moses sees God and the world, loses the sustaining glory, falls, and reflects on human nothingness.
2. **Temptation and resistance (vv. 12-23):** Satan demands worship; Moses tests the claim against divine glory, prior commandments, and his revealed identity. Calling upon God brings strength.
3. **Renewed revelation and commission (vv. 24-42):** Moses sees the earth and its inhabitants, asks about creation, hears of innumerable worlds and God's work, and is commanded to write.

The middle conflict is therefore not an incidental interruption. It tests whether Moses will retain what the first revelation taught before receiving more.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Several teachings are explicit. The Only Begotten is the Savior, is full of grace and truth, and is the agent through whom God creates (vv. 6, 32-33), supporting study of [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]. The Holy Ghost bears record of the Father and Son (v. 24), making the chapter relevant to [[The Godhead]]. God's works and words have no end (vv. 4, 38), while his declared work and glory concern human immortality and [[Eternal Life]] (v. 39).

The text should not be made to say more than it does. It coordinates "immortality" and "eternal life" but does not define their distinction here. It also affirms that creation serves God's purpose while leaving some of the corresponding wisdom undisclosed (v. 31).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The word **glory** performs several functions: it sustains Moses in God's presence, withdraws before his collapse, supplies a criterion for distinguishing Satan from God, and returns with renewed revelation. "Spiritual eyes" and "transfigured" qualify what the chapter means by seeing God face to face; the account does not portray this as ordinary unaided sight.

"Man is nothing" (v. 10) is immediately balanced by "I am a son of God" (v. 13). Reading either line without the other distorts the chapter's movement between humility and identity. The phrase "Adam, which is many" (v. 34) is unusually compressed and should not be assigned a precise meaning without further textual or historical argument.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter builds a sustained identity contrast: God calls Moses "my son" (v.6) and reaffirms "thou art after the similitude of mine Only Begotten" (v.16), while Satan addresses him only as "Moses, son of man" (v.12) and later claims for himself, "I am the Only Begotten" (v.19). Moses answers by asserting his own status: "I am a son of God, in the similitude of his Only Begotten" (v.13). The temptation scene turns on Moses testing Satan against what he already knows — the absence of glory, the ability to look on Satan with unaided eyes, and the content of God's prior commandments (vv.14-18) — discernment through memory and comparison, not a mere contest of titles.

The episode also follows a pattern shared with the Gospel accounts of Christ's wilderness temptation: a demand for worship (vv.12, 19), a citation of exclusive worship of God (v.15, "Worship God, for him only shalt thou serve"), and the rebuke "Get thee hence, Satan" (v.16), which is close to verbatim with Matthew 4:10 and Deuteronomy 6:13. This is a genuine verbal echo in the received English text and supports reading Moses as a type of Christ, but it does not by itself establish an ancient literary relationship; the parallel could equally reflect Joseph Smith's use of familiar King James idiom while dictating the text in 1830.

The chapter as a whole is organized around withdrawal and return: divine glory withdraws and Moses falls (v.9), Satan enters into the resulting vacancy, Moses calls upon God and receives strength, Satan departs, and glory returns together with an expanded vision (v.24) — a structural pattern of dependence on God running through the whole chapter.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Parallel / Type-Scene *(tentative)*
> The temptation of Moses shares a specific literary pattern with the Gospel accounts of Christ's wilderness temptation: Satan's demand to 'worship me' (Moses 1:12, 19), the counter-citation that God alone is to be worshipped (Moses 1:15), and the rebuke 'Get thee hence, Satan' (Moses 1:16) closely parallel the temptation of Jesus in Matthew 4, including its identical rebuke phrase in the KJV.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · accepted literary observation, disputed as ancient parallel

> [!evidence] Linguistic/Interpretive *(tentative)*
> The phrase 'which is many' in Moses 1:34 is read differently by different interpreters: some connect it to the Hebrew word adam (אָדָם), which denotes both the individual and generically 'mankind,' while others read it cosmologically, as meaning 'Adam' functions as a recurring title given to the first man on each of the 'worlds without number' just described.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · disputed, uncited
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrative place Satan's temptation between the two divine visions?
- Which tests does Moses actually use to distinguish God from Satan, and which of those tests depend on prior revelation?
- How does verse 13 qualify a potentially nihilistic reading of "man is nothing" in verse 10?
- How much of Moses's question about why creation exists is answered in verses 31 and 39, and what remains undisclosed?
- What is asserted—and what is not demonstrated—by the textual-loss claim in verses 23 and 41?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the burning-bush recollection in Exodus 3, the deliverance through water in Exodus 14, and the worship temptation in Matthew 4. These comparisons clarify the chapter's scriptural setting and recurring motifs. They should not, by themselves, be treated as proof of common authorship or historical priority. Further study of verses 23 and 41 should distinguish the chapter's internal restoration claim from external evidence about the transmission of the biblical text.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
