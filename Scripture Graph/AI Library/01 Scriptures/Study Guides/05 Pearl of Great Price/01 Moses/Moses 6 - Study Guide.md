# Moses 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Moses 6]] · My notes: [[Moses 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Moses 6 joins three kinds of continuity: descent through genealogy, knowledge through records and teaching, and covenant identity through ordinances. Its first half traces Adam's family to Enoch while repeatedly adding prophecy and instruction to the genealogical notices. Its second half recounts Enoch's call and embeds within his sermon a much earlier discourse between God and Adam. The resulting movement runs from Adam, through the generations, to Enoch, and then back to Adam to recover the foundational teaching.

Repentance connects the whole chapter. Adam calls his sons to repent; ancestral preachers teach repentance; Enoch receives the same commission; and the Adam discourse explains repentance through the [[Fall of Adam and Eve]], the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Baptism]], and the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Adam through Jared (vv. 1–25):** sacrifice, inspired records, literacy, genealogy, prophetic teaching, migration, and the spread of violence.
2. **Enoch's call (vv. 26–36):** the Spirit descends, God commissions a reluctant speaker, and Enoch is enabled to see beyond the natural eye.
3. **Public confrontation (vv. 37–47):** offense gives way to curiosity, fear, questioning, and trembling as Enoch explains his origin and authority.
4. **The Adam discourse (vv. 48–63):** Enoch recounts teachings concerning the Fall, accountability, Christ, rebirth, justification, sanctification, and the witness of creation.
5. **Adam's enactment (vv. 64–68):** Adam is baptized, receives the Holy Ghost, and is declared a son of God.

The structure makes the closing ordinance narrative the practical fulfillment of the preceding instruction.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter distinguishes inherited mortal conditions from inherited blame. Death, misery, temptation, and estrangement follow the Fall (vv. 48–49), but verse 54 denies that the sins of parents are answered upon their children. Accountability appears as children grow, encounter good and evil, and become agents (vv. 55–57). This distinction is central to [[Agency]], [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], and the rejection of infant guilt.

Verses 59–60 coordinate three elements of rebirth: water, Spirit, and Christ's blood. Water is associated with keeping the commandment, the Spirit with justification, and blood with sanctification. Adam then passes through water, receives the Spirit, and is pronounced a son of God. The ordinance sequence therefore ends with a transformed relationship to God, not merely ritual completion.

The passage calls this teaching the [[Plan of Salvation]] and grounds it in the blood of the Only Begotten. Its final invitation, 'thus may all become my sons,' gives the discourse a universal scope.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter uses repeated words to connect its parts. **Teach** joins the first generations, Enoch's upbringing, and Adam's duty to instruct his children. **Record** joins the book of remembrance to the Holy Ghost as the 'record of heaven.' **Call** moves from calling upon God's name, to calling people to repentance, to God's calling of Enoch. **Walk** changes from a command—'walk with me'—to the explanation of Enoch's authority—'he walked with God.'

The phrase 'Man of Holiness' is presented as the Father's name in Adam's language, with 'Son of Man' interpreted as the name of the Only Begotten. This is the chapter's own theological explanation; it is not independent linguistic evidence about a recoverable historical Adamic language.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The book-of-remembrance motif provides narrative cohesion. It first explains literacy and genealogy (vv. 5–8), then explains how Enoch's community claims knowledge of Adam (vv. 45–46). The genealogy is therefore more than a list of ancestors: it represents a chain of remembered revelation.

Several biblical call formulas converge in Enoch's story. His slow-speech objection resembles Moses in Exodus 4, while 'Choose ye this day' resembles Joshua 24. These parallels portray Enoch through familiar prophetic patterns. They establish literary relationships or shared scriptural idiom, but they do not by themselves establish whether the account is ancient, modern, or produced through the revelatory process claimed by the text.

The birth analogy in verses 59–60 moves from observable mortal birth to spiritual rebirth. Verse 63 then generalizes that technique: created things can bear witness of spiritual things. This makes analogy part of the chapter's stated method of revelation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How does the return of the book of remembrance in verses 45–46 change the function of the opening genealogy?

What is gained by placing God's instruction to Adam inside Enoch's sermon rather than narrating it directly?

How do verses 54–57 distinguish mortal fallenness, inherited guilt, personal sin, and accountability?

Does 'all things bear record of me' in verse 63 primarily interpret the birth analogy, or does it invite a broader sacramental reading of creation?

What changes between the audience being offended in verse 37 and trembling in verse 47?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the genealogical framework with [[Genesis 5]], marking every addition about prophecy, teaching, migration, violence, and records. Compare Enoch's call with Exodus 4:10–12 and the choice formula with Joshua 24:15. Read the Fall teaching beside [[2 Nephi 2]], especially its treatment of death, agency, opposition, and human existence. Then continue into [[Moses 7]] to see how the hesitant preacher of Moses 6 becomes a visionary and builder of [[Zion]].
