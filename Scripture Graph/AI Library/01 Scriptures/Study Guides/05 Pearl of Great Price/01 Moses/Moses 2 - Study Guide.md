# Moses 2 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Moses 2]] · My notes: [[Moses 2 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Moses 2 presents [[Creation]] as ordered divine activity accomplished through God's word. Its opening frame is important: this is not an impersonal narrator but a revelation addressed to Moses, who is commanded to write it (verse 1). The chapter moves from an unformed and dark earth to differentiated realms, living creatures, and finally humanity. Repeated declarations that the works are good culminate in the judgment that the completed whole is “very good” (verse 31).

The account also gives the Only Begotten a prominent role. God says that he created through his Only Begotten (verse 1), addresses the Only Begotten as one who was with him from the beginning (verse 26), and connects humanity's image with both God and the Only Begotten (verse 27). These statements make the chapter especially relevant to [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] and [[The Godhead]], while not supplying a complete systematic theology of either subject.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The six days form a cumulative sequence:

1. Light is divided from darkness (verses 3–5).
2. The firmament divides waters above from waters below (verses 6–8).
3. Sea and dry land are distinguished, and vegetation appears (verses 9–13).
4. Celestial lights govern day, night, seasons, days, and years (verses 14–19).
5. Water and sky are populated (verses 20–23).
6. Land animals and humanity are created, followed by provisions of food and the verdict “very good” (verses 24–31).

Days 1–3 can thus be read as establishing realms and days 4–6 as supplying their governors or inhabitants. This is a useful literary description, not proof that every feature belongs to a rigidly symmetrical scheme.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Human distinctiveness is expressed through being made in the divine image — explicitly including both male and female (2:26-27) — and through a delegated grant of dominion over the earth and its creatures (2:26, 28). This dominion is given alongside repeated blessing and multiply/replenish commands, not "prior to" any commandment. Reading dominion as accountable stewardship rather than unlimited use is a plausible ethical inference consistent with the repeated "good" evaluations of nonhuman creation, but the chapter itself never uses the word "stewardship" or otherwise limits the term "dominion."

The chapter also gives the Only Begotten — [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)|Jesus Christ]] in His premortal state — a prominent role: God says He created "by" the Only Begotten (2:1), addresses the Only Begotten as one who "was with me from the beginning" (2:26), and links humanity's image to both God and the Only Begotten (2:27). These statements are genuine textual additions absent from the KJV Genesis 1 parallel, though the chapter does not spell out a complete account of the Father-Son relationship.

The food provisions given to humans and animals (2:29-30) are exclusively seed-bearing plants, fruit, and clean herbs; the text does not explicitly state that no other food was ever eaten, so this should be read as a description of what is given here, not a universal or permanently binding prohibition.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter's characteristic formulas—“I, God, said,” “it was so,” “good,” and “the evening and the morning”—create rhythm and stress divine command, fulfillment, evaluation, and temporal order. Naming also marks ordered differentiation: God calls the light Day, the darkness Night, the firmament Heaven, the dry land Earth, and the gathered waters Sea.

Comparison with [[Genesis 1]] and [[Abraham 4]] is indispensable. Their extensive correspondence establishes a literary relationship, but competing explanations of that relationship must remain open. Verbal similarity alone cannot determine whether Moses 2 is restoration, inspired revision, direct textual adaptation, reliance on a shared tradition, or some combination of these.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should “day” be understood here: as an ordinary day, a literary unit, a revelatory presentation, or some combination? Moses 2 marks each day with evening-and-morning language but does not address modern scientific timescales.

What does creation in the divine image entail? The immediate context associates it with male and female humanity and delegated dominion, but it does not explicitly define whether the image is physical, relational, vocational, moral, or inclusive of several dimensions.

Why is light introduced before the sun, moon, and stars? The sequence is unmistakable, but the chapter does not explain the physical mechanism or settle whether the ordering is meant scientifically, symbolically, liturgically, or literarily.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Genesis 1]], Moses 2, and [[Abraham 4]] side by side, marking shared wording and genuine differences. Then continue into [[Moses 3]], noting how the broad six-day account is followed by the seventh day and a more localized treatment of humanity. Attention to what each text actually says is more reliable than assuming that every difference automatically proves either ancient independence or modern dependence.
