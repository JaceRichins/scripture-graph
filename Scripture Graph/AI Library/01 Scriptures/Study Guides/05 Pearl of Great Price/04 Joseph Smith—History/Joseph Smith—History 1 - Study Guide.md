# Joseph Smith—History 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] · My notes: [[Joseph Smith—History 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Joseph Smith—History 1 identifies itself as a retrospective first-person history written in an 1838 context to answer hostile reports and present facts available to the narrator. It moves from family background and religious conflict through the reported [[First Vision]], Moroni's visits, the plates and translation, and the reported restoration of the [[Aaronic Priesthood]]. A central narrative movement runs from uncertainty to claimed revelation and then to commissioned action.

The chapter is strong primary evidence for how Joseph Smith framed these events at the time of composition. That source status does not independently verify the historical occurrence or supernatural interpretation of the visions, plates, angelic messages, or priesthood conferrals.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful outline is:

- Verses 1–4: purpose, date, and family background.
- Verses 5–13: denominational conflict, uncertainty, and [[James 1]].
- Verses 14–26: the reported First Vision and Joseph's defense of his testimony.
- Verses 27–35: repentance, Moroni, and announcement of the plates.
- Verses 36–49: prophetic texts and four appearances of Moroni.
- Verses 50–60: the plate repository, annual instruction, receipt, and protection of the objects.
- Verses 61–67: relocation, early translation, Martin Harris, and Oliver Cowdery.
- Verses 68–75: inquiry about [[Baptism]], restoration of priesthood authority, baptism, spiritual gifts, and persecution.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Prayer]] as action taken amid limited knowledge. Joseph reads [[James 1]], reflects, decides, and prays in verses 11–17; seeks forgiveness and assurance in verses 28–33; and joins Oliver Cowdery in prayer about baptism in verse 68. Within the narrative, revelation responds to concrete moral and doctrinal questions, though this sequence should not be treated as a guaranteed universal formula.

Verse 17 portrays the Father and Son as distinguishable, making the passage relevant to [[The Godhead]] without supplying every later doctrinal formulation. Verses 69–72 assign the [[Aaronic Priesthood]] the keys of ministering of angels, the gospel of repentance, and baptism by immersion for remission of sins, while expressly withholding the power of laying on hands for the gift of the Holy Ghost. A later conferral of the [[Melchizedek Priesthood]] is anticipated but not narrated here.

Repentance also frames Moroni's commission: Joseph acknowledges weakness, seeks forgiveness, and reports being warned not to pursue the plates for wealth. Calling is therefore portrayed as conditional upon motive and obedience.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The account repeatedly uses first-person terms of cognition and perception: Joseph says he reflected, concluded, saw, heard, knew, and remembered. These expressions communicate claimed personal certainty, especially in verses 24–25, but personal certainty and independent verification remain different evidentiary categories.

The narrator also marks some limitations and source layers. Verse 1 limits the stated scope to facts in his possession; verse 20 introduces one inference with “It seems as though”; verses 20 and 41 acknowledge omitted material; and verse 63 identifies the Anthon episode as Martin Harris's report related afterward to Joseph. These signals enable source-sensitive reading but do not establish systematic source criticism or guarantee accuracy.

Contrasts including confusion and wisdom, darkness and light, weakness and power, poverty and sacred motive, persecution and testimony, and obscure scripture and opened understanding help organize the prose.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Repetition organizes the Moroni narrative: the messenger appears three times during the night and once the following day, repeating the message while adding warnings. The pattern emphasizes confirmation, retention, and preparation within the story, but literary repetition cannot demonstrate that each delivery occurred verbatim.

Two prayer cycles also create continuity. Religious confusion leads to prayer, opposition, light, a heavenly answer, and changed action in 1820; concern about sin leads to prayer, increasing light, a messenger, a commission, and prolonged preparation in 1823. The resemblance is literary and thematic rather than identity of experience.

Joseph's explicit comparison with Paul in [[Acts 26]] gives contested testimony a scriptural model: a claimed witness persists in affirming what he saw and heard despite rejection. Moroni's reported use of [[Malachi 4]], [[Isaiah 11]], [[Acts 3]], and [[Joel 2]] places the commission amid themes of judgment, covenant, Christ, spiritual outpouring, and approaching fulfillment. Treating these texts as one fully unified program remains an interpretation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Embedded Secondhand Testimony
> The Anthon episode is explicitly embedded secondhand testimony: Joseph introduces it as Martin Harris's later account, and Harris's speech supplies Anthon's alleged statements and actions. The chapter therefore directly establishes what Joseph says Harris reported, not by itself what Anthon actually said.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · source layer textually explicit · [[Embedded Secondhand Testimony in Joseph Smith—History 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Source Date And Proximity
> Because the chapter identifies itself as an 1838 history while recounting experiences beginning decades earlier, it is strong primary evidence for Joseph Smith's public account and self-understanding in 1838, but it is not a contemporaneous record of the earliest narrated events.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · source distinction broadly accepted · [[Source Date And Proximity in Joseph Smith—History 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Variation And Scriptural Interpretation *(tentative)*
> The narrator expressly notes that Moroni's wording of Malachi differed from the Bible available to him. The reported substitutions concerning priesthood, promises made to the fathers, and the agents who burn the wicked support reading the quotation as an interpretive adaptation rather than accidental verbatim repetition.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · variation certain; intention and origin disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Which portions are narrated as Joseph's direct experience, which are his interpretations, and which are reports received from others?
2. How might the stated 1838 corrective purpose shape the selection, omissions, and rhetoric of the account?
3. Why does the narrative report John conferring priesthood and then Joseph and Oliver ordaining one another after baptism?
4. How do Moroni's reported changes to [[Malachi 4]] alter the passage's emphasis, and what can or cannot be inferred about the origin of those changes?
5. How does the warning against seeking wealth interact with family poverty and the earlier silver-mine employment?
6. What can names, dates, places, and source attributions potentially corroborate, and which supernatural conclusions remain beyond their evidentiary reach?
7. How does the narrative associate [[Priesthood]], [[Ordinances]], and spiritual manifestations without necessarily establishing a universal sequence?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Joseph's analogy in verses 24–25 with Paul's defense in [[Acts 26]]. Compare verses 38–39 with [[Malachi 4]] and [[D&C 2]], identifying substitutions, additions, and omissions before assigning theological intention. Compare the priesthood declaration in verse 69 with [[D&C 13]].

Track source proximity throughout the chapter: Joseph narrates some experiences as his own, quotes messages remembered years later, and embeds Martin Harris's account in verses 63–65. These distinctions help prevent a reported claim, a narrator's interpretation, a textual parallel, and independent evidence from being treated as equivalent. Textual parallels can illuminate the chapter's place within [[Restoration of the Gospel]], but they cannot by themselves establish historicity or supernatural origin.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
