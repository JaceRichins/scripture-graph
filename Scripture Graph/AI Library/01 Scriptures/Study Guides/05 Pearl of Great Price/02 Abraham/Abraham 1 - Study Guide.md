# Abraham 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Abraham 1]] · My notes: [[Abraham 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
[[Abraham 1]] begins with Abraham recognizing the need for another residence while seeking greater righteousness, knowledge, peace, instruction, and the blessings of the fathers. His quest contrasts with the idolatry and violence attributed to his family and its priests. At the narrative center, Abraham prays while threatened with sacrifice; God hears him, an angel releases him, and Jehovah promises deliverance, [[Priesthood]], power, guidance, and future ministry. The chapter then discusses Egyptian descent, government, and competing priesthood claims before returning to famine, Abraham's father's limited repentance, and the preservation of [[Records and Sacred Writings]]. The chapter promises Abraham's removal but does not yet narrate the completed departure.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. Verses 1–4: Abraham's desired residence, character, learning, and priesthood quest.
2. Verses 5–14: Family apostasy, human sacrifice, danger to Abraham, and explanation of the accompanying figures.
3. Verses 15–20: Prayer, angelic release, divine commission, and judgment of the altar and priest.
4. Verses 21–28: An excursus on Egyptian descent, government, and priesthood claims.
5. Verses 29–31: Famine, Abraham's father's repentance concerning the murder attempt, and Abraham's purpose in preserving and writing ancestral knowledge.

Verses 15–20 form the main narrative pivot from threatened death to deliverance and commission. Verse 29 then resumes the Chaldean storyline after the Egyptian excursus.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Within the chapter's theological framework, [[Priesthood]] involves divine right, appointment, inherited responsibility, God's name, and power for ministry. Personal righteousness and civil competence are not treated as identical with priesthood authorization: Pharaoh is praised as righteous, wise, and just while his lineage is said to lack the priesthood right. The passage reports these lineage claims as part of its narrative; responsible study should not silently convert them into unrestricted modern principles.

The deliverance account connects [[Prayer]] with divine hearing and rescue, but the deaths of the daughters of Onitah prevent reading Abraham's experience as an automatic promise of immediate physical protection for every faithful person. Abraham's rescue is also joined to future service.

Abraham 1:21–27 contains lineage, curse, and priesthood language but does not mention skin color, premortal unworthiness, or racial inferiority. Current official Church teaching disavows theories that black skin signifies divine disfavor or that any race is inferior. That disavowal governs responsible present application while leaving historical questions about the passage and its reception open.

Verse 30 likewise requires precision about [[Repentance]]: it says Abraham's father repented of intending to kill him, not that he permanently abandoned every form of idolatry.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter uses “father” language in several ways. “The fathers” often denotes the authoritative patriarchal chain, “my fathers” denotes Abraham's family or ancestors who turned to idolatry, and “my father” in verse 30 refers to a singular parent. This supports a literary contrast between the sacred inheritance Abraham seeks and the familial pattern from which he must separate, although the wording does not limit “my fathers” to a present household.

The terms “right,” “appointment,” “conferred,” “High Priest,” and the promise to place God's name and priesthood upon Abraham describe related aspects of authority without fully explaining their chronology. They may distinguish inheritance, divine appointment, ordination, and empowerment, but that reconciliation remains interpretive.

The narrator also supplies glosses for Shagreel, Rahleenos, Pharaoh, and Egyptus. These are observable claims made by the text, not independent verification of the proposed meanings.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter combines autobiography with commentary about the record itself. Abraham interrupts the danger narrative to direct readers to figures, later promises a chronology, and closes by explaining that he writes preserved knowledge for his posterity. The text therefore makes transmission of knowledge part of its story.

A strong contrast runs between Abraham's desire for righteousness and knowledge and his family's commitment to idolatry. Another contrast places coercive priestly violence beside divine deliverance and ministry. These are persuasive literary readings rather than formally announced structures.

Abraham 1:16 has a close literary relationship with [[Genesis 12]] through its language of kindred, father's house, and another land. Abraham 1:19 also resonates with Abraham's wider biblical mission, though it specifically promises that God's name will be known through his ministry rather than repeating the Genesis blessing verbatim. Shared tradition, inspired expansion, direct literary dependence, and restoration are competing explanations; the parallel alone cannot determine direction, antiquity, or translation method.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Text-Image Integration
> Verses 12–14 explicitly direct the reader to figures placed at the beginning of the record and describe those figures as supplying visual information about the altar and gods. This is strong internal evidence that the narrative presents its image and prose as an integrated record.

> [!evidence] Intertextual Parallel
> Abraham 1:16 and Genesis 12:1 share a conspicuous cluster of ideas and language: leaving country or kindred and the father's house for a land not yet known or shown. Abraham 1:19 and Genesis 12:2–3 then associate Abraham's calling with a worldwide or multinational blessing. This strongly supports a deliberate literary or traditional relationship between the passages, but the textual parallel alone cannot determine the direction or historical origin of that relationship.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How do the priesthood expressions in verses 2–4 and 18 relate: inheritance, conferral, appointment, ordination, receiving God's name, and divine power?
2. Why does verse 29 refer to a famine prediction not reproduced in the preceding direct speech?
3. Why does the narrator pause after Abraham's deliverance to discuss Egypt's genealogy, government, and priesthood claims?
4. How should the deaths of the daughters of Onitah qualify readings of Abraham's rescue?
5. What does the favorable description of Pharaoh contribute to the distinction between moral governance and priesthood authorization?
6. How does Abraham's father's renewed idolatry in [[Abraham 2]] qualify the repentance reported in Abraham 1:30?
7. Which statements are internal narrative assertions, and which presently have independent historical or linguistic corroboration?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Abraham 1:16 and 19 with [[Genesis 12]], noting both the close departure language and the differences between worldwide knowledge of God's name and the Genesis blessing. Compare the famine passages as well, while observing that only Genesis explicitly makes famine the cause of travel. Then read [[Abraham 2]] for Abraham's father's renewed idolatry and the fuller statement of the [[Abrahamic Covenant]]. Follow Abraham 1:28 and 31 into later discussions of [[Records and Sacred Writings]], [[Creation]], and heavenly knowledge. Literary relationships should not be treated by themselves as proof of direction, antiquity, or translation method.
