## Overview

> Jesus again heals on the Sabbath—He teaches humility and gives the parable of the great supper—Those who follow Him must forsake all else.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter falls into four movements, joined mainly by setting and theme:

1. **Sabbath healing and legal defense** (14:1-6) — narrative controversy resolved by an appeal to ordinary practice (rescuing an animal from a pit).
2. **Banquet etiquette: seats and guest lists** (14:7-14) — two short teachings on humility and generosity, given at the same meal.
3. **Parable of the great supper** (14:15-24) — triggered by a guest's beatitude about the kingdom of God.
4. **Cost of discipleship sayings** (14:25-35) — addressed to the 'great multitudes' on the road rather than the dinner guests, closing with the salt saying and the 'ears to hear' formula also used at the close of parable teaching in [[Luke 8]] and elsewhere.

The first three sections are unified by the meal setting; the fourth marks a real change of audience and scene. Both halves share a concern with what people are, and are not, willing to give up, but the text does not by itself prove the chapter was assembled as a single deliberate literary unit rather than a chronological sequence of related teachings.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Sabbath as a day for mercy**: the healing of the man with dropsy, defended by the ass-or-ox-in-a-pit argument, presents compassionate action as consistent with, not opposed to, the Sabbath's purpose (14:1-6). See [[Sabbath Day]].
- **Humility precedes exaltation**: 'whosoever exalteth himself shall be abased; and he that humbleth himself shall be exalted' (14:11) is stated as a general principle, not limited to banquet seating, and recurs almost verbatim in [[Luke 18]]. See [[Humility]].
- **Hospitality without expectation of return**: hospitality extended to those who cannot repay (the poor, maimed, lame, blind) is tied to a future reward 'at the resurrection of the just' (14:12-14) rather than earthly reciprocity. See [[Resurrection]].
- **Comprehensive sacrifice as a condition of discipleship**: prioritizing Christ above family (14:26), bearing one's cross (14:27), and forsaking all one has (14:33) present discipleship as costly and total, though the text does not specify one identical practical disposal of possessions expected of every disciple. See [[Sacrifice]].
- **Deliberation before commitment**: the tower-builder and warring-king parables commend honestly assessing willingness to see a costly commitment through (14:28-32), without reducing discipleship to a guarantee of personal sufficiency.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Luke 14 is held together by a hospitality/meal motif running through its first three sections before pivoting to direct address of a traveling crowd. It contains a dense run of parables and parable-like sayings — the wedding seats, the guest list, the great supper, and the paired tower/war illustrations. Several elements are mechanically confirmed as recurring across the Synoptics rather than unique to Luke: the ass-or-ox-in-a-pit argument (14:5) parallels the sheep-in-a-pit argument in Matthew 12:11; the salt saying (14:34-35) parallels Mark 9:50 and Matthew 5:13; and the closing 'he that hath ears to hear, let him hear' formula (14:35) recurs in [[Luke 8]] (8:8), Matthew 11:15, Matthew 13:9, and Mark 4. These parallels show Luke drawing on well-attested traditional material, though the exact literary relationship (shared source, independent use, or authorial arrangement) is not something this chapter alone can settle. The threefold excuses in the great-supper parable (a field, oxen, a marriage) form a rhetorical rule-of-three that heightens the absurdity of refusing an urgent invitation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What is the relationship between the healing/Sabbath controversy (14:1-6) and the teachings that follow it at the same meal—are they connected by more than setting?
- Luke 14:26 says a disciple must 'hate' father, mother, wife, and children. How does this square with other scriptural commands to honor and care for family, and what does the idiom likely mean in context?
- In the parable of the great supper, who are the 'poor, maimed, halt, and blind' gathered first, and who are those found later in 'the highways and hedges'—are these meant as distinct groups?
- Why does Jesus tie the reward for feeding the poor specifically to 'the resurrection of the just' rather than to blessings in this life?
- What does it mean, practically, to 'forsake all' (14:33) in a way consistent with the more measured, deliberate 'counting the cost' Jesus just commended?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Luke 14:11 with [[Luke 18]] (parable of the Pharisee and the publican) for Luke's repeated use of the exaltation/humility maxim. Compare the salt saying (14:34-35) with Matthew 5:13 and Mark 9:50 to see how the same saying functions differently in each Gospel's literary context. For the Sabbath-healing debate, compare Luke 13:10-17 (an earlier, closely related Sabbath healing and defense in Luke) and Matthew 12:9-14.
