# Luke 7 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 7]] · My notes: [[Luke 7 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus heals the centurion’s servant—Jesus raises from death the son of the widow of Nain—He praises John the Baptist as more than a prophet—A woman anoints Jesus’ feet, and He forgives her sins.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Authority trusted (7:1–10):** a centurion trusts that Jesus can heal by a word.
2. **Compassion enacted (7:11–17):** Jesus raises the widow's only son at Nain.
3. **Identity tested (7:18–35):** Jesus answers John through observable works, identifies John as the promised messenger, and criticizes a generation that rejects both John's austerity and Jesus' table fellowship.
4. **Forgiveness disclosed (7:36–50):** the parable of two debtors reframes Simon's judgment of the woman and culminates in forgiveness, salvation, and peace.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter links [[Faith]] with confidence in Jesus' effective authority (7:7–10) and with salvation and peace (7:50). At Nain, Jesus' power is joined to unrequested compassion for a bereaved widow (7:11–15). His answer to John points to observable works and proclamation that resemble prophetic hopes of divine restoration (7:21–22), without turning the catalog into an exhaustive proof of every conclusion about his identity. In the final scene, both debtors are unable to pay and both are freely forgiven; the parable therefore favors understanding abundant love as a response to forgiveness. Verse 47's wording nevertheless leaves the precise relationship between love and forgiveness debated. These passages support study of [[Grace]], [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], and [[Justice and Mercy]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two verbal patterns sharpen the chapter. First, the elders call the centurion “worthy,” but he calls himself “not worthy” (7:4, 6–7); public honor and humble self-assessment are placed in tension. Second, the people and publicans “justified God,” while wisdom is “justified” by her children (7:29, 35). The repeated verb connects divine wisdom with the responses that vindicate it. The debt imagery of 7:41–43 also makes forgiveness concrete: neither debtor has the resources to erase the obligation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The Nain account substantially echoes Elijah's raising of a widow's son in [[1 Kings 17]], especially the restoration and delivery of the son to his mother. [[2 Kings 4]], where Elisha restores the Shunammite woman's son, belongs to the same relevant prophetic field, though its details are less exact in some respects. These parallels support reading the crowd's cry that 'a great prophet' has arisen within Israel's prophetic tradition; they do not prove direct literary dependence or a detailed one-to-one typology.

Jesus' answer to John draws on restoration language associated especially with [[Isaiah 35]] and [[Isaiah 61]], while Luke 7:27 applies a scriptural messenger saying associated with [[Malachi 3]] to John, possibly in wording also influenced by Exodus 23:20. In the final scene, Simon's private test of Jesus' prophetic perception becomes ironic when Jesus answers him and redirects his judgment. The two-debtors parable then supplies an interpretive key for the contrast between Simon's limited welcome and the woman's abundant love.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Intertext
> The raising at Nain closely recalls Elijah's restoration of a widow's son: both accounts concern a bereaved widow, the restoration of her son, and the prophet delivering the living son to his mother. This is substantial literary evidence that Luke presents Jesus in continuity with, and potentially as surpassing, Israel's prophetic tradition.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strong literary echo; intentional typology disputed · [[Literary Intertext in Luke 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Scriptural Intertext
> Jesus' report to John combines signs associated with Isaiah's restoration hopes, especially sight for the blind, hearing for the deaf, mobility for the lame, and good news for the poor. This strongly supports the interpretation that Jesus frames his works as signs of eschatological divine restoration.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted intertextual interpretation · [[Scriptural Intertext in Luke 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary_Parallel *(tentative)*
> The Nain narrative (a widow's only son restored to life, then 'delivered... to his mother') parallels the structure of Elijah raising the widow of Zarephath's son and Elisha raising the Shunammite woman's son in the Old Testament, and the crowd's response identifies Jesus within that prophetic pattern.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · Elijah echo strong; combined Elijah-Elisha typology disputed

> [!evidence] Harmonization_Question *(tentative)*
> This anointing at the house of a Pharisee named Simon (Luke 7:36-50) shares surface features — an alabaster box of ointment, feet/head anointing, a host named Simon — with the anointing accounts placed near the Passion (e.g., in a house at Bethany involving 'Simon,' shortly before the crucifixion, in the other gospels). However, Luke's episode differs in narrative placement (early Galilean ministry vs. final week), the woman's characterization (a known 'sinner' vs. an unnamed or named devoted disciple), and its point (forgiveness of sins vs. anointing for burial). Whether these are the same incident recalled differently or genuinely distinct episodes is disputed.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · harmonization question genuinely disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does John ask whether Jesus is the expected one (7:19)? The text records the question but does not specify whether it arose from doubt, a desire for confirmation, unmet expectations, or instruction for John's disciples.
- In what sense is the least in the [[Kingdom of God]] greater than John (7:28)? Possible readings involve covenantal position, participation in the inaugurated kingdom, or rhetorical paradox; the verse does not explicitly choose among them.
- Does “for she loved much” make love the cause of forgiveness or its evidence (7:47)? The preceding parable strongly favors love as the response to forgiven debt, but the wording has generated genuine interpretive debate.
- What does Simon see when Jesus asks, “Seest thou this woman?” (7:44)? The narrative contrasts knowing her social label with perceiving her faith, love, and need for mercy.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the centurion narratives in [[Luke 7]] and [[Matthew 8]], paying attention to Luke's use of Jewish elders and friends as intermediaries. Read Luke 7:18–35 beside [[Matthew 11]] to identify shared wording and differences in presentation. Study the Nain episode with [[1 Kings 17]] and [[2 Kings 4]], distinguishing close verbal correspondences from broader prophetic motifs. Compare Jesus' report to John with [[Isaiah 35]] and [[Isaiah 61]], and compare the messenger saying with [[Malachi 3]] and Exodus 23:20. Finally, examine the anointing accounts in [[Matthew 26]], [[Mark 14]], and [[John 12]] feature by feature; their similarities merit comparison, but the texts do not settle whether Luke narrates the same event.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
