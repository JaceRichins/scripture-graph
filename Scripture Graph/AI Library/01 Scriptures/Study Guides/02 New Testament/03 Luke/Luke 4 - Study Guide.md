# Luke 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 4]] · My notes: [[Luke 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus fasts forty days and is tempted by the devil—Jesus announces His divine sonship in Nazareth and is rejected—He casts out a devil in Capernaum, heals Peter’s mother-in-law, and preaches and heals throughout Galilee.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Testing in the wilderness (4:1–13):** the Spirit leads Jesus; the devil tests him; Jesus answers from scripture.
2. **Programmatic declaration and rejection at Nazareth (4:14–30):** Jesus reads from Isaiah, declares fulfillment, invokes rejected prophets, and escapes an attempt on his life.
3. **Authoritative ministry at Capernaum (4:31–41):** teaching, exorcism, and healing display the authority of his word.
4. **Mission beyond one city (4:42–44):** Jesus withdraws, resists efforts to retain him, and continues preaching throughout Galilee.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The devil twice frames temptation as a direct challenge to Jesus's identity ('If thou be the Son of God,' vv. 3, 9); Jesus refuses to prove sonship through self-serving or spectacular acts, relying instead on scripture (vv. 4, 8, 12). The devil's own citation of [[Psalm 91]] shows that scriptural quotation alone is not sufficient — how and why it is used matters. See [[Scripture Study]].

The Spirit leads and empowers Jesus throughout the chapter's opening movement (vv. 1, 14, 18), and in Nazareth he declares Isaiah's promise of a Spirit-anointed, liberating mission fulfilled 'this day... in your ears' (v. 21) — present, inaugurated fulfillment rather than a future hope. See [[Prophecy]].

Jesus's examples of the widow of Sarepta and Naaman the Syrian (vv. 25-27) challenge any assumption that being a prophet's own people guarantees privileged access to God's blessing. They plausibly anticipate a mission reaching beyond Israel, though this chapter alone does not state a full doctrine of Gentile inclusion. See [[Jews and Gentiles]].

Jesus's authoritative word governs an unclean spirit, a fever, and 'divers diseases' alike (vv. 35-40); this shared language of command should not be read as implying all illness is demonic, only that his authority extends to both spiritual and physical affliction. See [[Miracles]].

Jesus resists the Capernaum crowd's wish to keep him in one place, framing his continued travel through Galilee as fulfillment of being sent to 'preach the kingdom of God to other cities also' (v. 43). See [[Missionary Work]] and [[Kingdom of God]].

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Each of Jesus's three replies to the devil (vv. 4, 8, 12) is drawn from Deuteronomy 6-8, texts that themselves recount Israel's wilderness testing — inviting a reading in which Jesus, tested forty days as Israel was tested forty years, succeeds where Israel failed. This typology is not stated explicitly by the narrator, but is a strong cumulative pattern.

The chapter also draws two contrasts. First, the devil repeatedly conditions his challenges on Jesus's sonship ('If thou be the Son of God,' vv. 3, 9), while Jesus refuses to prove that identity on the devil's terms. Second, the Nazareth congregation identifies Jesus merely as 'Joseph's son' and rejects him, while the unclean spirits correctly identify him as 'the Holy One of God' and 'Christ the Son of God' — yet Jesus silences them too. Neither human familiarity nor accurate supernatural testimony is treated as sufficient recognition.

In quoting [[Psalm 91]] to support the temple-pinnacle test, the devil cites the promise of angelic protection while omitting part of the psalm's surrounding language; Jesus does not dispute the citation but answers with a deeper principle (v. 12) — a reminder that scripture can be selectively invoked to argue for presumption rather than faith.

The Nazareth declaration also functions programmatically: language about liberating good news (vv. 18-19) is followed by teaching, exorcism, and healing throughout the rest of the chapter — suggestive of deliberate literary design, though this does not by itself establish the historical order of events.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Jesus answer the devil's temptations with passages from Israel's wilderness experience in [[Deuteronomy 6]] and [[Deuteronomy 8]]?
- What distinguishes Jesus's use of scripture from the devil's use of [[Psalm 91]]?
- Why does Luke order the three temptations differently than [[Matthew 4]] does?
- What turns the Nazareth congregation's initial wonder at Jesus's words into murderous wrath?
- How far do the examples of the widow of Sarepta and Naaman support a Gentile-mission reading, and where would that exceed what Luke 4 explicitly says?
- Why does Jesus silence declarations of his identity from unclean spirits, even when accurate?
- What does Jesus's departure from a receptive crowd imply about the scope of [[Missionary Work]] and his sense of calling?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the temptation accounts in [[Matthew 4]] and [[Mark 1]], noting both shared material and differences in order and detail. Read [[Deuteronomy 6]], [[Deuteronomy 8]], and [[Psalm 91]] to examine the original settings of the passages used during the temptations. Compare the Nazareth reading with [[Isaiah 58]] and [[Isaiah 61]], then examine the underlying narratives in [[1 Kings 17]] and [[2 Kings 5]].
