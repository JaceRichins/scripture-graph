# Luke 6 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus heals on the Sabbath—He chooses the Twelve Apostles—He pronounces blessings upon the obedient and woes upon the wicked.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Two Sabbath controversies (6:1–11):** David and the shewbread; healing the withered hand.
2. **Prayer and appointment (6:12–16):** Jesus prays all night and chooses the Twelve.
3. **Crowds, healing, and instruction (6:17–19):** disciples and a geographically diverse multitude gather.
4. **Blessings and woes (6:20–26):** four paired reversals contrast present condition with future outcome.
5. **Merciful love beyond reciprocity (6:27–38):** love, blessing, prayer, giving, forgiveness, and measured judgment.
6. **Images of authentic discipleship (6:39–49):** blind guides, beam and mote, trees and fruit, and houses with or without foundations.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's ethical center is the imitation of divine mercy. Enemy-love is not treated as sentiment alone: it becomes doing good, blessing, praying, giving, and lending without calculated return (6:27–36). This connects [[Charity]] with [[Justice and Mercy]].

Luke also binds confession to [[Obedience]]. Calling Jesus “Lord” and hearing his words are inadequate without doing them (6:46–49). The firm foundation is therefore not information alone but enacted discipleship.

The blessing of the poor should be read alongside the corresponding woe to the rich (6:20, 24). The text challenges confidence founded on present abundance, but it does not state that poverty by itself automatically produces righteousness or that every wealthy person is condemned irrespective of conduct.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The temporal words “now” and “shall” organize much of 6:20–26: present hunger contrasts with future filling, present weeping with future laughter, and present laughter with future mourning. The first pair is asymmetrical in another way: the poor are told that the kingdom is theirs, while the rich are told that they have already received their consolation.

The repeated question “what thank have ye?” in 6:32–34 exposes ordinary reciprocity as insufficient. The following commands move discipleship beyond exchange toward generosity patterned on God's kindness.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly uses paired contrasts: lawful/unlawful, good/evil, save/destroy, blessings/woes, prophets/false prophets, mercy/judgment, good/corrupt trees, and founded/unfounded houses. These contrasts make the sermon cumulative rather than a loose anthology of sayings.

The ending is especially unified. The heart produces speech as a tree produces fruit (6:43–45); speech that calls Jesus “Lord” must then become action (6:46); and action determines whether the hearer's house survives the flood (6:47–49).

Comparison with [[Matthew 5]], [[Matthew 7]], and [[3 Nephi 14]] reveals substantial shared material but also preserves Luke's distinctive arrangement: a level-place setting, direct second-person blessings, matching woes, and the explicit command to imitate the Father's mercy.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Synoptic Sequence Parallel
> The placement of the grain-field controversy beside the healing of the withered hand closely parallels Mark and Matthew. The shared pairing strongly supports a common narrative tradition, though it does not by itself prove direct dependence on a particular written Gospel.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Sequence accepted; source relationship disputed · [[Synoptic Sequence Parallel in Luke 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual And Literary Parallel
> Substantial correspondences connect Luke's sermon with the Sermon on the Mount and, through its close relationship to Matthew, the Sermon at the Temple: beatitudes, enemy-love, judgment and the beam, fruit imagery, and the two builders occur in comparable clusters. This is strong evidence of a shared Jesus-tradition or literary relationship, but the comparison alone does not determine direction of dependence.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Correspondence accepted; dependence disputed · [[Textual And Literary Parallel in Luke 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Harmonization *(tentative)*
> Luke names an apostle "Judas the brother of James" (6:16) where Matthew 10:3 and Mark 3:18 list "Thaddaeus" (or Lebbaeus) in the corresponding slot; harmonizing these as the same person is a common but not textually certain identification.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · List difference accepted; identification disputed

> [!evidence] Historical-Terminological *(tentative)*
> Simon is called "Zelotes" (6:15); whether this designates formal membership in the militant Zealot political movement (which most historians date as coalescing around the Jewish revolt of AD 66-70) or simply describes personal zeal/fervor prior to any such formal party is disputed among historians.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · Epithet accepted; political affiliation disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How does Jesus' good/evil and save/destroy question change the terms of the Sabbath controversy?
2. What does the pairing of blessings with woes add that would be missed by reading the blessings alone?
3. Which enemy-love commands concern inward disposition, and which require observable action?
4. How does the beam-and-mote saying distinguish self-righteous correction from responsible help?
5. Why does the sermon end with hearing and doing rather than with hearing alone?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the paired Sabbath narratives with [[Mark 2]], [[Mark 3]], and [[Matthew 12]]. Then compare the sermon with [[Matthew 5]], [[Matthew 7]], and [[3 Nephi 14]], noting both overlap and difference before attempting harmonization. Luke 22:28–30 is also relevant to the possible Israel symbolism of the Twelve.
