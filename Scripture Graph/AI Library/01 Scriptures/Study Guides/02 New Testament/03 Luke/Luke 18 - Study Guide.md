# Luke 18 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 18]] · My notes: [[Luke 18 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus gives the parables of the unjust judge and the Pharisee and publican—He invites little children to come unto Him and teaches how to gain eternal life—He tells of His coming death and resurrection and gives sight to a blind man.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Persistent prayer and promised justice (18:1-8):** a widow continues appealing to an unjust judge; Jesus contrasts that judge with God's response to the elect.
2. **Two prayers and reversed evaluation (18:9-14):** a self-confident Pharisee and a penitent publican pray at the temple, but the publican returns justified.
3. **Receiving the kingdom and confronting wealth (18:15-30):** Jesus receives children, challenges a wealthy ruler, describes riches as a severe obstacle, and locates salvation's possibility with God.
4. **The road to Jerusalem (18:31-34):** Jesus predicts suffering, death, and [[Resurrection]], while the Twelve do not understand.
5. **Sight and discipleship near Jericho (18:35-43):** a blind man persists in seeking mercy, receives sight, follows Jesus, and prompts public praise of God.

Possible links among these units—humble dependence, persistent petition, and the contrast between incomprehension and sight—are suggestive literary readings rather than explicit structural claims.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter teaches persistent [[Prayer]] (18:1-8), rejects self-trust joined to contempt for others (18:9-14), and associates justification with confessed need for mercy and [[Humility]] (18:13-14). It presents childlike reception as necessary for entering the [[Kingdom of God]] without specifying whether the decisive quality is dependence, low status, receptivity, or some combination (18:16-17).

The ruler's obedience is not mocked, but his response shows that wealth obstructs his willingness to follow Jesus (18:18-27). Jesus' command to sell everything directly addresses this ruler; whether every disciple receives the identical command cannot be settled from this episode alone. The disciples' sacrifices are followed by a promise of present recompense and future eternal life, though the form of the present reward is not defined (18:28-30).

Jesus presents his coming suffering, death, and resurrection as the accomplishment of prophetic writings (18:31-33). In the final healing, the man's [[Faith]] is recognized as he receives sight, but faith should not be treated as an independent power that performs the miracle apart from Jesus (18:42-43).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Luke supplies explicit narratorial frames for both opening parables (18:1, 9). These frames strongly guide application without exhausting every implication. The temple prayers contrast comparative self-commendation with a brief plea for mercy (18:11-13); the target is self-trust and contempt, not the practices of [[Fasting]] or [[Tithing]] themselves.

Several juxtapositions invite further interpretation. Jesus speaks of **receiving** the kingdom like a child immediately before the ruler asks what he must **do** to inherit eternal life (18:17-18). The Twelve's incomprehension is followed by a blind man who recognizes Jesus as Son of David, receives sight, and follows him (18:34-43). The widow's continued petition also resembles the blind man's repeated cry despite opposition (18:1-8, 38-39). These are credible thematic comparisons, but inherited sequence or geographic itinerary may also explain the arrangement.

The Synoptic accounts preserve a real narrative variation near Jericho: Luke describes Jesus approaching the city, while [[Mark 10]] and [[Matthew 20]] describe departure; Mark names Bartimaeus, Matthew has two blind men, and Luke leaves one man unnamed. These differences should remain visible. They do not by themselves prove either a historical contradiction or a proposed harmonization.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Synoptic Literary Convergence
> Substantial agreement among Luke, Mark, and Matthew on the ruler episode, passion prediction, and Jericho healing supports the existence of stable shared narrative traditions or literary sources behind these passages. Because direct or indirect literary dependence can explain agreement, this convergence alone is only limited evidence for independent historical corroboration.

> [!evidence] Synoptic Textual/Geographical Variant *(tentative)*
> Luke places the healing of the blind man as Jesus 'was come nigh unto Jericho' (18:35) — i.e., approaching or entering the city. The parallel accounts in Mark 10:46 and Matthew 20:29 are widely cited (their exact wording is not quoted here since it is not part of the supplied Luke text) as describing Jesus leaving or departing Jericho instead. If that recollection of the parallel wording is accurate, this is a long-noted, verifiable Synoptic wording difference about the same healing tradition.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How do Luke's stated purposes in verses 1 and 9 guide interpretation without exhausting the meanings of the two parables?
- How can persistent faith coexist with the apparent delay described in verses 7-8?
- Why is the Pharisee rejected when [[Fasting]] and [[Tithing]] are not themselves condemned?
- Which childlike quality does the context emphasize: low status, dependence, receptivity, or something else?
- Is the ruler's sorrow best explained by disbelief, divided loyalty, attachment to wealth, or the painful cost of discipleship?
- What does Jesus' appeal to divine possibility add to the question, “Who then can be saved?”
- How might the Twelve's incomprehension affect the reading of the blind man's recognition and restored sight?
- What should remain unresolved when comparing Luke's Jericho setting with [[Mark 10]] and [[Matthew 20]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare Luke 18:35-43 with [[Mark 10]] and [[Matthew 20]], distinguishing the stable shared narrative core from differences in location, naming, and number of blind men.
- Compare the Passion prediction in Luke 18:31-34 with earlier predictions in [[Luke 9]] before calling it the third or most detailed prediction.
- Compare Luke 18:14 with [[Luke 14]] to determine the exact extent of the repeated exaltation/abasement saying.
- Examine D&C 101:81-92 and the index's six reported overlaps before asserting literary dependence or a precise textual parallel.
- Study the ruler episode alongside the Decalogue while noting that Luke's ordering of the commandments differs from Exodus and Deuteronomy.
