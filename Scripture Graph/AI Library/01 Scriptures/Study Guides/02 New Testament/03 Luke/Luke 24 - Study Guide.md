## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv. 1-12** — Women find the empty tomb; heavenly messengers announce the resurrection; the apostles initially reject the report as idle tales, though Peter runs to the tomb and departs wondering.
- **vv. 13-35** — Two disciples walk to Emmaus; the risen but unrecognized Jesus expounds Moses and the prophets; he is known in the breaking of bread and vanishes.
- **vv. 36-49** — Jesus appears to the assembled Eleven, demonstrates his physical body, opens their understanding of scripture, and commissions them as witnesses.
- **vv. 50-53** — The ascension at Bethany; the disciples return to Jerusalem and worship continually in the temple.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Bodily [[Resurrection]]**: Jesus explicitly denies he is a disembodied spirit, invites the disciples to touch his hands and feet, and eats before them (vv. 39-43). This establishes what Luke's narrative affirms about embodied resurrection; the narrative alone does not independently verify the event historically, only what Luke intends readers to understand by it.
- **Christ as the fulfillment of scripture**: Jesus reads his own suffering and glory out of 'Moses... the prophets... the psalms' (vv. 27, 44), modeling a christological reading of Israel's scriptures within Luke's narrative.
- **Witnesses and the promised power**: the disciples are named witnesses (v. 48) and told to wait in Jerusalem until 'endued with power from on high' (v. 49) — a promise this chapter does not itself name as the Holy Ghost, though Acts 1 makes that identification explicit.
- **[[Repentance and Forgiveness]]** preached 'among all nations' (v. 47) frames the resurrection's purpose as launching a universal message.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The English text repeats the verb 'opened' three times over the chapter — eyes opened (v. 31), scriptures opened (v. 32), understanding opened (v. 45) — a pattern independently noticed by two separate readings of this chapter, and likely reflecting a single repeated Greek verb, which strengthens the case that Luke intended the linkage between physical sight, scriptural exposition, and inward comprehension.

Necessity language also recurs: the Son of man 'must' suffer (v. 7), Christ 'ought' to suffer and enter glory (v. 26), scripture 'must' be fulfilled (v. 44), and it 'behoved' Christ to suffer and rise (v. 46) — framing the crucifixion and resurrection as scripturally necessary rather than merely tragic.

Some commentators suggest the Greek behind 'idle tales' (v. 11) elsewhere describes delirious raving, implying the apostles' reaction was closer to contemptuous dismissal than polite skepticism — worth holding loosely, since it rests on a lexical nuance not verifiable from the KJV text alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter moves through repeated cycles of doubt before recognition, though these belong to different characters rather than one continuous journey: the apostles disbelieve the women (v. 11), the Emmaus travelers fail to recognize Jesus (vv. 16-24), and the Eleven suppose they see a spirit (v. 37) — each resolved only by direct encounter, scripture, or physical evidence.

The Emmaus episode also reverses direction: the two travelers move away from Jerusalem in sadness but, once they recognize Jesus, return the same hour (v. 33) — a geographic reversal that anticipates Jerusalem's role as the starting point for proclamation in verse 47. The bread-breaking scene (v. 30) echoes, without exactly repeating, the Last Supper's action sequence in [[Luke 22]], and the Gospel's temple-centered close (v. 53) answers its temple-centered opening in [[Luke 1]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Evidence
> As evidence of Luke's representational intent, the invitation to inspect and handle Jesus's hands and feet, the explicit reference to flesh and bones, and the eating of food strongly establish that this narrative depicts the risen Jesus as bodily rather than as a disembodied spirit. These literary details do not by themselves verify the event historically.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-disciplined, appropriately scoped to narrative intent · [[Internal Narrative Evidence in Luke 24|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Multiple Attestation Of An Early Tradition *(tentative)*
> Luke reports, almost in passing and without narrating the event itself, that the risen Lord 'hath appeared to Simon' (v. 34); this otherwise-unnarrated appearance tradition is independently attested in Paul's early creedal summary that Christ 'was seen of Cephas' (1 Corinthians 15:5), which is often cited as multiple, at least partly independent, attestation for an early tradition of a resurrection appearance to Peter specifically.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · disputed independence of attestation

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Argument (Criterion Of Embarrassment) *(tentative)*
> That the empty-tomb tradition names women (Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James) as the first witnesses, and reports the male apostles as initially disbelieving them, is cited by some historians as an instance of the 'criterion of embarrassment': a detail unlikely to be invented by a movement seeking persuasive testimony, since women's testimony carried little standing in first-century Jewish legal custom.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed historiographical method

> [!evidence] Synoptic Comparison / Harmonization Dispute *(tentative)*
> The four Gospels differ on the number and description of the messenger(s) encountered at the tomb (Luke: two men in shining garments; Mark: one young man; Matthew: one angel; John: two angels), a divergence that critics read as evidence of legendary embellishment across retellings, while some harmonizing scholars read it as the kind of incidental variation expected among independent, non-collusive traditions.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, omits literary-dependence alternative
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Luke record two messengers at the tomb where Mark records one and Matthew describes an angel — and how much should a reader expect these accounts to harmonize versus reflect independent retellings?
- What is the significance of a private appearance to Simon (v. 34) that Luke mentions but never narrates?
- Which specific passages from Moses, the prophets, and the psalms did Jesus expound (v. 27, 44)? Luke asserts a comprehensive scriptural interpretation without supplying the citations.
- Should the Emmaus breaking of bread be read as [[Sacrament]] imagery, or as an ordinary meal that merely evokes earlier meals with Jesus? The wording permits but does not settle the connection.
- How does the command to wait for 'power from on high' (v. 49) develop in [[Acts 1]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
For the literary and thematic seams this chapter shares with other texts, see [[Acts 1]] (the ascension and the promise of power repeated), [[Luke 22]] (the bread-breaking echo of the Last Supper), [[Mark 16]] (a close parallel resurrection account, verified text overlap), [[John 20]] (a thematic parallel: empty tomb, initial disbelief, physical inspection of Jesus's body — no verbatim overlap), [[1 Corinthians 15]] (an early tradition of an appearance to Peter, of disputed independence from Luke's), and [[D&C 129]] (a modern text closely echoing the flesh-and-bones test of v. 39, with verified textual overlap).
