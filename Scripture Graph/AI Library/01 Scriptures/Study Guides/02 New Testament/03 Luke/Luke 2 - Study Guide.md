## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **Luke 2:1–7:** Registration, journey to Bethlehem, and birth.
- **Luke 2:8–20:** Angelic proclamation, the shepherds' visit, and their testimony.
- **Luke 2:21–40:** Circumcision, presentation in the temple, and the witness of Simeon and Anna.
- **Luke 2:41–52:** The twelve-year-old Jesus at Passover in the temple.

Similar growth notices open and close the final movement (2:40, 2:52), framing the age-twelve episode rather than the whole chapter's temple material. The wider annunciation–birth–naming–growth pattern given for Jesus here also parallels the sequence given for John the Baptist in [[Luke 1]], though Jesus's own annunciation occurs in Luke 1, not this chapter — the comparison spans both chapters rather than being confined to either one alone, and remains an interpretive literary reading rather than a proven compositional design.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's claims about [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] come through testimony: the angel calls him Saviour, Christ, and Lord (2:11); Simeon calls him God's salvation and 'a light to lighten the Gentiles, and the glory of thy people Israel' (2:32); Anna speaks of him to those awaiting Jerusalem's redemption (2:38). These declarations connect [[Salvation]] to both Israel and the Gentiles without erasing Israel's particular place — see [[Jews and Gentiles]].

The repeated attention to circumcision, purification, sacrifice, and Passover situates the holy family within the [[Law of Moses]]. Simeon's Spirit-led encounter (2:26-27) makes [[Revelation]] the means by which the child is recognized before his public ministry. The offering of 'a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons' (2:24) corresponds to the reduced purification offering permitted in Leviticus 12:8 for those unable to afford a lamb — meaningful evidence of the family's limited means at that time, though not proof of lasting poverty.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
'There was no room for them in the inn' (2:7) uses *kataluma*, the word Luke elsewhere uses for a private 'guest room' (Luke 22:11), rather than *pandocheion*, the commercial inn of the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:34). This distinction is real, but it does not by itself prove the family stayed in a relative's home — no house or relative is named, and *kataluma*'s range is broader than either a single reading allows; the text simply does not specify architecture or ownership.

The angelic hymn (2:14) reads differently depending on the underlying Greek manuscript: the earliest majuscules (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) carry the genitive *eudokias* ('peace among those he favors'), while the nominative *eudokia* behind the KJV ('good will toward men') has its own strong later manuscript support. The difference affects the verse's precise scope without settling a doctrinal dispute.

Jesus's reply about his 'Father's business' (2:49) translates a Greek phrase that can mean either being *in* the Father's house or occupied *with* the Father's affairs; the temple setting favors the former, but the wording doesn't force only one reading. 'Espoused wife' (2:5) reflects a legally binding Jewish betrothal, a status short of full marriage but already carrying legal obligations.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter alternates public proclamation with private reflection: angels proclaim, shepherds investigate and report, Simeon blesses and warns, Anna speaks to those awaiting redemption — while Mary 'kept all these things, and pondered them in her heart' (2:19) and again 'kept all these sayings in her heart' (2:51). Verse 50 adds a distinct, adjacent note — that the parents did not understand Jesus's temple saying — which should be read alongside, not folded into, Mary's pondering.

The chapter also juxtaposes named imperial power (Caesar Augustus, Cyrenius) with an unnamed manger, and glory in the highest with a lowly birth and modest sacrificial offering — a contrast consistent with Luke's recurring interest in reversal. Simeon's prophecy of division and sorrow (2:34-35) prevents the infancy narrative from becoming detached from the conflict and grief that will follow, without the text itself naming the crucifixion specifically.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · corroborates A:c7 with proper hedging · [[Social-Economic Inference From Legal Offering in Luke 2|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism
> The angelic hymn is rendered in the KJV as 'Glory to God in the highest, and on earth peace, good will toward men' (2:14), but the earliest surviving Greek manuscripts (e.g., Codex Sinaiticus, Vaticanus) carry the genitive eudokias rather than the nominative eudokia followed by most later manuscripts underlying the KJV, which shifts the sense toward 'peace among those whom he favors/of good will' rather than a universal wish of goodwill to all humanity.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.6 · accepted manuscript variant / disputed theological weight

> [!evidence] Historical/Chronological Criticism
> Luke's stated dating anchor — the census 'first made when Cyrenius was governor of Syria' (2:2) — sits in tension with the wider historical record: the only census under Quirinius independently attested (by Josephus, Antiquities 18.1.1, and implicitly by Acts 5:37's reference to unrest under 'Judas of Galilee' in the days of 'the taxing') occurred in AD 6, roughly a decade after the death of Herod the Great (c. 4 BC), during whose reign Luke (1:5) and Matthew (2:1) both place Jesus's birth.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.6 · genuine, widely-discussed historical-critical problem; correctly left unresolved · [[Historical/Chronological Criticism in Luke 2|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. What would an original audience have understood by 'no room in the inn' — and how does the *kataluma* wording change (or not change) that picture?
2. Why does Luke emphasize repeated compliance with the [[Law of Moses]] at every stage of Jesus's infancy?
3. How does Simeon's language hold together the roles of Israel and the Gentiles without collapsing one into the other?
4. What is gained by reading Jesus's listening, questioning, submission (2:51), and growth (2:52) together, rather than isolating only his extraordinary understanding at age twelve?
5. What does Mary model by 'keeping' and 'pondering' events she does not yet fully understand?
6. How should the chronological tension between Luke's census dating (2:1-2) and the wider historical record be weighed against the theological purposes of an infancy narrative?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
