# Luke 16 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 16]] · My notes: [[Luke 16 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus gives the parable of the unjust steward—He teaches of service and condemns divorce—He gives the parable of the rich man and Lazarus.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The steward anticipates dismissal (16:1-8):** Accused of waste, he changes two debt records to secure a future welcome and is commended for acting wisely.

2. **Applications concerning wealth and trust (16:9-13):** Jesus speaks of friends, faithfulness in small responsibilities, another person's property, true riches, and exclusive allegiance to God rather than mammon.

3. **The Pharisees, the kingdom, and the law (16:14-18):** Covetous hearers deride Jesus; he answers with God's knowledge of the heart and sayings about the kingdom, the law, and divorce. The precise relationship of the divorce saying to its neighbors is not stated, although it may illustrate the preceding affirmation of the law.

4. **The rich man and Lazarus (16:19-31):** Earthly inequality gives way to postmortem reversal, an impassable gulf, and a final appeal to Moses and the prophets.

The repeated opening, "a certain rich man" (16:1, 19), supports reading the two narratives together, but does not prove that all the chapter's material originated in one discourse.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Stewardship and allegiance (16:1-13).** The steward is explicitly commended for acting wisely, but the text does not fully explain the moral or economic character of the debt reductions. The following sayings demand faithfulness with worldly wealth and present service to God and service to mammon as incompatible allegiances. This makes material stewardship a question of both conduct and worship and connects naturally with [[Consecration]] and [[Honesty]].

**Kingdom, law, and marriage (16:16-18).** Jesus associates John with a transition from the law and the prophets to the preaching of the [[Kingdom of God]], then emphatically declares that no tittle of the law will fail. Verse 18 gives a stringent teaching about divorce and remarriage. The passage does not explicitly explain whether that saying illustrates verse 17 or how transition and permanence fit together; it therefore should not be used alone to settle the law's continuing application.

**Afterlife and accountability (16:19-31).** The rich man and Lazarus remain identifiable after death and experience comfort and torment separated by a fixed gulf, making the account relevant to [[Death and the Spirit World]]. Its closing emphasis is on hearing Moses and the prophets and responding in [[Repentance]]. The narrative does not teach that poverty automatically saves or wealth automatically condemns, and its imagery should not by itself be treated as a complete geography or chronology of the afterlife.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The term "mammon" functions as a personified rival master in verse 13, not merely as a neutral synonym for coins. The repetition of "faithful" and "unjust" across verses 10-12 turns the steward's story into an examination of character: conduct in what appears small discloses fitness for greater trust.

The contrast between what is "highly esteemed among men" and what God sees in the heart (16:15) supplies the evaluative vocabulary for the second parable. Purple clothing, fine linen, daily feasting, sores, crumbs, and dogs make social status vividly visible; the ensuing reversal exposes how differently that status may be judged by God.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter places two narratives beginning with "a certain rich man" around teachings about possessions, faithfulness, and accountability. This repetition makes it fruitful to read the narratives together: the first concerns action before an accounting, while the second depicts an irreversible condition after death. Because the opening formula may also be conventional, the pattern does not prove that the material originated as a single discourse.

The second narrative is structured through reversal: luxury and hunger, fine clothing and sores, comfort and torment, earthly proximity and a later impassable gulf. Lazarus is named while the rich man is not, but the reason for that distinction is unstated. The ending moves from a requested sight—a messenger from the dead—to the need to hear Moses and the prophets. For readers who know [[Luke 24]], the warning that some will remain unpersuaded even if one rises from the dead carries plausible resurrection irony, although its immediate reference is to the rich man's proposed messenger.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Doctrinal-Overlay Caution
> The afterlife imagery in vv.22-26 (Abraham's bosom, conscious torment, a fixed gulf) is drawn from a parable and should not, by itself, be treated as establishing a complete or literal cosmology of the afterlife.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · widely defensible evidentiary caution · [[Doctrinal-Overlay Caution in Luke 16|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary Structure *(tentative)*
> The repetition of "a certain rich man" near the beginning and again in verse 19, together with sustained themes of possessions and accountability, is meaningful literary evidence that the two parables are intended to be read in conversation: the first concerns using entrusted wealth before an accounting, and the second depicts an irreversible reckoning after death.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · real verbal pattern; intentional framing disputed

> [!evidence] Interpretive Crux *(tentative)*
> The parable's moral logic (vv. 8-9) is genuinely ambiguous: the text does not specify whether the steward's reduction of debts constitutes further dishonesty, a forgiving of his own commission/interest, or something else, and 'make to yourselves friends of the mammon of unrighteousness' has long been read variously (literally, ironically, or hyperbolically).
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · textual ambiguity accepted; interpretive history unsupported

> [!evidence] Narrative Foreshadowing And Irony *(tentative)*
> For readers of the completed Gospel, the warning that the brothers will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead creates a strong prospective irony in light of Jesus's resurrection. It supports Luke's larger presentation of unbelief as a moral and interpretive failure, not merely a shortage of spectacular evidence.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Exactly what feature of the steward does verse 8 commend, and which features does the surrounding teaching reject?

2. How do verses 10-13 constrain possible readings of the difficult command in verse 9?

3. Why does Luke place the Pharisees' concern with social justification between the two wealth parables?

4. What role does the rich man's gate play in establishing knowledge, opportunity, and responsibility?

5. Why does Abraham direct the brothers to scripture rather than grant the requested sign?

6. How do verses 16-18 relate to the wealth controversy, and where does the text leave that relationship unstated?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the two-master saying with [[Matthew 6]] and [[3 Nephi 13]]. Read Deuteronomy 15:7-11 and Isaiah 58:6-7 as examples of the witness available in Moses and the prophets concerning the poor. Compare the rich man's failure with the rich fool in [[Luke 12]] and with Zacchaeus's materially concrete repentance in [[Luke 19]]. [[Matthew 25]] and James 2 provide further canonical comparisons concerning treatment of the hungry, distressed, and socially dishonored.
