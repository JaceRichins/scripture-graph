# Luke 21 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 21]] · My notes: [[Luke 21 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus foretells the destruction of the temple and of Jerusalem—He tells of the signs to precede His Second Coming and gives the parable of the fig tree.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The widow and the temple (21:1–6):** A destitute widow gives everything she has; admiration of the temple is followed immediately by its predicted destruction.

2. **Warnings before the crisis (21:7–19):** Jesus warns against false claimants and panic, then prepares the disciples for persecution, testimony, betrayal, and endurance.

3. **Jerusalem's desolation (21:20–24):** The language becomes geographically concrete: armies, Judaea, flight, sword, captivity, and Gentile control.

4. **Cosmic signs and the Son of man (21:25–28):** The scope expands from Jerusalem to nations, heaven, earth, and redemption.

5. **Fig tree and temporal sayings (21:29–33):** Signs disclose nearness, while the saying about "this generation" raises a genuine interpretive problem.

6. **Watchfulness and narrative close (21:34–38):** The required response is disciplined readiness expressed through vigilance and [[Prayer]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The widow narrative supports a principle of [[Sacrifice]]: divine appraisal need not follow the monetary size of a gift. Yet the passage should not be used simplistically to demand that impoverished people surrender their last resources; Luke records Jesus' commendation of the widow but does not provide a general financial command here.

The persecution sayings connect [[Endurance]] with [[Testimony]]. Faithfulness does not guarantee immediate physical safety, since verse 16 anticipates death. The promise of verse 18 must therefore be read alongside, rather than against, that warning.

Readiness for the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]] is presented primarily as a condition of the heart. Jesus warns both against external deception and against internal dullness produced by excess and the cares of life.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several expressions resist overconfident interpretation. "The end is not by and by" explicitly discourages treating initial disturbances as proof of an immediate consummation. "The times of the Gentiles" is not defined within the chapter. "This generation" most naturally refers to contemporaries in ordinary usage, but the scope of "all" in the same sentence remains debated. "Not an hair of your head perish" cannot straightforwardly mean that every disciple will avoid bodily death, because verse 16 says that some will be killed.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Luke uses striking changes of scale: the chapter begins with two nearly worthless coins and moves to the downfall of a monumental temple, the fate of Jerusalem, upheaval among nations, and disturbances in heaven. It contrasts two kinds of seeing — onlookers admire impressive stones, while Jesus directs attention to vulnerability, judgment, and faithful readiness. Temple scenes bookend the chapter (21:1; 21:37-38), though whether this constitutes intentional literary design or simply reflects continuous Passion-week narration is not certain. The fig-tree comparison (21:29-31) appeals to ordinary observation as a model for discerning nearness, without supplying a date or functioning as a master key to every sign.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical Correspondence
> The prediction's references to encircling armies, flight, death, captivity, and Gentile domination correspond substantially to events associated with the Roman siege and destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE. This is strong evidence that Luke 21 engages the historical catastrophe of Jerusalem, but it does not by itself determine when or how the prediction originated.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · broadly accepted correspondence; properly bounded · [[Historical Correspondence in Luke 21|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Text-Critical / Interpretive Debate *(tentative)*
> Jesus' statement 'This generation shall not pass away, till all be fulfilled' (21:32) is a long-debated interpretive crux: read as the generation living in Jesus' own day, it raises the question of an apparently unfulfilled near-term expectation; other readings take 'generation' as referring to a people/lineage, or to the future generation that sees the signs described, resolving the tension differently.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.6 · genuinely disputed crux; honestly presented

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical / Redaction Analysis *(tentative)*
> Luke's description of the coming destruction in plain military terms — Jerusalem 'compassed with armies' (21:20) — rather than the more allusive Danielic 'abomination of desolation' language used in Matthew's and Mark's parallels, is cited by many critical scholars as evidence that Luke's final text reflects knowledge of the actual Roman siege of Jerusalem in AD 70.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.4 · disputed; 'many critical scholars' unsourced here

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the widow's placement before the temple prediction commend her devotion alone, implicitly critique a system that received her last resources, or permit both impressions?
- Where, if anywhere, does the discourse shift from Jerusalem's first-century crisis (21:20-24) to the coming of the Son of Man (21:25-33) — one continuous sequence, two horizons, or prophetic telescoping?
- What can 'there shall not an hair of your head perish' (21:18) mean when 21:16 says some disciples will be put to death?
- What does 'this generation shall not pass away, till all be fulfilled' (21:32) pick out — Jesus' contemporaries (including the possibility that the prophecy's Second-Coming element went unfulfilled in that generation), a people/lineage, or a future generation that sees the signs?
- Why does Luke describe the siege in plain military terms ('compassed with armies,' 21:20) rather than the more allusive language in the Matthew/Mark parallels, and what should that difference mean for how the discourse is read historically?
- How do the warnings against deception and panic (21:8-9) constrain modern attempts to read every disaster as a unique sign of the end?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Luke 21]] closely with [[Mark 13]] and [[Matthew 24]], noting both shared material and Luke's distinctive reference to Jerusalem surrounded by armies. Then compare [[Joseph Smith—Matthew 1]] and [[D&C 45]], which provide Restoration-era interpretations and expansions. [[Luke 19]] supplies an important earlier Lukan prediction of Jerusalem's encirclement and destruction. These comparisons can clarify interpretive possibilities, but differences among the accounts should be acknowledged rather than harmonized automatically.
