<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter falls into two broad movements:

1. **Hearing and doing the word (8:1-21)**
   - Women who minister to Jesus from their own resources (8:1-3)
   - The parable of the sower, told and interpreted (8:4-15)
   - The candle set on a candlestick; 'take heed how ye hear' (8:16-18)
   - True kindred are those who hear and do the word (8:19-21)
2. **Four miracles of authority (8:22-56)**
   - Calming the storm (8:22-25)
   - Legion cast out of the Gadarene man (8:26-39)
   - The woman with an issue of blood, healed en route (8:40-48)
   - Jairus's daughter raised from death (8:49-56)

The last two miracles are intercalated: the journey to Jairus's dying daughter is interrupted by the healing of the bleeding woman, and news of the daughter's death arrives during the delay (8:49). This 'story within a story' pattern matches Mark's arrangement of the same episodes ([[Mark 5]]:21-43); whether it reflects Luke's own literary design or simply preserved narrative order is not something the text itself settles.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Hearing and doing the word.** The sower parable and its interpretation, the candle saying, and the redefinition of Jesus' 'mother and brethren' (8:21) together teach that receiving truth is not passive; the same word produces different outcomes depending on the condition of the heart that receives it.
- **Faith under test.** [[Faith]] recurs across the miracle accounts — Jesus asks the frightened disciples 'Where is your faith?' (8:25), tells the healed woman 'thy faith hath made thee whole' (8:48), and tells Jairus to 'believe only' (8:50). The text presents faith as a recurring response invoked in crisis, not a guaranteed formula; it does not license the inference that an unhealed person simply lacked faith.
- **Christ's authority and its source.** Across four miracle accounts, Jesus commands wind and water, unclean spirits, chronic disease, and death itself, each yielding to his word. Notably, when Jesus sends the healed Gadarene man to 'shew how great things God hath done' (8:39), the narrator immediately reports that the man told what things Jesus had done — a juxtaposition worth noting but not by itself a settled statement about Jesus' precise relationship to God.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
**Numeric echo.** The narrative pairs two twelves: Jairus's daughter is 'about twelve years of age' (8:42) and the woman had her issue of blood 'twelve years' (8:43). Jesus also calls the healed woman 'Daughter' (8:48) while en route to Jairus's daughter, linking the two petitioners verbally. Any symbolic significance beyond this narrative pairing is not stated by the text and remains conjectural.

**Death, not mere sleep.** Though Jesus calls the girl's condition sleep (8:52), the surrounding narration treats her death as real — mourners weep and scorn his remark (8:52-53), and 'her spirit came again' before she arose (8:55). The 'sleepeth' language functions within the story as a statement about what Jesus is about to reverse, not a claim that she was never truly dead.

**Secrecy and disclosure.** The chapter gives opposite publicity instructions after two miracles: the restored Gadarene man is sent home to publish what happened (8:39), while Jairus's parents are charged to tell no one (8:56). The text does not explain the difference; readers should resist assuming a single, tidy rule governs when Jesus seeks publicity and when he seeks silence.

**Contrast in response to power.** The Gadarene crowd's fear leads them to ask Jesus to leave (8:37), while the healed man asks to stay and is instead sent to testify at home (8:38-39) — two very different reactions to the same demonstration of power, placed side by side.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Narrative And Christological Evidence
> Jesus tells the restored man to report what God has done for him, and the narrator immediately says that the man reports what Jesus has done for him. This verbal juxtaposition is internal evidence that Luke closely associates Jesus' saving action with God's action.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · disciplined evidentiary claim · [[Internal Narrative And Christological Evidence in Luke 8|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Narrative/Literary Criticism *(tentative)*
> The healing of the woman with an issue of blood is narrated as an interruption inserted inside the journey to raise Jairus's daughter (the daughter is dying/dead while the detour to the woman occurs), a pattern often called a 'Markan sandwich' or intercalation, preserved by Luke from his source material.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · structural observation sound; source claim disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Luke preserve only a single yield figure ('an hundredfold', 8:8) for the good ground, where Mark and Matthew record a graduated thirtyfold/sixtyfold/hundredfold?
- What distinguishes hearing with temporary joy (8:13) from keeping the word with patience (8:15)?
- Why does fear lead the disciples to wonder (8:25), the Gadarenes to ask Jesus to leave (8:37), and Jairus to receive an invitation to believe instead (8:50)?
- Why is the restored Gadarene man told to publish what happened (8:39) while Jairus's family is told to stay silent (8:56)?
- Does Luke 8:10 mean parables cause non-understanding in outsiders, expose an existing lack of receptivity, or both?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
