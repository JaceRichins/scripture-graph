# Luke 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 3]] · My notes: [[Luke 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> John the Baptist preaches and baptizes—Jesus is baptized, and God acclaims Him as His Son—Jesus’ genealogy back to Adam is given.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Historical setting and prophetic call (3:1–2):** Luke names seven civil and priestly officeholders before the word of God comes to John in the wilderness.

2. **Isaiah's prepared way (3:3–6):** John's ministry is presented through [[Isaiah 40]], with the quotation extending to salvation being seen by “all flesh.”

3. **Repentance tested by fruit (3:7–14):** ancestry is no substitute for repentance; the crowd, publicans, and soldiers receive concrete instructions.

4. **The coming one and judgment (3:15–18):** John denies being the Christ and announces one who will baptize with the Holy Ghost and fire, followed by wheat-and-chaff judgment imagery.

5. **John's imprisonment (3:19–20):** its placement plausibly closes John's public ministry as a literary unit, although Luke does not explain the ordering.

6. **Jesus's baptism (3:21–22):** Jesus is baptized and praying; heaven opens, the Holy Ghost descends, and the heavenly voice addresses him as the beloved Son.

7. **Genealogy (3:23–38):** the line runs backward from Jesus through David and Abraham to Adam and God.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
John joins [[Repentance]] to observable fruit rather than inherited privilege. His examples connect conversion with [[Service]], [[Honesty]], economic restraint, and responsible use of power.

The chapter associates John's [[Baptism]] with repentance and remission of sins, but it does not say that Jesus himself required repentance. John contrasts his water baptism with the mightier one's baptism “with the Holy Ghost and with fire.” The burning chaff in the next verse gives judgment substantial contextual weight, although purification or Pentecostal readings remain possible.

At Jesus's baptism, the narrative distinctly depicts the Son, the Holy Ghost descending like a dove, and a heavenly voice addressing Jesus. This makes the scene important to study of [[The Godhead]], while the passage itself does not define later Trinitarian or Restorationist metaphysics.

John's warning concerning Abraham challenges reliance on covenant ancestry without repentant fruit; it does not deny Abraham's importance or supply a complete theology of the [[Abrahamic Covenant]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Fruits worthy of repentance” makes repentance something evidenced by conduct. The repeated question “What shall we do?” turns warning into practical moral instruction. John's paired images of axe and tree, then fan, wheat, garner, chaff, and fire, shift from horticulture to harvest while preserving the same theme of impending evaluation. “As was supposed” qualifies the public identification of Jesus as Joseph's son before the genealogy begins.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The explicit citation of [[Isaiah 40]] frames John as the preparatory voice. Luke then contrasts inherited status with repentant response, John's water baptism with the coming one's greater work, and wheat with chaff.

Verses 10–14 form three group-specific exchanges: the crowd receives a general command to share, while publicans and soldiers receive occupation-related instructions. The baptism account parallels [[Matthew 3]] and [[Mark 1]], with Luke explicitly emphasizing that Jesus was praying.

Luke's genealogy moves backward from Jesus to Adam and God, whereas [[Matthew 1]] moves forward from Abraham to Jesus. Luke follows David through Nathan and Matthew through Solomon; their lines substantially diverge, briefly converge at Salathiel and Zorobabel, and separate again. Maternal-line, legal-line, and levirate harmonizations should be identified as proposals, since neither Gospel states such a solution.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Synoptic Comparison
> Between David and Joseph, Luke's line (through Nathan) shares almost no names with Matthew's line (through Solomon), apart from Zorobabel and Salathiel appearing in both. This is a long-recognized synoptic discrepancy without a scholarly-consensus resolution.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 1.0 · Divergence accepted; proposed resolutions disputed · [[Synoptic Comparison in Luke 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Historiographic Evidence
> The concentration of named rulers and offices is strong internal evidence that Luke intends to place John's ministry within public history rather than present it as an undated legend. It is only moderate evidence for the accuracy of every chronological or political detail.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · Broadly accepted literary inference · [[Internal Historiographic Evidence in Luke 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Corroboration *(tentative)*
> The mention of 'Lysanias the tetrarch of Abilene' (v.1) has long been debated by historians: the only Lysanias otherwise well attested in classical sources ruled Chalcis and died decades before this narrative (c. 36 BC), which some critics cited as a chronological error in Luke. Later epigraphic evidence for a tetrarch named Lysanias associated with Abila in the era of Tiberius has been used by other scholars to argue Luke's detail is defensible rather than anachronistic.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.4 · Historically disputed; primary evidence not adequately identified
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Luke report John's imprisonment before the account of Jesus's baptism, and how certain can we be that this reflects deliberate topical ordering?

- How do John's commands define “fruits worthy of repentance” for people with different resources and responsibilities?

- Does “fire” in Luke 3:16 refer primarily to judgment, purification, Pentecost, or some combination?

- Why is Jesus baptized in the setting of John's baptismal ministry when the chapter does not explain Jesus's rationale?

- What literary or theological work is done by tracing Jesus beyond Abraham to Adam and God?

- How should the substantial differences between Luke's and Matthew's genealogies constrain confidence in any proposed harmonization?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare Luke 3:3–6 with [[Isaiah 40]] and distinguish Isaiah's own wording from Luke's choice to quote farther into the passage than the shorter Synoptic citations.

- Read the baptism accounts in [[Matthew 3]] and [[Mark 1]], noting Luke's explicit reference to [[Prayer]] and his passive wording concerning Jesus being baptized.

- Compare the genealogies in [[Luke 3]] and [[Matthew 1]] name by name. Treat maternal-line, legal-line, and levirate explanations as hypotheses rather than conclusions stated by either text.

- Investigate the second Cainan in Luke 3:36 using critical editions of both Luke and Greek Genesis; the issue involves manuscript variation and should not be reduced to a simple Hebrew-versus-Septuagint contrast.

- Investigate “Lysanias the tetrarch of Abilene” through identified primary inscriptions and Josephus before treating the name as either a demonstrated error or clean archaeological corroboration.

- Trace the association of repentance, baptism, remission, and the Holy Ghost into [[Acts 2]].
