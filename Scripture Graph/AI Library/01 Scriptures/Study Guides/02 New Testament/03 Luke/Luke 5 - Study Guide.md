# Luke 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 5]] · My notes: [[Luke 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Peter, the fisherman, is called to catch men—Jesus heals a leper—He forgives sins and heals a paralytic—Matthew is called—The sick need a physician—New wine must be put in new bottles.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Luke 5:1-11 — Abundance and vocation:** Jesus teaches from Simon's ship, directs the catch, and calls the fishermen.
2. **Luke 5:12-16 — Cleansing and withdrawal:** Jesus touches and cleanses a man with leprosy, honors the Mosaic cleansing procedure, and withdraws to pray.
3. **Luke 5:17-26 — Forgiveness publicly contested:** Jesus forgives a paralytic and heals him as a visible demonstration of the Son of man's authority.
4. **Luke 5:27-32 — Calling and table fellowship:** Levi follows Jesus, hosts a feast, and becomes the setting for the physician saying and call to repentance.
5. **Luke 5:33-39 — Fasting and newness:** The bridegroom saying and paired parables address the timing of fasting and the difficulty of combining new and old.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins forgiveness and healing without saying that the paralytic's illness was caused by his personal sins. Jesus explicitly states that the healing is performed so observers may know that the Son of man has authority on earth to forgive sins (Luke 5:24). Faith appears as persistent, embodied action when the men overcome physical barriers to bring the paralytic before Jesus. Repentance remains central to Jesus's fellowship with sinners: his physician metaphor explains such fellowship as a restorative mission rather than approval of remaining unchanged. The chapter also treats [[Prayer]] and [[Fasting]] as meaningful practices whose form and timing are related to Jesus's presence and mission.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several episodes can be read through a loose sequence of need or failure, an authoritative word from Jesus, an observable result, and a human response. Simon moves from fruitless labor to abundance, confession, and vocation; the man with leprosy moves from petition to cleansing and prescribed testimony; the paralytic moves from immobility to forgiveness, walking, and glorifying God; and Levi moves from the receipt of custom to following and hospitality. This is a reader's aid rather than a demonstrated formal structure, and parts of the episode sequence parallel inherited Synoptic material.

The garment and wineskin parables warn about damaging combinations of new and old, but they do not explicitly identify every element. Luke 5:39 complicates a simple old-bad/new-good interpretation by acknowledging that a person accustomed to old wine does not immediately desire new. Comparison with [[Mark 1]], [[Mark 2]], [[Matthew 8]], and [[Matthew 9]] helps distinguish shared material from Luke's wording and arrangement.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Synoptic Literary Relationship
> The leper, paralytic, Levi, fasting controversy, and old-new sayings occur in closely corresponding Synoptic passages. Their shared sequence and content provide strong evidence of literary relationship or common tradition among the accounts, while leaving the precise direction and form of dependence open to source-critical analysis.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted literary relationship; mechanism disputed · [[Synoptic Literary Relationship in Luke 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Narrative And Theological Argument
> Within the narrative's argument, the paralytic's visible and immediate healing functions as evidence for Jesus's otherwise invisible claim to possess authority on earth to forgive sins. This is strong evidence for Luke's intended theological portrayal of Jesus's authority, not independent proof that the event occurred historically.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · strong narrative-theological argument · [[Narrative And Theological Argument in Luke 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Form/Redaction Criticism *(tentative)*
> Some scholars have proposed that the miraculous-catch story in Luke 5:1-11 and the miraculous catch in John 21:1-11 (set after the resurrection) derive from a single underlying tradition that the two Gospel writers placed at different points in the narrative, rather than reporting two distinct historical events. The verified index for this chapter shows zero direct verse-overlap between Luke 5 and John 21, which is consistent with the stories being differently worded even if traditio-historically related, but overlap counts alone cannot establish or rule out a common origin.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.4 · disputed and inadequately sourced

> [!evidence] Cumulative Literary Pattern *(tentative)*
> Across the chapter, Jesus commands nature's abundance, cleanses disease, forgives sins, restores mobility, calls socially disfavored people, and interprets religious practice. This repeated pattern strongly supports the observation that Luke 5 is organized to display comprehensive authority and the disruptive demands of Jesus's mission.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · plausible thematic synthesis, deliberate organization disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does Simon's movement from "Master" to "Lord" contribute to the characterization of his response?
- Why does Jesus pronounce forgiveness before healing the paralytic, and how does the later healing function in the dispute?
- Does "their faith" include the paralytic, his bearers, or both? The grammar alone does not settle the question.
- How does Jesus's command to follow Moses's cleansing procedure qualify claims that this episode simply rejects the [[Law of Moses]]?
- What does Luke 5:39 add to the garment and wineskin parables, and why might Luke preserve this resistance to the new?
- How do immediate relinquishment and subsequent hospitality complement one another in Levi's response?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the cleansing account with [[Mark 1]] and [[Matthew 8]], then compare the paralytic, Levi, fasting, garment, and wineskin material with [[Mark 2]] and [[Matthew 9]]. Note both close correspondence and meaningful differences without assuming that parallel accounts are independent witnesses. Further study could also examine the legal background of the priestly presentation in the [[Law of Moses]], the ambiguity of 'their faith' in Luke 5:20, and the several possible functions of the old-wine saying in Luke 5:39.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
