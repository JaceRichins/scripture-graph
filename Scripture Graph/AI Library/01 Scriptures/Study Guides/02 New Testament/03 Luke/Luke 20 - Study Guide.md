# Luke 20 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The chief priests oppose Jesus—He gives the parable of the wicked husbandmen—Render unto Cæsar and God that which is theirs—Jesus teaches the law of marriage.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Authority and John (vv. 1–8):** Jesus answers a demand about authority with a question the leaders refuse to answer.

2. **The vineyard and rejected stone (vv. 9–19):** A stewardship parable culminates in the heir's death, judgment on the tenants, and an appeal to Psalms 118.

3. **Tribute (vv. 20–26):** Spies try unsuccessfully to turn Jesus' words into grounds for action by the governor.

4. **Resurrection (vv. 27–40):** Jesus rejects the Sadducees' assumptions about resurrected life and reasons from [[Exodus 3]].

5. **David's son and Lord (vv. 41–44):** Psalms 110 complicates an inadequate account of the Christ.

6. **Warning about scribes (vv. 45–47):** Public honor and conspicuous [[Prayer]] are contrasted with the exploitation of widows.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects divine authority with responsiveness to genuine prophetic witness: the leaders cannot address Jesus' question because they are unwilling to take a truthful public position about John (vv. 1–8). The vineyard teaches accountable stewardship and judgment, while leaving the identity of the eventual recipients unnamed (vv. 9–19). The tribute saying recognizes obligations relevant to [[Government and Laws]] without reducing God's claim to a narrow remainder (vv. 20–26).

In the discussion of [[Resurrection]], Jesus teaches that resurrected life is not simply mortal social order continued unchanged: those described can no longer die (vv. 34–36). His appeal to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob grounds hope in God's continuing relation to persons who have died (vv. 37–38). The exchange also concerns [[Marriage]], but these verses alone do not fully define the status of relationships established before death.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Opponents repeatedly deliberate or act strategically: the leaders calculate the consequences of answering about John (vv. 5–7), the husbandmen conspire over the inheritance (v. 14), and spies feign righteousness to capture Jesus' words (v. 20). Jesus, by contrast, perceives their craftiness (v. 23). This pattern links the controversies to the parable without requiring every narrative detail to have a one-to-one symbolic referent.

The chapter also moves through three scriptural horizons: the rejected stone (Psalms 118), God's patriarchal name at the bush ([[Exodus 3]]), and David's Lord (Psalms 110). In each case scripture is used within an immediate controversy rather than cited as an isolated proof text.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why do the leaders evaluate answers about John by anticipated consequences rather than by whether an answer is true (vv. 5–7)?

- Who are the unspecified others who receive the vineyard in verse 16, and what conclusions go beyond Luke's wording?

- What belongs to Caesar, what belongs to God, and which boundaries does Jesus leave unstated (v. 25)?

- Does the angel comparison in verse 36 concern immortality specifically, or additional characteristics as well?

- How does God's continued identification with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob function in Jesus' resurrection argument (vv. 37–38)?

- Does the question about David's son reject that title, or expose its inadequacy when used without the greater title Lord (vv. 41–44)?
