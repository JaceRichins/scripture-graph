# Luke 11 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 11]] · My notes: [[Luke 11 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus gives the Lord’s Prayer—He discusses the casting out of devils—He acclaims Himself as greater than Jonah and Solomon—He rebukes the Pharisees and says that the blood of all the prophets may be required of their generation.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv.1-4** — The Lord's Prayer, given in response to a disciple's request ("teach us to pray, as John also taught his disciples," v.1). Luke's version is shorter than Matthew's parallel (Matthew 6:9-13), omitting the concluding doxology.
- **vv.5-13** — Two teachings on prayer: the parable of the friend at midnight (persistence, vv.5-8) and the ask/seek/knock saying with the good-gifts analogy, climaxing in the promise of the Holy Spirit (vv.9-13).
- **vv.14-26** — The Beelzebub controversy: an exorcism triggers an accusation, which Jesus answers with the divided-kingdom logic, the "finger of God" claim, the strong-man parable, and the warning about a returning unclean spirit.
- **vv.27-36** — A woman's blessing on Jesus' mother is redirected to those who "hear the word of God, and keep it" (vv.27-28); the sign of Jonah and the greater-than-Solomon/Jonah sayings follow (vv.29-32); the section closes with the single-eye/light-of-the-body teaching (vv.33-36).
- **vv.37-54** — A dinner with a Pharisee becomes an occasion for two matched sets of three woes: against Pharisees (vv.42-44) and, after a lawyer objects (v.45), against lawyers (vv.46-52), ending with the charge of taking away "the key of knowledge" (v.52).
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Persistent, expectant prayer**: the friend-at-midnight parable and the ask/seek/knock teaching present prayer as something to pursue with boldness, addressed to a Father more willing to give good gifts than any human parent (vv.5-13). Interpreters differ on whether the term traditionally rendered "importunity" (v.8) means persistence, boldness, or shamelessness, so the parable's exact logic is not fully settled.
- **The Holy Spirit as the Father's gift**: the ask/seek/knock teaching climaxes in the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]] rather than a general promise of "good things" (v.13; compare Matthew 7:11) — a distinctly Lukan emphasis that cautions against reading the promise as an unconditional guarantee of any requested outcome.
- **The kingdom of God present in power**: exorcism "with the finger of God" is presented as evidence that "the kingdom of God is come upon you" (v.20), tying Jesus' ministry to the [[Kingdom of God]].
- **Obedience over kinship or status**: "blessed are they that hear the word of God, and keep it" (v.28) redirects praise of Jesus' mother toward [[Obedience]]; the saying may qualify the basis of Mary's blessedness rather than deny it outright.
- **Judgment measured against response to revelation**: the queen of the south and the men of Nineveh, who responded to lesser revelation, are held up as witnesses at the [[Final Judgment]] against a generation confronted with "a greater than Solomon" and "a greater than Jonas" (vv.29-32).
- **Inward versus outward religion**: the cup-and-platter image (v.39) and the mint-and-rue [[Tithing]] example (v.42) press for inward integrity — "judgment and the love of God" — over selective outward observance; v.42 explicitly says the lesser practice "ought" still be done, so this corrects priorities rather than rejecting tithing.
- **The blood of the prophets and accountability** (vv.47-51): Jesus holds "this generation" accountable for "the blood of all the [[Prophets]], from the blood of Abel unto the blood of Zacharias." This Zacharias's identity is genuinely disputed — the manner of death fits Zechariah son of Jehoiada, killed in the temple court (2 Chronicles 24:20-22), while Matthew's parallel (Matthew 23:35) adds a patronymic, "son of Barachias," matching the later prophet Zechariah (Zechariah 1:1) instead. This language of generational accountability has often been misapplied to indict a whole people; in context it targets the specific religious leaders confronting Jesus, not Jews generally or in perpetuity.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several comparisons intensify the chapter's claims: an earthly parent versus the heavenly Father (11:11-13), a strong man versus a stronger one (11:21-22), and Jonah or Solomon versus one greater than either (11:31-32). Contrasts between light and darkness, outside and inside, entering and hindering, gathering and scattering, and first and last states reinforce the demand for an undivided response.

The phrase "finger of God" (11:20) has a clear verbal counterpart in Exodus 8:19. The echo supports an association with divine power, but a fully developed Exodus typology should not be claimed from the phrase alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly uses short analogies followed by pointed application. The midnight visitor leads into ask-seek-knock; parental gifts culminate in the Holy Spirit; the divided kingdom and stronger man answer the Beelzebub charge; and lamp and eye images probe spiritual perception. The final meal scene turns an observed washing practice into a sustained contrast between outward display and inward condition.

The concentration of parallels with [[Matthew 6]], [[Matthew 7]], [[Matthew 12]], and [[Matthew 23]] is important for Synoptic study. It establishes extensive shared material, but not by itself a direction of literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Parallelism
> Close parallels between multiple Luke 11 units and material in Matthew 6, Matthew 7, Matthew 12, and Matthew 23 strongly support a shared literary or traditional relationship. The parallels alone do not identify the direction of dependence or settle a particular Synoptic-source theory.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted · [[Textual Parallelism in Luke 11|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual Allusion *(tentative)*
> Luke's phrase "the finger of God" (11:20) verbally echoes Exodus 8:19, where Pharaoh's magicians, unable to reproduce the plague of lice, tell Pharaoh "this is the finger of God." This resonance supports (but does not prove) a deliberate exodus/plague allusion casting Jesus' exorcisms as a new act of divine deliverance from bondage.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · minority interpretation
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the midnight-friend parable argue that persistence alone moves God to act, or does it argue by contrast — a reluctant neighbor versus a willing Father (vv.5-13)? The term rendered "importunity" (v.8) can mean persistence, boldness, or shamelessness, and interpreters differ.
- Why does Luke's Lord's Prayer lack the doxology found in many manuscripts of Matthew's version, and what does "daily bread" (v.3) mean given the notorious difficulty of translating the underlying Greek term?
- What exactly is "the sign of Jonah" in Luke's telling — Jonah's person, his preaching, the Ninevites' repentance, or all three? Luke omits Matthew's three-days-and-three-nights burial typology (Matthew 12:40).
- What positive condition is implied after a life has been "swept and garnished" (vv.24-26)? The saying warns of a worse relapse but does not name what should fill the empty house.
- How do the lamp and eye sayings (vv.33-36) relate to the preceding demand for a sign — insufficient evidence, defective perception, or both? They may be an independently circulating saying placed here by catchword association rather than a deliberate sequel to the sign controversy.
- Who is the "Zacharias" of verse 51, and why does Luke, unlike Matthew, give no patronymic?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare this chapter directly with [[Matthew 6]] (Lord's Prayer), [[Matthew 7]] (ask/seek/knock), [[Matthew 12]] (Beelzebub controversy, sign of Jonah), and [[Matthew 23]] (woes) to see how the same core sayings are arranged differently across Gospels — note that verse counts for the woes differ by textual edition. For the Nephite teaching parallels, see [[3 Nephi 13]] and [[3 Nephi 14]]. For the internal Lukan doublet on the candle under a bushel, compare [[Luke 8]]. For the phrase "finger of God" (v.20), compare Exodus 8:19, while noting that a shared idiom does not by itself establish a deliberate Exodus allusion.
