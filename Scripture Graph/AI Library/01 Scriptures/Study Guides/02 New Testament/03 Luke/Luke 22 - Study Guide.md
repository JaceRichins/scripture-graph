# Luke 22 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 22]] · My notes: [[Luke 22 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus institutes the sacrament—He suffers in Gethsemane and is betrayed and arrested—Peter denies knowing Him—Jesus is smitten and mocked.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Conspiracy and betrayal agreement (vv. 1-6):** the leaders seek a way to act away from the multitude, and Judas agrees to cooperate.
2. **Preparation and [[Passover]] meal (vv. 7-23):** the room is found as Jesus described; he gives the bread and cup and announces betrayal.
3. **Instruction under pressure (vv. 24-38):** Jesus teaches servant leadership, promises kingdom responsibilities, warns Peter, and speaks of approaching opposition.
4. **Prayer, arrest, and healing (vv. 39-53):** prayer against temptation frames Jesus's submission; Judas arrives; a servant is wounded and healed.
5. **Peter's denials (vv. 54-62):** Peter denies Jesus three times, remembers the prediction, and weeps bitterly.
6. **Mockery and council hearing (vv. 63-71):** Jesus is abused, questioned about his identity, and treated as having supplied sufficient testimony from his own mouth.

Jesus's prediction in verse 34 and its fulfillment in verses 54-62 form a clear narrative frame around Peter's failure, remembrance, and remorse. Restoration itself is not narrated in this chapter.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Sacrament]] and [[Covenants]]:** Jesus associates the bread and cup with his body and blood and commands remembrance (vv. 19-20). [[1 Corinthians 11]] preserves a closely corresponding early tradition, although the relationship between the accounts does not establish a verbatim transcript. Luke 22:19b-20 also has a significant ancient textual variant that should be acknowledged in detailed study.
- **[[Service]] and authority:** Jesus answers the dispute over greatness by making his own service the model for leadership (vv. 24-27). His promises of a kingdom and thrones (vv. 29-30) show that this teaching redefines the exercise of authority rather than simply denying authority.
- **[[Prayer]] and [[Satan and Temptation]]:** Jesus prays for Peter and twice commands the disciples to pray against temptation (vv. 31-32, 40, 46). His own prayer joins candid desire with submission to the Father's will (v. 42).
- **[[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]:** the body-and-blood sayings and the prayer concerning the cup contribute directly to Christian readings of Jesus's self-giving suffering. The angel and sweat imagery in verses 43-44 belongs to a textually disputed unit, and the wording compares the sweat to drops of blood rather than requiring a medical diagnosis.
- **Failure and return:** Peter's denial is predicted without being minimized, yet Jesus also anticipates Peter's turning and future work of strengthening the brethren (vv. 31-34, 54-62). Explicit restoration occurs beyond this chapter.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV preserves a useful number shift in Luke 22:31-32: “you” refers to the disciples collectively, whereas “thee,” “thy,” and “thou” address Peter individually. Satan's threat concerns the group, but Jesus highlights Peter's personal recovery and responsibility.

“New testament” in verse 20 uses covenant language. Its scriptural background plausibly includes the blood of the covenant in [[Exodus 24]] and the promised new covenant in [[Jeremiah 31]], though Luke does not explicitly cite either passage there.

Verse 44 says the sweat was “as it were” great drops of blood. This is a comparison and should not automatically be converted into a medical diagnosis. Moreover, verses 43-44 have divided ancient manuscript support, so conclusions depending uniquely on the angel and bloody-sweat imagery should acknowledge that textual issue.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Luke repeatedly places revelation beside misunderstanding. The apostles dispute greatness after Jesus speaks of self-giving (vv. 19-27); Peter promises imprisonment and death but soon denies association with Jesus (vv. 33-34, 54-62); and the disciples produce swords, but their use ends with Jesus intervening and healing the victim (vv. 38, 49-51).

A sharp narrative irony follows Peter's denials. Jesus's prediction has just been fulfilled when the guards blindfold him and demand that he prophesy (vv. 61-64). The juxtaposition undercuts their mockery, whether or not it proves deliberate authorial arrangement.

The chapter also develops a spiritual-conflict motif: Satan enters Judas (v. 3), seeks to sift the disciples (v. 31), and the arrest occurs during what Jesus calls the adversaries' hour and “the power of darkness” (v. 53). The last expression need not be treated as a direct name for Satan.

Some expressions remain deliberately difficult for readers. “It is enough” (v. 38) admits literal, figurative, ironic, and situational readings, although the later intervention against sword use constrains interpretations. “Ye say that I am” (v. 70) is indirect in English, yet the council treats it as sufficient disclosure in verse 71.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Criticism
> Luke 22:43-44 has a major ancient textual variant: the verses are absent from important early witnesses, including Papyrus 75 and Codex Vaticanus, but present in many other manuscripts. Their place in the manuscript tradition is therefore genuinely disputed even though they belong to the canonical text supplied for this study.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · variant universally recognized; originality disputed · [[Textual Criticism in Luke 22|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Early Literary Attestation
> The close correspondence between Luke's words over the bread and cup and 1 Corinthians 11 shows that substantially similar teachings about Jesus's final meal circulated outside Luke's narrative by the time 1 Corinthians was written.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted with dependence unresolved · [[Early Literary Attestation in Luke 22|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism *(tentative)*
> Luke 22:43-44 (the angel strengthening Jesus and his sweat becoming 'as it were great drops of blood') is textually disputed. The verses are absent from important early Alexandrian witnesses (the original hands of Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, among others) and are bracketed as a probable early interpolation in modern critical editions (e.g., NA28), though they are alluded to by Justin Martyr in the mid-2nd century, showing early circulation.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.7 · core variant accepted; details require correction

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism *(tentative)*
> The latter portion of the cup saying (Luke 22:19b-20, 'which is given for you...shed for you') is present in the great majority of Greek manuscripts but is absent from Codex Bezae (D) and some Old Latin witnesses, making it one of the classic 'Western non-interpolations.' Earlier critical editions (e.g., Westcott-Hort) treated the shorter text as possibly original; most modern textual scholarship retains the longer, traditional text given its overwhelming external support.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.6 · variant accepted; formulation disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How does the promise of thrones in verses 29-30 qualify—or intensify—the command to lead through service?

Why does Luke explicitly say the disciples were sleeping “for sorrow” (v. 45), and how should that detail affect judgments about their failure?

Does “It is enough” in verse 38 mean that two swords are sufficient, or does it end a conversation the disciples have misunderstood? How do verses 49-51 constrain either reading?

What does the sequence of prediction, denial, remembrance, and weeping suggest about the relationship between failure and Peter's later charge to strengthen others?

How much theological weight should be placed on verses 43-44 in light of their divided manuscript attestation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the meal accounts in [[Matthew 26]], [[Mark 14]], and [[1 Corinthians 11]], noting their common core and Luke's references to cups in Luke 22:17 and 22:20. Detailed study should also consult the manuscript evidence for Luke 22:19b-20, since some Western witnesses preserve a shorter form without the latter material.

Compare the arrest scenes in [[Matthew 26]], [[Mark 14]], and [[John 18]]. All report the servant's injury, but Luke alone explicitly narrates Jesus healing him. Study Luke 22:37 beside [[Isaiah 53]], while distinguishing Luke's application of the passage from larger claims about Isaiah's original historical setting. Finally, compare Luke's remembrance language with [[3 Nephi 18]] and examine the disputed manuscript transmission of Luke 22:43-44 before building arguments that depend specifically on the angel or sweat imagery.
