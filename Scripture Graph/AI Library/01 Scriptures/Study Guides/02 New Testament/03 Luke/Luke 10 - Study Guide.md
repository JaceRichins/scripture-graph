# Luke 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 10]] · My notes: [[Luke 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus calls, empowers, and instructs the Seventy—They preach and heal—Those who receive Christ’s disciples receive Christ—The Father is revealed by the Son—Jesus gives the parable of the good Samaritan.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter divides into seven units: (1) commissioning of the Seventy, vv.1–12; (2) woes on unrepentant cities, vv.13–16; (3) the Seventy's return and Jesus's response, vv.17–20; (4) Jesus's prayer of thanksgiving and the Father-Son saying, vv.21–22; (5) the private beatitude to the disciples, vv.23–24; (6) the lawyer's question, the Great Commandment, and the parable of the [[Parables of Jesus|Good Samaritan]], vv.25–37; (7) Martha and Mary, vv.38–42. Units (6) and (7) both begin with someone addressing Jesus (the lawyer testing him, Martha appealing to him) and end with Jesus redirecting the conversation toward what matters most — though whether Luke intended this as a deliberate literary pairing, or simply preserved two adjacent traditions, is not something the text itself settles.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Several teachings stand out: (1) response to Jesus's authorized messengers is treated as response to Christ and to the Father who sent him (v.16); (2) the [[Kingdom of God]] is announced as already 'come nigh,' independent of whether it is received (vv.9–11); (3) the lawyer correctly states the law but is told 'this do, and thou shalt live' — right answers are paired with required action; (4) the Samaritan turns compassion into costly, sustained care, and Mary's attentive hearing is commended as 'that good part' — together these scenes hold hearing, [[Service]], and devotion as complementary rather than competing responses to Christ, without one erasing the value of the other; (5) true rejoicing is relocated from visible power over spirits to having one's name 'written in heaven' (v.20), a call to humility within [[Missionary Work]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The change from "Who is my neighbour?" (10:29) to "Which now of these three ... was neighbour?" (10:36) is central. Jesus does not provide the lawyer with a list of persons who qualify. He asks him to recognize neighborly conduct and then commands, "Go, and do thou likewise" (10:37).

The lawyer's twofold answer in 10:27 corresponds to love of God in [[Deuteronomy 6]] and love of neighbor in [[Leviticus 19]]. Jesus's approval makes these commands the interpretive frame for the Samaritan story.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several reversals organize the chapter. Lamb-like messengers are given authority over the enemy; truths hidden from the wise and prudent are revealed to babes; prophets and kings desired what the disciples now see; and the Samaritan, rather than the priest or Levite, becomes the model of mercy. These reversals elevate dependence, receptivity, and compassionate action rather than status alone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical/Social Background
> The parable's rhetorical force trades on well-attested first-century Jewish-Samaritan mutual hostility (reflected elsewhere in the New Testament, e.g. John 4:9, and rooted in the centuries-long religious/ethnic estrangement between the two groups): Jesus casts the culturally despised outsider, not the religious professionals, as the moral exemplar, subverting the lawyer's assumption that 'neighbour' names a bounded in-group.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · broadly accepted historical background, well-hedged · [[Historical/Social Background in Luke 10|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Close Textual Reading
> The wording supports a cautious reading that Jesus addresses Martha's distracted, anxious condition and her demand concerning Mary, rather than issuing a general condemnation of service itself.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-calibrated evidentiary claim · [[Close Textual Reading in Luke 10|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Source Criticism *(tentative)*
> Luke 10's woes against Chorazin, Bethsaida, and Capernaum, and its saying about things hidden from the wise and revealed to babes (vv.13-15, 21-22), overlap closely in wording with Matthew 11 (six verified verses of overlap), which many New Testament scholars attribute to a shared underlying source tradition behind Matthew and Luke; the observation of verbal overlap is broadly accepted, while the specific explanatory model (a 'Q' document, Luke's use of Matthew, or a Farrer-style alternative) remains a genuinely disputed question among scholars.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · overlap accepted; source model disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why is the kingdom declared 'come nigh' both when a city receives the messengers and when it rejects them (vv.9, 11)?
- What is gained by Jesus changing the lawyer's question from who counts as a neighbor to who acted as one (vv.29, 36)?
- Does 'I beheld Satan as lightning fall from heaven' (v.18) describe a present, ongoing, or premortal event — and how much weight should one verse be asked to bear either way?
- Why does Jesus distinguish joy in spiritual power from joy that one's name is written in heaven (vv.17–20)?
- Does Jesus correct Martha for serving, for being distracted and troubled, or for her demand about Mary — or some combination (vv.40–42)?
- Why does Jesus answer the lawyer's first question (v.25) by asking him to answer his own (v.26), rather than answering directly?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the sending discourse with [[Luke 9]] and [[Matthew 9]], the city woes and Father-Son saying with [[Matthew 11]], and the lawyer's summary of the law with [[Deuteronomy 6]] and [[Leviticus 19]]. The comparisons help distinguish material shared with other traditions from emphases produced by Luke 10's particular sequence.
