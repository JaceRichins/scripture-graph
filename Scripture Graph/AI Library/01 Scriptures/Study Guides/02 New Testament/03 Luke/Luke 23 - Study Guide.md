# Luke 23 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Luke 23]] · My notes: [[Luke 23 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus is taken before Pilate, then to Herod, and then to Pilate again—Barabbas is released—Jesus is crucified between two thieves—He is buried in the tomb of Joseph of Arimathæa.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Luke 23:1–5 — First hearing before Pilate:** political accusations and Pilate's first verdict.
2. **Luke 23:6–12 — Hearing before Herod:** curiosity, silence, mockery, and return to Pilate.
3. **Luke 23:13–25 — Sentence and exchange:** Pilate's repeated verdicts, the crowd's demand, Barabbas released, and Jesus delivered.
4. **Luke 23:26–32 — The way to Calvary:** Simon bears the cross and Jesus warns the daughters of Jerusalem.
5. **Luke 23:33–43 — Jesus on the cross:** forgiveness, mockery, disputed responses, and the promise of paradise.
6. **Luke 23:44–49 — Death and reactions:** darkness, the torn veil, Jesus's final prayer, and responses from witnesses.
7. **Luke 23:50–56 — Burial:** Joseph's dissent and service, the unused tomb, the women's observation, and Sabbath rest.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Luke 23 contributes directly to [[Crucifixion of Jesus Christ]], [[Justice and Mercy]], [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], and [[Death and the Spirit World]]. The second criminal acknowledges his guilt, affirms Jesus's innocence, and appeals to Jesus's kingdom; Jesus promises that he will be with him in paradise that day (23:40-43). The saying supports postmortem fellowship but does not by itself define paradise's location, duration, relationship to resurrection and final judgment, or a complete spirit-world framework.

The Barabbas episode is suggestive for [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]: a man imprisoned for sedition and murder is released while Jesus, repeatedly declared free of capital fault, is condemned. That reversal can be read as an image of substitution, but Luke does not present it as a complete theory of atonement.

The supplied canonical text also records Jesus praying for his executioners' forgiveness (23:34). This is a powerful expression of mercy, although the sentence's place in Luke's earliest recoverable text is disputed and should be acknowledged when substantial doctrine is built upon it.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Royal and saving language recur throughout the chapter. Jesus is accused of claiming to be “Christ a King” (23:2), questioned as “the King of the Jews” (23:3), mocked under that title (23:36-38), and addressed by a criminal anticipating his kingdom (23:42). Forms of “save” recur in the taunts (23:35, 37, 39). The resulting irony contrasts escape from the cross with the saving authority expressed in Jesus's promise of paradise.

“Paradise” in verse 43 names the promised destination or state but does not define its complete relationship to heaven, the world of spirits, resurrection, or final judgment. “Malefactors” (23:32, 39) means criminals, and “Jewry” (23:5) is archaic English for Judea.

Luke calls the execution site “Calvary” in the KJV. Luke's Greek uses the word meaning “Skull,” whereas Matthew and Mark preserve the Semitic name Golgotha and then explain its meaning; these are different linguistic designations of the same traditional location.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Luke develops several reversals. Barabbas, imprisoned for violence, is released while Jesus is condemned despite repeated findings of innocence. Mockers demand that Jesus save himself, while the criminal who acknowledges guilt and appeals to Jesus receives a promise of paradise. Political and religious authorities reject Jesus, while a criminal, a Roman centurion, Joseph the counsellor, and the Galilean women respond through confession, praise, service, or continued witness.

Several scriptural echoes deepen the account. The division of garments and casting of lots closely parallels Psalm 22:18. Jesus's final prayer echoes Psalm 31:5. The cry to mountains and hills resembles Hosea 10:8, though the clauses appear in reversed order in the KJV. These are useful intertextual connections, but Luke does not formally label them as fulfillment citations here, and verbal correspondence alone cannot establish authorial intent.

Jesus's warning to the daughters of Jerusalem redirects their lament toward coming suffering (23:28-31), cohering with warnings in [[Luke 19]] and [[Luke 21]]. The precise referents of the green and dry trees remain disputed.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Cumulative Narrative Testimony
> The chapter repeatedly characterizes Jesus as innocent or righteous through testimony attributed to Pilate, the repentant malefactor, and the centurion. This convergence is strong evidence for Luke's deliberate literary and theological emphasis on Jesus's innocence, although it is not by itself independent historical corroboration of every reported declaration.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly affirmed as literary evidence · [[Cumulative Narrative Testimony in Luke 23|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Typological Parallel
> The detail that the soldiers "parted his raiment, and cast lots" (Luke 23:34) verbally echoes Psalm 22:18, but unlike John (19:24), Luke does not explicitly present the act as fulfillment of that psalm.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · verbal parallel accepted; intention disputed · [[Intertextual/Typological Parallel in Luke 23|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Explicit Dominical Saying Within Narrative
> Luke 23:43 strongly supports the claim that Jesus promised postmortem fellowship to the criminal, but the single saying does not by itself define the location, duration, or complete doctrinal relationship of paradise to resurrection and final judgment.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · affirmed with interpretive diversity · [[Explicit Dominical Saying Within Narrative in Luke 23|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism / Manuscript Evidence *(tentative)*

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Luke repeat declarations of Jesus's innocence through several different characters?
- How does the demand 'save thyself' differ from the kind of salvation Jesus offers in verses 42–43?
- What does the contrast between Barabbas and Jesus contribute to the chapter's treatment of justice?
- Which meanings of the torn temple veil arise from the narrative itself, and which depend on other scriptural texts?
- What can verse 43 establish about paradise, and which larger doctrinal questions must be answered from additional passages?
- How do Joseph and the Galilean women function as witnesses and disciples when many others have rejected or mocked Jesus?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Matthew 27]], [[Mark 15]], and [[John 19]] for shared Passion events and differences in wording, sequence, and distinctive material. Continue into [[Luke 24]], observing how the women who saw the burial return to the tomb.

Psalm 22, Psalm 31, Hosea 10, and Isaiah 53 provide useful backgrounds. When considering Isaiah 53:12, also read [[Luke 22]], where Jesus explicitly applies the words about being reckoned among transgressors to himself; the relationship of that citation to the two criminals in Luke 23 remains interpretive rather than automatically proven.

Relevant topical studies include [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Death and the Spirit World]], [[Kingdom of God]], [[Temples]], [[Sabbath Day]], and [[Government and Laws]].
