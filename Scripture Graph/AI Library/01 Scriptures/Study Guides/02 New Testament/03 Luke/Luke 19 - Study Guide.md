## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Salvation and the lost.** Jesus declares that salvation has come to Zacchaeus's house and summarizes his mission as seeking and saving the lost (19:9-10). His welcome also challenges the crowd's reduction of Zacchaeus to the label “sinner.” See [[Salvation]].

- **Repentance and restitution.** Zacchaeus speaks of giving half his goods to the poor and restoring fourfold where he has taken anything by false accusation (19:8). This can be read as materially consequential [[Repentance]] or as a public defense of his established conduct. The present-tense wording does not decisively settle the question.

- **Stewardship during an interval.** The parable answers an expectation that the [[Kingdom of God]] would appear immediately (19:11). The nobleman's departure, return, and accounting emphasize responsible action with what has been entrusted, but do not supply a timetable or require every detail to be an allegory of the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]].

- **Kingship, compassion, and judgment.** Jesus receives royal acclamation, yet weeps over Jerusalem before warning of its destruction (19:38-44). Luke thus places judgment within a scene of grief rather than detached triumph.

- **The temple and prayer.** Jesus's action and composite prophetic rebuke identify the temple as a house of [[Prayer]] while condemning its depicted misuse (19:45-46). The compressed account does not establish that all pilgrimage commerce was intrinsically wrong or that every participant was personally fraudulent.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
- **“Occupy till I come” (19:13).** Here “occupy” means to conduct business or put the entrusted money to work. Verse 15 confirms this sense by asking how much each servant gained “by trading.”

- **“Time of thy visitation” (19:44).** In the immediate narrative, the phrase concerns Jerusalem's failure to recognize a decisive divine encounter associated with Jesus's arrival. Its full theological scope is interpretive, but the surrounding warning clearly gives the failure an accountability dimension.

- **“Peace in heaven, and glory in the highest” (19:38).** The wording resembles the angelic proclamation of glory and peace in [[Luke 2]]. A deliberate literary echo is plausible, but not marked as an explicit quotation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
- **Seeing and recognition.** Zacchaeus seeks to see Jesus and is himself seen and addressed by him (19:3-5). Later Jesus beholds Jerusalem, while the things belonging to its peace are hidden from its eyes (19:41-42). This supports a literary contrast between reception and failed recognition without proving a formal motif designed around every occurrence.

- **Peace and impending destruction.** The disciples proclaim “peace in heaven” (19:38), but Jesus immediately laments that Jerusalem has not recognized what belongs to its peace and predicts siege (19:41-44). The juxtaposition creates a strong narrative irony.

- **Prophetic temple criticism.** The temple saying combines the house-of-prayer language of [[Isaiah 56]] with the den-of-thieves rebuke of [[Jeremiah 7]]. It is best understood as a composite prophetic allusion rather than as a quotation from only one passage.

- **Acclamation followed by grief.** Royal praise gives way immediately to tears, warning, temple conflict, and lethal opposition. Luke's sequence therefore complicates any uncomplicated reading of the entry as triumph alone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Composite Scriptural Citation
> Jesus interprets his temple action by combining the prophetic descriptions of God's house as a house of prayer and of a corrupted sanctuary as a den of thieves. These correspond to Isaiah 56:7 and Jeremiah 7:11 and frame the action as prophetic criticism of temple practice.
> — strength 1.0 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted composite scriptural citation · [[Explicit Composite Scriptural Citation in Luke 19|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Narrative-Purpose And Parable Structure
> The sequence of departure, reception of a kingdom, return, and subsequent accounting strongly supports reading the parable as correcting an expectation of immediate consummation and emphasizing responsible action during an interval. It does not supply a timetable for the kingdom's final appearance.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted with scope qualifications · [[Narrative-Purpose And Parable Structure in Luke 19|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Scriptural Allusion And Narrative Symbolism
> The unused colt, public procession, spread garments, and acclamation of Jesus as the coming King provide strong textual evidence that Luke presents the entry into Jerusalem in royal and scripturally resonant terms. The colt recalls Zechariah 9:9, while the acclamation adapts Psalm 118:26 by explicitly naming “the King.”
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · royal presentation clear; Zechariah allusion inferred · [[Scriptural Allusion And Narrative Symbolism in Luke 19|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical/Critical Scholarship (Composition Dating) *(tentative)*
> The siege details in Luke 19:43-44 (a surrounding trench, encirclement, leveling of the city with 'not one stone upon another') closely match the actual course of the Roman siege and destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70. This specificity is cited by some scholars as evidence that Luke's Gospel was composed or finalized after AD 70, with the prophecy shaped by knowledge of the outcome.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · major critical argument, genuinely disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Does Luke 19:8 present a new pledge, an existing practice, or a deliberately public declaration whose narrative effect includes both? What changes, and what remains constant, under each reading?
2. Why does the third servant's stated fear fail as a defense when judged by his own description of the ruler?
3. How does Luke's explicit explanation in Luke 19:11 govern interpretations of the parable more securely than speculative identifications of every detail?
4. What kind of kingship is displayed by a King who receives praise yet weeps over the city that resists him?
5. How do Isaiah 56 and Jeremiah 7 together sharpen the meaning of Jesus's temple action?
