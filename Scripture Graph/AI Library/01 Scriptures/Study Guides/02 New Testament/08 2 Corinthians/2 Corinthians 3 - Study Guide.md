## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The community as Christ's letter (vv. 1–3):** Living people, not recommendation letters, authenticate the ministry.

2. **God-given sufficiency (vv. 4–6):** God makes ministers competent for the new covenant, characterized by the life-giving Spirit.

3. **A comparison of glories (vv. 7–11):** Paul reasons from the acknowledged glory of the stone-associated ministry to the greater glory of the ministries of Spirit and righteousness.

4. **Veil and openness (vv. 12–16):** Hope produces bold speech; the Mosaic veil becomes a metaphor for impaired covenantal reading and is removed in Christ.

5. **Liberty and transformation (vv. 17–18):** Unveiled encounter with the Lord's glory culminates in Spirit-enabled transformation.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects divine grace with ministry: human agents are not self-sufficient, but God makes them able ministers (vv. 4–6). Its central contrast should be read within the argument about covenant ministries. The text calls the earlier ministry glorious even while describing its glory as surpassed (vv. 7–11); this is more precise than saying the [[Law of Moses]] was simply evil or worthless.

The final verses connect [[Freedom and Liberty]] with the Spirit's presence and connect [[Conversion]] with transformation into the Lord's image. Here liberty is embedded in turning to the Lord and being changed by the Spirit, not defined as unrestricted personal choice.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase “letter killeth” is bounded by nearby contrasts: ink and Spirit, stone and heart, condemnation and righteousness, passing and enduring glory. Reading it as a universal opposition between literal wording and spiritual interpretation exceeds what the immediate context establishes.

“Old testament” in verse 14 occurs within a discussion of Moses and covenant ministry. Readers should be cautious about assuming that the phrase refers straightforwardly to the later fixed collection of books commonly called the Old Testament.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The argument repeatedly uses paired contrasts: ink/Spirit, stone/heart, letter/Spirit, death/life, condemnation/righteousness, passing/remaining, veil/open face, and bondage/liberty. These contrasts converge on the movement from external inscription and obstructed sight to inward transformation and unveiled vision.

The imagery continues into [[2 Corinthians 4]], where veil, glory, image, and light shining in hearts reappear. Reading the two chapters together clarifies that transformed perception and the revelation of Christ remain central.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Disputed Exegesis *(tentative)*
> Verse 17's statement "the Lord is that Spirit" is one of the most exegetically disputed identifications in Paul's letters, read variously as (a) a functional statement about Christ's Spirit-empowered ministry, (b) evidence of fluid early-Christian language about Christ and the Spirit prior to later fixed doctrinal formulations, or (c) "the Lord" of v.16 (the Exodus text's YHWH) now understood/experienced through the Spirit.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · genuinely disputed verse, superlative claim unsupported

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does Paul intend "the letter killeth" (v.6) as a general principle about reading scripture, or specifically about the ministry of the old covenant, given his elaboration in vv.7-11?
- What exactly is "done away" in verses 7, 11, and 13 — Moses' fading radiance, the Mosaic ministry itself, or both?
- Who or what is the subject that "turn[s] to the Lord" in verse 16 — an individual reader, hardened hearts collectively, or Israel as a people?
- How should "the Lord is that Spirit" (v.17) be understood in relation to other New Testament language distinguishing the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost?
- Does "beholding as in a glass" (v.18) picture seeing the Lord's glory directly, seeing it reflected as in a mirror, or both?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
