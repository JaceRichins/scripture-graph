# 2 Corinthians 7 — Study Guide

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **7:1 — Concluding hinge:** The appeal to cleansing and holiness follows from "these promises," most immediately those at the end of [[2 Corinthians 6]]. (Some critical scholars regard 6:14-7:1 as a displaced or non-Pauline unit, since 7:2's appeal reads as a direct resumption of 6:11-13; this is disputed, not settled.)

- **7:2-4 — Appeal and assurance:** Paul asks to be received, rejects condemnation, and expresses affection and confidence.

- **7:5-7 — Distress and comfort:** Conflict and fear in Macedonia give way to comfort through Titus's arrival and report.

- **7:8-12 — Sorrow and repentance:** Paul explains his changing reaction to the corrective letter and distinguishes godly sorrow from worldly sorrow by their results, building a sevenfold anaphoric list ("yea, what...") in v.11 that catalogs the practical fruits of that sorrow.

- **7:13-16 — Relational restoration:** Titus's joy, the Corinthians' reception of him, and Paul's renewed confidence complete the movement toward reconciliation.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verses 9-10 give one of the New Testament's clearest statements distinguishing two kinds of sorrow: "Godly sorrow worketh repentance to salvation not to be repented of: but the sorrow of the world worketh death." Sorrow itself is not the goal — Paul evaluates it by what it produces: he denies rejoicing merely because the Corinthians were made sorry, and rejoices because their sorrow led to repentance. Verse 11 shows this extended beyond emotion into earnest concern, zeal, and effort to address the matter — but because this is one pastoral episode tied to one crisis, the list should not be converted into a rigid universal formula for judging another person's repentance. This is a key text for [[Repentance and Forgiveness]].

Verse 1 grounds ethical self-cleansing ("filthiness of the flesh and spirit") in "the fear of God," tying moral effort to reverence — relevant to [[Sin]] and [[Obedience]]. Pairing "flesh and spirit" suggests a holistic conception of purity that may include internal disposition, not merely external behavior, though the verse does not specify a detailed anthropology.

The chapter also portrays divine comfort working through ordinary human presence: Paul says God comforted him, then identifies Titus's arrival and report as the immediate means of that comfort — relevant to [[Adversity]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV uses forms of "repent" in two distinguishable ways. In 7:8 Paul means that he regretted sending a painful letter, whereas in 7:9–10 repentance describes the Corinthians' moral response. Context, not the English word alone, controls the sense.

The KJV's "revenge" in 7:11 is liable to misunderstanding. In this disciplinary list it more naturally concerns redress, vindication, or punishment than private retaliation. The exact action Paul has in mind remains unstated.

The phrase "not to be repented of" in 7:10 conveys the absence of subsequent regret or reversal, but the KJV syntax is compressed. It should not be used to build a detailed sequence of salvation without comparison to the sentence as a whole.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 11's list — "what carefulness ... what clearing of yourselves ... what indignation ... what fear ... what vehement desire ... what zeal ... what revenge" — is built on anaphora (repeated "yea, what") for rhetorical intensity, almost forensic in cataloguing the Corinthians' response. Separately, many critical scholars treat 2 Corinthians 6:14-7:1 as an interpolated or displaced unit, since 7:2's "Receive us" reads as a seamless continuation of 6:11-13 without it; this is genuinely disputed and should not be treated as settled.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs

> [!evidence] Internal Textual And Pastoral Evidence
> The chapter provides strong textual evidence that Paul evaluates sorrow by what it produces rather than treating emotional pain as an end in itself: he denies rejoicing merely because the Corinthians were made sorry, rejoices because their sorrow led to repentance, contrasts salvation with death, and enumerates the responses their sorrow produced.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-supported close reading · [[Internal Textual And Pastoral Evidence in 2 Corinthians 7|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Source/Textual Criticism *(tentative)*
> Verse 2's appeal ('Receive us; we have wronged no man...') reads as a direct resumption of the appeal in 6:11-13, which is why a substantial number of critical scholars regard the intervening material (6:14-7:1) as a displaced or non-Pauline unit rather than part of the original argument's flow.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.3 · disputed, minority-to-mixed per critique

> [!evidence] Historical/Textual Criticism *(tentative)*
> The 'letter' Paul says caused the Corinthians sorrow (v.8) is widely discussed by scholars as possibly a separate, non-extant 'severe letter' distinct from canonical 1 Corinthians, since 1 Corinthians' tone does not obviously match the severity implied here.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.4 · genuinely disputed among scholars, but no citable source supplied here

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What promises most naturally supply the antecedent of "these promises" in 7:1, and does the phrase reach beyond the immediate context of [[2 Corinthians 6]]?

- Which responses in 7:11 are inward dispositions, which are observable actions, and which may concern communal discipline?

- Does Paul distinguish godly and worldly sorrow primarily by their source, their orientation, their fruits, or some combination of these?

- What does the chapter actually permit readers to conclude about the wrongdoer, the injured party, and the earlier letter? Which identifications depend on reconstruction from elsewhere in the epistle?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the comfort language of [[2 Corinthians 1]] with its narrative realization here: God comforts Paul through Titus, while Titus has himself been refreshed by the Corinthians. For scriptural comparisons concerning productive and unproductive sorrow, examine [[Mormon 2]] and [[Alma 42]]. These parallels clarify the theme but should not be treated as evidence that every passage defines the psychological experience of repentance identically.
