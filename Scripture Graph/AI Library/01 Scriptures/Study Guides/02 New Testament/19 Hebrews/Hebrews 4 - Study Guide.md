## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has four movements:

1. **The promise remains (4:1–5):** proclamation must be received with faith, and the argument connects God’s rest with [[Genesis 2]].
2. **A renewed “To day” (4:6–11):** the later appeal in [[Psalms 95]] shows that Joshua’s entry into Canaan did not exhaust the promise; the audience is therefore urged to enter diligently.
3. **Exposure before God (4:12–13):** God’s living word discerns inward thought and intention, and no creature is hidden.
4. **Access through the high priest (4:14–16):** Jesus’ heavenly priesthood, sympathy, and sinlessness ground the commands to hold fast and approach the throne of grace.

These divisions are interpretive rather than explicit. Verses 12–13 may be read chiefly as the climax of the preceding warning or as a transition toward verses 14–16. The rest argument begins in [[Hebrews 3]], and the high-priest discussion continues in [[Hebrews 5]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Hebrews 4 teaches that hearing a divine promise is not sufficient without [[Faith]]: the earlier hearers received no profit because the message was not joined with faith. The command to “labour” to enter rest calls for diligent perseverance, although the chapter does not fully define how faith, obedience, and works relate within that exhortation.

The conclusion centers confidence on [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]], the sympathetic and sinless great high priest. Human weakness is acknowledged without making sin inevitable. Although every person is exposed before God, believers are invited to seek mercy and [[Grace]] in time of need.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several dimensions of “rest” converge in the chapter: God’s creation rest, the land-rest associated with Joshua, entry experienced by believers, and a rest that still remains. Hebrews 4:9 uses the distinctive Greek term *sabbatismos*, while surrounding verses use *katapausis* or related forms. This gives the remaining rest a genuine [[Sabbath Day]] resonance, but the word alone neither commands nor abolishes a particular form of weekly observance.

In verse 8, the KJV’s “Jesus” refers to Joshua; both names represent the same Greek form, and the reference to David’s later proclamation fixes the historical identity. The oath in verses 3 and 5 has a negative sense in context, though describing its form specifically as Hebrew rather than Septuagintal or Jewish Greek remains uncertain.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The rest argument is a sustained scriptural rereading rather than a collection of isolated proof texts. God’s rest in [[Genesis 2]] supplies a pattern; Israel’s failure supplies the warning; and the later “To day” of [[Psalms 95]] keeps the invitation open.

Verses 12–13 turn attention to the searching power of God’s word and the hearer’s exposure before God. They can be read as the climax of the warning in verses 1–11 and also prepare, by sequence, for the invitation of verses 14–16. The emotional movement is striking: complete exposure is followed not by an assurance that judgment is unreal, but by an invitation to approach a sympathetic high priest for mercy and grace.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Literary-Intertextual Argument
> Within the author's argument, David's later proclamation of "To day" is strong textual evidence that the promised rest was not exhausted by Israel's entrance into Canaan: a further opportunity remained when the psalm was voiced and remains relevant to the audience.

> [!evidence] Authorship/Historical *(tentative)*
> Hebrews does not name its author, and while it is traditionally associated with Paul (as reflected in some Bible headings and hymnody), authorship has been disputed since antiquity (e.g., differences in style noted by early writers such as Origen) and remains an open question among biblical scholars.

> [!evidence] Linguistic/Idiomatic *(tentative)*
> The clause 'if they shall enter into my rest' (v.3, echoed in v.5) reflects a Hebrew oath-formula convention in which an unfinished conditional ('if...') functions as an emphatic negative ('they shall surely not...'), so the oath is best read as God swearing the wilderness generation would NOT enter His rest, matching v.6's statement that 'they...entered not in because of unbelief.'

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does "rest" have one controlling meaning, or does the author intentionally allow creation, Canaan, Sabbath, present salvation, and future consummation to overlap?
- What does it mean practically to "labour" to enter a rest characterized by ceasing from one's works?
- Should "the word of God" in 4:12 be read principally as the proclaimed warning, as scripture more generally, or as divine speech in a broader sense?
- How do the commands to fear, labor, hold fast, and come boldly qualify one another?
- How does Jesus' sinlessness deepen, rather than diminish, the claim that he understands temptation?
