# Hebrews 7 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Hebrews 7]] · My notes: [[Hebrews 7 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The Melchizedek Priesthood brings exaltation and administers the gospel—It is received with an oath and covenant—The superiority of the Melchizedek Priesthood over the Aaronic Priesthood is explained—Salvation comes through the intercession of Christ.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Melchizedek portrayed (vv. 1–3):** king of Salem, priest of the most high God, receiver of Abraham's tithe and blessing, and one "made like unto the Son of God."
2. **Greater than Abraham and Levi (vv. 4–10):** the argument proceeds through tithing, blessing ("the less is blessed of the better," v. 7), and Levi's ancestral inclusion in Abraham.
3. **A different priesthood and a changed law (vv. 11–19):** Levitical service could not bring the perfection the law promised; the new priest comes from Judah and is constituted "after the power of an endless life" (v. 16).
4. **The oath and a better testament (vv. 20–22):** Psalm 110:4 supplies an oath absent from the appointment of mortal priests, making Jesus "surety of a better testament."
5. **Permanent mediation and a decisive sacrifice (vv. 23–28):** death multiplied former priests, but Christ continues, intercedes "to the uttermost" (v. 25), and offers himself once (v. 27).
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents Christ's priesthood, after the order of [[Melchizedek Priesthood]] rather than the [[Aaronic Priesthood]]/Levitical pattern, as superior because it rests on a divine oath (quoting Psalm 110:4) rather than hereditary descent, making Jesus "surety of a better testament" (v. 22) — see [[Covenants]]. Because Christ "continueth ever," his priesthood is unchangeable and he permanently makes intercession for those who come to God through him (v. 25), tying his priesthood directly to [[Salvation]]. His single self-offering (v. 27) is contrasted with the repeated sacrifices of mortal high priests (see [[Sacrifice]]), underscoring the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. Abraham's tithe to Melchizedek (v. 2) is the chapter's founding example cited for [[Tithing]], though the author's immediate purpose is to establish comparative status, not to command Christian tithing practice. The claim that the [[Law of Moses]] and its priesthood "made nothing perfect" (v. 19) is this author's specific argument about the Levitical/cultic arrangement tied to priestly descent — it should not be read as a full verdict on the Law generally, nor should this chapter be pressed into a complete institutional account of the [[Aaronic Priesthood]] and [[Melchizedek Priesthood]] as later organized. Doctrine and Covenants 107:2-4 explains why the higher priesthood bears Melchizedek's name (out of reverence, to avoid overusing the name of Deity), but does not resolve what Hebrews 7:3 means about Melchizedek's own nature.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Hebrews supplies its own glosses in v. 2: the name Melchizedek is interpreted as "King of righteousness," and his title "King of Salem" is then interpreted as "King of peace" — two separate glosses built into the text itself, not layered on by later commentators. The negations in v. 3 ("without father, without mother, without descent, having neither beginning of days, nor end of life") are disputed language. One reading treats them as an argument from the silence of Melchizedek's portrayal in Genesis 14, where no genealogy, birth, or death is recorded. A related reading takes the phrase as describing the pattern of a priesthood order conferred independent of hereditary descent, rather than commenting on one man's biography — a reading with some kinship to [[Alma 13]]'s description of a priesthood "without beginning of days or end of years" after "the order of the Son." Either way, the description need not be pressed into a literal claim that Melchizedek himself had no parents or never died; v. 3 says he was "made like unto" the Son — a comparison, not an identification.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is a sustained scriptural argument built from [[Genesis 14]] and [[Psalm 110]]. It draws significance from both what the Melchisedec narrative says and what it leaves unrecorded. Its comparisons repeatedly move from temporary to permanent: mortal priests versus the living priest, inherited qualification versus endless life, an earlier commandment versus a better hope, and recurring offerings versus a single self-offering. This cumulative pattern—not any isolated phrase—carries the chapter's case.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Intertextual Scriptural Argument
> The repeated appeal to Psalm 110:4 is strong textual evidence that Hebrews constructs its argument from an earlier scriptural oracle about an enduring priest. It does not, without additional argument, prove that every reader of Psalm 110 before Hebrews understood the oracle in precisely the same christological way.

> [!evidence] Explicit Contextual Identification

> [!evidence] Explicit Sacrificial Contrast

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does Hebrews 7:3 ("without father, without mother...") describe Melchizedek's literal biography, the silence of the Genesis record, or the pattern of a priesthood order not tied to descent? What does the parallel language in Alma 13:7-9 suggest?
- Does "made like unto the Son of God" (v. 3) identify Melchizedek with Christ, or only compare him to Christ in a specific respect (an enduring, non-hereditary priestly office)?
- What kind of "perfection" is in view in vv. 11 and 19, and how does "drawing nigh unto God" help define it?
- How narrowly or broadly should "a change also of the law" (v. 12) be read — does it concern only priestly/cultic legislation, or more?
- How do Christ's continuing intercession (v. 25) and his once-for-all offering (v. 27) fit together rather than compete?
- Doctrine and Covenants 107:2-4 explains why the higher priesthood is named for Melchizedek; how does that differ from, and leave open, the question this chapter raises about Melchizedek's own nature?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Genesis 14]] for the brief narrative underlying vv. 1-10, then [[Psalm 110]] for the oath cited in vv. 17 and 21. Compare [[Hebrews 5]] and [[Hebrews 6]] for the introduction of Christ's Melchizedek-order priesthood, and [[Alma 13]] for a Book of Mormon description of a priesthood "without beginning of days or end of years." [[Hebrews 8]], [[Hebrews 9]], and [[Hebrews 10]] develop the better covenant, heavenly ministry, and once-for-all sacrifice that this chapter introduces.
