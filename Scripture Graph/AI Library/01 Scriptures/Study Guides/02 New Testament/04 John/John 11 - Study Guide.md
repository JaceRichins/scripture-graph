# John 11 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[John 11]] · My notes: [[John 11 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus testifies that He is the Resurrection and the Life—Mary and Martha testify of Him—He raises Lazarus from the dead—Caiaphas speaks prophetically of the death of Jesus.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Illness, delay, and danger (vv. 1–16):** Lazarus becomes ill; Jesus delays and then resolves to return to Judaea despite the threat of stoning.
2. **Martha's encounter (vv. 17–27):** Martha moves from lament and future hope to a confession of Jesus as Christ and Son of God.
3. **Mary, mourning, and Jesus's tears (vv. 28–38):** Mary's parallel lament brings Jesus into the community's grief.
4. **The tomb and the raising (vv. 39–44):** Jesus prays publicly and commands Lazarus to come forth.
5. **Belief and the death plot (vv. 45–54):** Reactions to the miracle lead to the council, Caiaphas's statement, and the decision to kill Jesus.
6. **Approaching Passover (vv. 55–57):** Pilgrims seek Jesus while the authorities order that his location be reported.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's doctrinal center is Jesus's declaration, “I am the resurrection, and the life” (John 11:25). Martha already affirms “the resurrection at the last day” (11:24); Jesus then locates resurrection and life in his own person. The passage therefore joins future [[Resurrection]] with present [[Faith]] in Jesus, without requiring readers to treat Martha's earlier hope as mistaken.

Lazarus's return from the tomb enacts Jesus's life-giving authority within the narrative, but it should not simply be equated with final, immortal resurrection. The chapter describes Lazarus emerging still bound in graveclothes and being loosed by others.

Faith and grief are not presented as opposites. Martha believes while questioning and expressing practical concern, and Jesus knows what he will do while still becoming troubled and weeping. Hope does not require emotional detachment from death.

Jesus's public [[Prayer]] presents the sign as testimony concerning his relationship with the Father: he speaks for the witnesses' benefit, “that they may believe that thou hast sent me” (11:42).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three repeated verbal patterns organize the chapter:

- **Glory and belief:** God's glory frames the illness in verse 4 and reappears at the tomb in verse 40; belief is the stated purpose in verses 15 and 42 and the response of many witnesses in verse 45.
- **Sleep and death:** The disciples' misunderstanding in verses 11–14 moves the dialogue from metaphor to plain speech. The metaphor communicates death's reversibility under Jesus's authority but, by itself, does not define the condition of the dead.
- **The sisters' repeated lament:** Martha in verse 21 and Mary in verse 32 use the same words. The repetition highlights shared trust in Jesus's power as well as shared pain over his absence.

The expression “the Jews” must be read locally rather than as a label for a uniformly hostile people. In this chapter Jewish mourners comfort the family, many believe, and a particular leadership council resolves to kill Jesus.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter concentrates irony and reversal. Jesus's stated love stands beside his delay. The act that restores Lazarus's life leads through the witnesses' report to a plan for Jesus's death. Caiaphas proposes that one man die to preserve the nation, while the narrator interprets the same words as prophecy that Jesus will die for others and gather God's scattered children.

The sisters' identical laments create a deliberate parallel: Martha's encounter develops through extended teaching and confession, while Mary's leads into communal grief, Jesus's tears, and movement toward the tomb. Jesus's disturbance is clear, but the text does not isolate one cause; grief, compassion, indignation at death, and reaction to unbelief remain possible and potentially overlapping readings.

A comparison with [[John 20]] is suggestive but should remain modest. Lazarus emerges still wrapped and must be loosed; Jesus's burial cloths are found left behind. The contrast is consistent with a distinction between Lazarus's restoration and Jesus's resurrection, though deliberate typology is not proven.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary Evidence
> The four-day burial, Martha's expectation of decomposition, the closed tomb, and Lazarus's emergence in graveclothes form strong internal evidence that the narrator intends readers to understand Lazarus as genuinely dead, not merely asleep or mistakenly diagnosed.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Narrative intent unambiguous · [[Internal Literary Evidence in John 11|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Philological/Translation *(tentative)*
> The KJV phrase 'he groaned in the spirit, and was troubled' (John 11:33, repeated in substance at 11:38) translates a Greek verb (embrimaomai) that in other Greek usage more commonly denotes anger, indignation, or a snort of strong emotion than gentle sorrow, so the underlying tone of Jesus's reaction is philologically ambiguous between grief and something closer to indignation (e.g., at death itself, at unbelief, or at the mourners).
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Lexical nuance plausible but specific emotional readings disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrator place the affirmation of Jesus's love immediately before the report of his delay?
- What changes—and what does not change—in Martha's understanding between verses 21 and 40?
- Is Jesus's distress directed chiefly toward death, the mourners' pain, unbelief, or some combination? The text presents the emotion more clearly than it identifies its single cause.
- Why does a life-giving miracle generate both belief and a decision to kill Jesus?
- How does the narrator distinguish Caiaphas's intended political meaning from the theological meaning assigned to his words?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
