# John 20 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[John 20]] · My notes: [[John 20 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
John 20 moves from an opened tomb and competing explanations to encounters with the risen Jesus, commissioned witnesses, and an explicit statement of the Gospel's purpose. Mary Magdalene is the first person in the chapter to see and recognize Jesus. He then appears among the fearful disciples, shows his hands and side, gives peace, sends them, and commands them to receive the Holy Ghost. Thomas's demand for bodily evidence leads to the confession, “My Lord and my God.” The conclusion turns from the first witnesses to later readers: selected signs were written so that they might believe and have life through Christ's name. The chapter is therefore simultaneously about [[Resurrection]], [[Faith]], [[Testimony]], and [[Records and Sacred Writings]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The opened tomb and incomplete understanding (20:1–10).** Mary infers removal of the body; Peter and the other disciple inspect the grave clothes; the other disciple believes, although the scripture about resurrection is not yet understood.

2. **Mary moves from grief to witness (20:11–18).** She sees without recognizing, recognizes Jesus when he calls her name, and is sent to the disciples.

3. **The gathered disciples move from fear to mission (20:19–23).** Jesus gives peace, shows his wounds, sends them, and speaks of the Holy Ghost and forgiveness.

4. **Thomas moves from refusal to confession (20:24–29).** Jesus answers his demand for evidence; Thomas responds, “My Lord and my God.”

5. **The reader is invited to believe (20:30–31).** The narrator explains that a purposeful selection of signs has been written to lead to belief and life.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The risen Jesus is presented as continuous with the crucified Jesus: he shows the disciples his hands and side and later invites Thomas to examine those wounds (John 20:20, 27). The text records the invitation but does not say that Thomas actually touched him. It also twice places Jesus among disciples gathered behind shut doors without explaining how he entered (John 20:19, 26).

Jesus grounds the disciples' mission in his own sending by the Father, breathes on them, commands them to receive the Holy Ghost, and speaks of remitting and retaining sins (John 20:21–23). This joins mission, Spirit, and responsibility concerning forgiveness, but the chapter alone does not define whether verse 23 refers primarily to gospel proclamation, community discipline, an ordinance, or some combination. These themes support study of [[Resurrection]], [[Missionary Work]], [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], and [[Repentance and Forgiveness]].

Thomas directly addresses Jesus as “My Lord and my God” (John 20:28), while the purpose statement identifies Jesus as the Christ and Son of God and connects belief in him with life through his name (John 20:31). These statements are theologically weighty but do not, by themselves, resolve every later debate about [[The Godhead]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The verbs and ideas of seeing and believing recur throughout the chapter. Mary sees the moved stone but initially interprets it as evidence of removal. The other disciple sees the grave clothes and believes. Mary, the disciples, and Thomas see Jesus; later readers receive written signs and testimony instead of direct sight. This progression gives substance to the blessing on those who have not seen.

The repeated question of where Jesus has been “laid” sustains Mary's mistaken framework until Jesus calls her by name. Her reply, “Rabboni,” marks the movement from failed visual recognition to personal recognition.

Jesus says, “my Father, and your Father; and to my God, and your God” (20:17). The balanced wording joins Jesus' followers to his relationship with God while preserving a verbal distinction between “my” and “your”; it should not be flattened into claims more specific than the sentence itself.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly transforms an incomplete but understandable response: apparent body removal gives way to resurrection witness, Mary's weeping to recognition, the disciples' fear to peace and mission, and Thomas's refusal to confession. The conclusion extends that movement to readers, who are invited to believe through selected written signs rather than through direct sight (John 20:29–31).

The repeated concern with where Jesus has been “laid” structures Mary's misunderstanding (John 20:2, 13, 15). She asks the unrecognized Jesus where his own body has been placed, creating narrative irony that ends when he calls her by name (John 20:16).

A possible new-creation resonance deserves cautious attention. Jesus' act of breathing while speaking of the Holy Ghost (John 20:22) provides the strongest comparison with God's life-giving breath in [[Genesis 2]]. The first-day setting, darkness, garden, and gardener can contribute to that reading, but each also has an ordinary narrative explanation. The chapter never explicitly labels the cluster as a creation allusion.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual/Theological
> Thomas responds to Jesus directly with 'My Lord and my God' (John 20:28), a second-person address ascribing the title 'God' to Jesus, and the text records no rebuke or correction from Jesus for this address.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · Direct address established; doctrinal implications disputed · [[Textual/Theological in John 20|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Textual Comparison
> Jesus tells Mary 'Touch me not; for I am not yet ascended to my Father' (John 20:17), yet only about a week later, before any ascension has occurred in the narrative, he explicitly invites Thomas to 'reach hither thy hand, and thrust it into my side' (John 20:27). Read together, these two statements are in tension if v17 is understood as a general, standing prohibition on touching the resurrected body.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · Textual contrast established; interpretation disputed · [[Internal Textual Comparison in John 20|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Inferential Apologetic Argument *(tentative)*
> The orderly separation of the napkin from the linen clothes (John 20:6-7) is sometimes cited as evidence against a theft/grave-robbery explanation for the empty tomb, on the reasoning that thieves would be unlikely to pause and neatly arrange burial cloths.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.4 · Popular but speculative apologetic inference

> [!evidence] Doctrinal Interpretation Of Narrative Setting *(tentative)*
> The authority to remit and retain sins (John 20:22-23) is granted corporately to the gathered disciples together with the reception of the Holy Ghost, situating it within a structured/apostolic setting rather than presenting it as a purely private transaction between an individual and God.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Corporate setting established; institutional reading disputed

> [!evidence] Literary Intertextuality *(tentative)*
> A cluster of creation-like details plausibly supports a new-creation reading: the narrative begins on the first day while it is dark, the tomb is located in a garden and Mary supposes Jesus is the gardener, and Jesus breathes while imparting the Holy Ghost. These details resemble the first creation and God's breathing life into the first human, although intentional allusion is an interpretation rather than an explicit statement.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · Plausible but uneven cumulative allusion

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What precisely does the other disciple believe in John 20:8, given the qualification in verse 9?

- Why does Mary fail to recognize Jesus until he speaks her name?

- Does Thomas actually touch Jesus, or is Jesus' invitation itself sufficient for his confession?

- How should “Touch me not” in verse 17 be understood alongside Jesus' invitation to Thomas in verse 27?

- What is the relationship between receiving the Holy Ghost in John 20:22 and the events of [[Acts 2]]?

- Which aspects of belief in this chapter depend on direct sight, testimony, Scripture, signs, or the written Gospel?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the resurrection narratives in [[Matthew 28]] and [[Luke 24]] without assuming in advance that every difference must be either a contradiction or an easy harmony. Compare the invitation to examine Christ's wounds with [[3 Nephi 11]]. Read John 20:28 beside [[John 1]]:1 to trace the Gospel's presentation of Jesus, and John 20:8–9 beside [[John 2]]:19–22 for the relationship among resurrection, memory, Scripture, and belief. For the possible creation imagery, compare John 20:1, 15, and 22 with [[Genesis 1]] and [[Genesis 2]].
