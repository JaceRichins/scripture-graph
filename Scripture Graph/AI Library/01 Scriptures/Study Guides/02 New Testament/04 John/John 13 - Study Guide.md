## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **13:1-3** — Setting: Jesus' hour has come; he knows his origin, authority, and destiny.
2. **13:4-17** — Jesus washes the disciples' feet, addresses Peter's resistance ('ye are clean, but not all'), and commands mutual service as an example.
3. **13:18-30** — Jesus cites Psalm 41:9 and discloses his betrayer; the beloved disciple asks who it is; Judas receives the sop and departs — 'and it was night.'
4. **13:31-35** — Jesus announces his glorification and gives the new commandment to love one another ([[Charity]]).
5. **13:36-38** — Peter pledges his life for Jesus; Jesus predicts Peter will deny him three times before the cock crows.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Service as the pattern of true greatness.** Knowing 'that the Father had given all things into his hands' (13:3), Jesus uses that authority to take a servant's place, then commands the disciples to do likewise (13:14-15) — see [[Service]] and [[Humility]].

**Love as the mark of discipleship.** The 'new commandment' (13:34-35) makes Jesus' own self-giving love the standard and mutual love a visible sign of discipleship — see [[Charity]]. Love of neighbor is not new to scripture; what is new here is the example just enacted and the standard 'as I have loved you.'

**Cleansing and participation.** Jesus tells Peter that without being washed he has 'no part' with him (13:8), suggesting the footwashing carries meaning beyond ordinary hospitality. The chapter does not itself identify the washing with [[Baptism]] or any specific ordinance, and that identification should be held tentatively.

**Glory located in the passion.** Jesus calls the unfolding events — beginning with Judas's departure — the moment the Son of Man 'is glorified' (13:31-32), a distinctly Johannine framing in which glory encompasses the coming suffering, not only the resurrection.

**Foreknowledge and betrayal.** Jesus foreknows both Judas's betrayal and Peter's denial, and the text attributes the betrayal partly to Satan's influence (13:2, 27) without explaining how that influence relates to Judas's own responsibility — a tension the chapter leaves open rather than resolves.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
A knowledge/misunderstanding motif runs through the chapter: Jesus knows his hour, origin, authority, and betrayer, while Peter does not yet understand the washing (13:7) and the table companions misunderstand Jesus' words to Judas (13:28).

Jesus' act of laying aside his garments and taking them up again (13:4, 12) has been read by some as an enacted preview of his statement that he has power to 'lay down' his life and 'take it again' ([[John 10]] 10:17-18); the underlying Greek vocabulary for laying aside/taking up does overlap between the passages, though this alone does not prove the parallel was intended by John rather than being ordinary language for removing and resuming a garment or a life.

John 13:18's citation — 'he that eateth bread with me hath lifted up his heel against me' — draws directly on Psalm 41:9's picture of betrayal by a trusted companion, explicitly marked by the text itself as scripture being fulfilled.

The phrase 'whither I go, ye cannot come' (13:33, 36) echoes words Jesus had spoken to hostile questioners in [[John 8]] (8:21); the repetition is a real verbal echo, though the two settings carry different meanings — unbelief and death in sin in chapter 8, a temporary separation followed by reunion ('thou shalt follow me afterwards') in chapter 13.

The narrator's note that Judas 'went immediately out: and it was night' (13:30) is explicitly a time marker; a symbolic reading tied to John's broader light/darkness imagery is plausible but not stated by the verse itself.

Peter's confident pledge to lay down his life (13:37), immediately met with a prediction of denial (13:38), is completed later in the Gospel when a third denial is followed at once by the cock's crow ([[John 18]]).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Fulfillment Citation
> John 13:18's citation ('He that eateth bread with me hath lifted up his heel against me') is presented by the text itself as scripture being fulfilled, and is a clear allusion to Psalm 41:9, applying a psalm about betrayal by a trusted friend to Judas's act.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · broadly accepted · [[Fulfillment Citation in John 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Predictive Claim
> Within the narrative, Jesus states that he is disclosing the betrayal beforehand so that its occurrence may become a reason for the disciples to believe. This securely identifies the narrative's intended evidentiary function, but the passage by itself is not independent historical proof that the prediction preceded the event.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 1.0 · accepted observation / disputed historical significance · [[Internal Predictive Claim in John 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intra-Narrative Prediction And Fulfillment
> John's later account narrates a third denial by Peter followed immediately by the cock crowing, creating a direct literary fulfillment of Jesus' prediction in John 13:38. This is strong evidence of deliberate narrative correspondence but, because prediction and fulfillment occur in the same Gospel, only limited independent evidence for historical foreknowledge.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 1.0 · accepted observation / disputed historical significance · [[Intra-Narrative Prediction And Fulfillment in John 13|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Redaction/Omission Pattern
> Unlike the Synoptic accounts of the Last Supper (which narrate Jesus instituting the bread and cup), John's Last Supper account in this chapter contains no institution of the sacrament and instead centers on the foot washing — a well-noted feature of John's version of the meal.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · accurate literary contrast, keep modest · [[Redaction/Omission Pattern in John 13|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does John's account of the Last Supper include no institution of the bread and cup, unlike the Synoptics — does John presuppose that tradition, relocate its themes elsewhere (e.g., [[John 6]]), or have a different purpose in view?
- What does Jesus mean in telling Peter, 'If I wash thee not, thou hast no part with me' (13:8) — is the emphasis on cleansing, fellowship, or something the chapter does not spell out?
- In what sense is the commandment to love one another 'new' (13:34), given that love of neighbor is already commanded in the Law?
- How should the chapter's language of Satan's influence on Judas (13:2, 27) be understood alongside Judas's own responsibility for his act?
- Does the threefold structure of Peter's denial anticipate the threefold questioning of [[John 21]], and if so, what might that suggest about restoration after failure?
