# John 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[John 15]] · My notes: [[John 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus is the vine; His disciples are the branches—He discourses on the perfect law of love—His servants have been chosen and ordained by Him—The world hates and fights true religion—He promises the Comforter, the Spirit of Truth.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The true vine and fruitful branches (15:1-8).** The Father cultivates; Jesus supplies the branches' life; abiding branches bear fruit.
2. **Love, joy, and friendship (15:9-17).** Abiding in love is connected with obedience, full joy, mutual love, self-giving, disclosure, and chosen vocation.
3. **Hatred from the world (15:18-25).** The disciples should expect treatment continuous with Jesus' own; Jesus' words and works also intensify his opponents' accountability.
4. **Twofold witness (15:26-27).** The Spirit of truth testifies of Jesus, and the disciples bear witness from their experience with him.

The repeated command to love one another in verses 12 and 17 frames the friendship and vocation material between them. Verse 16 also recalls the opening image by returning to fruit that should remain.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The vine image depicts grace as sustained dependence: fruitful discipleship is possible only through continuing union with [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]. Yet dependence is not passive. Abiding becomes concrete in [[Obedience]], mutual [[Charity]], enduring fruit, and witness.

The prayer promises of verses 7 and 16 should be read with their conditions intact. They concern people in whom Jesus' words abide and whom he has appointed to bear fruit; they are not presented as unrestricted authorization for every desire.

Verses 22-24 contribute an important principle concerning [[Sin]]: greater revelation can bring greater accountability. The verses do not describe Jesus' opponents as formerly sinless; they explain why rejection after his words and works leaves them without an excuse for this rejection.

The closing promise contributes to study of [[The Godhead]] and the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]]: the Father, Son, and Spirit are distinguishable within the discourse, while the Spirit's work is specifically Christ-centered testimony.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The dominant KJV term is “abide,” with nine forms in verses 4-10. The underlying Greek verb *menō* also stands behind “continue” in verse 9 and “remain” in verse 16, giving the passage greater lexical unity than the English wording immediately reveals. The Textus Receptus behind the KJV also uses this verb in verse 11, whereas major critical editions have a different reading there.

A second Greek echo joins verses 2 and 3: *kathairei* (“prunes” or “cleans,” rendered “purgeth”) and *katharoi* (“clean”) are cognate. This supports literary cohesion between the Father's cultivation and Jesus's declaration that the disciples are clean through his word. It does not establish the language or exact wording of Jesus's original oral teaching.

The KJV's “ordained” in verse 16 translates an ordinary verb meaning to place or appoint. The word can describe a genuine commission, but by itself it does not identify a particular later ordination rite.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The repeated command to love one another in verses 12 and 17 frames the teaching on self-giving love, friendship, disclosure, choosing, and lasting fruit. Verse 16 also recalls the opening vine image by returning to fruit that should remain.

The “true vine” plausibly evokes scriptural depictions of Israel as a vine or vineyard, especially [[Psalm 80]] and [[Isaiah 5]]. Against this background, Jesus can be read as the locus or representative of faithful covenant life and fruitfulness. John 15 does not explicitly cite either passage, however, and familiar agricultural imagery remains an important alternative explanation.

Verse 25 explicitly introduces scriptural fulfillment and closely echoes Psalm 35:19 and Psalm 69:4. The safest conclusion is that opposition to Jesus is interpreted through a scriptural pattern of the righteous sufferer hated without cause; the exclusive source cannot be identified with confidence.

The chapter also employs contrasts such as fruitful and fruitless branches, abiding and separation, friends and servants, and disciples and the hostile world. The servant language in verses 15 and 20 serves different immediate comparisons—knowledge in verse 15 and shared treatment in verse 20—although readers may still recognize a literary seam between the sayings.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary-Linguistic
> The Greek text makes a verbal connection between the Father's pruning and the disciples' cleanness: kathairei in John 15:2 and katharoi in John 15:3 are cognate forms. The KJV partially obscures the echo by translating them as “purgeth” and “clean.” This strongly supports a deliberate transition from the vine metaphor to Jesus' declaration that his word has cleansed the disciples.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted philological observation · [[Literary-Linguistic in John 15|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Scriptural Intertext
> The wording “They hated me without a cause” has close antecedents in Psalm 35:19 and Psalm 69:4. This is strong evidence that John frames opposition to Jesus through scriptural patterns of the unjustly hated righteous sufferer, although the precise Psalm intended is uncertain.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · intertext certain; precise source disputed · [[Scriptural Intertext in John 15|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Literary Parallel *(tentative)*
> The "true vine" self-designation plausibly draws on a recurring Old Testament figure in which Israel/Judah is depicted as God's vine or vineyard (e.g., Isaiah's song of the vineyard, the vine brought out of Egypt in the Psalms, and the desolated vine in Ezekiel), which would reposition Jesus (and abiding discipleship) rather than national/covenant identity as the source of fruitfulness.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · probable background; disputed interpretation

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What kinds of “fruit” are specified or implied by the immediate context: obedience, love, witness, new disciples, or some combination?
- How do Jesus' words abiding in disciples shape what they ask in prayer?
- Why does the command to love one another frame the teaching about friendship and chosen vocation?
- How can disciples distinguish opposition caused by fidelity to Jesus from opposition caused by their own misconduct? John 15 promises persecution but does not teach that every criticism proves faithfulness.
- What does the pairing of the Spirit's testimony with eyewitness testimony suggest about the relationship between spiritual conviction and historical witness?
