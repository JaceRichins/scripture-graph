# John 12 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[John 12]] · My notes: [[John 12 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Mary anoints Jesus’ feet—His triumphal entry into Jerusalem is recounted—He foretells His death—To receive Christ is to receive the Father.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Anointing and opposition at Bethany (vv. 1–11):** Mary's costly act is linked to Jesus's burial; Lazarus attracts both seekers and a death plot.
2. **Royal entry into Jerusalem (vv. 12–19):** the crowd receives Jesus with language from Psalm 118, and the colt evokes Zechariah 9:9. The disciples understand the scriptural connection retrospectively.
3. **The hour of death and glory (vv. 20–36):** Greeks seek Jesus; he responds by announcing his hour, using the dying grain to explain fruitful death and calling servants to follow him.
4. **Unbelief and concealed belief (vv. 37–43):** Isaiah 53:1 and Isaiah 6:10 frame rejection, while fear of exclusion prevents some rulers from confessing their belief.
5. **Concluding declaration (vv. 44–50):** Jesus summarizes his relation to the Father, his mission as light and salvation, and the judging function of his word.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Jesus's death is not presented merely as something done to him. He acknowledges distress, yet says that he came to this hour for a purpose (vv. 27–28). The grain-of-wheat image joins [[Sacrifice]] to fruitfulness, and verses 25–26 extend that pattern to discipleship.

The chapter also distinguishes salvation from the denial of judgment. Jesus says that he came to save the world (v. 47), but the rejected word will judge in the last day (v. 48). Judgment arises in relation to the revelation offered rather than from an absence of saving intent.

Verses 44–50 strongly unite Jesus's mission with the Father's authority while continuing to distinguish sender from sent. Jesus reveals the Father, speaks the Father's commandment, and identifies that commandment as eternal life.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly redefines apparent defeat as glory. Jesus's approaching death is his "hour" of glorification (v. 23); a seed dies and bears fruit (v. 24); and being "lifted up" signifies the manner of his death while also carrying an elevated resonance (vv. 32–33).

Light and darkness supply a second controlling contrast. The crowd is urged to walk and believe while the light is present (vv. 35–36), and Jesus later summarizes his mission as coming into the world as light (v. 46). The imagery makes response urgent: delay permits darkness to overtake the hearer.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two juxtapositions are especially meaningful. First, Lazarus's restored life generates both witness and a plan to kill him (vv. 9–11, 17–18), showing that a sign can disclose character rather than mechanically produce faith. Second, the Pharisees complain that "the world" has followed Jesus, and the next people introduced are Greeks seeking him (vv. 19–21). This likely functions as narrative irony and prepares for Jesus's promise to draw all people when lifted up (v. 32), although the text never explicitly says that the Greeks themselves met Jesus.

The narrator also makes retrospective interpretation explicit. The disciples do not understand the royal-entry symbolism at first; after Jesus is glorified, they remember the relevant scriptures and actions (v. 16). This offers an internal account of how the Gospel connects remembered events with [[Records and Sacred Writings]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Intertextual/Quotation Analysis *(tentative)*
> John frames Jesus's donkey-riding entry as a scriptural fulfillment ('as it is written') using the words 'Fear not, daughter of Sion: behold, thy King cometh, sitting on an ass's colt' (John 12:15), which is commonly identified with Zechariah 9:9, though the opening 'Fear not' does not match the traditional 'Rejoice greatly' opening of that oracle, suggesting a paraphrase or conflation rather than a verbatim citation.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · Zechariah connection established; exact source form disputed

> [!evidence] Literary Sequencing *(tentative)*
> The immediate movement from the Pharisees' complaint that the world has followed Jesus to the arrival of Greeks wishing to see him is strong evidence of deliberate narrative irony and anticipates the widening scope of Jesus's mission.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · Juxtaposition observable; deliberate irony disputed

> [!evidence] Prophecy Interpretation *(tentative)*
> John's application of Isaiah 53:1 to unbelief in Jesus treats the Isaiah 53 'Suffering Servant' material as messianic prophecy fulfilled in Jesus; this is a New Testament interpretive move rather than a claim about the original historical referent of Isaiah 53, whose identity (the nation of Israel, a righteous remnant, or an individual servant/prophet) is disputed among biblical scholars independent of Christian reading.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · Christological reuse clear; whole-chapter implication uncertain

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the request of the Greeks prompt Jesus to announce that his hour has come?
- How do Mary's costly devotion and Judas's stated concern for the poor expose different motives?
- What distinction does the chapter make between believing privately and confessing publicly?
- How can the statements about divinely described hardening in verses 38–40 be read alongside the fear and love of praise described in verses 42–43?
- In what sense can Jesus come to save rather than judge while his word still judges in the last day?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the anointing with [[Matthew 26]] and [[Mark 14]], attending to differences in the woman's identity, the anointed part of Jesus's body, the objectors, and each narrator's emphasis. Read Psalm 118:25–26 and Zechariah 9:9 beside the entry narrative. Then compare John's explicit use of Isaiah 53:1 and Isaiah 6:10 with their original contexts. These comparisons clarify how John combines royal expectation, suffering, revelation, and unbelief without requiring every source passage to carry exactly the same emphasis in its original setting.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
