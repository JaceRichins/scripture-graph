# John 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[John 1]] · My notes: [[John 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Christ is the Word of God—He created all things and was made flesh—John baptizes Jesus and testifies that He is the Lamb of God—John, Andrew, Simon, Philip, and Nathanael believe in Christ and follow Him.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The Word and his coming into the world (1:1–18):** The Word is with God, is God, creates all things, gives life and light, is rejected by some, received by others, and becomes flesh.

2. **John's testimony (1:19–34):** John denies being the Christ, identifies himself as Isaiah's preparing voice, explains his baptism, and testifies that Jesus is the Lamb and Son of God upon whom the Spirit remained.

3. **The first followers (1:35–51):** Witness leads to encounter: two disciples follow, Andrew brings Simon, Jesus calls Philip, and Philip invites Nathanael. The section ends with the promise of heaven opened upon the Son of man.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **The Word made flesh:** John 1:1, 14 identify Jesus as pre-existently divine ("the Word was God") and as taking on mortality ("the Word was made flesh, and dwelt among us"), foundational statements about [[Condescension of God]] and [[The Godhead]]. The prologue is strong evidence for what this Gospel teaches about Jesus's nature, though it does not itself settle later debates about the precise relationship between Father and Son.
- **Grace and truth alongside the law:** John 1:16-17 contrasts "the law...given by Moses" with "grace and truth" that "came by Jesus Christ." The verse does not call the law false or graceless; it affirms the law's divine origin while emphasizing what comes through Christ.
- **Spiritual rebirth:** John 1:12-13 describes becoming a "son of God" through receiving and believing on Christ's name, a birth attributed to God rather than to blood or human will.
- **Christ as the Lamb of God:** John's twofold declaration (1:29, 36) associates Jesus with taking away sin on a universal scale. Passover, temple sacrifice, and Isaiah's suffering servant are all plausible backgrounds for this title; the chapter itself specifies none of them exclusively.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter supplies its own translations for several terms: “Rabbi” as “Master” (1:38), “Messias” as “the Christ” (1:41), and “Cephas” as “A stone” (1:42). These explanatory glosses help readers follow titles or names drawn from languages other than the narrative's Greek setting.

“Word,” “life,” “light,” “darkness,” “grace,” and “truth” form the prologue's principal conceptual vocabulary. Their meanings develop through repetition rather than through formal definitions. In particular, the statement that darkness “comprehended” the light not (1:5) should not be made to bear a single detailed doctrine without examining the underlying language and translation history.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Witness language unifies the chapter: John comes to bear witness (1:6-8), bears witness to the incarnate Word (1:15), gives his record to the Jerusalem delegation (1:19-28), and bears record of the Spirit and the Son of God (1:32-34). That public testimony becomes personal invitation as Andrew and Philip bring others to Jesus. The narrative half is also organized as a sequence of days, advancing recognition of Jesus's identity through escalating titles (Rabbi, Lamb of God, Messias, Son of God, King of Israel, Son of man).

The repeated invitation "Come and see" (1:39, 46) links inquiry with direct encounter: Jesus offers it to the first two followers, and Philip repeats it to answer Nathanael's skepticism about Nazareth.

The closing saying (1:51) evokes [[Genesis 28]]'s vision of angels ascending and descending, relocating the image from Jacob's ladder to "the Son of man." This supports reading Jesus as a focal point of heaven's disclosure, though the chapter does not explain the allusion further.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Textual Evidence
> John 1 provides strong internal textual evidence that the Gospel portrays Jesus as preexistent and divine: the Word is placed before creation and credited with creating all things, becomes flesh, and is identified in the ensuing exposition with Jesus Christ.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-scoped internal evidence claim · [[Internal Textual Evidence in John 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Philological *(tentative)*
> The KJV phrase "only begotten" (John 1:14, 1:18) translates Greek monogenes, a term whose core sense is "one of a kind / unique" rather than necessarily "the only one begotten"; this is a live philological question, and most modern English translations render it as "one and only" or "only" rather than "only begotten."
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed philology, omits 1:18 variant

> [!evidence] Historical/Background *(tentative)*
> The three titles John denies (Christ, Elias, "that prophet") map onto distinct figures of Second Temple Jewish end-time expectation: a Messiah, a returning Elijah (based on Malachi 4:5), and "the prophet" like Moses (based on Deuteronomy 18:15). The chapter's framing of this interrogation supports, but does not by itself prove, that such categories were live, distinguishable expectations among Jerusalem's religious leaders in this period.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · accepted observation, disputed historical claim
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the narrator emphasize both that the Word was “with God” and that the Word “was God” (1:1)? What conclusions are secure from those two statements, and which depend on later theological systems?

What does the contrast between Moses and Jesus in 1:17 affirm, and what does it not say about the [[Law of Moses]]?

Why are John's denials in 1:20–21 as important to his testimony as his positive declarations about Jesus?

How does each person in 1:35–51 come to believe, and how much understanding does each title or confession demonstrate at that stage?

What details does the narrator leave unexplained—the unnamed disciple, the fig tree, the tenth hour, and the promised greater things—and why should interpreters resist filling those gaps with certainty?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare John 1:1–5 with [[Genesis 1]] for the shared sequence of beginning, creation, and light. Compare John 1:23 with [[Isaiah 40]] to examine how the wilderness voice prepares the Lord's way. Read John 1:32–34 alongside [[Matthew 3]], [[Mark 1]], and [[Luke 3]], noting that John reports the Baptist's testimony rather than directly narrating Jesus's baptism. Compare John 1:51 with [[Genesis 28]], paying attention to how the ascending-and-descending imagery is relocated to the Son of man. [[D&C 93]] offers a Restoration-era reuse and expansion of several themes and expressions from the prologue.
