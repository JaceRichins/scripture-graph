# John 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[John 5]] · My notes: [[John 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus heals an invalid on the Sabbath—He explains why men must honor the Son—Jesus promises to take the gospel to the dead—Man is resurrected, judged, and assigned his glory by the Son—Jesus obeys the divine law of witnesses.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **John 5:1-9 — Healing at Bethesda.** Jesus heals a man who had been infirm for thirty-eight years.
2. **John 5:10-18 — Sabbath controversy.** Carrying the bed on the Sabbath provokes opposition; Jesus answers by appealing to the Father's own continuing work, which the Jews hear as a claim to equality with God.
3. **John 5:19-30 — Life and judgment through the Son.** The Son acts in full alignment with the Father, has been given authority to give life and to execute judgment, and describes both a present passing from death to life and a future bodily resurrection.
4. **John 5:31-40 — Corroborating witnesses.** John the Baptist, Jesus's works, the Father, and the scriptures are named as testimony to his identity.
5. **John 5:41-47 — Diagnosis of unbelief.** Jesus traces the hearers' unbelief to seeking honor from one another, and closes by naming Moses himself as their accuser.

Jesus later appears to recall this healing in [[John 7]] (7:21-23), where he again defends a Sabbath healing as "one work" — a probable, though not textually certain, backward reference.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**[[The Godhead]]**: vv19-30 hold together two threads without collapsing either one. The Son shares the Father's work and is to be "honoured... even as they honour the Father" (v23), having been given authority to judge (v22) and "to have life in himself" (v26). At the same time, "The Son can do nothing of himself, but what he seeth the Father do" (v19), and Jesus "seek[s] not mine own will, but the will of the Father" (v30). The passage supplies real material for both an exalted and a functionally-dependent reading of the Son; it does not by itself settle later creedal debates about their metaphysical relationship.

**[[Resurrection]] and [[Final Judgment]]**: the Father "hath committed all judgment unto the Son" (v22), and "the hour is coming...when the dead shall hear the voice of the Son of God" (v25). Believers are described as already having "passed from death unto life" (v24), while a future hour brings a bodily resurrection: "they that have done good, unto the resurrection of life; and they that have done evil, unto the resurrection of damnation" (v29). This is a two-fold division; the text itself does not subdivide further into multiple degrees of glory.

**[[Scripture Study]]**: "Search the scriptures...they are they which testify of me" (v39) makes scripture a witness to Christ, but Jesus's complaint is that the hearers study without coming to him (v40) — the criticism targets refusal to believe, not the practice of study itself. The closing appeal to the [[Law of Moses]] makes Moses himself a witness for or against the hearers (v45-46).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV's description of an angel troubling the pool's water (the end of v3 and all of v4) is one of the best-known textual variants in the New Testament: it is absent from the earliest surviving Greek manuscripts (including P66, P75, Sinaiticus, and Vaticanus) and is generally regarded by textual critics as a later scribal explanation added to account for the impotent man's remark in v7 ("while I am coming, another steppeth down before me"). No doctrine in the chapter depends on these verses, though they do shape how readers picture the healing's mechanism.

Several other phrases repay close attention:
- "The hour is coming, and now is" (v25) sits ambiguously beside the unambiguous future "hour" of v28 ("all that are in the graves shall hear his voice"); readers differ on whether v25's "dead" are the spiritually dead, an inaugurated resurrection reality, or both.
- "If I bear witness of myself, my witness is not true" (v31) is best read in light of the witnesses that follow — the issue may be the sufficiency of unsupported self-testimony rather than a claim that Jesus's own testimony is factually false.
- "Sin no more, lest a worse thing come unto thee" (v14) does not identify the man's thirty-eight-year infirmity as punishment for a specific sin, and does not specify what the "worse thing" is.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two motifs bind the chapter's halves together. A visible **work** — the healing — triggers the controversy, and Jesus later argues that his works are themselves a witness that the Father sent him (v36). **Life** runs the same course: the man's restored body in vv1-9 expands into teaching about [[Eternal Life]] and bodily resurrection in vv24-29.

The witness argument builds cumulatively — John the Baptist, works, the Father, scripture, and finally Moses — and ends ironically: those who trust Moses are accused by the very writings in which they place their confidence (vv45-46).

Two further parallels are worth noting without over-claiming: "If I bear witness of myself, my witness is not true" (v31) stands in surface tension with "Though I bear record of myself, yet my record is true" in [[John 8]] (8:14), usually resolved by distinguishing legal sufficiency from objective truth; and Jesus's healing command, "Rise, take up thy bed, and walk" (v8), echoes the synoptic paralytic healing in [[Mark 2]] ("Arise, and take up thy bed, and walk," 2:9) — almost certainly a different event, but a striking shared idiom.

The doubled "Verily, verily" formula (vv19, 24, 25) is a Johannine signature marking the discourse's most solemn claims.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism
> The description of an angel periodically troubling the pool's water (John 5:4, and the last clause of v3) is absent from the earliest extant Greek witnesses (including Papyrus 66, Papyrus 75, Codex Sinaiticus, and Codex Vaticanus) and is bracketed or footnoted as a probable later scribal addition in modern critical editions, even though it stands in the KJV text translated from later manuscripts.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted among textual critics · [[Textual Criticism in John 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Theological Evidence
> John 5 provides strong internal textual evidence for an elevated portrayal of the Son: he gives life, receives authority to judge, and is to be honored as the Father is honored. The same discourse also distinguishes the Son from the Father and describes authority and life as given by the Father.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · accepted observation / disputed theological synthesis · [[Internal Theological Evidence in John 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Explicit Doctrinal Statement
> John 5:28-29 is strong textual evidence that this discourse teaches a future resurrection followed by differentiated outcomes associated with what people have done.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted · [[Explicit Doctrinal Statement in John 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Consistency *(tentative)*
> Jesus's warning 'sin no more, lest a worse thing come unto thee' (v14) could be read as implying a link between the man's prior sin and his 38-year infirmity, but this reading sits in tension with Jesus's explicit denial of a universal sin-suffering causation in the case of the man born blind ('Neither hath this man sinned, nor his parents,' John 9:3); both texts should be weighed together rather than using one to settle the other.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · disputed; tension somewhat overstated

> [!evidence] Archaeology *(tentative)*
> Archaeological excavations near St. Anne's Church in Jerusalem uncovered a double-pool complex with surrounding colonnades that many scholars identify with the Bethesda described in John 5:2, which is sometimes cited as evidence that the evangelist had accurate, pre-70 CE topographical knowledge of Jerusalem rather than inventing the site for theological purposes.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · site identification accepted; sourcing unverifiable

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. What specific "feast of the Jews" is referenced in v1? John does not say, and the identification affects how scholars reconstruct the length of Jesus's public ministry from John's feast-markers.
2. What is the "worse thing" threatened in v14, and does the warning imply anything about the cause of the man's original infirmity?
3. Does "the dead" who "shall hear the voice of the Son of God" in v25 refer to the spiritually dead, the physically dead, or both — and how does that relate to the explicit bodily resurrection of vv28-29?
4. Is v18's charge of "making himself equal with God" the Jews' hostile inference, the narrator's own framing, or both?
5. Which of Moses's writings does Jesus have in mind in v46? The chapter does not name a specific passage.
<!-- SG:END questions -->
