# John 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[John 3]] · My notes: [[John 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus tells Nicodemus that men must be born again—God so loved the world that He sent His Only Begotten Son to save men—John the Baptist testifies that he that believes on the Son has everlasting life.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
Verses 1-10 move through Nicodemus's questions and Jesus's correction of his physical, literalistic reading of 'born again.' Verses 11-21 broaden into heavenly witness, the Son of Man 'lifted up' like Moses' serpent (vv.14-15), God's love in sending the Son (vv.16-17), and the language of light and darkness (vv.19-21). Verses 22-26 narrate a bridge: Jesus's and John's overlapping baptizing ministries and a dispute about purifying. Verses 27-36 give John the Baptist's final recorded testimony — the bridegroom's friend who rejoices in another's increase (vv.29-30) — closing with heavenly-testimony language that echoes vv.11-13. Exactly where Jesus's own words end and the evangelist's commentary begins (around v.15-16), and likewise where John the Baptist's words end and narration resumes (around v.31), is a genuine and long-disputed question that punctuation alone cannot settle; the thematic throughline — belief yields life, unbelief yields condemnation — holds regardless of how that line is drawn.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
This chapter is foundational for [[Conversion]], [[Baptism]], [[Faith]], [[Salvation]], and [[Eternal Life]]. Being 'born again'/'born of water and of the Spirit' (vv.3,5) is presented as necessary, not optional, for entering the kingdom of God, and the chapter's own narrative about baptizing (vv.22-26) lends contextual support to reading 'water' in connection with [[Baptism]] — though the chapter never uses that word, and some readers instead hear 'water' as a callback to Nicodemus's own question about natural birth (v.4). God's love is presented as the motive for sending the Son to save rather than condemn the world (vv.16-17). Judgment in vv.18-21 is tied to a person's response to light and to whether their deeds are evil or 'wrought in God' — a present, disclosive dimension of judgment — but the passage does not eliminate a divinely-rendered dimension: v.18 speaks of one already 'condemned,' and v.36 of God's wrath 'abiding' on the unbeliever, language stronger than mere self-selection. References to Father, Son, and Spirit throughout (vv.5-6, 16-17, 34-35) supply material for [[The Godhead]] without offering a systematic formulation. John the Baptist's 'He must increase, but I must decrease' (v.30) models [[Humility]] toward Christ.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter contains three of John's Gospel's distinctive doubled 'Verily, verily' sayings (vv.3, 5, 11), a formula the Synoptics use only in single form. The word behind 'born again' can also mean 'born from above'; Nicodemus's confused reply about re-entering the womb (v.4) suggests he hears 'again,' and the ambiguity may be a deliberate double meaning rather than simply a translation puzzle — though this depends on the Greek text John wrote, not necessarily on the original spoken exchange. The wind/Spirit comparison in v.8 likewise trades on a word that can mean both 'wind' and 'spirit.' Verse 3 speaks of 'seeing' the kingdom of God while verse 5 speaks of 'entering' it — a shift that may be rhetorically significant but is not explained in the text itself. 'Doeth truth' (v.21) is a notable turn of phrase: truth here is something enacted, not merely affirmed.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly contrasts origins and responses: flesh and Spirit, earth and heaven, darkness and light, rejection and reception, condemnation and life. Nicodemus's approach 'by night' (v.2) precedes a discourse that later associates avoiding the light with evil deeds; the juxtaposition is suggestive, but the text never directly states that Nicodemus himself is characterized this way. The Gospel later recalls the detail explicitly — John 19:39 identifies Joseph of Arimathea's helper as 'Nicodemus, which at the first came to Jesus by night' — confirming the author treated the detail as worth remembering, though the burial scene doesn't itself say whether this was a public or still-cautious act. John the Baptist offers a contrasting literary pattern: rather than treating Jesus's rising following as rivalry, he casts himself as the bridegroom's friend who rejoices in another's increase (vv.29-30).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Scriptural Typology
> John 3 explicitly compares Moses lifting up the wilderness serpent with the necessary lifting up of the Son of Man, connecting belief in the Son with eternal life. This is strong textual evidence for a deliberate typological comparison, though it does not by itself define every intended correspondence between the serpent episode and Jesus.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · well-established · [[Explicit Scriptural Typology in John 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Incidental Historical/Geographical Detail *(tentative)*
> John baptized at Aenon near Salim specifically 'because there was much water there,' a detail sometimes cited as incidental support that John's baptism required a substantial body of water consistent with immersion.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · disputed

> [!evidence] Interpretive/Theological Argument *(tentative)*
> The phrase 'born of water and of the Spirit' (v.5) is widely read, including in Latter-day Saint teaching, as referring to baptism paired with reception of the Holy Ghost; however, this reading is not unanimous among biblical scholars, some of whom read 'water' as a reference to ordinary physical birth set in contrast with spiritual rebirth, or as an allusion to Ezekiel's promise of end-time cleansing water and a new spirit.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · disputed

> [!evidence] Literary Motif *(tentative)*
> Nicodemus's nighttime arrival and the later contrast between darkness and light create a possible literary association between his incomplete understanding and darkness. The association is plausible but remains implicit because the narrator does not directly explain why Nicodemus came at night.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does 'water' in John 3:5 refer to [[Baptism]], to Nicodemus's own image of natural birth (v.4), or to a single unified image of spiritual renewal?
- Where does Jesus's direct speech to Nicodemus end and the evangelist's commentary begin (v.15? v.21?) — and does John the Baptist's testimony similarly give way to narration before v.36?
- Why does Jesus shift to 'we' in v.11 — a rhetorical plural, an appeal to a wider circle of witnesses, or a contrast with Nicodemus's own opening 'we know' (v.2)?
- What does the dispute over purifying between John's disciples and 'the Jews' (v.25) suggest about how contemporaries understood the relationship between John's baptism and Jesus's?
- Is Nicodemus's nighttime visit meant to carry symbolic weight given the chapter's later light/darkness language, or is it simply a chronological detail?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare John 3:14-15 with [[Numbers 21]] and [[Helaman 8]] to see how the brazen-serpent narrative is applied to Christ, and with [[John 12]], where 'lifted up' is tied explicitly to the manner of Jesus's death. The water-and-Spirit language (v.5) is sometimes compared with [[Ezekiel 36]]'s promise of cleansing water and a new spirit, though direct dependence is not demonstrated by the text itself. The love-and-sending language of vv.16-17 has a close parallel in [[1 John 4]]. The chapter's recurring testimony/witness vocabulary also recurs throughout John generally (e.g., [[John 5]] on judgment and belief, [[John 6]] on belief unto eternal life) rather than being unique to this chapter's two discourses.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
