## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Renewed creation and covenant presence (21:1-4):** a new heaven and earth, the descending city, and God dwelling with his people.
2. **Divine declaration and contrasting destinies (21:5-8):** all things made new, water offered to the thirsty, inheritance for the conqueror, and the second death for persistent evil. The declaration 'I am Alpha and Omega, the beginning and the end' (v. 6) also opens ([[Revelation 1]]) and closes ([[Revelation 22]]) the book, and 'It is done' (v. 6) repeats the proclamation that closed the bowl judgments ([[Revelation 16]]) — the same words now marking the completion of renewal rather than judgment.
3. **The bride-city displayed and measured (21:9-21):** Jerusalem's walls, tribal gates, apostolic foundations, cubic dimensions, and precious materials. The three gates per side, each named for a tribe of Israel, closely track the city plan of [[Ezekiel 48]].
4. **Life in the city (21:22-27):** God and the Lamb as temple and light, nations entering through perpetually open gates, and the exclusion of defilement.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**God dwelling with his people.** Verse 3's declaration that 'the tabernacle of God is with men' answers the temple/tabernacle theme running through scripture: God's presence, once mediated through a tent and veil, becomes direct and permanent. This governs the chapter's picture of [[Salvation]]: freely given life (v. 6), filial inheritance (v. 7), release from death and sorrow (v. 4), and entrance into God's illuminated city (vv. 24-27).

**God and the Lamb as the temple.** Verse 22 states there is no temple in the city 'for the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are the temple of it.' The singular predicate places Father and Son together in one shared temple-function and source of light (v. 23) — relevant to [[Temples]] and [[The Godhead]] — though the verse establishes shared function and presence rather than settling later, more technical questions about their relationship.

**Final judgment and exclusion.** Verses 8 and 27 both draw a firm boundary: certain classes of persistent sin have 'their part in the lake which burneth with fire,' and only those written in the Lamb's book of life may enter, tying to [[Final Judgment]].

**The earth's celestial glory (LDS reading).** The chapter's official heading reads this vision through [[Kingdoms of Glory]], understanding the earth as destined to be sanctified and 'crowned with glory, even with the presence of God the Father' (D&C 88:19) — a doctrine drawn from modern revelation that extends, rather than directly restates, this chapter's own wording.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The new-creation announcement (v. 1) recalls [[Isaiah 65]]'s 'new heavens and a new earth,' and the description of nations and kings walking in the city's light through gates that never shut, needing no sun or moon (vv. 23-26), closely reworks [[Isaiah 60]]'s vision of a light-filled Zion. The angel's measuring of the city (vv. 15-17) and its gates named for the twelve tribes (vv. 12-13) closely parallel [[Ezekiel 48]]'s concluding temple-city vision — with a pointed inversion: Ezekiel's vision culminates in an elaborate temple, while Revelation declares the final city needs no separate temple at all (v. 22). Taken together, these are not isolated echoes but a sustained, deliberate reworking of earlier prophetic city-visions.

The city's equal length, breadth, and height (v. 16) may also evoke the cube-shaped Holy of Holies of Solomon's temple, which had the same equal proportions (1 Kings 6:20) — an intriguing but unproven allusion, since cube/foursquare shapes elsewhere in the ancient world can signal totality or perfection without necessarily invoking a temple's inner sanctuary. Similarly, the twelve named foundation stones (vv. 19-20) may echo the twelve stones of the high priest's breastplate (Exodus 28:17-20), though the specific stones and their order differ between the two lists, so the correspondence remains suggestive rather than exact.

The phrase 'the twelve apostles of the Lamb' (v. 14) also appears in [[1 Nephi 13]], which the verified text-overlap index confirms shares several verses of language with this chapter — a real overlap worth noting, though it may reflect a shared KJV-era idiom about 'the Lamb and his apostles' as much as any deliberate literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Intertextuality
> The convergence of multiple specific correspondences with Isaiah and Ezekiel is strong evidence that Revelation 21 deliberately reworks earlier prophetic visions of Zion, restoration, and divine presence, rather than merely sharing one isolated image with them.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted · [[Literary Intertextuality in Revelation 21|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Tension / Interpretive Crux *(tentative)*
> Verses 24 and 26 describe 'the nations' and 'kings of the earth' streaming into the city with their glory, language that reads as broadly inclusive, yet verse 27 immediately restricts entry to those written in the Lamb's book of life, excluding anything that defiles or works abomination or a lie. The chapter does not resolve how these two statements fit together, and this is a genuine, long-debated interpretive crux rather than a settled point.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · reframed — not an established contradiction

> [!evidence] Literary/Typological Parallel *(tentative)*
> The twelve-stone foundation catalog may echo the twelve stones of the high priest's breastplate, since both traditions enumerate exactly twelve named precious stones tied to the twelve tribes/apostles motif, though the specific stone names and their order differ between the two lists.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does 'no more sea' (v. 1) describe physical geography, the symbolic removal of chaos and threat, or both?
- How should readers relate the city's international openness (vv. 24, 26 — the nations and kings bring their glory in) to its firm exclusion of anything that defiles (v. 27)? Verse 24 already qualifies the nations as 'saved,' which may resolve more of the apparent tension than it first seems to pose.
- Are the city's measurements (12,000 furlongs cubed; a 144-cubit wall) meant literally, symbolically, as multiples of twelve signifying completeness, or some combination?
- What changes when the bride is shown not as an individual woman but as an entire inhabited city (vv. 9-10)?
- In Latter-day Saint thought, how does this vision of new Jerusalem descending relate to the modern-day gathering to [[Zion]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Revelation 22]] as the continuation of this vision rather than as an unrelated scene. Compare Revelation 21:1 with Isaiah 65:17; Revelation 21:23–26 with Isaiah 60:3, 11, 19–20; and Revelation 21:12, 15 with Ezekiel 40:3 and 48:30–35. These comparisons clarify how the chapter presents [[Zion]] through inherited prophetic language while modifying earlier temple-city expectations.
