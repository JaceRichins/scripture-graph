## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Measured sanctuary and trampled city (11:1–2):** The temple, altar, and worshippers are measured, while the outer court and holy city are given to the Gentiles for forty-two months.
2. **Commissioned witnesses (11:3–6):** Two witnesses prophesy for 1,260 days in sackcloth and are described as olive trees and candlesticks.
3. **Death and public humiliation (11:7–10):** After finishing their testimony, they are killed by the beast and denied burial while the earth-dwellers celebrate.
4. **Divine vindication (11:11–13):** God restores them to life and calls them upward before their enemies; an earthquake follows.
5. **The seventh trumpet (11:14–18):** Heaven proclaims divine reign, judgment, and reward.
6. **The opened heavenly temple (11:19):** The ark appears amid lightning, voices, thunder, earthquake, and hail.

Temple scenes therefore bookend the canonical chapter, although whether this constitutes a deliberately designed inclusio remains an interpretive judgment.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter emphasizes faithful [[Testimony]] and prophetic witness under persecution. The witnesses are protected until their testimony is complete, but the text does not promise immunity from suffering; their death is answered by divine restoration and vindication. Verses 15–18 place that drama within the triumph of the [[Kingdom of God]], the rewarding of God's servants, and [[Final Judgment]]. Their return to life also makes [[Resurrection]] a direct and important study connection. The ark may evoke God's enduring [[Covenants]], though the chapter does not explicitly explain its significance.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Forty-two months and 1,260 days are equivalent if calculated with a schematic thirty-day month, but Revelation 11 does not explicitly state either that calendar or the equivalence. Together with the three and one-half days, these periods can be read as presenting suffering as severe yet bounded; literal, symbolic, and combined readings remain disputed.

The city is called Sodom and Egypt "spiritually." These names characterize it through biblical associations such as corruption and oppression rather than functioning as ordinary geographical identifications. The additional clause, "where also our Lord was crucified" (11:8), strongly points toward Jerusalem while leaving room for wider symbolic meaning.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter draws on several earlier scriptural patterns with differing degrees of clarity. The olive-tree and candlestick imagery strongly recalls [[Zechariah 4]]. The witnesses' power over rain evokes Elijah, while blood and plagues evoke Moses and the Exodus. Their restoration to life closely resembles [[Ezekiel 37]], where divine breath enters the dead and they stand upon their feet. The movement from a beast's warfare against God's servants to judgment and divine dominion also parallels [[Daniel 7]].

These correspondences strongly support reuse of Israel's prophetic imagery, but they do not identify the witnesses as Moses, Elijah, or any other named figures. Their death, restoration, and ascent also bear a looser resemblance to the vindication of Jesus, although the chronology and sequence are not identical.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does the measuring protect or mark: a physical sanctuary, God's worshipping people, divine ownership, or overlapping meanings?
- Are the two witnesses individual prophets, representative figures, or both?
- Are the forty-two months and 1,260 days chronological, symbolic, or both, and what justifies the calendar used?
- Does the remnant's giving glory to God in verse 13 indicate repentance or compelled acknowledgment?
- How should [[Doctrine and Covenants 77]] guide a Latter-day Saint reading while preserving the symbolic and literary features visible in Revelation itself?
