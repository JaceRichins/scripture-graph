# Revelation 17 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Revelation 17]] · My notes: [[Revelation 17 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The woman shown (17:1–6):** John is taken into the wilderness and sees the richly adorned woman, her golden cup, her name, and her violence against the saints.

2. **The beast interpreted (17:7–14):** The angel explains its career, the seven heads, and the ten horns. The section culminates in the kings' war against the Lamb and the Lamb's victory.

3. **The woman interpreted and destroyed (17:15–18):** The waters are identified as peoples and nations, the horns turn against the woman, and she is identified as the great ruling city.

Revelation 21:9–10 repeats the angelic invitation and spirit-transport pattern while revealing the bride and holy Jerusalem, creating a strong contrast between two woman-cities.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] as the Lamb whose authority exceeds every political claim: he is Lord of lords and King of kings, and those with him are called, chosen, and faithful. Hostile powers can wage war against him, but their authority is temporary and their defeat is assured.

The vision also associates [[Final Judgment]] with the collapse of corrupt power. Babylon's own allies become agents of her destruction. Revelation 17:13–17 attributes agreement and action to the kings while also placing events within God's purpose; it does not explain precisely how human agency and divine sovereignty relate.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The name “Babylon” draws on scriptural memories of oppressive empire. [[Jeremiah 51]] offers especially close background: Babylon dwells upon many waters and is pictured as a golden cup that makes the earth drunk. Revelation adapts those images and identifies the waters as peoples, multitudes, nations, and tongues.

The beast's description as one who “was, and is not” resembles an inversion of Revelation's language for God's enduring existence. This plausibly casts the beast as a doomed imitation of divine sovereignty, although deliberate parody remains an interpretation.

The angel explains several symbols, but not every detail. In particular, the identities and enumeration of the kings remain disputed.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The imagery joins splendor with horror: purple, scarlet, gold, jewels, pearls, and a golden cup accompany abomination and bloodshed. It also stages a sharp reversal. The woman appears to dominate peoples and kings while riding the beast, but powers allied with that beast eventually destroy her.

[[Revelation 17]] and [[Revelation 21]] form a strong antithetical pair. In both scenes one of the seven vial-bearing angels invites John to come and see, John is carried away in the spirit, and a woman associated with a city is revealed. The wilderness vision exposes Babylon; the mountain vision reveals the bride and holy Jerusalem.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical Identification
> In the first-century setting, the combination of seven mountains, worldwide royal influence, luxury, and violence against Jesus's witnesses strongly supports Rome as the woman's primary historical referent. Calling the city Babylon continues an established Jewish and early Christian practice of using an earlier imperial oppressor as a symbolic name for Rome. This historical identification need not exhaust the image's broader application to oppressive imperial systems.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broad historical-critical consensus · [[Historical Identification in Revelation 17|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Identification *(tentative)*
> Ancient writers already called Rome 'the city on seven hills,' and the angel's own gloss that the woman is 'that great city, which reigneth over the kings of the earth' (17:18) fits the Roman Empire's status in the late first century. This supports (but does not require) reading the immediate, first-audience referent of 'Babylon' as imperial Rome.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.7 · historically strong but insufficiently sourced here

> [!evidence] Internal Chronological Marker *(tentative)*
> Some scholars use the 'five fallen, one is, one not yet come' sequence of kings (17:10) to try to date Revelation's composition to a specific emperor's reign, but there is no agreed starting point for counting Roman emperors, so the method yields conflicting candidates.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · disputed and assumption-dependent
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Which symbols does the angel explicitly interpret, and which details remain open?

- What does the woman's destruction by powers allied with the beast suggest about the stability of corrupt political systems?

- How strongly do the seven mountains and ruling-city description point to Rome, and why need that historical identification not exhaust the symbol?

- Why is identifying Rome generally more secure than constructing a precise list of seven emperors?

- How does the contrast between [[Revelation 17]] and [[Revelation 21]] clarify the book's competing visions of communal identity, power, and glory?

- What does verse 17 affirm about God's purpose, and what questions about human agency does it leave unanswered?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
