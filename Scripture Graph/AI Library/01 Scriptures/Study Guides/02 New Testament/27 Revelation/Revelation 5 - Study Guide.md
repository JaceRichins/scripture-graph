# Revelation 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Revelation 5]] · My notes: [[Revelation 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Revelation 5 continues the throne-room vision of [[Revelation 4]] without interruption. A sealed book in God's right hand cannot be opened, or even looked upon, by anyone in heaven, on earth, or under the earth (5:1-3), and John weeps (5:4). An elder announces that the 'Lion of the tribe of Juda, the Root of David' has prevailed to open it, but John instead sees 'a Lamb as it had been slain' (5:5-6), who takes the book (5:7). A new song grounds the Lamb's worthiness in his death and in redeeming 'every kindred, and tongue, and people, and nation' (5:9-10). Worship then widens outward — from the four beasts and elders, to an innumerable company of angels, to every creature in heaven, earth, under the earth, and the sea — praising the Lamb alongside the one on the throne (5:11-14). The book itself is not yet opened; the seals begin to be broken in [[Revelation 6]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The inaccessible book (5:1-4):** no creature in heaven, earth, or under the earth is found worthy to open or even look on the book, and John weeps.
2. **The worthy Lamb (5:5-7):** the announced Lion/Root of David appears instead as a slain-yet-standing Lamb and takes the book from the throne.
3. **The new song (5:8-10):** the four beasts and twenty-four elders, with harps and incense identified as the prayers of saints, sing that the Lamb is worthy because he was slain and has redeemed a multinational people made kings and priests.
4. **The widening doxology (5:11-14):** an innumerable company of angels acclaims the Lamb, then every creature in heaven, earth, under the earth, and the sea directs praise to both the one on the throne and the Lamb, and the elders worship.

The threefold 'heaven...earth...under the earth' language of v.3 recurs (with the sea added) in v.13, framing a movement from universal inability to universal praise — though this exact three-realm phrase is also a common totality formula elsewhere (compare Philippians 2:10), so the correspondence may be a conventional idiom rather than a tightly engineered inclusio. The book itself is not opened in this chapter; only the search for a worthy opener is resolved.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter grounds worthiness in sacrificial death: the Lamb is 'worthy' because he 'wast slain' (v.9), reframing the announced Lion's 'prevailing' through the lens of the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] rather than replacing power or conquest outright. Those redeemed 'by thy blood' from every nation are made 'kings and priests' who 'shall reign on the earth' (v.10) — language that recalls, without simply restating, the covenant description of Israel as 'a kingdom of priests' (Exodus 19:6); the images are related (a corporate priestly nation vs. an individual dual office) but not identical, and Revelation extends a priestly-royal vocation to a multinational redeemed people rather than displacing Israel's. The golden vials 'full of odours, which are the prayers of saints' (v.8) present [[Prayer]] as a symbolic offering received in the heavenly court. Verse 13's shared doxology to 'him that sitteth upon the throne, and...the Lamb' gives the Lamb an exceptionally exalted role in this vision, bearing on questions treated under [[The Godhead]] — though this scene alone does not settle every question about the Father-Son relationship.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter's most consequential image is the Lion-Lamb juxtaposition. John is told to behold a conquering Lion but actually beholds a Lamb marked by slaughter (5:5–6). The subsequent song explains the relationship: the Lamb is worthy because he was slain (5:9). Power is therefore presented through sacrificial victory, not merely through coercive force.

Seven recurs in the seals, horns, eyes, Spirits, and seven-part acclamation (5:1, 5–6, 12). Completeness is a plausible symbolic value, but the chapter itself explicitly interprets only the seven eyes as the seven Spirits of God.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The vision moves from silence and grief to expanding praise: no one can open the book (v.3); once the Lamb takes it, the throne attendants sing (v.8), an innumerable company of angels answers (v.11), and finally every creature in heaven, earth, under the earth, and the sea joins the doxology (v.13-14). The countless-host formula 'ten thousand times ten thousand' (v.11) closely echoes Daniel's throne-court vision (Daniel 7:10), and the golden vials of incense identified as 'the prayers of saints' (v.8) anticipate the same image in [[Revelation 8]] and recall the Psalmist's comparison of prayer to incense (Psalm 141:2). These are strong probable allusions rather than certain literary citations, since Revelation never names its sources and shared apocalyptic idiom is a live alternative explanation for each.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does the sealed book contain? Revelation 5 describes its appearance and inaccessibility but never states its contents directly; [[D&C 77]] offers a Restoration-specific interpretive key that should be read as external to this chapter's own wording.
- Why is John told of a Lion but shown a Lamb? The new song ties worthiness to the Lamb's death, but whether this is a deliberate literary reversal or simply the coexistence of two messianic images remains debated.
- Does the shared doxology of v.13 ('him that sitteth upon the throne, and...the Lamb') equate the Lamb with God, or portray an exalted but distinct heavenly figure receiving derivative honor?
- What do the Lamb's seven horns signify? The text explicitly glosses the seven eyes as 'the seven Spirits of God' (v.6) but leaves the horns uninterpreted.
- Does the redeemed community's identity as 'kings and priests' who 'shall reign on the earth' (v.10) point specifically to Revelation's later millennial reign (compare Revelation 20:4,6), or describe a broader eschatological vocation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
