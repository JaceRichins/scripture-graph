## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter unfolds in three movements:

1. **Seals 1-4** (Revelation 6:1-8) — a repeated pattern: a living creature says 'Come and see,' and a horse and rider appear. The pattern is consistent but not identical in every detail (only the first seal is introduced with 'the noise of thunder,' and each rider is described differently).
2. **Seal 5** (Revelation 6:9-11) — the scene shifts from action to persons: martyred souls beneath the altar plead for judgment and are given white robes and a period of waiting.
3. **Seal 6** (Revelation 6:12-17) — cosmic and universal: creation convulses, and every named class of people, from kings to bondmen, responds identically, in terror.

Doctrine and Covenants 77:6-7 offers a Latter-day Saint framework for reading the seven seals (traditionally, one per thousand years of earth's temporal history); that is an interpretive overlay worth noting, not something stated in Revelation 6 itself.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Read together, the seals resist a simple picture of either unchecked evil or instant retribution. Authority and instruments of harm — the crown, the sword, the power given the fourth rider — are each described as 'given' (Revelation 6:2, 4, 8) rather than self-generated, which many readers take as implying bounded, permitted authority; the text itself never names who gives it, so this remains an interpretation rather than a stated doctrine. The martyrs of the fifth seal are heard and clothed in white but told to 'rest yet for a little season' rather than being given immediate vengeance (Revelation 6:9-11) — justice acknowledged but deferred, not denied. The sixth seal's roll call of kings, great men, rich men, captains, and slave and free alike (Revelation 6:15) makes the point that rank and power offer no refuge from 'the wrath of the Lamb' (Revelation 6:16) — a phrase that sits in deliberate tension with the Lamb's identity, established in [[Revelation 5]], as the one who was slain. The chapter presents that tension without resolving it.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The passive construction 'was given' (traditionally called a 'divine passive') recurs several times across the first six seals (Revelation 6:2, 4, 8, 11) — though not at every stage, since seals 3 and 6 lack it — and is often read as implying an unnamed divine agent behind these events, though the text itself never names the giver. The chapter is also selective in its detail: only the fourth rider is given a name ('Death,' with Hell following, Revelation 6:8), while the first rider is never identified, which is part of why his character remains genuinely disputed. Separately, the KJV's 'pale horse' (Revelation 6:8) translates a Greek color term (*chloros*) more often rendered 'green' or sickly yellow-green — the same root as English 'chlorophyll' — suggesting a corpse-like pallor rather than simple paleness; a widely noted point in commentaries, though it rests on the underlying Greek rather than the English text alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Revelation 6 draws on established Old Testament judgment imagery rather than inventing new figures. The sixth seal's collapsing heavens — the sky rolled up like a scroll, stars falling like untimely figs (Revelation 6:13-14) — echoes Isaiah 34:4's scroll-and-falling-fig imagery; the resemblance is thematic and structural rather than word-for-word (a mechanical check finds no verbatim overlap), so it is better read as reuse of shared prophetic vocabulary than as direct citation. The fourth rider's instruments — sword, hunger, death, and beasts (Revelation 6:8) — closely match the 'four sore judgments' of Ezekiel 14:21 (sword, famine, evil beasts, and pestilence), a recognized parallel in Revelation scholarship, again without claiming verbatim quotation. The terrified cry 'Fall on us, and hide us' (Revelation 6:16) reuses the appeal to the mountains found in Hosea 10:8; Jesus invokes the same image on the road to the crucifixion, foretelling that the people of Jerusalem would one day cry these very words themselves (Luke 23:30) — the same Old Testament image reapplied across three different moments of impending judgment. The first horseman's identity is genuinely ambiguous: his white horse and crown recall the explicitly Christ-identified white-horse rider of [[Revelation 19]], but his place among three otherwise destructive figures leads many readers to resist a straightforward identification with Christ.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical/Economic Background *(tentative)*
> The third seal's pricing — a full day's wage (a 'penny,' i.e., a denarius, elsewhere in the New Testament equated with a laborer's daily wage) buying only enough wheat for one person, or barley for a household, with oil and wine spared — reads as a scene of severe grain-price inflation consistent with famine conditions, since normal Roman-era grain prices were markedly cheaper relative to a denarius.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Dating Argument *(tentative)*
> Some historical-critical readers connect 'hurt not the oil and the wine' to a reported edict of the emperor Domitian ordering a reduction of provincial vineyards (to boost grain production), which met resistance and was reportedly not enforced — used as one argument for dating Revelation to Domitian's reign (c. AD 95). This remains one disputed proposal among several read­ings of the phrase.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.3 · disputed, minority dating argument
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Is the first horseman a positive figure (Christ, the gospel) or another agent of calamity, given his place among three riders of war, famine, and death?
- Is 'the fourth part of the earth' (Revelation 6:8) a literal fraction, or stylized language for partial, non-final judgment?
- Does 'hurt not the oil and the wine' (Revelation 6:6) point to any specific historical situation, or is it conventional famine imagery whose precise reason is left unstated?
- How should modern readers hold together the 'wrath of the Lamb' (Revelation 6:16) with the Lamb's identity, introduced in [[Revelation 5]], as the one who was slain?
