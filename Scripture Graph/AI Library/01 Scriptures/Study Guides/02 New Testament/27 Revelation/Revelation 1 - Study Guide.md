## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Prologue and blessing (vv. 1–3):** origin, mediation, purpose, and expected response to the revelation.
2. **Greeting and doxology (vv. 4–8):** grace and peace, titles of Christ, redemption, royal-priestly vocation, and the promise of his coming.
3. **Vision setting and commission (vv. 9–11):** John on Patmos, in the Spirit, commanded to write to seven churches.
4. **Vision of the risen Christ (vv. 12–18):** symbolic appearance, John's collapse, reassurance, and Christ's authority over death.
5. **Writing command and interpretation (vv. 19–20):** a temporal frame and an explicit explanation of the stars and candlesticks.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter grounds Christian hope in Christ's death and continuing life: he "was dead" but is "alive for evermore" and possesses the keys of hell and death (vv. 17–18). Redemption is described both as cleansing from sin and as transformation into a people who serve God as kings and priests (vv. 5–6). The promised coming of Christ is public and morally consequential rather than merely private or inward (v. 7). These themes connect naturally with [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], [[Resurrection]], [[Priesthood]], and [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter uses imagery familiar from earlier prophetic visions. The cloud-coming figure recalls [[Daniel 7]], while the clothed figure with a golden girdle, fiery appearance, brass-like feet, powerful voice, and an overwhelmed seer strongly recalls [[Daniel 10]]. The pierced-and-mourned figure in verse 7 resembles [[Zechariah 12]]. These convergences make scriptural reuse highly likely, but some individual details may belong to a broader apocalyptic vocabulary rather than derive from only one passage. The chapter also supplies its own symbolic key in verse 20: stars represent the churches' angels, and candlesticks represent the churches.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Intertextuality
> The visionary figure's white hair, golden girdle, fiery or penetrating appearance, brass-like feet, and overwhelming voice substantially overlap imagery in Daniel 7 and Daniel 10. This is strong evidence that Revelation 1 deliberately participates in the symbolic language of earlier biblical visions, though identifying every individual detail with a single source is less certain.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · one of the least disputed parallels · [[Literary Intertextuality in Revelation 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Divine-Identity-Christology *(tentative)*
> Divine self-declarations from Isaiah ('I am the first, and I am the last,' Isaiah 44:6/48:12) are echoed in the titles applied within this chapter — 'Alpha and Omega... the Almighty' (v.8) and 'I am the first and the last... I am he that liveth, and was dead... alive for evermore' spoken by the glorified figure (vv.11,17-18) — applying language reserved for YHWH in the Hebrew Bible to Jesus Christ.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed speaker identity in v.8
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does "the Lord's day" in verse 10 designate a recurring day of worship, an eschatological day, or something else? The phrase alone does not settle the question.
- Are the angels of the churches in verse 20 heavenly beings, human messengers, or symbolic representatives of the congregations?
- Who is speaking in verse 8? The title "the Almighty" favors reference to God, while the first-and-last language elsewhere is applied to the risen Christ; interpreters should not erase the local ambiguity.
- How should "shortly" and "the time is at hand" be related to the book's first-century audience and to its wider eschatological horizon?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the vision carefully with [[Daniel 7]], [[Daniel 10]], and [[Zechariah 12]], noting both similarities and differences. Then compare Revelation 1 with [[Revelation 22]] to see how titles and time statements frame the entire book. [[Exodus 19]] and [[1 Peter 2]] provide useful context for the royal-priestly identity of believers.
