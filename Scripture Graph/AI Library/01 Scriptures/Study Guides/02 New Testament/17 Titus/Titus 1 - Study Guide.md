# Titus 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Titus 1]] · My notes: [[Titus 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Titus 1 moves from theological commission to practical crisis. The letter presents Paul as connecting [[Faith]], [[Truth]], godliness, [[Hope]], and [[Eternal Life]] (vv. 1–3), assigning Titus to appoint qualified local leaders (vv. 5–9), and explaining that trustworthy leadership is needed because deceptive teaching is disrupting households (vv. 10–16). Belief and conduct remain joined throughout: truth accords with godliness, leaders must embody and defend sound teaching, rebuke aims at sound faith, and works can deny a profession of knowing God. This summarizes the letter's own presentation; disputed questions of Pauline authorship and the historical setting cannot be resolved from the chapter alone.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Commission and theological foundation (vv. 1–4):** promise, manifestation, preaching, and greeting.

2. **Titus's assignment and leadership qualifications (vv. 5–9):** elders are to be appointed in every city; household character, personal discipline, hospitality, and doctrinal competence are required.

3. **Reason for qualified leadership (vv. 10–14):** disruptive teachers must be answered, with correction aimed at soundness in faith.

4. **Purity, conscience, and conduct (vv. 15–16):** inner defilement and disobedient works expose the emptiness of merely professed knowledge of God.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's account of [[Eternal Life]] rests on God's truthful promise rather than human reliability (v. 2). Yet divine purpose is communicated through a human stewardship of preaching (v. 3). Leadership likewise joins calling and character: appointment alone is not the whole qualification; the steward must be morally disciplined and capable of preserving sound teaching (vv. 5–9).

Verses 15–16 refuse to separate doctrine from moral life. Purity is connected with mind and conscience, while claimed knowledge of God is evaluated through works. This does not teach salvation by outward performance alone; it does teach that conduct can contradict a religious profession.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The transition from “elders” in verse 5 to “a bishop” in verse 7 most naturally reads as an explanation of the kind of person Titus should appoint. This is good evidence of functional overlap within the passage, but it should not be pressed into a complete theory of all later church offices.

The phrase “husband of one wife” in verse 6 is compact and disputed. It clearly makes marital integrity relevant, but the wording alone does not decisively settle whether the primary concern is monogamy, sexual fidelity, remarriage, or eligibility of unmarried men. Likewise, “faithful children” may emphasize believing children, trustworthy children, or both.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several contrasts organize the chapter: promise before the world versus manifestation in due times (vv. 2–3); faithful word versus vain talk (vv. 9–10); sound doctrine versus household-subverting teaching (vv. 9–13); purity versus defilement (v. 15); and profession versus works (v. 16). These contrasts make the leadership list integral to the argument: leaders must embody the stable, truthful alternative to the opponents' speech and conduct.

The quotation in verse 12 is rhetorically forceful but ethically difficult. Because it is a sweeping negative statement about Cretans, it should not be converted into a timeless ethnic judgment. Within the argument it functions as a locally recognizable accusation, followed by a stated corrective purpose: becoming sound in the faith.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Terminological And Syntactic
> The movement from appointing plural “elders” in verse 5 to explaining what “a bishop” must be in verse 7 is substantial internal evidence that the terms overlap functionally in this argument.

> [!evidence] Literary Quotation And Rhetorical Adaptation
> The letter quotes a voice characterized as one of the Cretans' own prophets and employs that local cultural testimony within its rebuke.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 1.0 · Observation accepted; source and ethnographic force disputed · [[Literary Quotation And Rhetorical Adaptation in Titus 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Scholarship *(tentative)*
> Many critical New Testament scholars regard Titus as pseudonymous (written in Paul's name by a later author), citing non-Pauline vocabulary/style and the relatively developed church offices described in 1:5-9, compared with the undisputed Pauline letters (Romans, 1-2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon).
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · Major scholarly position; authorship remains disputed

> [!evidence] Classical Literary Source *(tentative)*
> Titus 1:12 quotes an unnamed Cretan source ("one of themselves, even a prophet of their own") making a self-referential claim that Cretans are always liars — traditionally identified since patristic times (e.g. Clement of Alexandria, Jerome) as the 6th-century BC Cretan poet Epimenides.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · Ancient conventional attribution, not secure identification

> [!evidence] Textual/Stylistic Pattern *(tentative)*
> The salutation "Grace, mercy, and peace" (Titus 1:4) is a distinctive tripartite formula shared with 1 Timothy 1:2 and 2 Timothy 1:2, whereas most other Pauline letter openings (e.g. Romans 1:7; 1 Corinthians 1:3; Galatians 1:3) use only "Grace... and peace" without "mercy."
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.7 · True in the supplied KJV; text-critical basis disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does the opening connect truth specifically with godliness rather than treating truth as information alone?

Does the shift from elders to bishop indicate interchangeable terms, or a relationship between overlapping but distinguishable roles?

How should “husband of one wife” and “faithful children” be applied without claiming more precision than the wording supplies?

Does “This witness is true” endorse every word of the Cretan generalization, or affirm its relevance to the particular deceivers under discussion?

How does the purpose clause in verse 13 constrain the manner and goal of sharp rebuke?

What limits on “all things are pure” are supplied by verses 15–16 themselves?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the leadership qualifications with [[1 Timothy 3]] and the elder-overseer language with [[Acts 20]]. Compare the treatment of purity with [[Romans 14]] and the test of professed knowledge with [[1 John 2]]. [[2 Timothy 1]] provides a useful parallel to the movement from divine purpose before the world to historical manifestation. Further historical study should consult representative arguments both for and against Pauline authorship rather than treating the question as settled. Text-critical study should also note that important Greek editions omit “mercy” from Titus 1:4, so the KJV's three-part greeting should not bear substantial authorship weight by itself.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
