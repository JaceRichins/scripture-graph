# 1 Peter 2 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Peter 2]] · My notes: [[1 Peter 2 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter moves from spiritual growth to communal identity and then to public conduct under social pressure. Believers approach Christ, the living and chosen cornerstone, and become living stones in a spiritual house (1 Peter 2:4-5). Scriptural language gives them a covenant vocation: they are to proclaim God, who called them from darkness into light (1 Peter 2:9-10). Their resulting conduct includes resisting sin, doing good among outsiders, honoring civil authorities without using liberty as a cover for wrongdoing, and enduring wrongful suffering after Christ's pattern. The conclusion grounds both example and salvation in Christ, who bore sins and brings wandering sheep back to himself (1 Peter 2:21-25).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Growth after tasting grace (1 Peter 2:1-3):** vice is laid aside and the word is desired as nourishment.

2. **A people built around Christ (1 Peter 2:4-10):** the stone imagery develops into a spiritual house, priesthood, and covenant people.

3. **Visible conduct among outsiders (1 Peter 2:11-17):** inward restraint and public good works accompany qualified social and civic duties.

4. **Wrongful suffering and Christ's pattern (1 Peter 2:18-25):** the discussion of servants leads into Christ's innocence, nonretaliation, sin-bearing death, and shepherding care.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins several dimensions of the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] without reducing them to one formula. Christ suffers "for us" and leaves an example (1 Peter 2:21); he bears sins so that believers may die to sins and live to righteousness (1 Peter 2:24); his stripes heal (1 Peter 2:24); and he restores wandering sheep (1 Peter 2:25). The text therefore combines representation, moral transformation, healing, and return.

Its language of a holy and royal [[Priesthood]] is corporate and vocational: the community is built into a spiritual house to offer spiritual [[Sacrifice]] and proclaim God's praises (1 Peter 2:5, 9). The chapter itself does not specify how this collective identity maps onto ordained offices.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several KJV word choices can mislead a modern reader: 'peculiar people' (v. 9) renders a Greek phrase meaning a people who are God's own treasured possession, not an 'odd' or eccentric people. 'Lively stones' (v. 5) uses the same underlying idea as 'living stone' in v. 4 — the KJV simply varies its English. 'Conversation' (v. 12) means conduct or manner of life, not spoken dialogue. 'Froward' (v. 18) means perverse, harsh, or unreasonable, describing the difficult masters servants are told to remain subject to.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 6-8 assemble three separate Old Testament stone texts into a single messianic catena: the 'chief corner stone, elect, precious' of [[Isaiah 28]]:16, the 'stone which the builders disallowed...made the head of the corner' of [[Psalm 118]]:22, and the 'stone of stumbling, and rock of offence' of [[Isaiah 8]]:14. The combination lets one image carry both God's choice of Christ and the divided human response to him — precious to believers, a stumbling stone to the disobedient.

Verses 22-25 form an even more concentrated sequence, repeatedly evoking [[Isaiah 53]]: Christ's innocence and freedom from guile (compare Isaiah 53:9), his silent, non-retaliatory suffering (compare Isaiah 53:7), his bearing of sins and the healing 'stripes' (compare Isaiah 53:4-5, 12), and the 'sheep going astray' who return (compare Isaiah 53:6). This sustained pattern is strong literary evidence of intentional engagement with Isaiah's Suffering Servant song, though it does not by itself settle every theological model of the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] or the servant's original historical referent.

Verses 13-20 also draw on a wider New Testament convention pairing submission to authority (citizens, servants, and later wives and husbands) with an appeal to conscience or divine approval; that material continues directly into [[1 Peter 3]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does "whereunto also they were appointed" in 1 Peter 2:8 refer primarily to appointment to disobedience, to stumbling as its consequence, or to the scripturally foretold role of the stumbling stone?

- How does the command to submit to human ordinances relate to the carefully differentiated commands to fear God but honor the king in 1 Peter 2:17? The chapter states the duties but does not fully map their limits in cases of conflict.

- How should a modern reader distinguish the passage's counsel for faithful endurance from interpretations that might pressure victims to remain in preventable abuse?

- Does the application of Israel's covenant vocabulary to the audience imply replacement, inclusion, vocation, or some combination? First Peter 2 alone does not settle the broader question.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the stone catena with its Old Testament sources at [[Isaiah 28]], [[Psalm 118]], and [[Isaiah 8]] (the same Isaiah 8 material is preserved in [[2 Nephi 18]], though that is a shared-source relationship rather than a direct link). Compare the covenant-title language of verse 9 with [[Exodus 19]] and [[Isaiah 43]], and the 'not a people/now the people' language of verse 10 with [[Hosea 2]] (and Paul's reuse of the same Hosea material in [[Romans 9]]). For the sustained Isaiah 53 allusions in verses 22-25, read [[Isaiah 53]] alongside this chapter. The submission material continues into [[1 Peter 3]]. See also [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] and [[Priesthood]] — this chapter's priesthood language describes the community corporately and does not itself define ordained priesthood office.
