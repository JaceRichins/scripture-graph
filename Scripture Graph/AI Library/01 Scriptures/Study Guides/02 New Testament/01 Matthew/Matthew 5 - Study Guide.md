# Matthew 5 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus preaches the Sermon on the Mount—Its teachings replace and transcend some aspects of the law of Moses—All are commanded to be perfect like their Father in Heaven.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Setting and teaching audience (vv. 1–2):** Jesus ascends the mountain, sits, and teaches.
2. **[[Beatitudes]] (vv. 3–12):** Eight third-person blessings are followed by direct address to persecuted disciples.
3. **Salt and light (vv. 13–16):** Disciples are commissioned to exert visible, God-glorifying influence.
4. **Law, fulfillment, and greater righteousness (vv. 17–20):** Jesus denies destroying the law and prophets and introduces the standard developed afterward.
5. **Six ethical case studies (vv. 21–48):** killing and anger; adultery and lust; divorce; oaths; retaliation; neighbours and enemies.

The last case study is climactic: love must extend beyond reciprocal affection, and the Father's impartial provision becomes the model.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects membership in the [[Kingdom of God]] with qualities and conduct rather than status alone. Mercy, purity of heart, peacemaking, endurance under persecution, reconciliation, disciplined desire, truthful speech, generosity, and enemy-love all receive attention.

“Greater” righteousness is portrayed through transformed dispositions and relationships as well as observable acts. Anger, contempt, and lust matter; reconciliation takes precedence over completing an altar offering; and good works are meant to glorify the Father. The text therefore resists a contrast in which external obedience is irrelevant, since Jesus commends both doing and teaching the commandments.

The closing appeal to the Father's conduct places love of enemies within the imitation of God. Its relation to [[Charity]] is direct, but the examples do not answer every later ethical question about self-defense, criminal justice, or governmental responsibility.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several terms reward closer attention: 'jot' and 'tittle' (v18) likely refer to the smallest Hebrew letter and a distinguishing stroke on similar letters, emphasizing meticulous continuity of the law. 'Raca' (v22) is an Aramaic term of contempt roughly meaning 'empty-headed' or 'worthless.' The 'farthing' (v26) renders a small Roman coin (kodrantes), underscoring that the debt described is trivial yet inescapable once judgment is rendered. 'Perfect' (v48) translates Greek teleios, which in koine usage often carries the sense of complete, mature, or having reached one's intended purpose, rather than the modern connotation of flawlessness.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The Beatitudes form a tight inclusio: the refrain 'for theirs is the kingdom of heaven' opens (v3) and closes (v10) the eight third-person sayings before the discourse pivots to direct second-person address in v11. The body of the chapter (vv21-48) is built from six recurring 'Ye have heard... but I say unto you' (or 'It hath been said...') units — the introductory wording varies rather than repeating a single fixed formula, but each cites received teaching and reframes it with the speaker's own authority.

Several sayings use vivid, arguably hyperbolic imagery (plucking out an eye, cutting off a hand, turning the other cheek, walking a second mile) to press an ethical point; the text itself does not label these as literal instruction or rhetorical exaggeration.

The chapter's closest literary relative is not another Gospel but [[3 Nephi 12]], which reproduces most of Matthew 5 nearly verbatim as part of the [[Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)]] — a documented, extensive instance of King James Version language in the Book of Mormon. This fact has genuinely disputed historical-critical implications: it is consistent with a revelatory translation that used familiar biblical idiom, with direct literary dependence on the printed KJV during dictation, or some combination of the two. Differences between the two chapters, not just similarities, deserve close comparison.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Redaction-Critical/Synoptic Comparison *(tentative)*
> Matthew's divorce saying contains an exception clause ('saving for the cause of fornication') permitting divorce in a case of sexual immorality; this qualifier is a distinctive feature of Matthew's version of the saying when compared with the unqualified prohibitions attributed to Jesus elsewhere in the Synoptic tradition (e.g., Mark 10:11-12; Luke 16:18).
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · documented Matthean variant; scribal-addition alt unsupported

> [!evidence] Redaction-Critical/Synoptic Comparison *(tentative)*
> Matthew's first beatitude reads 'poor in spirit,' whereas the parallel saying in Luke's Sermon on the Plain is commonly rendered simply 'Blessed be ye poor' (Luke 6:20), a divergence often discussed by scholars as reflecting differing theological emphases (spiritualized humility in Matthew vs. a more directly economic concern in Luke) between the two Gospel traditions.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · documented synoptic variant, honestly hedged as disputed

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical/Second Temple Background *(tentative)*
> The antithesis in verse 43 quotes 'Thou shalt love thy neighbour' from the law (cf. Leviticus 19:18) but pairs it with 'and hate thine enemy,' a clause with no direct Old Testament command; this likely reflects a popular or sectarian extension of the law current in Second Temple Judaism (a similar sentiment appears in the Dead Sea Scrolls' Community Rule, which enjoins members to 'love all the sons of light... and hate all the sons of darkness'), which Jesus' teaching directly reverses.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · plausible background, single-witness parallel, imprecise citation

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How do verses 17-20 constrain interpretation of the 'But I say unto you' sayings that follow?
- In what sense must righteousness exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees, and which examples clarify that comparison?
- Why does reconciliation take precedence over completing the altar offering (vv23-24)?
- Do the sayings about the eye, the hand, the other cheek, and the second mile call for literal action, or use deliberate exaggeration to press a point — and does that question change how radical the demand really is?
- How does the immediately preceding argument about impartial love shape the meaning of 'perfect' in verse 48?
- Which differences between [[Matthew 5]] and [[3 Nephi 12]] are significant enough to affect interpretation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
