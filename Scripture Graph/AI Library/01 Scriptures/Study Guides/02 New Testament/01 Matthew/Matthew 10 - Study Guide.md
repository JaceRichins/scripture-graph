# Matthew 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Matthew 10]] · My notes: [[Matthew 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus instructs and empowers the Twelve Apostles and sends them forth to preach, minister, and heal the sick—Those who receive the Twelve receive the Lord.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. **Authority and names of the Twelve (vv. 1–4):** Jesus grants power, and the apostles are listed.
2. **Immediate mission instructions (vv. 5–15):** Israel is the stated field; proclamation, healing, material simplicity, hospitality, and rejection are addressed.
3. **Persecution and testimony (vv. 16–23):** The messengers are vulnerable but must be prudent; the Spirit will aid them before hostile authorities.
4. **The disciple and the master (vv. 24–33):** Hostility is expected, but fear is countered by divine care and the demand for public confession.
5. **Division and the cost of allegiance (vv. 34–39):** Following Jesus may divide households and entails cross-bearing and the surrender of life.
6. **Reception and reward (vv. 40–42):** Receiving the messenger reaches through Jesus to the Father, and even modest service is remembered.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Central threads include: delegated [[Priesthood|authority]] granted to the Twelve before they are sent (v.1), covering both [[Miracles]] of healing and exorcism; the principle that ministry is given and given away freely rather than sold ('freely ye have received, freely give,' v.8); persecution borne as [[Testimony]] — explicitly named as such in v.18 ('for a testimony against them and the Gentiles') — sustained by [[Revelation]] through the Spirit (vv.19-20); the reciprocal doctrine that confessing or denying Christ before others determines being confessed or denied before the Father (vv.32-33); the cost of discipleship expressed through [[Family]] division and taking up one's cross ([[Sacrifice]]); [[Endurance]] to the end as the condition of salvation (v.22); and [[Service]] — even a cup of cold water given in Christ's name carries guaranteed reward (v.42).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter is dense with memorable idiom: 'the kingdom of heaven is at hand' (v.7) as an urgent announcement formula; the proverbial pairing 'wise as serpents, and harmless as doves' (v.16), joining prudence with innocence rather than either naivety or predatory cunning; 'shake off the dust of your feet' (v.14) as a symbolic act of dissociation from an unreceptive place; and the recurring judgment formula 'more tolerable... in the day of judgment' (v.15), which Matthew reuses in chapter 11. The 'sword' of verse 34 is immediately explained through family variance and household enmity (vv.35-37), supporting a reading of relational division rather than a call to violence. 'These little ones' (v.42) is linked in the same verse with 'a disciple,' so it should not be read as limited only to literal children.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several sayings in this chapter recur elsewhere in scripture. The family-division language of vv.35-36 draws closely on [[Micah 7|Micah 7:6]]'s oracle of household enmity. 'He that receiveth you receiveth me, and he that receiveth me receiveth him that sent me' (v.40) is echoed almost word-for-word in John 13:20. 'He that findeth his life shall lose it: and he that loseth his life for my sake shall find it' (v.39) is repeated nearly verbatim in [[Matthew 16|Matthew 16:25]] and appears with variation in Mark, Luke, and John — evidence the saying circulated widely, though not proof of independent historical attestation; the companion cross-bearing saying (v.38) is only thematically, not verbally, close to Matthew 16:24. The chapter also builds on a series of rhetorical contrasts — sheep and wolves, serpents and doves, darkness and light, confession and denial, finding and losing life — that sharpen the choices facing a disciple. The synoptic apostle lists (Mark 3:16-19; Luke 6:14-16) name 'Lebbaeus, whose surname was Thaddaeus' (v.3) differently; tradition treats this as the same person also called Judas the brother/son of James, though the manuscript history behind the name is more tangled than a simple naming variant.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual-Interpretive Crux *(tentative)*
> Matthew 10:23 ('Ye shall not have gone over the cities of Israel, till the Son of man be come') is a well-known interpretive crux: read literally as the Second Coming it appears unfulfilled within the apostles' lifetimes, which has generated several competing scholarly and devotional explanations.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · genuinely disputed interpretive crux

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Philological *(tentative)*
> The Book of Mormon's use of a 'more tolerable... day of judgment' idiom (as in Alma 9) closely mirrors Jesus's KJV phrasing in Matthew 10:15/11:24, even though Alma's sermon is set roughly 80 years before Jesus's ministry.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does 'till the Son of man be come' (v.23) refer to — a limited-mission timeframe, the destruction of Jerusalem, the Second Coming, or something else? Interpreters disagree.
- Where, if anywhere, does the discourse shift from the Twelve's immediate mission (vv.5-15) to the experience of later disciples (vv.16-23 mention governors, kings, and Gentiles)?
- How should the restriction to Israel (vv.5-6) be read alongside the anticipated 'testimony against... the Gentiles' (v.18) and Matthew's later universal commission (Matthew 28:19)?
- Given Luke 22:35-36's later reversal of the no-purse-no-scrip instruction, was that provisioning rule (vv.9-10) meant as a permanent missionary law or a one-time, context-bound directive?
- Is 'I came not to send peace, but a sword' (v.34) purely relational/social division, or does it carry additional meaning?
- What should be made of the naming variant for 'Lebbaeus, whose surname was Thaddaeus' (v.3) across the synoptic apostle lists?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the kingdom proclamation ('the kingdom of heaven is at hand,' v.7) with [[Matthew 4]]; the endurance language ('he that endureth to the end shall be saved,' v.22) with [[Matthew 24]]; the cross-bearing and life-losing sayings (vv.38-39) with [[Matthew 16]]; and the reception of messengers (v.40) with John 13:20. Reading Matthew 10:35-36 beside [[Micah 7]] clarifies that the household-conflict language draws on an earlier prophetic description rather than being an isolated saying. D&C 75 gives a modern parallel of paired missionary instructions, and Alma 9 uses similar 'more tolerable... day of judgment' language.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
