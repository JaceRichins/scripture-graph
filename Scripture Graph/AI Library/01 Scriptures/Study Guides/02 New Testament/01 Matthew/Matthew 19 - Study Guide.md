# Matthew 19 — Study Guide

## Overview

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Movement and healing (vv. 1–2):** Jesus enters the region of Judæa beyond Jordan.

2. **Marriage, divorce, and celibacy (vv. 3–12):** A hostile legal question leads from [[Genesis 1]] and [[Genesis 2]] to Moses's concession and then to differing capacities concerning marriage.

3. **Children and the kingdom (vv. 13–15):** Jesus reverses the disciples' exclusion of little children.

4. **The wealthy young man (vv. 16–22):** Commandment keeping leads to a personalized demand for generosity and discipleship.

5. **Riches, salvation, and reward (vv. 23–30):** Human impossibility is answered by divine possibility; relinquishment is promised recompense, but the first-last saying unsettles claims to rank.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The marriage teaching distinguishes God's creational purpose from a legal accommodation to human hardness. This supports a high view of marital unity without erasing the chapter's debated exception clause or supplying a complete pastoral policy for every broken marriage.

The encounter with the wealthy man holds [[Obedience]], care for the poor, and following Jesus together. His sorrow shows that outward commandment keeping can coexist with a governing attachment. Yet the chapter does not present dispossession as a technique by which people purchase [[Salvation]]: Jesus finally locates possibility with God.

The promise to the Twelve joins [[Apostles]] with Israel's future judgment, while the first-last reversal warns that sacrifice does not justify ordinary calculations of prestige.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two verbal contrasts carry much of the argument. The Pharisees say Moses "command[ed]" divorce documentation, whereas Jesus says Moses "suffered" divorce because of hardened hearts. Later, the disciples ask, "Who then can be saved?" and Jesus contrasts what is "impossible" with men with what is "possible" with God. In both cases, Jesus reframes the assumptions embedded in the question rather than merely choosing one offered answer.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The children and the wealthy man form a suggestive juxtaposition. The children are brought, hindered by the disciples, and welcomed; the wealthy man approaches voluntarily but leaves sorrowful when asked to relinquish his possessions. The contrast supports a theme of receiving the kingdom without relying on status or assets, although Matthew does not explicitly state that the episodes were placed together to create this contrast.

A second contrast joins the wealthy man's departure to Peter's declaration, "we have forsaken all, and followed thee." Even this favorable comparison is checked by the closing reversal: "many that are first shall be last; and the last shall be first."
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Intratextual Legal-Rhetorical Evidence
> The contrast between the Pharisees' word "command" and Jesus's word "suffered" is strong textual evidence that Matthew presents the divorce certificate as a regulated concession rather than the governing ideal for marriage.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strong contextual inference · [[Intratextual Legal-Rhetorical Evidence in Matthew 19|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Contextual Literary Evidence
> The disciples interpret the camel-and-needle saying as raising the question of who can be saved, and Jesus answers with a contrast between human impossibility and divine possibility. This context strongly supports reading the image as severe hyperbole about the incapacity of wealth or human effort to secure entry into God's kingdom, rather than as a merely difficult maneuver.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · strong contextual reading · [[Contextual Literary Evidence in Matthew 19|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Synoptic Textual Comparison *(tentative)*
> Matthew's version of Jesus's divorce ruling includes an exception clause ('except it be for fornication') permitting divorce in cases of sexual immorality; this clause is absent from the parallel sayings in Mark 10:11-12 and Luke 16:18.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · textual difference certain; interpretation disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What exactly is the antecedent of "this saying" in Matthew 19:11: the disciples' conclusion about not marrying, Jesus's strict teaching about marriage, or the whole exchange?

How should the exception in Matthew 19:9 be understood without making it either meaningless or broader than the text warrants?

Does the wealthy man's command apply identically to every disciple, or does the episode reveal a universal demand for undivided allegiance through a command tailored to his particular attachment?

How does the promise of recompense in Matthew 19:29 relate to the status-reversing warning in Matthew 19:30?
