# Matthew 23 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Matthew 23]] · My notes: [[Matthew 23 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus pronounces woes upon the scribes and Pharisees—They will be held responsible for killing the prophets—They will not escape the damnation of hell.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Instruction and warning (vv. 1–12):** Jesus distinguishes the leaders' teaching from their conduct, condemns burdens imposed without assistance and honor-seeking display, and defines greatness through [[Service]] and [[Humility]].
2. **Pronouncements of woe (vv. 13–36):** repeated direct-address woes condemn obstruction, exploitation, corrupt proselytizing, evasive oath distinctions, neglected moral priorities, concealed corruption, and continuity with those who killed [[Prophets]]. The supplied text contains seven occurrences of the exact scribes-and-Pharisees refrain, although verse 14 is textually disputed in the manuscript tradition.
3. **Lament and desolation (vv. 37–39):** Jesus addresses Jerusalem directly, expresses his desire to gather its children, announces desolation, and closes with a future use of the Psalm 118 acclamation.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Moral priority without selective dismissal:** Judgment, mercy, and faith are the "weightier matters," yet lesser duties are not simply to be abandoned (v. 23). Precision cannot compensate for injustice, and emphasis on central duties need not excuse disregard for smaller ones.
- **Greatness through [[Service]] and [[Humility]]:** Jesus opposes visibility, rank, and self-exaltation with brotherhood, service, and humility (vv. 5–12).
- **Integrity and inward cleansing:** The cup and tomb images connect visible righteousness with the need for inward moral integrity (vv. 25–28).
- **Invitation and refusal:** The lament holds Jesus's expressed desire to gather Jerusalem together with its refusal (v. 37). This is relevant to [[Agency]], but the verse alone does not resolve broader questions about grace, foreknowledge, or collective responsibility.
- **Teaching and conduct:** Verses 2–3 distinguish what the leaders say from how they act. The exact authority implied by "Moses' seat," and the scope of "whatsoever they bid you," remain interpretive questions rather than a settled doctrine of institutional authority.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"Hypocrites" is developed through concrete contrasts rather than functioning merely as an insult: saying versus doing, visible devotion versus exploitation, clean exterior versus corrupt interior, and honoring dead prophets versus persecuting living messengers. The cup and tomb images make concealed moral contradiction the controlling idea.

The gnat-and-camel saying compresses the same criticism into deliberate disproportion: minute care accompanies the consumption of something vastly larger. Its point is illuminated by verse 23, where meticulous tithing coexists with neglect of judgment, mercy, and faith.

"Moses' seat" associates the scribes and Pharisees with an authoritative teaching position tied to Moses, but the expression alone does not identify the exact institutional boundaries or historical operation of that authority.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The discourse changes addressee and intensity: it begins with third-person description of the leaders (vv. 2–7), turns to instruction for the disciples (vv. 8–12), moves into repeated direct-address woes (vv. 13–36), and ends by addressing Jerusalem itself (vv. 37–39).

Repeated contrasts create rhetorical unity: saying versus doing, burdens versus withheld help, minor precision versus neglected weightier duties, and clean or beautiful exteriors versus inward corruption. The images include burdens, enlarged religious dress, a strained gnat and swallowed camel, a cup clean only outside, and whitewashed tombs containing uncleanness. The closing hen image changes the tone from accusation to protective longing.

The supplied text repeats "Woe unto you, scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites!" seven times, producing clear anaphora. Numerical significance should not be assumed, especially because verse 14 is textually disputed.

The sequence from Abel to Zacharias may bracket scriptural history from Genesis to Chronicles, but this remains tentative. Matthew calls Zacharias the son of Barachias, whereas the closely resembling death in [[2 Chronicles 24]] concerns Zechariah son of Jehoiada.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary Pattern
> The repeated contrast between visible righteousness and hidden corruption is strong internal evidence that moral incongruity is a central organizing concern of the chapter: public works conceal inconsistent conduct, a clean exterior conceals extortion and excess, and beautiful tombs conceal uncleanness.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Pattern accepted; formal design unproven · [[Internal Literary Pattern in Matthew 23|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical Crux *(tentative)*
> Verse 35 names the last of the murdered righteous as 'Zacharias son of Barachias, whom ye slew between the temple and the altar,' pairing him with Abel as bookends ('from the blood of righteous Abel unto...'). This plausibly forms a first-to-last merism spanning the Hebrew canon in its traditional Jewish ordering (Genesis first, Chronicles last), since 2 Chronicles 24:20-22 records a Zechariah murdered near the temple/altar precinct by royal command — but that Zechariah is identified there as the son of Jehoiada, not Berechiah. The patronymic 'son of Berechiah' instead matches the separate prophet Zechariah of Zechariah 1:1, who has no recorded violent death in the Hebrew Bible.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · Identification disputed; discrepancy secure

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism *(tentative)*
> Verse 14 (the woe concerning devouring widows' houses and long prayers for pretence) is widely regarded in New Testament textual criticism as a later scribal addition absent from the earliest surviving Greek manuscripts of Matthew, likely imported from the parallel sayings in Mark and Luke; most modern critical translations bracket, footnote, or omit it. This matters for study because it means the tidy count of seven identical 'woe' refrains in the chapter may be a later manuscript development rather than part of the earliest recoverable text of Matthew.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · Mainstream conclusion, insufficiently sourced here
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How does verse 23 prevent both legalistic precision and the dismissal of smaller duties?
2. Which forms of honor in verses 5–12 are intrinsically problematic, and which become problematic because they are sought as status?
3. Why does Jesus move from cup and tomb imagery to the history of rejected prophets?
4. How does the lament in verse 37 change the emotional and theological force of the preceding woes?
5. What safeguards keep modern readers from directing this intrajewish prophetic denunciation against an ethnic or religious population?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare Matthew 23:5 with Deuteronomy 6:8 and Numbers 15:38–40 to distinguish the commanded symbols from the ostentatious motive criticized here.
- Read Matthew 23:11–12 beside Matthew 20:25–28 for the repeated servant-greatness pattern, and study Matthew 23:23 alongside [[Tithing]] and [[Justice and Mercy]].
- Compare Matthew 23:35 with Genesis 4:8–10 and [[2 Chronicles 24]], retaining the unresolved mismatch between the names Barachias and Jehoiada.
- Compare the gathering image in Matthew 23:37 with [[3 Nephi 10]]. The textual parallel is strong, but it does not by itself determine the historical relationship between the passages.
- Compare Matthew 23:39 with Matthew 21:9 and Psalm 118:26. The shared acclamation is clear, while its future significance in Matthew 23 remains open to more than one interpretation.
