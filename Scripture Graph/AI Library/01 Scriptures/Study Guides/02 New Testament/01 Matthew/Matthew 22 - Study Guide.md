# Matthew 22 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Matthew 22]] · My notes: [[Matthew 22 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus gives the parable of the marriage of the king’s son—Pay tribute to Cæsar and to God—Worldly marriages endure in this life only—The first commandment is to love the Lord—Jesus asks, What think ye of Christ?
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The royal wedding feast (vv. 1–14):** Invitations are rejected, servants are abused, the invitation is widened, and the guests are inspected.
2. **Cæsar and God (vv. 15–22):** Pharisees and Herodians pose a political trap concerning tribute.
3. **Resurrection and marriage (vv. 23–33):** Sadducees use a levirate-marriage case to challenge resurrection; Jesus answers from God's power and [[Exodus 3]].
4. **The two great commandments (vv. 34–40):** [[Deuteronomy 6]] and [[Leviticus 19]] are joined as the foundation of the law and prophets.
5. **David's son and Lord (vv. 41–46):** Jesus cites [[Psalms 110]], reverses the questioning, and leaves his opponents unable to answer.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The wedding parable places broad invitation beside accountability: the servants gather people described as “both bad and good,” but the king still inspects the guests. The garment’s meaning is not defined, so particular doctrinal identifications remain interpretations.

Jesus distinguishes what is associated with Cæsar from what belongs to God without providing a complete rule for resolving every conflict between civil and divine claims (see [[Government and Laws]]).

Jesus appeals to both “the scriptures” and “the power of God” in answering the Sadducees. Matthew 22:30 specifically says that people neither marry nor are given in marriage in the resurrection; conclusions about continuing relationships or distinct categories of marriage require other texts or theological premises (see [[Marriage]], [[Resurrection]], and [[Eternal Marriage]]).

Love of God and love of neighbor are presented as the two commandments on which all the law and prophets hang, making love an organizing principle for faithful obedience rather than an isolated addition to it.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The tribute answer turns on the coin's "image and superscription." Jesus begins with something his opponents can identify and then distinguishes what is Caesar's from what is God's. The text does not explicitly say that humans bear God's image, so that familiar extension should be labeled an interpretive analogy rather than part of Jesus' stated argument.

The statement that all the law and prophets "hang" on two commandments depicts the many commands as dependent upon love of God and neighbor. In the final exchange, the paired titles "son" and "Lord" create the unresolved tension Jesus asks the Pharisees to explain.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The controversy sequence progressively reverses control. Pharisees and Herodians attempt a political trap; Sadducees construct a resurrection dilemma; a lawyer tests Jesus about the law. Jesus answers each challenge and then poses the final question himself. Matthew 22:46 supplies the narrative climax: no one can answer, and further questioning ceases.

The tribute scene also uses irony. The questioners call Jesus truthful and impartial while acting deceptively, and their praise is immediately followed by his recognition of their wickedness. The wedding parable repeats the rejected-messenger pattern of [[Matthew 21]], linking refusal of invitation with refusal to hear authorized servants.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Scriptural Intertextuality
> The identifiable appeals to Exodus 3:6, Deuteronomy 6:5, Leviticus 19:18, and Psalm 110:1 are strong textual evidence that Matthew frames these disputes as interpretation of Israel's scriptures.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Broadly accepted intertextual observation · [[Scriptural Intertextuality in Matthew 22|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Synoptic Literary Relationship
> The corresponding sequence of controversies in Matthew 22, Mark 12, and Luke 20 is strong internal evidence that the Synoptic accounts are literarily or traditionally related. Mark includes the tribute, resurrection, greatest-commandment, and Davidic-Lord episodes; Luke preserves the tribute, resurrection, and Davidic-Lord sequence.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Parallel sequence accepted; dependence model disputed · [[Synoptic Literary Relationship in Matthew 22|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Doctrinal Interpretation *(tentative)*
> The chapter heading's gloss that 'worldly marriages endure in this life only' reads verse 30 as referring specifically to marriages not sealed by priesthood authority, implying by contrast that a different category of marriage could continue beyond mortality — a distinction not stated or defined within Matthew 22 itself.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.6 · LDS paratextual interpretation; distinction not explicit in Matthew

> [!evidence] Grammatical/Rhetorical Argument *(tentative)*
> A common reading of vv31-32 treats the present-tense formula 'I am the God of Abraham...' (rather than 'I was') as Jesus's argument that the patriarchs continue to exist in relation to God, and thus that resurrection/continued life is implied by the Exodus text itself.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · Common but disputed explanation
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What can safely be inferred from the wedding garment when the parable never defines it?
- Does "render unto Cæsar" establish a detailed political theory, or does it deliberately leave readers to discern what belongs to Caesar and what belongs to God?
- What does Matthew 22:30 explicitly say, and which broader conclusions about relationships after resurrection depend on other texts or theological premises?
- Does Jesus reject the designation "Son of David," or require an account large enough to explain why David also calls the Messiah Lord?
- How do love of God and love of neighbor guide difficult cases where a claimed religious duty harms a neighbor?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the controversy sequence with [[Mark 12]] and [[Luke 20]], noting both shared order and differences. Study the Sadducees' legal premise in [[Deuteronomy 25]] and Jesus' resurrection text in [[Exodus 3]]. Then compare Matthew 22:37–40 with [[Deuteronomy 6]] and [[Leviticus 19]], and the final Christological question with [[Psalms 110]]. These comparisons expose how Matthew's argument depends upon close reading of earlier scripture without, by themselves, resolving questions of Synoptic dependence or historical wording.
