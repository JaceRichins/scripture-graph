# Matthew 27 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Matthew 27]] · My notes: [[Matthew 27 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus is accused and condemned before Pilate—Barabbas is released—Jesus is mocked, crucified, and buried in the tomb of Joseph of Arimathæa.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **27:1–10 — Decision and aftermath of betrayal:** Jesus is delivered to Pilate; Judas returns the silver; the priests purchase the potter's field.
2. **27:11–26 — Roman hearing:** Pilate questions Jesus, offers Barabbas, yields to the crowd, and delivers Jesus for crucifixion.
3. **27:27–44 — Mockery and crucifixion:** Soldiers stage a parody of kingship; Jesus is crucified and publicly taunted.
4. **27:45–54 — Death and signs:** Darkness precedes Jesus' cry and death; the veil and earth are torn; the centurion and his companions confess him as God's Son.
5. **27:55–61 — Witnesses and burial:** Women observe, and Joseph of Arimathaea places the body in a new tomb.
6. **27:62–66 — The tomb secured:** Leaders request a guard because they remember Jesus' resurrection prediction.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter is central to [[Crucifixion of Jesus Christ]] and [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], but it mainly narrates events rather than giving a systematic explanation of how the death saves. Such explanation should therefore draw on the wider canon rather than be read into every detail here.

The torn temple veil can be read as a sign that Jesus' death changes access to God's presence, but Matthew does not explicitly decode the symbol. The raised saints and the prediction remembered by Jesus' opponents orient the closing scenes toward [[Resurrection]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly uses contested titles—"King of the Jews," "King of Israel," and "Son of God." Speakers often intend accusation or mockery, while the narrative invites readers to hear the titles differently. Jesus' cry in Matthew 27:46 is transliterated and then translated, directly invoking the opening of [[Psalm 22]].

The KJV wording that Judas "repented himself" should not, by itself, settle whether his remorse constitutes the fuller repentance envisioned elsewhere in the Gospel; the narrative records confession, restitution, departure, and suicide without announcing divine forgiveness.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Matthew structures the crucifixion around irony and inversion: Jesus is mockingly dressed in a scarlet robe and crown of thorns, saluted as "King of the Jews" by soldiers, and that same title is then nailed above his head in earnest as his accusation. Those who taunt him to "come down" and "save himself" unknowingly state the scene's theological stakes. The garment-casting, head-wagging mockery, and cry of dereliction all echo [[Psalm 22]], tying the crucifixion to a psalm of lament that turns toward vindication — though whether the correspondence reflects narrative shaping, independent memory, or both is not settled by the text alone. Physical details — named witnesses, a specific tomb, a sealed stone — build narrative continuity into [[Matthew 28]] without themselves constituting independent historical verification.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Burial And Witness Continuity
> The chapter names Joseph of Arimathaea as the person who obtains and buries Jesus' body, describes a new rock-hewn tomb and its stone, and places Mary Magdalene and the other Mary opposite the tomb. These details provide strong internal narrative continuity between Jesus' death, burial, and the resurrection account that follows.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 1.0 · well supported, historicity undetermined · [[Burial And Witness Continuity in Matthew 27|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Testimony
> Within the narrative, Jesus' innocence is asserted by parties with different relationships to the proceedings: Judas calls his blood innocent, Pilate's wife calls him just, and Pilate publicly calls him just. This is strong evidence for Matthew's deliberate innocence motif, but only internal narrative evidence concerning the historical verdict.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · well supported, internal narrative only · [[Internal Narrative Testimony in Matthew 27|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Narrative-Apologetic Feature
> The chief priests and Pharisees remember Jesus' prediction of rising after three days and ask Pilate to secure the tomb because they anticipate a claim that the disciples stole the body. The sealing and watch therefore function narratively as a preemptive response to a theft explanation.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 1.0 · accepted observation, disputed cause · [[Narrative-Apologetic Feature in Matthew 27|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary/Apologetic Function
> The sealing and guarding of the tomb (found only in Matthew) appears to anticipate and rebut a specific counter-claim — that the disciples stole the body — which Matthew makes explicit later (Matthew 28:11-15); this suggests the detail served an apologetic function for Matthew's original audience.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · accepted observation, disputed cause · [[Literary/Apologetic Function in Matthew 27|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Prophetic-Fulfillment Citation *(tentative)*
> Matthew attributes the thirty-pieces-of-silver/potter's-field fulfillment quotation to 'Jeremy the prophet,' but the closest wording match in the Old Testament is Zechariah 11:12-13, not Jeremiah; this is a long-noted textual/attribution crux rather than a simple copying error.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · disputed citation puzzle, broadly noted
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Matthew attribute the potter's-field prophecy to "Jeremy" when the closest verbal match is Zechariah 11:12-13, and what does that citation puzzle suggest about how Matthew combines prophetic sources?
- What does it mean that Matthew alone records the raising of "many bodies of the saints" at Jesus' death (27:52-53), and why does he specify they appeared only after Jesus' own resurrection?
- How do the echoes of [[Psalm 22]] in the crucifixion scene shape interpretation without predetermining conclusions about which details are independent memory versus scriptural framing?
- What might the torn temple veil signify, and how much of that meaning comes from Matthew's own text versus later theological reflection?
- How should modern readers understand the crowd's declaration in 27:25 in its immediate narrative setting, without extending it into collective or hereditary blame?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

For the difficult citation in Matthew 27:9–10, compare [[Zechariah 11]], [[Jeremiah 19]], and [[Jeremiah 32]] rather than assuming that a single proposed solution is certain. Continue into [[Matthew 28]] to see how the guard and theft allegation develop.
