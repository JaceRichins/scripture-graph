## Overview

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Herod and John (14:1–12):** Herod's fear, oath, and concern for his guests culminate in John's execution.
2. **Provision in the wilderness (14:13–21):** Jesus's withdrawal is interrupted by a crowd; compassion leads to healing and abundant food.
3. **Jesus and the disciples at sea (14:22–33):** Solitary [[Prayer]] is followed by Jesus's approach, Peter's attempt, rescue, and the confession of Jesus as Son of God.
4. **Healing at Gennesaret (14:34–36):** Recognition of Jesus leads people to gather the sick from the surrounding region.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Faith]] to dependence upon Jesus rather than to confidence in one's own steadiness. Peter genuinely obeys the command to come, yet fear and doubt overtake him; his brief prayer, “Lord, save me,” receives an immediate response before correction (14:28–31). The feeding account also depicts [[Service]] as participation in Christ's provision: the disciples cannot create the abundance, but they receive and distribute what Jesus blesses (14:16–19). The narrative's portrayal of [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] progresses from compassion for the vulnerable to authority over scarcity, sickness, and the sea.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Matthew repeatedly uses terms of rapid action: Jesus “straightway” sends the disciples away (14:22), “straightway” speaks amid their fear (14:27), and “immediately” catches Peter (14:31). The pacing emphasizes decisive intervention. The chapter also carefully distinguishes Herod as “the tetrarch” in 14:1 while narratively calling him “the king” in 14:9; the latter can function as a less technical courtly designation and should not erase the more precise title.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two meals stand near each other in a striking, though interpretive, contrast. Herod's birthday meal ends with John's head presented on a dish (14:6–11); Jesus's wilderness meal arises from compassion and ends with the multitude filled and food remaining (14:14–20). The sequence may reflect deliberate literary design, but it also follows naturally from John's death prompting Jesus's withdrawal.

The feeding repeats the take-bless-break-give sequence found in [[Matthew 26]], creating a genuine verbal connection without establishing sacramental intent. Wilderness provision may recall [[Exodus 16]], although the multiplied bread and surplus more closely resemble [[2 Kings 4]].

Jesus's mastery of the sea resonates thematically with Job 9:8 and Psalm 77:19, but neither passage is directly quoted. Comparison with [[Mark 6]] and [[John 6]] highlights Matthew's distinctive inclusion of Peter's attempt, fear, appeal, and rescue. The phrase “the hem of his garment” also recalls the individual healing in [[Matthew 9]], now broadened into a communal scene at Gennesaret.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Gospel Comparison
> The Peter water-walking episode is absent from Mark's and John's parallel accounts of Jesus walking on the sea, suggesting it is unique to Matthew's telling of this shared event.

> [!evidence] Biblical Typological Resonance *(tentative)*
> A multitude receiving miraculous food in a deserted place can reasonably evoke Israel's wilderness provision. The setting, hunger, divine provision, and abundance support an Exodus resonance, but Matthew does not explicitly identify the food as manna or explain the episode as a formal Exodus typology.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does Matthew place the account of John's death immediately before the feeding of the multitude? Does the contrast between Herod's table and Jesus's wilderness meal shape the reader's understanding of true kingship? What does Peter's experience suggest about the difference between initiating an act of faith and continuing in trust? Why does Jesus rescue Peter before rebuking his doubt? Should the twelve baskets be read symbolically as an Israel motif, or simply as a concrete sign of abundance?
