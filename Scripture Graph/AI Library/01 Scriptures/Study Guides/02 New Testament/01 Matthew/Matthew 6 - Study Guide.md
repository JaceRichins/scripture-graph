# Matthew 6 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus continues the Sermon on the Mount—He teaches the disciples the Lord’s Prayer—They are commanded to seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Devotion before the Father (6:1–18):** almsgiving (vv. 1–4), prayer (vv. 5–15), and fasting (vv. 16–18). The prayer section is enlarged by the model prayer and an explanation about forgiveness.

2. **Treasure and allegiance (6:19–24):** earthly and heavenly treasure, the sound or evil eye, and the impossibility of serving both God and mammon.

3. **Trust and present faithfulness (6:25–34):** food, clothing, birds, lilies, and tomorrow become illustrations in an argument for seeking God's kingdom first.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The Father is portrayed as seeing hidden conduct (Matthew 6:4, 6, 18), knowing needs before prayer (v. 8), forgiving those who forgive (vv. 14–15), and caring for creatures and disciples (vv. 26, 30, 32). Prayer is therefore neither a performance nor a means of informing an ignorant God. It addresses God reverently, seeks his will, requests daily provision, joins divine forgiveness to human forgiveness, and asks for deliverance.

The chapter also treats discipleship as a question of allegiance. Treasure directs the heart (v. 21), and service cannot finally be divided between God and mammon (v. 24). Seeking the kingdom first (v. 33) does not erase bodily needs; it places them within trust in the Father.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Matthew 6:1–18 is marked by conspicuous repetition. The almsgiving, prayer, and fasting units each name a practice, expose public display, announce that the performers already have their reward, direct the disciple toward secrecy, and promise the Father's reward. This parallel construction makes motive—not merely outward compliance—the controlling issue.

Verses 19–24 use three compact images—treasure, eye, and masters. Their precise relationship is not fully explained, but each asks where a person's life is directed. Verses 25–34 then use observations of birds, bodily limits, lilies, grass, and the arrival of tomorrow to turn an abstract call to trust into concrete reflection.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. If the Father already knows what is needed (v. 8), what purposes of prayer become visible in verses 9–13?

2. How does the plural language of the model prayer change a reading focused only on private spirituality?

3. What forms of recognition might function today like the public rewards criticized in verses 1–18?

4. How should the severe reciprocity of verses 14–15 shape the study of [[Repentance and Forgiveness]]?

5. Where is the boundary between prudent preparation and the anxious preoccupation challenged in verses 25–34?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[3 Nephi 13]] closely with Matthew 6, recording both agreements and differences before drawing conclusions about their relationship. Within Matthew 6, trace every occurrence of "Father," "reward," "treasure," and "take no thought" to see how the chapter moves from religious motives to material trust.
