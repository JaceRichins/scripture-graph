# Matthew 18 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Matthew 18]] · My notes: [[Matthew 18 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Matthew 18 is Matthew's fourth major discourse (sometimes called the "Community Discourse"), answering the disciples' opening question about greatness (v.1) not with a ranking but with a child set among them: entering the [[Kingdom of God]] requires conversion and childlike humility (vv.2-4). From there the chapter moves through warnings against causing a believer to stumble (vv.6-9), the parable of the lost sheep (vv.10-14), a graduated procedure for addressing a brother's trespass (vv.15-20), and Peter's question about limits on forgiveness, answered by the parable of the unforgiving servant (vv.21-35). A plausible unifying thread is care for "these little ones" (v.6) — a phrase that begins with a literal child (vv.2-4) and, by verse 6, clearly extends to "little ones which believe in me." How far that extension carries into the later material on discipline and forgiveness is an interpretive judgment the text does not spell out, not a settled fact.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter can be divided into five movements: (1) [[Humility]] as the condition of greatness, framed by a child (vv.1-5); (2) warnings against causing a believer to stumble, including the hand/foot/eye sayings (vv.6-9); (3) the parable of the lost sheep, grounding the Father's will that none of "these little ones" perish (vv.10-14); (4) a graduated process for addressing a brother's trespass — private, small-witness, whole-church — paired with sayings about binding/loosing and gathered prayer (vv.15-20); and (5) Peter's question about limits on forgiveness, answered by the parable of the unforgiving servant (vv.21-35). Matthew is often read as organized around five major teaching discourses, each traditionally closing with a formula before narrative resumes (cf. Matthew 19:1); this is a widely used scholarly structuring device, not something the text states about itself.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Key threads: (1) humility and conversion as conditions for entering the kingdom, not just markers of status within it (vv.3-4); (2) the seriousness of causing another believer to stumble (vv.6-9); (3) the Father's will that none of the "little ones" be lost, illustrated by the shepherd who leaves the ninety-nine (vv.12-14); (4) a restorative process for correction — beginning privately and aimed at "gaining" the brother (v.15) — that only escalates to the whole community as a last resort (vv.15-17), joined to plural binding/loosing language (v.18) that in Matthew 16:19 was addressed to Peter alone; and (5) forgiveness without a numerical ceiling (vv.21-22), grounded in the incomparable scale of the forgiveness disciples have themselves received (vv.23-35).

Two cautions are worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over. First, the chapter's official heading states that "all of the Twelve receive the keys of the kingdom," but vv.18-20 never use the words "keys" or "Twelve" — the heading is an interpretive gloss connecting this passage to Matthew 16:19, not a restatement of what the verses themselves say. Second, the parable's ending (vv.32-34) depicts the lord revoking mercy already granted — a genuinely severe detail. The passage supports the claim that received forgiveness creates an obligation to forgive; it should not be flattened into a technical formula for whether divine forgiveness, once given, can be lost. Nor does forgiving "from your hearts" (v.35) by itself settle questions of trust, restitution, or protection from ongoing harm.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"Little ones" (vv.6, 10, 14) is not restricted to the literal child of vv.2-5; by v.6 it explicitly names believers, though how uniformly that sense carries through the rest of the chapter is disputed. "Offend"/"offences" shifts from harm caused to a believer (vv.6-7) to the causes of sin within oneself (vv.8-9). The parable's numbers are deliberately extreme: "ten thousand talents" set against "an hundred pence" is, on any plausible ancient conversion, a disproportion on the order of hundreds of thousands to one — best read as intentional hyperbole rather than a precise figure. Some commentators also connect Jesus' "seventy times seven" (v.22) to Lamech's boast of vengeance "seventy and sevenfold" in [[Genesis 4]]; the underlying Greek wording is close enough that a deliberate echo is plausible, but this remains a minority scholarly observation rather than an established consensus reading.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly argues by disproportion: the greatest becomes the one who humbles himself; a shepherd's concern for one wandering sheep outweighs the ninety-nine that remain; a servant forgiven an incalculable debt is condemned for refusing to forgive a comparatively trivial one. These reversals of ordinary expectation — of status, of numbers, of what mercy is "owed" — carry the chapter's ethical argument: those who have received extraordinary concern or mercy have correspondingly little excuse for withholding it from others. The bind/loose and "two or three gathered" sayings (vv.18-20) sit at the structural center of the discourse, linking correction, shared prayer, and Christ's promised presence, though the text's own wording does not restrict that presence-promise solely to disciplinary settings.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Criticism
> Matthew 18:11 ('For the Son of man is come to save that which was lost') is absent from the earliest surviving Greek manuscripts of Matthew and is bracketed or dropped in modern critical editions and many modern translations; mainstream textual critics generally regard it as an early scribal addition drawn from the nearly identical wording of Luke 19:10, rather than as original to Matthew.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · standard textual-critical observation, appropriately caveated · [[Textual Criticism in Matthew 18|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Numerical/Historical Analysis
> Using standard first-century monetary equivalences (a talent commonly reckoned around 6,000 denarii, a denarius roughly a day's wage), the first servant's debt of ten thousand talents is on the order of hundreds of thousands of times larger than the hundred pence owed by his fellow servant, indicating the parable is using deliberately incalculable, hyperbolic figures rather than a realistic financial comparison.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · reasonable, appropriately approximate · [[Numerical/Historical Analysis in Matthew 18|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Contrast
> The contrast between ten thousand talents and one hundred pence, reinforced by the matching pleas and opposite responses, is strong internal evidence that the parable is designed to expose the forgiven servant's gross inconsistency. It strongly supports the lesson that received mercy creates an obligation to extend mercy.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-calibrated, avoids manufactured ratios · [[Internal Narrative Contrast in Matthew 18|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Linguistic Scholarship *(tentative)*
> New Testament scholarship on Matthew commonly notes that the Greek phrase behind 'seventy times seven' in Matthew 18:22 matches the wording used for Lamech's boast in the Greek Septuagint translation of Genesis 4:24, which would make the echo a specific verbal allusion rather than only a thematic coincidence.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · minority/unverified claim

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
How should the graduated correction process in vv.15-17 be reconciled with the command to forgive without limit in vv.21-22 — are these in tension, or do they address different situations (seeking a repentant response versus a wronged person's inward disposition)? Does the shift from singular address in Matthew 16:19 to plural "ye" in 18:18 change who holds binding/loosing authority, or restate the same grant for a gathered group? What does forgiving "from your hearts" require — and what does it not require regarding trust, restitution, or protection from continued harm? Are vv.19-20 primarily about prayer in general or specifically about the church's discernment in cases like vv.15-17? Does the disputed manuscript status of v.11 matter for how the chapter is studied, given that its idea (saving the lost) is independently present in vv.12-14?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the witness procedure with [[Deuteronomy 19]] and its restatement in D&C 6, which reuses the "two or three witnesses...every word may be established" formula. Compare the binding-and-loosing saying with Matthew 16:19 (addressed to Peter alone) and its parallels in D&C 128 and Helaman 10, which apply similar "bind/seal...loosed in heaven" language to priesthood sealing authority. Compare the bodily-member imagery with [[Matthew 5]] (which names the eye and hand, not the foot). For the lost sheep, compare Luke 15; for forgiveness, compare [[Matthew 6]] and Luke 17. These are worthwhile parallels to read alongside this chapter, not evidence that every parallel shares an identical purpose or context.
