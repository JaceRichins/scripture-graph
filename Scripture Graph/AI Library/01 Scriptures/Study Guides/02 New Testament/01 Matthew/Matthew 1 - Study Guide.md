## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Matthew 1:1:** Programmatic identification of Jesus.
2. **Matthew 1:2–16:** Genealogy from Abraham through David and the exile to Joseph and Mary.
3. **Matthew 1:17:** Summary in three groups of fourteen.
4. **Matthew 1:18–19:** Mary's pregnancy and Joseph's intended response.
5. **Matthew 1:20–21:** Angelic explanation and command to name Jesus.
6. **Matthew 1:22–23:** Fulfillment citation from [[Isaiah 7]].
7. **Matthew 1:24–25:** Joseph's obedience and Jesus's birth and naming.

The movement from Abraham to David, through exile, and finally to Christ makes the genealogy a compact theological account of Israel's history, not merely a list of ancestors.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Matthew joins identity and mission. “Jesus” is associated with deliverance from sins, while “Emmanuel” interprets his coming as God's presence with humanity. The chapter therefore presents salvation as more than political restoration, despite its strong royal and Davidic setting. Joseph's conduct also places compassion and [[Obedience]] together: he initially seeks to avoid Mary's public humiliation, then acts on revelation despite the personal cost.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Matthew 1:23 presents Isaiah's sign as “a virgin shall be with child.” Matthew's wording follows the Greek scriptural tradition, which uses *parthenos*; the Hebrew word *almah* in Isaiah 7:14 denotes a young woman and does not, by itself, settle the question of virginity. This lexical distinction does not decide how Matthew understood fulfillment or whether he was applying Isaiah typologically.

Verse 25 states in the supplied text that Joseph “knew her not till she had brought forth her firstborn son.” The wording clearly excludes sexual relations before the birth but does not conclusively report what happened afterward. “Firstborn” need not imply later children, and many modern critical Greek editions do not include that word in Matthew 1:25.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The genealogy is visibly composed rather than exhaustive. Comparison with [[1 Chronicles 3]] shows omitted generations, while Matthew 1:17 announces a threefold pattern of fourteen. The recurring “begat” formula then changes at Matthew 1:16: Joseph is Mary's husband, and Jesus is born of Mary. This grammatical turn prepares for the conception account in verses 18–25.

The women named or evoked in the genealogy—Thamar, Rachab, Ruth, the wife of Urias, and Mary—have prompted several interpretations. A defensible common denominator is that their stories prevent the ancestry from appearing simple or socially uniform. Claims that all five represent exactly the same category, however, usually overstate the evidence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Structure And Intertextual Comparison
> The omissions and explicit fourteen-generation arrangement strongly support the conclusion that Matthew has shaped inherited genealogical material for literary and theological purposes; the passage should not be treated as though it claimed to list every biological generation.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · Broad scholarly agreement · [[Literary Structure And Intertextual Comparison in Matthew 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Translation/Text-Critical
> Matthew 1:23 quotes Isaiah's sign of a 'virgin' who 'shall be with child' and bear a son called Emmanuel/Immanuel. The underlying Hebrew term in Isaiah 7:14 (almah) denotes a young woman of marriageable age and does not by itself specify virginity; the rendering 'virgin' follows the Greek Septuagint's parthenos. This translation choice sits at the center of a longstanding Jewish-Christian and text-critical debate over Isaiah's original sense.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · Lexical facts accepted; prophetic interpretation disputed · [[Translation/Text-Critical in Matthew 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Explicit Scriptural Citation And Reception History
> Matthew's citation of Isaiah is strong evidence for the evangelist's christological reading of Israel's scriptures. Whether Isaiah 7:14 originally functioned as a direct prediction exclusively about Jesus remains disputed because Isaiah places the sign within an eighth-century BCE crisis involving Ahaz.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Matthew's reading accepted; Isaiah's original referent disputed · [[Explicit Scriptural Citation And Reception History in Matthew 1|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Numerical Literary Pattern *(tentative)*
> A frequently proposed explanation for the repeated fourteen is that the numerical value of the Hebrew consonants in David's name totals fourteen, fitting Matthew's pronounced Davidic emphasis. This is plausible but not stated by Matthew.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · Frequently proposed but unproved interpretation

> [!evidence] Literary Allusion *(tentative)*
> Matthew 1:1 opens 'The book of the generation of Jesus Christ,' closely echoing the KJV wording that opens Adam's genealogy: 'This is the book of the generations of Adam' (Genesis 5:1). Some read this as Matthew deliberately framing Jesus's genealogy as inaugurating a new epoch comparable to Genesis.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · Verbal resemblance accepted; specific Adam allusion disputed

> [!evidence] Literary/Numerological *(tentative)*
> A recognized scholarly hypothesis holds that the fourteen-generation structuring alludes to the numerical value of the Hebrew consonants in 'David' (dalet-vav-dalet = 4+6+4 = 14), reinforcing the genealogy's emphasis on Davidic descent through a numerical device (gematria) rather than only through explicit statement.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · Recognized but unproved scholarly conjecture
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does Matthew begin with Abraham while [[Luke 3]] extends Jesus's genealogy back to Adam? What theological work is accomplished by dividing Israel's history around David and the Babylonian exile? Why does Matthew preserve the women in the genealogy, and which proposed explanation best accounts for all of them? Does Matthew employ [[Isaiah 7]] as a direct prediction, a typological pattern, or both? How do Joseph's restraint in verse 19 and obedience in verses 24–25 illuminate the relationship between [[Justice and Mercy]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Matthew 1:2–16 with [[Ruth 4]], [[1 Chronicles 3]], and [[Luke 3]]. These comparisons reveal both inherited genealogical material and deliberate differences in selection and arrangement; they do not by themselves explain every difference or establish direct dependence on one source.

Read Matthew 1:22–23 within the wider setting of [[Isaiah 7]], where the Emmanuel sign addresses King Ahaz during an immediate political crisis. [[2 Nephi 17]] preserves that Isaianic setting for additional Latter-day Saint study. Holding Isaiah's original context together with Matthew's later christological application permits a more precise study of [[Prophecy]] than treating either context in isolation.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
