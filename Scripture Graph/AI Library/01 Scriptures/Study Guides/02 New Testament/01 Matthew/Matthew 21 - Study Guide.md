# Matthew 21 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Matthew 21]] · My notes: [[Matthew 21 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Matthew 21 opens the climactic conflict of Jesus' ministry in Jerusalem. Presented explicitly as fulfillment of prophecy, Jesus enters the city as a meek king (vv.1-11), then acts with authority in the temple, expelling the sellers and moneychangers and healing the blind and lame there (vv.12-17). A fruitless fig tree withers at his word, prompting an explicit teaching on undoubting faith and believing [[Prayer]] (vv.18-22). When chief priests and elders challenge his authority, he answers with a counter-question about John's baptism that they refuse to resolve (vv.23-27). Two parables follow — the two sons, and the wicked husbandmen — that press the leaders on the gap between profession and obedience and warn that the [[Kingdom of God]] will be given to those who bear its fruits (vv.28-46). A recurring 'fruit' motif links the fig tree to both parables, and the chapter closes with the leaders recognizing themselves as the parables' target yet moving to seize Jesus rather than repent.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The King enters Jerusalem (vv.1-11):** the arrival is framed as fulfillment of prophecy, the crowd's acclamation, and competing descriptions of who Jesus is.
2. **Jesus acts and heals in the temple (vv.12-17):** he expels sellers and moneychangers, then heals the blind and lame and defends children's praise with scripture.
3. **The fig tree and believing prayer (vv.18-22):** a fruitless tree withers, and Jesus draws from it a lesson about [[Faith]] and [[Prayer]].
4. **Authority questioned (vv.23-27):** chief priests and elders challenge Jesus, who answers with a counter-question about John's baptism that they decline to resolve.
5. **Parable of the two sons (vv.28-32):** enacted [[Repentance]] and [[Obedience]] are contrasted with unfulfilled verbal assent.
6. **Parable of the wicked husbandmen (vv.33-46):** rejected servants, a murdered son, the rejected-stone citation, and the leaders' recognition that the parable targets them.

The word 'fruit'/'fruits' recurs across the fig tree (v.19) and both halves of the husbandmen parable (vv.34, 41, 43), forming a verbal thread that links these otherwise distinct episodes.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Matthew 21 joins Christology with discipleship. Jesus is acclaimed as Son of David and presented, via explicit prophetic fulfillment, as a meek king — yet his authority is immediately contested (vv.9, 23). His actions in the [[Temples|temple]] contrast sacred use ('house of prayer,' healing, children's praise) with what he calls a 'den of thieves,' though the text itself does not specify the precise nature of the abuse. Believing [[Prayer]] is tied to receiving what is asked (vv.21-22) — a promise this chapter states without qualification, though later scripture qualifies it. The parable of the two sons teaches that doing the Father's will matters more than verbal assent: [[Repentance]] and [[Obedience]] are shown in deed, not words. The parable of the wicked husbandmen presents the [[Kingdom of God]] as a trust for which stewards are accountable, to be given to 'a nation bringing forth the fruits thereof' (v.43) — a fruit-based rather than status-based standard, though the text does not name the recipient and should not be read as a verdict on any entire people.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly draws on inherited scriptural language. The entry's fulfillment quotation blends 'daughter of Zion' address language (echoing [[Isaiah 62]]) with the meek, donkey-riding king of [[Zechariah 9]]; the crowd's acclamation separately draws on [[Psalm 118]]. The temple saying joins [[Isaiah 56]] ('house of prayer') and [[Jeremiah 7]] ('den of thieves'/robbers), while the defense of children's praise echoes [[Psalm 8]] — though Matthew's wording ('perfected praise') follows the Greek Septuagint rendering rather than the Hebrew text underlying most English Old Testaments ('ordained strength'), so readers checking their own Bibles may see different words there. Psalm 118 returns in the rejected-stone saying. The repetition of 'fruit'/'fruits' in verses 19, 34, 41, and 43 creates a strong lexical thread, though the precise symbolic relationship between the fig tree and the parables remains interpretive.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
A 'fruit' motif unifies otherwise distinct episodes: the fig tree's leaves without fruit (v.19), the vineyard's withheld fruits (v.34), fruits 'in their seasons' (v.41), and a nation 'bringing forth the fruits thereof' (v.43). [[Psalm 118]] frames the chapter's temple conflict from two directions — the crowd's entry acclamation (v.9) and the rejected-stone citation (v.42) — better described as a repeated echo than a strict inclusio. Matthew also orders the material so that enacted authority (the entry, the temple action, healing) precedes debated authority (vv.23-27), and builds a rejection motif — resistance to children's praise, rejection of John, the husbandmen's violence, and finally the leaders' own plot against Jesus — that climaxes in the citation of the stone the builders rejected. Compare Matthew's single-day account of the fig tree (vv.18-20) with [[Mark 11]]'s two-day structure, a useful case study in how the Synoptic Gospels can arrange the same underlying material differently.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Scriptural Framing
> Matthew's explicit fulfillment formula and prophetic citation provide strong evidence that the narrator intends the entry to be read as the arrival of a meek king in fulfillment of scripture.

> [!evidence] Literary Repetition And Thematic Coherence
> Repeated fruit language links the fruitless fig tree with the vineyard parable and the declaration about a nation producing the kingdom's fruits. This is meaningful internal evidence for a chapter-level concern with productive response and accountable stewardship.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-hedged, model of honest evidence framing · [[Literary Repetition And Thematic Coherence in Matthew 21|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Synoptic Redaction-Criticism *(tentative)*
> Matthew's compression of the fig-tree episode into a single day, compared to Mark's two-day structure, is a commonly cited example of Matthew's general tendency toward topical/temporal telescoping of material relative to Mark.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · uncited generalization

> [!evidence] Liturgical/Historical Background *(tentative)*
> The crowd's use of Hallel/Psalm-118 language ('Hosanna,' 'blessed is he that cometh') is consistent with pilgrim liturgy customarily sung during Passover week, supporting (without proving) the traditional Passover-week setting of the entry.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · plausible background, weak independent evidentiary value

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism *(tentative)*
> Verse 44 ('whosoever shall fall on this stone shall be broken...') is textually uncertain: it is absent from a number of early witnesses (including Codex Bezae and some Old Latin manuscripts) and is regarded by many textual critics as a likely scribal harmonization drawn from the parallel saying in Luke 20:18, though it is retained in the Byzantine tradition underlying the King James Version used here.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Matthew pairs the fig tree with the two parables in close sequence — why place these together, and what cumulative point about response and fruitfulness is being built?
- What does the leaders' response in vv.45-46 — perceiving the parable's target, yet seeking to arrest Jesus rather than repenting — suggest about the relationship between recognizing truth and acting on it?
- What does Matthew explicitly identify as fulfillment or explanation (e.g., the faith/prayer lesson in vv.21-22), and what do readers often infer without an explicit textual basis (e.g., that the fig tree represents Israel)?
- Who is the immediate referent of 'you' in verse 43, and what would be required to extend that judgment beyond the leaders present in this scene?
- Does the temple scene condemn commerce as such, or specifically its corrupt practice and obstruction of prayer?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
