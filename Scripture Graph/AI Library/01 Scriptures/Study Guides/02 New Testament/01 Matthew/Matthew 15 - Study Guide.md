# Matthew 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Matthew 15]] · My notes: [[Matthew 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The scribes and Pharisees contend against Jesus—He heals the daughter of a gentile woman—He feeds the four thousand.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Tradition tested by commandment (vv. 1-9):** a handwashing accusation is answered with the more serious example of neglected parental duty.

2. **Defilement explained (vv. 10-20):** Jesus contrasts what enters the mouth with the moral evils that proceed from the heart. Peter requests an explanation, but Matthew does not specify that it was given privately.

3. **The Canaanite woman's appeal (vv. 21-28):** silence, an Israel-focused mission statement, and a difficult bread metaphor precede the woman's reply, Jesus' commendation of her faith, and her daughter's healing.

4. **Healing and provision (vv. 29-39):** bodily restoration leads to praise; Jesus' compassion then leads to the feeding of four thousand.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The opening controversy connects [[Family]], [[Ten Commandments]], and [[Obedience]]: a religious tradition cannot legitimately cancel the divine duty to honor and assist one's parents. Within the immediate handwashing controversy, verses 10-20 locate moral defilement in heart-sourced thoughts, words, and actions rather than in eating with unwashed hands; the passage should not automatically be expanded into a ruling on every dietary or ritual command.

The Canaanite narrative contributes to [[Jews and Gentiles]] without removing the episode's tension. Jesus states a priority toward Israel, yet hears the outsider's appeal, praises her [[Faith]], and heals her daughter. The final scenes present [[Miracles]] in association with restoration, praise, compassion, and provision.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter develops a concentrated mouth-heart contrast. Isaiah's indictment concerns mouths and lips honoring God while hearts remain distant (vv. 7-9). Jesus then contrasts what enters the mouth with what comes out (v. 11), and the explanation identifies the heart as the origin of defiling thoughts, words, and deeds (vv. 18-20). This progression makes outward profession, ritual concern, and inward moral reality part of one argument.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Bread recurs at major transitions: eating bread with unwashen hands (v. 2), the children's bread and the woman's crumbs (vv. 26-27), and seven loaves distributed until the multitude is filled (vv. 33-37). The recurrence can help readers see thematic cohesion, although bread is common enough that elaborate symbolism should not be claimed with certainty. The feeding also forms a deliberate narrative pair with [[Matthew 14]]; [[Matthew 16]] later recalls the different numbers from both accounts. The healing list in verse 31 also resembles the restoration imagery of [[Isaiah 35]], but the absence of an explicit fulfillment formula makes “scriptural echo” safer than “direct fulfillment quotation.”
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Cross-Reference
> Matthew later has Jesus separately recall the feeding of five thousand with twelve baskets and the feeding of four thousand with seven baskets. This is strong internal evidence that Matthew's final narrative intends readers to distinguish the two feeding accounts.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted final-form observation · [[Internal Narrative Cross-Reference in Matthew 15|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Translation *(tentative)*
> Jesus's quotation in 15:8-9 diverges noticeably in wording, especially in its final clause, from the phrasing of the same oracle as rendered at Isaiah 29:13 in this canon ('their fear toward me is taught by the precept of men'), suggesting Matthew's Greek text followed a different textual/translation tradition (commonly identified with the Septuagint) rather than reproducing a rendering that matches the Hebrew-based KJV Old Testament wording.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · wording difference accepted; source identification unresolved

> [!evidence] Source Criticism *(tentative)*
> The close structural and verbal similarity between the feeding of the five thousand (Matthew 14) and the feeding of the four thousand (Matthew 15), reflected in a verified multi-verse text overlap between the two chapters, has led some scholars to propose the two accounts are literary doublets from a single underlying tradition, while the text as it stands narrates them as two distinct events on separate occasions with different numbers.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · recognized dispute but insufficiently sourced
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
Why does Matthew preserve Jesus' silence and difficult bread metaphor before the Canaanite woman's request is granted? The text reports the sequence but does not explicitly state whether its purpose is testing, instruction of the disciples, dramatization of mission priority, or some combination.

How narrow is the conclusion in verse 20? The immediate conclusion concerns eating with unwashen hands; readers should distinguish that explicit claim from broader conclusions about every dietary or ritual command.

Does the phrase “the God of Israel” indicate that the healing multitude was predominantly Gentile? It may fit the surrounding geography and outsider theme, but the chapter never identifies the crowd's ethnicity.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Compare the tradition, defilement, and Gentile-woman narratives with [[Mark 7]], noting which explanatory details occur only in Mark.
- Compare the four-thousand feeding with [[Mark 8]] and the five-thousand feeding in [[Matthew 14]]. Then read [[Matthew 16]]:9-10, where Jesus recalls the two sets of numbers separately.
- Compare Matthew 15:8-9 with Isaiah 29:13, distinguishing the clear attribution to Isaiah from unresolved questions about wording and textual tradition.
- Compare the healing summary in Matthew 15:30-31 with Isaiah 35:5-6. Treat the resemblance as a possible echo because Matthew supplies no explicit fulfillment formula.
