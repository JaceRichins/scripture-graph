# Matthew 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Matthew 3]] · My notes: [[Matthew 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> John the Baptist preaches in Judæa—Jesus is baptized, and the Father acclaims Him as His Beloved Son.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The wilderness herald (vv. 1–4):** John proclaims repentance and is identified with Isaiah's wilderness voice.
2. **Baptism and warning (vv. 5–10):** crowds confess their sins, while religious leaders are told that ancestry without good fruit is insufficient.
3. **The coming mightier one (vv. 11–12):** John contrasts his baptism with the coming one's work and describes judgment through threshing imagery.
4. **Jesus' baptism (vv. 13–17):** John objects, Jesus appeals to fulfilling all righteousness, and the Spirit and heavenly voice attend the baptism.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects [[Repentance]] with fruit—that is, with consequences visible in conduct—rather than with inherited status alone (vv. 8–10). It presents Jesus' [[Baptism]] as an act that fulfills righteousness, but does not fully define that expression (vv. 13–15). The closing scene is important for [[The Godhead]] because Jesus, the Spirit, and the heavenly speaker are narratively distinguishable (vv. 16–17); the scene does not, by itself, settle every later metaphysical question about their unity or distinction.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
**'Generation of vipers'** (Greek *gennēmata echidnōn*, lit. 'offspring of vipers') is a harsh idiom for treacherous character; Matthew has Jesus reuse the identical phrase toward similar audiences later (Matt 12:34; 23:33).

**'Kingdom of heaven'** (v. 2) is Matthew's characteristic wording for God's reign.

**'Fruits meet for repentance'** (v. 8) — 'meet' is archaic for 'fitting' or 'suitable'; the phrase means fruit appropriate to genuine repentance, not merely a verbal claim of having repented.

**'Whose shoes I am not worthy to bear'** (v. 11) — Matthew has John speak of *bearing* (carrying) the sandals, a servant's task; the parallel Gospel accounts instead have John speak of being unworthy to *unloose the latchet*. The small variation across accounts is worth noting rather than harmonizing away.

**The 'fan'** (v. 12) is a winnowing implement and the 'floor' a threshing floor; the image separates wheat from chaff, with the chaff burned in 'unquenchable' (inextinguishable) fire — language elsewhere in the New Testament tied to the finality of judgment.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
John's camel-hair garment and leather girdle (v. 4) recall the description of Elijah in [[2 Kings 1]], where a leather girdle is the detail most clearly shared — the underlying description of Elijah as a 'hairy man' is ambiguous between personal hairiness and a hairy garment, so the parallel rests most securely on the girdle. Matthew doesn't name Elijah here, so an Elijah-like characterization is a plausible, not certain, reading. The chapter also uses repeated agricultural imagery — fruitful and unfruitful trees, wheat and chaff — to turn repentance and judgment into concrete pictures; the unfruitful-tree/fire warning of v. 10 reappears in Jesus's own teaching in [[Matthew 7]] and [[3 Nephi 14]], tying the forerunner's words to the Messiah's own. At the baptism, sight and sound converge: the Spirit descends visibly 'like a dove,' and a voice interprets Jesus's identity.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Scriptural Interpretation
> Matthew 3:3 is strong evidence that the Gospel writer interpreted John's ministry through Isaiah 40:3, but the author's identification is not independent proof that Isaiah originally predicted John specifically or that the narrated events occurred exactly as described.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · model example of separating observation from theological conclusion · [[Explicit Scriptural Interpretation in Matthew 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary And Intertextual Resemblance
> The clothing correspondence between John and Elijah is meaningful literary evidence for Elijah typology, but clothing alone is not decisive proof of conscious imitation or authorial intent.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · well-calibrated hedge · [[Literary And Intertextual Resemblance in Matthew 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Doctrinal/Textual Observation
> Matthew 3:16-17 presents, within two verses, a voice from heaven (the Father), a man being baptized (the Son), and a dove-like descent (the Holy Ghost)—one of the New Testament's clearest scenes depicting all three members of the Godhead as simultaneously present and distinguishable.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation · [[Doctrinal/Textual Observation in Matthew 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Narrative-Theological Differentiation
> The baptism scene simultaneously differentiates Jesus, the descending Spirit of God, and the heavenly speaker who calls Jesus his Son. It therefore strongly supports a narrative distinction among Father, Son, and Spirit, while leaving their precise metaphysical relationship unstated.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · best-calibrated Godhead claim in either proposal · [[Narrative-Theological Differentiation in Matthew 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Textual/Prophetic Parallel *(tentative)*
> Centuries before Matthew was composed, the Book of Mormon records Lehi and Nephi (c. 600 BC) prophesying of a forerunner who would 'prepare the way of the Lord' and baptize the Messiah with water, who would then be baptized with the Holy Ghost and fire—anticipating both the office (John the Baptist) and the specific water/Spirit-and-fire baptismal language of this chapter.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.6 · believing-scholarship intertextual note; historicity contested
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What kinds of “fruit” would count as evidence of repentance in the immediate context, and what details does the text leave unspecified?
- Does “with the Holy Ghost, and with fire” describe one experience, different outcomes for different people, or overlapping images of purification and judgment?
- What does “fulfil all righteousness” explain clearly, and what theological questions about Jesus' baptism remain unanswered by this chapter?
- How much doctrine about [[The Godhead]] can responsibly be inferred from the narrative distinction among Jesus, the Spirit, and the heavenly voice?
