# Matthew 4 — Study Guide

## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Testing in the wilderness (4:1–11):** fasting, three temptations, three scriptural replies, and angelic ministry.
2. **Light and proclamation in Galilee (4:12–17):** relocation to Capernaum, fulfillment language from [[Isaiah 9]], and the announcement of the kingdom.
3. **The first disciples (4:18–22):** two parallel call-and-response scenes involving fishermen.
4. **A summary of expanding ministry (4:23–25):** teaching, preaching, healing, spreading fame, and crowds from several regions.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The temptation account connects [[Obedience]], [[Fasting]], and [[Satan and Temptation]]. Jesus refuses to use power for appetite, to force divine protection, or to obtain rule through idolatry. His replies from [[Deuteronomy 6]] and [[Deuteronomy 8]] place worship and obedience above self-serving displays of power.

Jesus' message joins [[Repentance]] to the nearness of the [[Kingdom of God]] (Matthew 4:17). The call of the fishermen then shows a practical response to that announcement: they follow Jesus and are promised a new work of gathering people. The closing verses unite proclamation with compassionate healing rather than treating word and deed as unrelated ministries.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated formula “It is written” gives the confrontation a scriptural rhythm, but Matthew also depicts the devil quoting [[Psalm 91]]. The important contrast is therefore not mere possession of a scriptural phrase; it is whether scripture is used consistently with loyalty to God.

“Kingdom of heaven” in Matthew 4:17 and “gospel of the kingdom” in Matthew 4:23 bind the opening sermon to the later ministry summary. “Fishers of men” converts the disciples' familiar occupation into an image of their future calling without yet explaining the full shape of that work.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
A strong intertextual pattern links Matthew 4:1–11 with Israel's wilderness experience. Jesus spends forty days in the wilderness and answers from the portion of Deuteronomy that reflects on Israel's testing. This supports a reading of Jesus as faithfully reenacting Israel's story, though the chapter never explicitly equates forty days with forty years.

The chapter also moves through widening circles: wilderness solitude, Capernaum, all Galilee, Syria, and crowds from multiple surrounding regions. Correspondingly, Jesus moves from personal testing to proclamation, disciple-making, and public healing.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Scriptural Interpretation
> The explicit fulfillment formula and Isaiah quotation strongly establish that Matthew presents the location of Jesus' Galilean ministry as theologically significant: his arrival is portrayed as light reaching a region associated with darkness and death.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong authorial framing · [[Explicit Scriptural Interpretation in Matthew 4|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary And Intertextual Pattern
> The combination of forty days in the wilderness and three replies drawn from Deuteronomy 6–8 provides substantial literary support for reading Jesus as reenacting Israel's wilderness testing while remaining obedient. This is a well-grounded interpretation, although Matthew does not explicitly explain the forty days as a symbol of Israel's forty years.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Strong, widely recognized interpretation · [[Literary And Intertextual Pattern in Matthew 4|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does each of Jesus' answers come from [[Deuteronomy 6]] or [[Deuteronomy 8]]?
- What distinguishes trusting God from testing God in Matthew 4:6–7?
- How does the promise of the world's kingdoms in Matthew 4:8–10 contrast with Jesus' proclamation of the kingdom of heaven in Matthew 4:17?
- What costs of discipleship does Matthew emphasize by naming nets, ship, and father?
- Why does Matthew connect the beginning of the Galilean ministry with light in [[Isaiah 9]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

Read [[Deuteronomy 6]], [[Deuteronomy 8]], and [[Psalm 91]] to examine the original contexts of every citation. Compare the call of the fishermen with [[Mark 1]], noting the repeated language of immediate response.
