# Mark 9 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mark 9]] · My notes: [[Mark 9 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus is transfigured on the mountain—He casts out an unclean spirit—He teaches concerning His death and resurrection, who will be greatest, and the condemnation of those who offend His little ones.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Kingdom and transfiguration (9:1–13):** Three disciples see Jesus transfigured with Moses and Elias; the heavenly voice commands them to hear Jesus. The descent turns immediately toward suffering, resurrection, and the coming of Elias.

2. **Failed exorcism and dependent faith (9:14–29):** The disciples cannot cast out a destructive spirit. The father's mixed belief and unbelief receive an answer from Jesus, while the disciples learn their need for prayer.

3. **Passion prediction and status reversal (9:30–37):** Jesus predicts death and resurrection. The disciples instead dispute greatness, and Jesus defines first place as becoming servant of all and receiving a child.

4. **Boundaries, stumbling, and peace (9:38–50):** Jesus corrects an attempt to exclude another worker, warns severely against causing believers to stumble, and concludes with the command to have peace.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- The father's plea, “Lord, I believe; help thou mine unbelief” (9:24), portrays genuine [[Faith]] that honestly seeks help for what it lacks.
- Mark 9:28-29 connects ministry with [[Prayer]] and, in the supplied canonical reading, [[Fasting]]. The textual history of “and fasting” should be considered before building doctrine on that phrase alone.
- Jesus again predicts his death and [[Resurrection]] (9:31), while the disciples remain unable to understand him.
- Jesus answers the dispute over greatness by joining [[Humility]] with [[Service]]: the would-be first must be last and servant of all, and receiving a child in his name is receiving him and the One who sent him.
- The outside-exorcist episode corrects the disciples' attempt to prohibit a man merely because he does not follow their immediate group. Its scope should not be expanded into a claim that communal or doctrinal boundaries never matter.
- The closing warnings use severe judgment imagery to stress the danger of causing believers to stumble and refusing to abandon sin.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several expressions require care:

1. **“Elias” (9:11-13)** is the KJV form of Elijah. Mark 9 does not explicitly name the person meant by the already-come Elias. John the Baptist is a contextually strong interpretation, made explicit in [[Matthew 17]], but it should not be rewritten as Mark's own identification.
2. **“Offend” (9:42-47)** concerns causing someone to stumble or fall into sin, not merely causing irritation or hurt feelings. The hand, foot, and eye sayings use deliberately severe language; reading them as moral hyperbole is strong but remains interpretation.
3. **“Prayer and fasting” (9:29)** reflects the supplied canonical text. Some early Greek witnesses omit “and fasting,” so textual history should be distinguished from quotation of the canonical form used here.
4. The manuscript tradition also varies at 9:23, in the repetition represented by verses 44 and 46, and in part of 9:49. These variants do not erase the teachings in the supplied text, but they matter when claims concern the earliest recoverable wording.
5. The undying-worm and unquenched-fire refrain closely echoes [[Isaiah 66]]. “Salted with fire” remains unusually difficult and may evoke judgment, purification, sacrifice, testing, or overlapping ideas.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Contrast and repetition organize much of the chapter: mountain glory is followed by suffering below; the command to hear Jesus is followed by disciples who do not understand him; a dispute over greatness is answered by a child held in Jesus' arms; and warnings about rivalry, exclusion, and stumbling culminate in the command to live at peace. The disciples' failed exorcism and the later success of a man outside their immediate group create a suggestive thematic contrast, although Mark supplies no explicit callback joining the episodes.

The Transfiguration also draws on a network of biblical theophany motifs. [[Exodus 24]] and [[Exodus 34]] illuminate the mountain, six-day interval, cloud, Moses, and radiance, while [[Deuteronomy 18]] offers a possible background for “hear him.” These comparisons support a Sinai-like presentation but do not prove direct dependence or exclude other scriptural imagery. The judgment refrain recalls [[Isaiah 66]], and the salting of sacrifice recalls [[Leviticus 2]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does Mark 9:1 refer primarily to the following Transfiguration, or to the resurrection, the church's mission, judgment, or the final coming of God's kingdom?
- Is the already-come Elias in 9:13 meant to be recognized as John the Baptist, and what does Mark's failure to name him contribute?
- What weight should devotional or doctrinal interpretation place on “and fasting” when the phrase's textual history is disputed?
- Who are “these little ones” in 9:42: literal children, vulnerable believers, or both?
- How should Mark 9:40 be compared with Matthew 12:30? Do the different controversies adequately explain their opposite surface formulations?
- Are the child's apparent death and rising in 9:26-27 meant to echo Jesus' predicted death and resurrection, or are they simply features of the healing account?
- Does “salted with fire” signify judgment, purification, sacrifice, testing, or a combination, and how does the closing command to live at peace guide its application?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the Transfiguration, Elias discussion, and healing narrative in [[Matthew 17]] and [[Luke 9]], noting both their agreement and their distinctive wording. Read the passion predictions and teachings about greatness beside [[Mark 8]] and [[Mark 10]]. For scriptural backgrounds, examine [[Exodus 24]], [[Exodus 34]], [[Deuteronomy 18]], [[Malachi 4]], [[Leviticus 2]], and [[Isaiah 66]]. A critical Greek apparatus can clarify the textual history of Mark 9:23, 9:29, 9:44, 9:46, and 9:49 while keeping that inquiry distinct from the supplied canonical text.
