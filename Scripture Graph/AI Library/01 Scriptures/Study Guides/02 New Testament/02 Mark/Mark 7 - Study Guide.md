# Mark 7 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mark 7]] · My notes: [[Mark 7 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus reproves the Pharisees for their false traditions and ceremonies—He casts a devil out of the daughter of a Greek woman—He opens the ears and loosens the tongue of a person with an impediment.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Tradition tested by commandment (7:1–13):** A dispute about washing leads to Isaiah's lip-and-heart indictment and the Corban example.
2. **Defilement traced to the heart (7:14–23):** Jesus addresses the crowd, then explains the saying privately to his disciples.
3. **A Gentile mother's petition (7:24–30):** A dialogue about children, bread, dogs, and crumbs ends in the daughter's deliverance.
4. **Ears and tongue opened (7:31–37):** Jesus heals a deaf man with impaired speech, and the witnesses proclaim the event despite a command to silence.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Jesus condemns traditions that make God's word ineffective and excuse neglect of identifiable duties (7:8–13). In the Corban example, religious language does not justify withholding assistance from parents, connecting [[Obedience]], [[Family]], and practical [[Service]]. The passage targets traditions that displace divine command; it does not establish that every inherited religious practice is invalid.

Verses 15 and 20–23 locate moral defilement in what proceeds from the heart. The list includes thoughts, desires, actions, speech, and dispositions, so inward purity cannot be reduced either to feelings alone or to visible rule-keeping. This teaching relates directly to [[Sin]] and [[Repentance]].

The Syrophenician account contributes to the theme of [[Jews and Gentiles]]. The word "first" plausibly indicates Israel's priority rather than permanent exclusion, and the woman's request is granted. The episode supports an expanding horizon of mercy but does not alone state a complete doctrine of Gentile mission or reveal why Jesus initially used the children-and-dogs saying.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Mark supplies unusually visible explanations in this chapter: unwashen clarifies defiled hands (7:2), verses 3–4 describe washing customs, Corban is explained as a gift (7:11), and Ephphatha is translated as "Be opened" (7:34). Together these glosses suggest readers who could not be assumed to know every Jewish custom or Semitic expression.

The phrase "purging all meats" in 7:19 has significant interpretive and grammatical questions. In the supplied canonical wording, it should not be treated without argument as an unambiguous narrator's declaration that every dietary distinction was immediately abolished. The unmistakable emphasis of verses 20–23 is that moral evil arises from within.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly contrasts outward expression with inward reality: lips are contrasted with the heart (7:6), and what enters a person is contrasted with the evils that proceed from within (7:15–23). Bread appears in both the washing controversy and the children's-bread metaphor. Commands to hear and understand also resonate with the later opening of a deaf man's ears, though this may reflect ordinary Markan language rather than deliberate chapter-wide architecture.

The ending also develops a concealment-and-publicity contrast. Jesus "could not be hid" in 7:24, and witnesses increasingly publish the final healing despite his command in 7:36. [[Mark 1]] offers a close parallel to the latter pattern. The statement that Jesus makes the deaf hear and the speech-impaired speak probably echoes [[Isaiah 35]], but Mark does not identify it as a formal quotation or fulfillment.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. What criteria within verses 8–13 distinguish a corrupting tradition from a legitimate religious practice?
2. Why does Jesus move from the language of food and washing to a list containing actions, desires, speech, and dispositions?
3. What interpretive difference does the word "first" make in Jesus' response to the Syrophoenician woman?
4. How does the movement from "Hearken" in verse 14 to opened ears in verse 35 shape the final miracle?
5. What becomes clearer—and what remains different—when Mark 7 is compared carefully with [[Matthew 15]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the chapter's explicit use of [[Isaiah 29]] with its probable resonance with [[Isaiah 35]]. Study the two feeding narratives in [[Mark 6]] and [[Mark 8]] alongside the Syrophoenician woman's bread-and-crumbs dialogue. The comparison may clarify how Mark develops the themes of provision, Israel's priority, and ministry beyond Israel, but conclusions should be based on the full narrative rather than the bread vocabulary alone.
