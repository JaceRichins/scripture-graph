# Mark 2 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mark 2]] · My notes: [[Mark 2 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus forgives sins, heals a paralytic, eats with tax gatherers and sinners, and announces that He is Lord of the Sabbath.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Forgiveness and healing (vv. 1-12):** Friends carry a paralyzed man through a crowded house and lower him through the roof. Jesus pronounces forgiveness, which provokes a silent objection from scribes ("who can forgive sins but God only?"), and then heals the man as a visible sign tied to that claim of authority.
2. **Levi and the disputed meal (vv. 13-17):** Levi leaves his tax booth to follow Jesus; a meal that follows includes "publicans and sinners," prompting criticism that Jesus answers with the physician saying.
3. **Fasting and newness (vv. 18-22):** Jesus explains his disciples' present non-fasting through the image of a wedding and the bridegroom's presence, anticipates a future fast, and adds two paired sayings about new cloth and new wine damaging old settings.
4. **The Sabbath dispute (vv. 23-28):** Answering an accusation about grain-picking, Jesus recalls David's eating of the shewbread in [[1 Samuel 21]], states that "the sabbath was made for man," and concludes that "the Son of man is Lord also of the sabbath."
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects to [[Repentance and Forgiveness]], [[Faith]], [[Miracles]], [[Fasting]], and the [[Sabbath Day]]. Twice the title "Son of man" is paired with a claim of unique authority — over sin (2:10) and over the Sabbath (2:28) — and in both cases a visible act (a healing; the argument from David's precedent) is offered as a sign supporting the claim, though the narrative's own logic is not the same as independent proof of Jesus' identity. The chapter never states that the man's paralysis was caused by a specific sin, so a causal reading goes beyond the text. Jesus' statement that he "came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance" (2:17) frames his meal with "publicans and sinners" as mission rather than approval, though "righteous" here may be rhetorical rather than a literal class needing no repentance. The teaching that "the sabbath was made for man" (2:27) centers Sabbath observance on human good without stating that Sabbath obligation is abolished.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Each of the four episodes follows a similar shape: an action by Jesus or his disciples, an objection from onlookers, and a concise pronouncement from Jesus. Concrete narrative details — the crowded doorway (2:2), the roof broken open to lower the bed (2:4) — give the opening scene an especially vivid, particular quality. The bridegroom saying (2:19-20) and the paired images of new cloth and new wine (2:21-22) are figurative sayings whose precise referents the chapter does not spell out, anticipating the parabolic teaching found later in Jesus' ministry.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual-Critical
> Jesus dates David's eating of the shewbread to 'the days of Abiathar the high priest' (2:26), but the underlying Old Testament episode (David fleeing to the priest at Nob) narrates Ahimelech, Abiathar's father, as the officiating priest; Abiathar becomes high priest only afterward. This discrepancy between Mark's wording and the earlier narrative is a well-known text-critical crux.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.8 · genuine, well-known textual crux · [[Textual-Critical in Mark 2|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Sign
> Within the narrative's own logic, the visible healing is offered as evidence that the Son of man has authority to perform the otherwise invisible act of forgiving sins.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · well-calibrated, explicit textual basis · [[Internal Narrative Sign in Mark 2|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Christological *(tentative)*
> The scribes' unspoken objection assumes a premise ('who can forgive sins but God only?') that Jesus never contradicts; instead he offers the visible healing as proof of an invisible authority claim, so the episode's rhetorical force depends on readers accepting the scribes' theological premise as sound.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · disputed christological inference

> [!evidence] Textual/Harmonization *(tentative)*
> Levi the son of Alphaeus (Mark 2:14) is traditionally identified with the apostle Matthew, largely because the parallel calling narrative names the tax collector 'Matthew' instead of 'Levi.' Mark's own text never applies the name Matthew to Levi, and Mark elsewhere separately names 'James the son of Alphaeus' among the Twelve, so the identification of Levi as an apostle at all rests on harmonization across Gospels rather than on Mark 2 alone.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · disputed, honestly labeled
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does "their faith" (2:5) refer only to the four bearers, or does it also include the paralytic himself?
- What relationship does the narrative draw between forgiveness and healing, and what does it leave unstated (the text never says the man's sins caused his paralysis)?
- Is Levi (2:14) the same person as the apostle Matthew? Mark itself never applies that name to Levi; the identification rests on harmonizing with Matthew 9:9.
- Is "the bridegroom shall be taken away" (2:20) meant as an early foreshadowing of Jesus' death, or only as an analogy about future absence?
- What do the new cloth, old garment, new wine, and old bottles represent? The chapter supplies the images without decoding them.
- Mark 2:26 names Abiathar as high priest in the shewbread episode, while [[1 Samuel 21]] narrates Ahimelech as the officiating priest (Abiathar appears afterward, in [[1 Samuel 22]], as Ahimelech's son). Mark supplies no resolution to this naming difficulty.
<!-- SG:END questions -->
