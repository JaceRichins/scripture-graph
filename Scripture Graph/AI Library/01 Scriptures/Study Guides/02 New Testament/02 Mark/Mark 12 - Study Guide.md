# Mark 12 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mark 12]] · My notes: [[Mark 12 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus gives the parable of the wicked husbandmen—He speaks of paying taxes, celestial marriage, the two great commandments, the divine sonship of Christ, and the widow’s mites.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Rejected authority (12:1-12):** The vineyard tenants reject servants and kill the wellbeloved son; the rejected-stone scripture announces reversal.

2. **Questions intended to test Jesus (12:13-27):** Pharisees and Herodians raise tribute; Sadducees raise resurrection through a levirate-marriage case.

3. **The central commands (12:28-34):** Love of God and neighbor is placed above sacrificial performance.

4. **Jesus questions and warns (12:35-40):** He complicates the title “Son of David” and exposes honor-seeking, exploitative scribes.

5. **The widow at the treasury (12:41-44):** Her tiny gift is judged greater because it is her entire livelihood.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Rejection and vindication of the Son**: the parable's wellbeloved son is killed and cast out of the vineyard (12:7-8), and Jesus immediately applies the rejected-stone saying from Psalm 118:22-23 (12:10-11). Mark doesn't spell out a one-to-one allegorical key, but the leaders' own recognition that the parable targeted them (12:12) anchors a reading in which rejection is followed by divine reversal.
- **[[Resurrection]] and the "God of the living"**: against Sadducean denial of resurrection, Jesus argues from God's continuing self-identification as "the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (12:26) that God is "not the God of the dead, but of the living" (12:27). Mark 12:25 says the resurrected "neither marry, nor are given in marriage; but are as the angels" — this answers the Sadducees' specific case, but the verse alone doesn't settle whether previously formed marriage covenants are said to end or simply not to be newly formed; that question is genuinely disputed.
- **The two great commandments**: love of God (Deuteronomy 6:4-5) and love of neighbor (Leviticus 19:18) are joined as the sum of the law (12:29-31); a scribe's agreement that this exceeds ritual sacrifice (12:33) draws Jesus's comment that he is "not far from the kingdom of God" (12:34) — approval, though not a claim that he has entered it.
- **Government and God's claims**: "Render to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (12:17) distinguishes a civil obligation from a religious one without defining where either claim ends.
- **Consecration and cost**: the widow's gift of "all that she had, even all her living" (12:44) sets relative cost, not absolute amount, as the standard by which Jesus evaluates an offering.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The tribute answer turns on the coin's “image and superscription.” Jesus identifies what bears Caesar's mark but leaves unstated the full extent of what belongs to God. Interpretations connecting this silence with humanity bearing God's image are suggestive, but that connection is not explicit in Mark 12.

In the resurrection dispute, Jesus cites God's words, “I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob,” and then supplies the conclusion that God is God of the living. Readers should distinguish Jesus' stated conclusion from later reconstructions of the argument based solely on verb tense.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The vineyard scene combines imagery from [[Isaiah 5]] with the rejected stone of Psalm 118, giving the parable a scriptural pattern of entrusted stewardship, rejection, judgment, and reversal.

The chapter repeatedly contrasts appearance with reality: flattering questioners conceal hypocrisy; large gifts may cost less than tiny ones; prestigious scribes devour widows' houses; and the rejected one becomes the cornerstone. The narrator marks a hinge at 12:34 — "no man after that durst ask him any question" — shifting Jesus from respondent to questioner as he raises his own question about David's son (12:35-37). The widow episode that closes the chapter is especially complex because it follows the condemnation of those who exploit widows: it may praise her self-giving, indict the surrounding institution, or intentionally sustain both readings at once.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Doctrinal Tension
> Jesus states that 'when they shall rise from the dead, they neither marry, nor are given in marriage; but are as the angels which are in heaven' (Mark 12:25). Read at face value, this statement is in tension with the doctrine of eternal, sealed marriage; it is honestly one of the more difficult verses for a theology that teaches marriage continues after resurrection, and deserves to be presented as a genuine difficulty rather than explained away by default.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What belongs to Caesar, and what limits follow from the prior claim that everything ultimately belongs to God?

- Does Mark 12:25 describe only the forming of marriages after resurrection, or also the status of relationships formed before death? What conclusions exceed the verse's wording?

- Why does Jesus call the discerning scribe “not far” from the [[Kingdom of God]] rather than saying he has entered it?

- Is the widow presented primarily as an example of devotion, as a victim of the system condemned in Mark 12:40, or as both?

- How do the son, rejected stone, and David's Lord jointly contribute to Mark's portrayal of [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
