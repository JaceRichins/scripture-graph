# Mark 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mark 10]] · My notes: [[Mark 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus teaches the higher law of marriage—He blesses little children—Jesus counsels the rich young man, foretells His own death, and heals blind Bartimæus.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter unfolds in four movements connected by Jesus's journey "in the way" to Jerusalem (vv.17, 32, 52):
1. **Marriage and children** (vv.1-16) — the divorce controversy with the Pharisees, clarified privately with the disciples, followed by Jesus blessing the children.
2. **Wealth and the kingdom** (vv.17-31) — the rich man's question and refusal, Jesus's teaching on riches, and Peter's claim of sacrifice answered by the reversal "many that are first shall be last."
3. **The way to Jerusalem** (vv.32-45) — the third and most detailed passion prediction, followed immediately by James and John's request for status and Jesus's teaching that greatness means service.
4. **Bartimaeus at Jericho** (vv.46-52) — a healing that becomes a hinge into the Jerusalem entry of [[Mark 11]], as the newly-sighted beggar follows Jesus "in the way" where the Twelve have struggled to.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Marriage]] and [[Family]]**: Jesus grounds marriage in the creation ordinance rather than the Mosaic concession, and applies the prohibition on remarriage-as-adultery symmetrically to husband and wife (vv.6-12) — notable for its era.
- **[[Kingdom of God]]**: entrance requires being received "as a little child" (v.15); the text does not specify which childlike quality (innocence, dependence, humility) is meant, so that should be read as an open question rather than settled doctrine.
- **[[Consecration]]**: Jesus's call to the rich man to "sell whatsoever thou hast, and give to the poor" (v.21) is addressed to this individual; the broader warning that follows (vv.23-27) generalizes the danger of trusting riches without claiming an identical command for every disciple.
- **[[Service]] and [[Humility]]**: greatness is redefined as ministering and being "servant of all" (vv.42-44), grounded in Jesus's own mission.
- **[[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]**: v.45's "ransom for many" is a foundational statement that Christ's death was purposeful and beneficial to others; the verse does not specify the mechanism or recipient of the ransom, so it should not be read as settling any single atonement theory.
- **[[Faith]]**: Bartimaeus's restored sight is attributed directly to his faith (v.52).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Put away” means divorce in this passage. The contrast between what Moses “suffered” or permitted and what God established “from the beginning” is central to Jesus's argument.

The comparison of a camel passing through a needle's eye functions in context as an image of human impossibility; verse 27 supplies the interpretive resolution by locating possibility with God.

The “cup” and “baptism” of verses 38-39 are paired figures for Jesus's coming ordeal. Here “baptism” describes being overwhelmed by suffering, not the ordinance discussed under [[Baptism]].

“Ransom for many” expresses costly deliverance for others, but the metaphor alone does not define a complete theory of atonement.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The question “What would ye that I should do for you?” addressed to James and John is closely echoed when Jesus asks Bartimaeus what he wants. The brothers ask for honor; Bartimaeus asks for sight. This makes the final healing an apt contrast to the disciples' continuing failure to perceive the character of Jesus's mission.

Road imagery reinforces the contrast. Jesus is leading toward Jerusalem and death, the disciples follow in fear and dispute status, and Bartimaeus ends the chapter following Jesus “in the way.” This pattern is textually visible, though the degree of deliberate symbolic design cannot be demonstrated from repetition alone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs

> Mark 10:45 is strong internal textual evidence that the narrative presents Jesus's impending death as purposeful service benefiting others through a ransom metaphor, rather than merely as an unforeseen victimization. The precise mechanism implied by the metaphor remains open to interpretation.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does Jesus's appeal to creation contribute that the debate over Mosaic permission did not?
- Which childlike quality, if any, does the text itself specify in verses 14-15?
- Why does Mark state that Jesus loved the petitioner immediately before recording the costly command?
- How does “with persecutions” qualify readings of the hundredfold promise?
- What changes when the request of James and John is read beside the request of Bartimaeus?
- Which conclusions about the Atonement are firmly supported by “ransom for many,” and which require additional texts?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the divorce teaching with its Matthean parallel, which includes an exception clause for fornication ([[Matthew 19]] 19:9) — a real Synoptic difference worth investigating rather than harmonizing away. Compare the wealth and children episodes with [[Luke 18]], and the passion prediction, honor request, and Jericho healing with [[Matthew 20]] (which attributes the request to the mother of James and John). Trace the "cup" of suffering forward into [[Mark 14]], and compare the "ransom for many" of v.45 with the Suffering Servant language of Isaiah 53, keeping in mind that direct literary dependence there remains debated.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
