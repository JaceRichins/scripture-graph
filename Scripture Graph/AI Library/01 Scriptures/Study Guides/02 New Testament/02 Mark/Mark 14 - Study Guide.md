# Mark 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mark 14]] · My notes: [[Mark 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus is anointed with oil—He eats the Passover, institutes the sacrament, suffers in Gethsemane, and is betrayed by Judas—Jesus is falsely accused, and Peter denies that he knows Him.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Plot, anointing, and betrayal (14:1-11):** The authorities seek a discreet arrest. An unnamed woman gives a costly gift that Jesus interprets in relation to burial; Judas approaches the chief priests to betray Jesus, and they promise him money.
2. **Passover and covenant meal (14:12-26):** Jesus foretells the preparations, identifies the coming betrayal, and gives bread and the cup as his body and covenant blood.
3. **Predicted scattering and prayer (14:27-42):** Confident promises of loyalty give way to repeated sleep while Jesus prays in anguish and submission.
4. **Arrest and flight (14:43-52):** Judas's signal enables the arrest; a bystander's resistance with a sword fails, the disciples flee, and an unidentified young man escapes naked.
5. **Hearing and denial (14:53-72):** Peter enters the courtyard, Jesus is examined amid disagreeing testimony, and the narrative returns to Peter's three denials. This arrangement creates a strong contrast between Jesus's answer before the high priest and Peter's denial outside.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The meal joins [[Sacrament]], [[Covenants]], and sacrifice: the bread is identified with Jesus’s body and the cup with blood “shed for many” (14:22-24). The text thereby interprets Jesus’s death as benefiting others, in harmony with Mark 10:45.

Jesus’s [[Prayer]] in [[Gethsemane]] presents obedient submission without minimizing suffering. “Not what I will, but what thou wilt” follows an earnest request that the cup be removed (14:36). Faithful obedience here is not emotional detachment; it is willing trust expressed amid sorrow.

The apostles’ conduct warns against confidence unsupported by watchfulness. Peter and the others promise loyalty unto death (14:29-31), yet sleep, flee, and deny. Jesus’s command to watch and pray therefore addresses a danger the narrative immediately demonstrates.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Abba” (14:36) preserves an Aramaic form of address alongside “Father.” Its presence is linguistically noteworthy, but it cannot by itself prove a verbatim transcript or eyewitness authorship.

The KJV’s “new testament” in 14:24 belongs to covenant language. Exodus 24:8 supplies the prominent biblical association between covenant and blood, while Jeremiah 31:31 promises a new covenant. “Shed for many” also recalls the Gospel’s earlier statement that the Son of man gives his life for many (Mark 10:45).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several contrasts carry the chapter's meaning. An unnamed woman relinquishes ointment valued at more than three hundred pence; immediately afterward Judas approaches the chief priests to betray Jesus, and they promise him money (14:3-11). The adjacency invites comparison, although Mark does not explicitly state Judas's motive. Jesus answers the high priest's question about his identity, while Peter nearby denies knowing him (14:54-72). Mockers demand that Jesus prophesy, even as his earlier prediction about Peter reaches exact narrative fulfillment (14:30, 65, 72).

Prediction and fulfillment provide cohesion. The disciples find the room as described (14:13-16); the flock scatters (14:27, 50); and three denials culminate in the second cockcrow (14:30, 66-72). This strongly establishes Mark's literary portrayal of Jesus's foreknowledge, but it cannot by itself determine how or when each prediction entered the tradition.

Mark 14:62 combines imagery from [[Psalm 110]] and [[Daniel 7]]. The presently accused Jesus speaks of the Son of Man at the right hand of power and coming with heaven's clouds, reversing the scene's apparent authority. The imagery strongly conveys authority and vindication; whether it refers primarily to heavenly enthronement, historical judgment, the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]], or overlapping horizons remains disputed.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Synoptic Textual-Comparative
> When the high priest asks 'Art thou the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?', Mark records Jesus answering unambiguously, 'I am,' whereas Matthew's parallel has the more indirect 'Thou hast said,' and Luke's parallel has 'Ye say that I am' — a checkable wording difference across the three Synoptic trial accounts.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · English textual difference accepted; idiomatic force disputed · [[Synoptic Textual-Comparative in Mark 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual-Comparative
> Mark's version of the cup-saying ('shed for many') omits the phrase 'for the remission of sins' found in Matthew's parallel account of the same words, a checkable textual difference between the two Gospels.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · Textual difference accepted; editorial significance disputed · [[Textual-Comparative in Mark 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Pattern
> Within Mark’s narrative, several predictions receive explicit fulfillment: the two disciples find the Passover arrangements as Jesus described; the disciples flee after the shepherd saying; and Peter’s three denials conclude with the second cockcrow and his recollection of Jesus’s words. This is strong evidence that the narrator presents Jesus as foreknowing and interpreting the coming events.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong internal narrative pattern; historical inference limited · [[Internal Narrative Pattern in Mark 14|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Mark place the woman’s gift immediately before Judas’s bargain (14:3-11)? What different valuations of Jesus emerge?
- Does “the poor with you always” cancel concern for the poor, or does the rest of 14:7—and its connection with Deuteronomy 15:11—preserve that obligation?
- What does Jesus’s request to remove the cup add to the meaning of his submission (14:35-36)?
- How do Peter’s promises, sleep, distance, denials, and tears trace the progression of his failure (14:29-31, 37-40, 54, 66-72)?
- Why might Mark alternate attention between Jesus inside the hearing and Peter outside it (14:54-72)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
