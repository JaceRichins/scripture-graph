# Mark 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mark 14]] · My notes: [[Mark 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Mark 14 moves from preparations for Jesus's death to the collapse of his followers' promised loyalty. An unnamed woman prepares him for burial; Judas prepares betrayal; Jesus gives the Passover bread and cup an interpretation centered on his body and covenant blood; and his prayer in [[Gethsemane]] joins profound distress with willing obedience. The chapter ends by alternating between Jesus's testimony and Peter's denials.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Plot, anointing, and betrayal (14:1-11):** The authorities seek a discreet arrest. An unnamed woman gives a costly gift that Jesus relates to burial; Judas approaches the chief priests, who promise him money.

2. **Passover and covenant meal (14:12-26):** The disciples find the room as Jesus predicted. At the meal he identifies the betrayer and gives the bread and cup as his body and covenant blood.

3. **Predicted scattering and prayer (14:27-42):** Promises of loyalty give way to repeated sleep while Jesus prays in anguish and submission.

4. **Arrest and flight (14:43-52):** Judas's signal enables the arrest. Armed resistance fails, all the disciples flee, and an unidentified young man escapes naked.

5. **Hearing and denial (14:53-72):** Disagreeing testimony gives way to a direct question about Jesus's identity. Jesus answers affirmatively; outside, Peter repeatedly denies knowing him.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The meal joins [[Passover]], [[Sacrament]], [[Covenants]], and [[Sacrifice]]. The bread is identified with Jesus's body and the cup with blood “shed for many” (14:22-24). Within Mark, this interpretation coheres with the Son of man giving his life “for many” in [[Mark 10]].

Jesus's [[Prayer]] in [[Gethsemane]] presents obedient submission without minimizing suffering. His request that the cup be removed is real, as is his resolution: “not what I will, but what thou wilt” (14:36). Obedience here is not emotional detachment but willing trust amid sorrow.

The disciples' conduct warns against confidence unsupported by watchfulness. Peter and the others promise loyalty unto death (14:29-31), yet they sleep, flee, and deny (14:37-40, 50, 66-72). The command to watch and pray therefore addresses a danger the narrative immediately demonstrates.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Abba” (14:36) is a transliterated Aramaic address preserved in the Greek text and followed by the Greek equivalent rendered “Father.” This is consistent with an Aramaic setting or an inherited prayer formula, but it cannot by itself establish a verbatim transcript or eyewitness authorship.

The KJV’s “new testament” in 14:24 is covenant language. [[Exodus 24]] provides a strong association between covenant and blood, while [[Jeremiah 31]] supplies conceptually relevant new-covenant language. “Shed for many” also recalls [[Mark 10]]; [[Isaiah 53]] is a plausible but less certain background rather than an explicit citation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Contrasts carry much of the chapter’s meaning. A woman relinquishes ointment valued at more than three hundred pence; immediately afterward Judas approaches the chief priests, who promise him money (14:3-11). The sequence invites comparison, although Mark does not state Judas’s motive. Jesus affirms his identity before the high priest, while Peter nearby denies knowing him (14:54-72). Mockers command Jesus to prophesy as his earlier prediction about Peter reaches narrative fulfillment (14:30, 65, 72).

Prediction and fulfillment provide cohesion: the disciples find the room as described (14:13-16), the flock scatters (14:27, 50), and three denials culminate in the second cockcrow (14:30, 66-72). This strongly establishes Mark’s literary portrayal of Jesus’s foreknowledge, but it does not determine the historical timing, exact wording, or transmission history of each prediction.

Mark 14:62 combines imagery associated with [[Psalm 110]] and [[Daniel 7]]. The accused Jesus speaks of the Son of Man at the right hand of power and coming with heaven’s clouds, reversing the scene’s apparent authority. The imagery supports a reading of enthronement and vindication, while its relationship to judgment and the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]] remains disputed.

The unnamed young man’s flight in 14:51-52 intensifies the scene of abandonment, but Mark gives neither his identity nor an explicit explanation of his significance.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Narrative And Literary Evidence
> The fulfilled predictions concerning the prepared room, the disciples' scattering, and Peter's denials provide strong evidence that Mark deliberately characterizes Jesus as possessing foreknowledge and that prediction-and-fulfillment is a major device organizing the chapter.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted literary observation · [[Narrative And Literary Evidence in Mark 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Synoptic Textual-Comparative
> When the high priest asks 'Art thou the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?', Mark records Jesus answering unambiguously, 'I am,' whereas Matthew's parallel has the more indirect 'Thou hast said,' and Luke's parallel has 'Ye say that I am' — a checkable wording difference across the three Synoptic trial accounts.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · English textual difference accepted; idiomatic force disputed · [[Synoptic Textual-Comparative in Mark 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual-Comparative
> Mark's version of the cup-saying ('shed for many') omits the phrase 'for the remission of sins' found in Matthew's parallel account of the same words, a checkable textual difference between the two Gospels.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · Textual difference accepted; editorial significance disputed · [[Textual-Comparative in Mark 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Pattern
> Within Mark’s narrative, several predictions receive explicit fulfillment: the two disciples find the Passover arrangements as Jesus described; the disciples flee after the shepherd saying; and Peter’s three denials conclude with the second cockcrow and his recollection of Jesus’s words. This is strong evidence that the narrator presents Jesus as foreknowing and interpreting the coming events.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong internal narrative pattern; historical inference limited · [[Internal Narrative Pattern in Mark 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Legal-Historical Inference From Narrative *(tentative)*
> The high priest declares Jesus's answer 'blasphemy' immediately after Jesus affirms being 'the Christ, the Son of the Blessed' and claims future session at God's right hand and coming with the clouds of heaven. This supports the narrative claim that Jesus's self-identification (including the Danielic/enthronement claim, not the messianic title alone) was treated as the operative legal offense, but it does not establish the precise legal reasoning applied, since Mark does not record the high priest's argument in technical terms.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · narrative sequence clear; legal reconstruction disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Mark place the woman's gift immediately before Judas's bargain (14:3-11)? What contrasting valuations of Jesus result?

- Does “the poor with you always” cancel concern for the poor, or do the rest of 14:7 and [[Deuteronomy 15]] preserve that obligation?

- What does Jesus's request to remove the cup add to the meaning of his submission (14:35-36)?

- How do Peter's promises, sleep, distance, denials, and tears trace the progression of his failure (14:29-31, 37-40, 54, 66-72)?

- Why does the narrative place Jesus's affirmative answer between Peter's entrance into the courtyard and his denials (14:54-72)?

- How does the failed witness testimony differ from Jesus's earlier temple prediction in [[Mark 13]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
