## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Mark 16:1–4 — Arrival:** Three women come with spices and discover the displaced stone.
2. **Mark 16:5–7 — Announcement:** A young man declares that Jesus has risen and directs the disciples toward Galilee.
3. **Mark 16:8 — Fearful departure:** The women flee in trembling, amazement, and silence.
4. **Mark 16:9–14 — Appearances and unbelief:** Reports from witnesses are repeatedly rejected before Jesus appears to the eleven.
5. **Mark 16:15–18 — Commission and signs:** The gospel is to be preached universally; belief, baptism, and accompanying signs are described.
6. **Mark 16:19–20 — Ascension and mission:** Jesus is received into heaven, and the disciples preach with divine confirmation.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's central claim is [[Resurrection]]: the one who was crucified is declared risen. The narrative also emphasizes the difficulty of receiving testimony; even Jesus's associates initially reject reports from multiple witnesses.

Mark 16:15–20 joins [[Missionary Work]] with the Lord's continuing action. The disciples preach, but the Lord works with them and confirms the word. The signs belong naturally with [[Spiritual Gifts]] and [[Miracles]], though the passage does not require every believer to display every sign.

Doctrinal use of Mark 16:9–20 should acknowledge its textual history. Its uncertain Markan authorship does not make every teaching in it false, and major themes such as resurrection, mission, baptism, spiritual gifts, and ascension have support elsewhere in scripture.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two wording details merit care. First, Mark calls the tomb figure a “young man,” not explicitly an angel. Identifying him as angelic is a reasonable interpretation informed by his white garment and by parallel accounts, but it remains an interpretation of Mark's wording.

Second, Mark 16:16 is grammatically asymmetrical: belief and baptism appear in the positive promise, while only unbelief appears in the condemnation clause. Any conclusion about exceptional cases involving baptism must therefore come from a broader doctrinal synthesis, not from inserting words into this verse.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The movement from the women's intended act of anointing a dead body to the announcement 'he is risen' produces the chapter's decisive reversal (vv. 1-8): fear is not a minor detail, since the women are affrighted at the tomb and finally flee trembling and amazed, telling no one.

If verse 8 is read as the earliest ending of Mark, the reader is left with an announced resurrection and an uncompleted command — a striking, unresolved close. Whether this was a deliberate literary strategy or the result of a lost final leaf remains genuinely debated among scholars; the Longer Ending (vv. 9-20) resolves much of this tension by having witnesses speak, the risen Jesus appear, and the disciples finally preach everywhere.

Separately, some readers have noted a possible correspondence between the unnamed young man who fled naked at Jesus's arrest (Mark 14:51-52) and the young man clothed in white who proclaims the resurrection (16:5) — an interesting but speculative literary observation, not a scholarly consensus.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual Criticism
> Mainstream New Testament textual scholarship holds that Mark 16:9-20 (the 'Longer Ending') is absent from the two oldest and most reliable complete Greek manuscripts of Mark (Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, 4th century), is missing or marked with asterisks/obeli in a number of other early witnesses, and differs from Mark 1:1-16:8 in vocabulary and style; most modern critical Greek editions (e.g., Nestle-Aland/UBS) print it with a note flagging it as a probable later addition rather than part of the earliest recoverable text.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted among textual critics · [[Textual Criticism in Mark 16|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism
> External manuscript evidence, the existence of more than one alternative ending, and internal linguistic and compositional considerations strongly support the mainstream critical judgment that Mark 16:9–20 is a secondary conclusion rather than part of Mark's earliest recoverable text.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted among textual critics

> [!evidence] Textual/Source Criticism
> Because Mormon 9's 'signs following believers' passage closely matches the King James wording of Mark 16:17-18 — a passage (16:9-20) that mainstream textual scholarship regards as likely absent from the earliest recoverable Greek text of Mark — the parallel is worth weighing honestly as part of the broader, well-documented phenomenon of KJV-derived language appearing throughout the Book of Mormon's English translation.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed · [[Textual/Source Criticism in Mark 16|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why might the earliest form of Mark end on fear and silence (16:8) — a deliberate literary choice, or a lost original ending?
- Why do the disciples repeatedly reject resurrection testimony from Mary Magdalene and the two travelers (16:11, 13) before Jesus appears to them himself (16:14)?
- Why single out Peter by name in verse 7, and what might that have meant to him specifically?
- Does 'them that believe' in 16:17 describe the believing community collectively, or promise every sign to every individual believer?
- How should Mark 16:9-20 be used doctrinally while honestly acknowledging its uncertain manuscript history?
