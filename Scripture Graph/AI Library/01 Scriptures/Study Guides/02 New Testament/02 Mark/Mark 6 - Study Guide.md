# Mark 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mark 6]] · My notes: [[Mark 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus sends forth the Twelve—John the Baptist is beheaded by Herod—Our Lord feeds the five thousand, walks on the water, and heals multitudes.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Rejection in Jesus's own country (6:1-6):** astonishment, offense, unbelief, limited healing, and continued village teaching.
2. **The Twelve sent (6:7-13):** delegated authority, paired mission, restricted provisions, repentance, exorcism, anointing, and healing.
3. **John's death recalled (6:14-29):** competing explanations of Jesus, John's rebuke of Herod, and execution under oath and public pressure.
4. **The apostles return and the multitude is fed (6:30-44):** intended rest gives way to compassion, teaching, and abundant provision.
5. **Jesus on the sea (6:45-52):** solitary [[Prayer]], walking on the sea, reassurance, and the disciples' hardened heart.
6. **Healing in Gennesaret (6:53-56):** rapid recognition and widespread efforts to bring the sick to Jesus.

John's death is placed between the Twelve's departure and return. This bracketing can invite comparison between commissioned ministry and the danger faced by a faithful prophetic witness, although it may also serve to explain Herod's reaction and cover the interval while the Twelve are away.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents authority for ministry as something Jesus gives: the Twelve do not appoint themselves, and their commission joins proclamation with care for afflicted people (6:7-13). Their message is explicitly [[Repentance]], while their deeds include deliverance and healing.

Faith and unbelief matter, but the narrative resists a simplistic formula. Unbelief characterizes Jesus' own country, yet he still heals a few people there (6:5-6). Conversely, the disciples participate in distributing miraculous bread and still fail to understand its significance (6:41-52). Experience of a miracle is not identical to spiritual perception.

Jesus' compassion responds to people who are "as sheep not having a shepherd" (6:34). He meets more than one kind of need: he teaches before he feeds, commands his disciples to participate in providing food, and later receives the sick brought from across Gennesaret.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV form **Elias** is the New Testament spelling used for the Old Testament prophet Elijah; the two names do not designate different people.

Mark repeatedly calls Antipas **king Herod**, while his formal Roman administrative title is commonly identified as tetrarch. The wording may reflect ordinary royal or popular usage rather than a technical claim about rank.

The supplied KJV text ends Mark 6:11 with a saying about Sodom, Gomorrha, and the day of judgment. Many modern critical editions omit that sentence from Mark because important early witnesses lack it and its wording closely parallels Matthew 10:15. This textual judgment concerns the saying's transmission in Mark, not its presence in Matthew or its canonical use in the KJV tradition.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The placement of John's death between the Twelve's departure and return creates a bracketing structure. It plausibly places successful ministry beside the cost of prophetic witness, though chronology and Herod's reaction may also explain the placement.

Two nearby meals form a striking possible contrast. Herod's elite supper ends with John's death, while Jesus's wilderness meal begins in compassion and leaves the crowd filled. The comparison is interpretive because Mark does not explicitly pair the meals.

The feeding repeats the take-bless-break-give sequence found over the bread in [[Mark 14]]. This establishes a narrative connection without making the feeding itself the [[Sacrament]].

The sea scene also has possible epiphanic overtones: biblical texts portray God mastering the sea and passing by human witnesses, while Mark describes Jesus walking on the sea and intending to pass by the disciples. His words, "it is I," may carry added resonance in that setting but can also be ordinary self-identification. The parallels support, but do not prove, a deliberate divine-epiphany reading.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Prosopographical Index Correction
> The 'Philip' named in Mark 6:17 as Herodias's first husband and Herod Antipas's brother is a different person from Philip the apostle; the chapter's verified index data appears to tag this mention as 'Philip (apostle)', which would be a misidentification worth correcting in the graph. Historians further note a complication: Josephus names Herodias's first husband as another son of Herod the Great (sometimes distinguished from 'Philip the Tetrarch,' who is elsewhere said to have married Salome), so the exact identity of 'Philip' here is itself debated among historians independent of the apostle question.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · index error certain; exact Herodian normalization disputed · [[Prosopographical Index Correction in Mark 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] External Historical Corroboration
> Josephus independently reports that Herod Antipas imprisoned and executed John the Baptist. This is substantial external corroboration for the basic association of John's death with Herod, while Josephus explains the execution through Herod's fear of John's political influence rather than Mark's banquet, oath, and Herodias narrative.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted external corroboration · [[External Historical Corroboration in Mark 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] New Testament Textual Criticism
> The supplied KJV text includes a final saying in Mark 6:11 about Sodom and Gomorrha and the day of judgment. Modern critical editions generally omit this sentence from Mark because it is absent from important early witnesses and is commonly judged to have entered the passage through influence from the parallel saying in Matthew 10:15.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · mainstream textual-critical judgment · [[New Testament Textual Criticism in Mark 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Synoptic Textual Comparison *(tentative)*
> Mark's count of 'about five thousand men' fed is explicitly clarified in Matthew's parallel account as counting only men, 'beside women and children,' meaning the total crowd Mark describes was likely larger than the stated figure.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.8 · wording verified; historical supplementation inferred

> [!evidence] Geographical Crux *(tentative)*
> Jesus sends the disciples toward Bethsaida (v45), but they land instead at Gennesaret (v53), a discrepancy long noted by commentators as a geographical crux in Mark's narrative, since the text does not explain the change of landing point.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.8 · unexplained route difference; significance disputed

> [!evidence] Cumulative Intertextual Pattern *(tentative)*
> The feeding narrative gathers biblical motifs associated with Israel's wilderness traditions: a deserted place, sheep without a shepherd, an organized multitude, bread supplied beyond ordinary resources, a sea episode, and Jesus withdrawing to a mountain. This cluster supports a shepherd-of-Israel or new-exodus reading more strongly than any single detail would, but the degree of deliberate allusion remains disputed.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · specific echoes plausible; cumulative pattern disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Mark interrupt the Twelve's mission report with the death of John? How does John's fate change the reader's understanding of successful ministry?
- What should the disciples have understood from the loaves before they encountered the contrary wind (6:52)? Compare [[Mark 8]].
- Why are Jesus' neighbors offended by the same wisdom and works that astonish them?
- What differences between Herod's feast and Jesus' feeding reveal Mark's portrait of authority?
- How do the needs for rest in 6:31 and compassionate service in 6:34 qualify one another?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the hometown rejection with [[Matthew 13]], the missionary instructions with [[Matthew 10]] and [[Luke 9]], and John's death, the feeding, and the sea crossing with [[Matthew 14]]. [[John 6]] gives another account of the feeding and sea episode while developing the bread theme differently. Compare [[Mark 8]] for the return of the bread, perception, and hardened-heart themes.

For possible scriptural backgrounds to the feeding, compare [[Numbers 27]], [[Ezekiel 34]], [[Psalm 23]], [[Exodus 16]], and [[Exodus 18]]. The shepherd language and organization by hundreds and fifties offer the most specific correspondences; a comprehensive new-exodus pattern remains debated.

For historical comparison, Josephus's *Jewish Antiquities* 18.116-119 associates John's execution with Herod Antipas but emphasizes concern about John's influence over the people. Josephus corroborates the basic execution, not every feature or motive in Mark's court narrative. For the longer KJV ending of Mark 6:11, consult a modern critical apparatus or Bruce Metzger's *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament*.
