# Mark 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Mark 5]] · My notes: [[Mark 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Jesus casts out a legion of devils, who then enter the swine—A woman is healed by touching Jesus’ clothes—He raises Jairus’s daughter from the dead.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Across the sea: Legion and the swine (5:1–20).** The man’s condition is described, the spirits submit to Jesus, the swine perish, the inhabitants ask Jesus to leave, and the restored man becomes a witness in Decapolis.

2. **Jairus’s request begins (5:21–24).** A synagogue ruler asks Jesus to save his dying daughter.

3. **The woman interrupts the journey (5:25–34).** Twelve years of suffering end when she approaches Jesus in faith; Jesus brings the hidden encounter into personal and public recognition.

4. **Jairus’s story resumes (5:35–43).** News of death transforms the original request for healing into a test of fear and belief, after which Jesus raises the girl.

The Jairus–woman–Jairus arrangement is a strong literary intercalation. The middle story informs the surrounding one through shared themes of daughters, twelve years, touch, fear, faith, and restoration.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter strongly supports studying [[Faith]], [[Miracles]], and [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]. Faith is explicit in Jesus’ words to the woman and Jairus, but the narrative does not present it as an impersonal technique that obligates God to heal. Its focus is trust in Jesus amid fear and apparent impossibility.

Jesus’ compassion has a restorative direction. The formerly possessed man is sent home to his friends; the woman is called Daughter and sent in peace; the girl is restored to family life. The raising of the girl is meaningfully related to [[Resurrection]], but it should be distinguished from permanent, glorified resurrection: Mark depicts her returning to ordinary embodied life.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV’s statement that “virtue had gone out of him” in 5:30 uses “virtue” in the older sense of effective power, as the surrounding healing narrative makes clear. In 5:41 Mark preserves “Talitha cumi” and immediately translates it. This bilingual feature gives the command vividness and is consistent with transmission from an Aramaic-speaking setting, although it cannot by itself prove eyewitness origin.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly juxtaposes fear with manifestations of restoring power: the inhabitants fear after seeing the healed man, the woman approaches fearing and trembling, Jairus is told not to fear, and the household moves from scorn to astonishment. Reactions to Jesus are therefore not uniformly faithful simply because a miracle is witnessed.

There is also a striking publicity contrast. The restored man is explicitly sent to tell his friends and publishes the event in Decapolis, connecting the episode with [[Missionary Work]]. At Jairus’s house, however, Jesus strictly commands secrecy. Mark supplies no explicit explanation, so proposed reasons should remain interpretations rather than textual facts.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary-Christological Correspondence
> Jesus tells the restored man to report what the Lord has done for him, while the narrator says that the man reports what Jesus has done for him. This close verbal correspondence supports a christologically significant identification of Jesus’ action with the Lord’s compassionate action, although agency language is also possible.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · Wording accepted; christological force remains debated · [[Literary-Christological Correspondence in Mark 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Traditional/Patristic Argument *(tentative)*
> The retention of untranslated Aramaic with an appended gloss (5:41) is sometimes cited as consistent with the ancient tradition (reported by Papias via Eusebius) that Mark's Gospel draws on Peter's eyewitness reminiscence, on the reasoning that such vivid, precise detail suggests a Semitic-language source close to the events.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.3 · Possible but weak and insufficiently sourced

> [!evidence] Literary-Historical Reading *(tentative)*
> Some scholars read 'Legion' as carrying a secondary, politically charged resonance, since a Roman legion was a large military unit and the episode occurs in Gentile territory that had experienced Roman military presence; on this reading, the drowning of the 'legion' of spirits in the sea evokes hoped-for deliverance from occupying power.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.3 · Disputed and insufficiently sourced

> [!evidence] Textual/Theological Interpretation *(tentative)*
> Jesus's statement that 'the damsel is not dead, but sleepeth' (5:39) is met with derision from the mourners (5:40), who evidently understood her to be genuinely dead; the phrase has been read either as Jesus's characteristic idiom asserting that, from the standpoint of his power, death is temporary and reversible, or (by a minority/skeptical reading) as suggesting she had not actually died.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Alternative recognized; death-and-restoration reading stronger
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the loss of the swine and the sight of the restored man lead the inhabitants to ask Jesus to leave?

- How does the woman’s successful appeal prepare Jairus to hear, “Be not afraid, only believe” after his daughter’s death?

- What is gained by calling the healed woman “Daughter” inside a story about Jairus’s daughter?

- Why is the Decapolis man commissioned to speak while the witnesses at Jairus’s house are commanded to remain silent?

- How do the purity-law backgrounds of [[Leviticus 15]] and [[Numbers 19]] sharpen the significance of touch without being explicitly cited by Mark?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the full sequence with [[Luke 8]] and the exorcism with [[Matthew 8]]. Matthew’s two possessed men and Mark’s extended focus on one restored man offer a useful reminder that parallel accounts can select and arrange details differently. Read the sea crossing in [[Mark 4]] together with [[Mark 5]]: the adjacent episodes create a sustained portrait of authority over destructive nature, unclean spirits, chronic illness, and death.
