# Romans 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Romans 10]] · My notes: [[Romans 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Israel's zeal and the righteousness of God (10:1-4):** Paul prays for Israel but criticizes zeal lacking knowledge and submission.
2. **The near word of faith (10:5-10):** Language drawn from Moses is applied to Christ, belief, confession, and resurrection.
3. **One Lord available to all (10:11-13):** Jew and Greek are placed on equal footing before the Lord.
4. **Sending, preaching, and hearing (10:14-18):** A chain of rhetorical questions explains the role of proclamation, followed by the problem of disobedience.
5. **Israel's knowledge and resistance (10:19-21):** Moses and Isaiah frame unexpected reception by outsiders and God's sustained appeal to Israel.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Righteousness of faith vs. righteousness of the law (10:1-5).** Paul does not deny Israel's zeal but diagnoses it as uninformed and self-directed—an attempt to establish one's own righteousness rather than submit to God's (10:2-3). Moses' own words about doing the law to live by it (10:5, echoing [[Law of Moses]]) frame the contrast Paul develops with the 'righteousness of faith.'

**Confession and belief (10:9-10).** Paul pairs believing in the heart that God raised Jesus from the dead with confessing 'the Lord Jesus' with the mouth. This pairing names the content of saving confession; it should not be read as an exhaustive salvation formula detached from Paul's wider teaching on grace, baptism, and endurance elsewhere in Romans.

**Universal access through 'whosoever' (10:11-13).** Twice anchored in an Old Testament 'whosoever,' salvation is explicitly offered without ethnic restriction—central to the larger [[Jews and Gentiles]] argument of Romans 9-11.

**Faith comes by hearing (10:14-17).** Faith arises from the proclaimed word rather than being self-generated, grounding the necessity of [[Missionary Work]]—though the chapter does not specify the office or procedure by which preachers are 'sent' (10:15).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two repeated contrasts organize the chapter: God's righteousness versus self-established righteousness (10:3), and heart-belief versus mouth-confession (10:9-10). The word 'end' in 10:4 deserves caution—readings such as termination, goal, and fulfillment carry different implications for the [[Law of Moses]], and no single English word should carry more interpretive weight than the wider argument permits. In verses 6-8 Paul does more than repeat [[Deuteronomy 30]]'s language about the word being 'nigh thee'; his parenthetical comments explicitly redirect it toward Christ's descent and resurrection. Separately, Paul's quotation in 10:11 that the believer 'shall not be ashamed' (from Isaiah 28:16) reflects a Greek Old Testament wording tradition that renders the underlying Hebrew idiom somewhat differently than translations based on the Hebrew text, which speak of not needing to 'make haste'—a recognized and unremarkable feature of how New Testament writers quoted scripture for Greek-speaking audiences.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 14-15 form a chain argument (a classic 'sorites'): each clause repeats the verb of the previous clause while adding a new prior condition—calling requires believing, believing requires hearing, hearing requires a preacher, preaching requires being sent. This builds backward from an individual's calling on the Lord to the institutional necessity of being sent, argued almost entirely through rhetorical questions.

The chapter's second half (10:14-21) is also densely scriptural, drawing on [[Deuteronomy 30]], [[Isaiah 28]], [[Joel 2]], [[Isaiah 52]], [[Isaiah 53]], Psalm 19, [[Deuteronomy 32]], and [[Isaiah 65]]. These citations are not all used the same way—some supply direct promises, some rhetorical questions, others a narrative pattern of unexpected Gentile reception and Israel's resistance—so readers should be cautious about assuming a single formal citation technique or that Paul's use exhausts each passage's original meaning.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual/Translation History
> Paul's quotation in Romans 10:11 renders the outcome for the believer as being 'not ashamed,' which reflects the Greek Septuagint's wording of Isaiah 28:16 rather than the Hebrew Masoretic sense captured in most English Old Testament translations, which speak instead of the believer not needing to 'make haste' (i.e., not panicking or fleeing). This is a recognized instance of a New Testament author following the Septuagint's wording where it diverges somewhat from the Hebrew.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · broadly accepted

> [!evidence] Literary Intertextuality
> The concentration of identifiable scriptural language and explicit attributions to Moses and Isaiah is strong textual evidence that Romans 10 deliberately constructs its argument through Israel's scriptures.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- In what sense is Christ 'the end of the law' (10:4)—its termination, its goal, or a fulfillment that is somehow both? How does the chapter's continued reliance on Moses and Isaiah as authorities bear on that question?
- Why does Paul distinguish confession 'with the mouth' from belief 'in the heart' (10:9-10)? Is public confession constitutive of salvation, or its natural expression?
- Paul answers 'Have they not heard?' (10:18) with a text about creation's wordless testimony (Psalm 19:4) rather than direct gospel preaching—why choose that text, and does it function literally, rhetorically, or typologically?
- How does Paul's reuse of Deuteronomy 30:12-14 (originally about the accessibility of the law of Moses) to describe 'the word of faith' about Christ shape how later readers should treat Old Testament texts reapplied to New Testament realities?
- How should God's still-stretched-out hands toward a 'disobedient and gainsaying people' (10:21) qualify readings that treat Israel as simply abandoned, especially given the argument continues into [[Romans 11]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Romans 9]], [[Romans 10]], and [[Romans 11]] as one sustained argument before drawing conclusions about Israel. Compare Romans 10:6-8 with Deuteronomy 30:11-14; Romans 10:13 with Joel 2:32; Romans 10:14-17 with Isaiah 52:7 and Isaiah 53:1; and Romans 10:20-21 with Isaiah 65:1-2. These comparisons illuminate both Paul's continuity with scripture and the interpretive changes produced by his Christ-centered setting.
