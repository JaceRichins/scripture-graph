# Romans 16 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Romans 16]] · My notes: [[Romans 16 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Paul sends greetings to various Saints—He counsels the Saints to avoid those who cause divisions—The Saints should be wise concerning good and innocent concerning evil.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter falls into five units:
- **Commendation of Phebe (vv. 1-2):** Paul asks the recipients to receive her and assist her in her business, noting she has been a helper to many, including himself.
- **Greetings to believers and households (vv. 3-16):** Paul names roughly two dozen individuals and several household groups, traditionally read as being in Rome, though a minority of scholars have argued this greeting list originally addressed Ephesus, where several named figures — including Priscilla and Aquila — are otherwise attested; the unit closes with the call to 'salute one another with an holy kiss.'
- **Warning against divisive teachers (vv. 17-20):** A sharp warning to mark and avoid those who cause division through 'good words and fair speeches,' followed by a promise that God will bruise Satan under the Saints' feet.
- **Greetings from Paul's companions (vv. 21-24):** Timotheus, Lucius, Jason, Sosipater, Tertius (the scribe), Gaius, Erastus, and Quartus each send greetings.
- **Closing doxology (vv. 25-27):** A benediction on 'the revelation of the mystery' now made manifest through the gospel; ancient manuscripts differ on where this doxology belongs in the letter, a genuine and unresolved text-critical question.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The doxology (vv. 25-27) offers a compact statement of revelation theology: the gospel is a 'mystery... kept secret since the world began' but 'now made manifest... by the scriptures of the prophets... for the obedience of faith,' echoing the letter's opening language about obedience among the nations (Romans 1:5) and connecting the gospel's reach to [[Missionary Work]] and [[Jews and Gentiles]]. Ancient manuscripts place this doxology in different locations (after chapter 14, after chapter 15, here, or not at all), so its content should be read as an authentic part of the New Testament witness while its precise original placement and authorship remain an open text-critical question rather than settled fact. Verse 20's promise that 'the God of peace shall bruise Satan under your feet shortly' ties the [[Obedience]] and discernment urged in vv. 17-19 to a larger promise of victory over [[Satan and Temptation]], grounded throughout in [[Grace]] (vv. 20, 24).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several terms carry weight beyond their English translation. Phebe is called a 'servant' of the church (Greek *diakonos*, v. 1) and a 'succourer' (*prostatis*, patron/benefactor, v. 2); the same word *diakonos* appears elsewhere in Paul's letters (e.g., Philippians 1:1) for the office later called deacon, but this verse alone cannot settle whether Phebe held a formally defined office or performed a broader kind of recognized service. The phrase 'of note among the apostles' (v. 7, Greek *episēmoi en tois apostolois*) is genuinely ambiguous between Andronicus and Junia being counted among noteworthy apostles and being merely well regarded by the apostles — a live scholarly dispute. That question is separate from Junia's gender: 'Junia' is a well-attested Roman feminine name, while a corresponding masculine 'Junias' lacks clear independent attestation, so most current scholarship reads the name as a woman's, even though her apostolic status remains disputed. 'Holy kiss' (v. 16, *philēma hagion*) names a greeting practiced across several Pauline churches; the text does not specify whether it functioned as a formal liturgical rite or simply a customary communal greeting.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The greeting list functions as more than social courtesy — it maps Paul's personal connections to a congregation he had not yet visited in person, using mutual acquaintances (Priscilla and Aquila, Andronicus and Junia) to establish credibility. The abrupt warning against divisive people (vv. 17-19) interrupts this warm catalog, a rhetorical jolt underscoring the seriousness of the concern amid otherwise routine closings. Tertius's first-person aside (v. 22) is a rare moment where the letter's material production becomes visible within the text itself. The doxology's closing language ('made known to all nations for the obedience of faith') echoes Romans 1:5, framing the whole letter — though if the doxology is a later liturgical addition, as some textual critics argue, that echo may reflect a later hand deliberately closing the letter rather than Paul's own original design.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Onomastic/Historical
> Junia was a well-attested Roman feminine name in antiquity, while a corresponding masculine form 'Junias' is not clearly attested as an independent name apart from being hypothesized as a contraction; on this basis many text critics favor identifying Junia in Romans 16:7 as a woman.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · majority scholarly view, not minority · [[Onomastic/Historical in Romans 16|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Authorship-Production Evidence
> Tertius's first-person statement is strong internal textual evidence that a named scribe participated in producing the epistle. It does not specify whether he took dictation, prepared a final copy, or exercised any compositional discretion.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted · [[Internal Authorship-Production Evidence in Romans 16|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Social-Network Evidence
> The density of names, relationships, households, and commendations is strong internal evidence that Romans presents Paul as connected to an extensive network of coworkers and believers. It is moderately strong evidence for a corresponding historical network, although the chapter alone cannot independently verify every identity or role.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted · [[Internal Social-Network Evidence in Romans 16|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism
> The closing doxology has a well-documented history of manuscript placement variation: in different ancient witnesses it appears after Romans 14, after Romans 15, at the end of Romans 16 as here, in more than one of these locations, or is missing altogether, making its original position (and in some scholars' view, its authorship) a genuine text-critical question distinct from the doctrinal content of the passage itself.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation · [[Textual Criticism in Romans 16|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Grammatical Criticism *(tentative)*
> The Greek phrase behind 'of note among the apostles' (episēmoi en tois apostolois) is grammatically ambiguous and has been read two ways in scholarship: an 'inclusive' reading, that Andronicus and Junia were themselves outstanding apostles, and an 'exclusive' reading, that they were well-regarded by the apostles without being counted among them.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · genuinely disputed

> [!evidence] Archaeological Correlation *(tentative)*
> Erastus, called 'the chamberlain [oikonomos, city treasurer] of the city' in this verse, is often proposed to be identifiable with an 'Erastus' named in a first-century Latin pavement inscription discovered near the theater in Corinth, which records that an Erastus laid the pavement 'in return for his aedileship' at his own expense.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Did Phebe's title *diakonos* correspond to a recognized church office, or a broader description of service and patronage?
- What in verses 17-19 distinguishes dangerous, self-serving division from ordinary disagreement or legitimate correction?
- Was Junia (v. 7), whose name is most likely feminine, regarded as an apostle herself, or as someone highly regarded by the apostles — two separate questions that scholarship answers differently?
- What does Tertius's statement (v. 22) reveal about how Paul's letters were physically produced, and what does it leave unanswered about how much freedom a scribe might exercise?
- Is the Erastus of verse 23 the same Erastus named in a first-century pavement inscription at Corinth, and if so, what does that suggest about the social status of some early converts?
- Did the closing doxology (vv. 25-27) originally belong at the end of chapter 16, or was it composed or relocated later in the transmission of Romans?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
