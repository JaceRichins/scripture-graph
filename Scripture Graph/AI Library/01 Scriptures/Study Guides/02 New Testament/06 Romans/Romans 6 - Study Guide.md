# Romans 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Romans 6]] · My notes: [[Romans 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Romans 6 answers a potential distortion of [[Grace]]: if grace increases where sin increases, should believers continue sinning? Paul's answer is emphatic. Those baptized into Christ are identified with his death and resurrection and are therefore called to walk in newness of life. The chapter then shifts from burial-and-resurrection imagery to the analogy of competing masters. Freedom from sin means becoming committed to righteousness and God, with holiness and [[Eternal Life]] as the fruit.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has two closely related movements:

1. **Death and life with Christ (6:1–14).** A grace-based objection is rejected; [[Baptism]] signifies burial with Christ, participation in his death, and new life patterned on his [[Resurrection]]. This identity leads to commands not to let sin reign.
2. **Two forms of service (6:15–23).** A second grace-based objection is rejected; yielding determines whom one serves. Service to sin ends in shame and death, while service to God bears fruit unto holiness and everlasting life.

The repeated sequence—question, “God forbid,” and “Know ye not”—binds the two movements into one argument.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds divine gift and human response together. Eternal life is explicitly God's gift through Jesus Christ, not wages earned by righteous performance. Yet grace is transformative: recipients are commanded to reckon themselves alive to God, resist sin's reign, and yield their bodies as instruments of righteousness. In this presentation, [[Grace]] opposes sin's dominion rather than moral effort, while [[Obedience]] expresses the believer's new allegiance.

Romans 6 also gives [[Baptism]] a Christ-centered meaning. Baptism points to death, burial, resurrection, and a changed manner of life. The passage strongly establishes that symbolism, although it does not by itself answer every doctrinal question about how baptism operates.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Paul repeatedly personifies sin as a ruler or master: sin can reign, receive obedience, exercise dominion, and pay wages. Righteousness and God are presented as the alternative service. This language makes conduct a matter of allegiance rather than isolated acts.

The contrast between “wages” and “gift” in verse 23 is especially important. Death is what sin pays; eternal life is what God gives through Jesus Christ. The asymmetry keeps the argument from implying that everlasting life is simply compensation earned by human service.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several images overlap without being identical: baptismal burial, crucifixion of the old man, resurrection life, royal dominion, bodily instruments, slavery, fruit, wages, and gift. Together they describe conversion from several angles. The movement is from changed identity (“dead indeed unto sin”) to embodied practice (“yield your members”) and finally to contrasting ends—death or eternal life.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Structure
> The recurrence of question, rejection, and "Know ye not" language supports reading Romans 6 as a deliberate two-panel argument: verses 1–14 develop death-and-resurrection participation, while verses 15–23 develop the analogy of competing forms of service.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Broadly supported outline, not a rigid formal scheme · [[Literary Structure in Romans 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Ordinance Symbolism
> The explicit association of baptism with burial, death, resurrection, and new life strongly supports baptismal symbolism centered on Christ's death and resurrection.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong symbolism; doctrinal mechanics unresolved · [[Ordinance Symbolism in Romans 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Philological *(tentative)*
> Verse 23 closes the chapter's slavery metaphor with an economic contrast: 'wages' (something earned/owed) versus 'gift' (something freely given). Some NT lexicons note the underlying Greek term rendered 'wages' (opsōnion) elsewhere denotes a soldier's pay or rations (e.g., Luke 3:14; 1 Corinthians 9:7), which would sharpen the sense that death is what sin's service literally earns, in deliberate contrast to eternal life as unearned grace.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · Economic contrast accepted; military nuance uncertain
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What false inference about [[Grace]] is rejected in both verses 1–2 and verse 15?
- How do the identity statements in verses 3–11 support the commands in verses 12–13?
- In what sense is becoming a servant of righteousness a form of [[Freedom and Liberty]]?
- Why does Paul describe death as wages but eternal life as a gift?
- Which claims in verses 3–5 concern present ethical renewal, and which express future resurrection hope?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->

Then follow the argument into [[Romans 7]] and [[Romans 8]], where law, sin, death, freedom, and resurrection life receive further development. [[2 Corinthians 5]] offers a complementary image of transformed life in Christ.
