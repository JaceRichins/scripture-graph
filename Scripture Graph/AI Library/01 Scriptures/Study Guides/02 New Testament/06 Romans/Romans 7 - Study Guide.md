# Romans 7 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> The law of Moses is fulfilled in Christ—Paul delights in the law of God after the inward man.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **vv.1-6** — Marriage-law analogy: the law binds only the living; believers, 'dead to the law by the body of Christ,' are free to belong to the risen Christ. Note the asymmetry: in the illustration (vv.2-3) it is the *husband* who dies to free the wife, but in the application (v.4) it is the *believer* who dies (through union with Christ) — a limited analogy illustrating that death ends a binding legal claim, not a point-by-point allegory of marriage, divorce, or remarriage.
- **vv.7-13** — Defense of the law: 'Is the law sin? God forbid.' The law is holy and good; its role is to define and expose sin ('I had not known sin, but by the law'). Sin, not the commandment, is the active agent — it 'takes occasion by the commandment,' deceives, and kills.
- **vv.14-25** — First-person confession of inward conflict: mind versus flesh, culminating in the cry of v.24 and the thanksgiving of v.25. The chapter's last clause returns to the still-felt tension rather than resolving it outright.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter distinguishes [[Law of Moses]] from [[Sin]]: the law makes sin known and is repeatedly called holy, just, and good (v.12), even while Paul argues believers are no longer bound to it (vv.4, 6). This keeps 'dead to the law' from implying that God's law was itself evil — sin exploits the commandment to produce death; the commandment does not.

Release is Christ-centered rather than self-achieved: believers become dead to the law through Christ's body, belong to the risen Christ, and are enabled to serve 'in newness of spirit' (v.6). This links the chapter to the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] and to the broader argument spanning [[Romans 6]]–[[Romans 8]].

The chapter also portrays moral desire as insufficient by itself to defeat sin: the speaker can approve the good and still fail to perform it (vv.15-20). Romans 7 diagnoses this predicament; its context points to Jesus Christ, and in Romans 8 the Spirit, rather than unaided willpower, as the source of deliverance. Who exactly is voicing that struggle is a disputed question the chapter itself leaves open.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The word 'law' does not carry one uniform sense across the chapter: in vv.1-7 it functions as an authoritative legal/covenantal order (the [[Law of Moses]]); in v.22 it is 'the law of God'; and in v.23 'another law' and 'the law of sin' describe a competing internal power. Reading every occurrence as identical to the Mosaic code flattens the argument. Likewise 'flesh' (v.18, 'in me, that is, in my flesh') is narrowed by Paul himself to describe sin's indwelling power rather than asserting the physical body is inherently evil.

Sin is personified throughout: it 'takes occasion,' 'works' desire, 'revives,' 'deceives,' 'dwells,' 'wars,' and 'brings into captivity' — language that separates the commandment's goodness from sin's misuse of it. The recurring formula 'What shall we say then? ... God forbid' (v.7) is part of Paul's characteristic diatribe style, used elsewhere in Romans to raise and reject false inferences from his own argument.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 14-25 are written as vivid, present-tense first-person confession, and the identity and life-stage of the speaking 'I' is genuinely disputed: readings range from Paul's ongoing Christian experience, to his memory of life before conversion, to (in a minority but scholarly view associated with Stanley Stowers) a rhetorical device in which Paul speaks *in character* as Israel or humanity generally under the law. The text itself does not settle this.

The marriage illustration (vv.1-4) is a limited legal analogy — death terminates a binding legal claim — rather than a fully mapped allegory; because the believer, not the law, is what 'dies' in the application, details about husband, wife, and remarriage should not be pressed further than Paul's own stated point.

Separately, the chapter's climactic line 'O wretched man that I am!' (v.24) is worded identically to the opening of Nephi's psalm, 'O wretched man that I am!' (2 Nephi 4:17) — a striking, mechanically-verified phrase match, though the two passages otherwise pursue different purposes (Paul's argument about law and sin versus Nephi's personal lament and hope). The shared wording may reflect a common English idiom available to both texts rather than evidence of literary dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Who is the first-person 'I' in verses 7-25: Paul autobiographically, Paul speaking representatively, Israel under the law, Adam-like humanity, or some combination?

- Does the passage describe life before conversion, continuing conflict within a believer, or a rhetorical portrait not confined to one chronological stage?

- Which occurrences of 'law' mean the [[Law of Moses]], and which mean a governing principle or power?

- How should the final statement of verse 25 relate to the preceding thanksgiving and the declaration of freedom in [[Romans 8]]?

- How far may the marriage analogy be extended before details foreign to Paul's stated application are introduced?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Exodus 20:14-17 — the Decalogue's prohibition against coveting, directly quoted in v.7.
- [[Romans 6]] — introduces the 'dominion' language (6:14) and the death-with-Christ/new-life pattern that Romans 7 continues.
- [[Romans 8]] — directly resolves the 'who shall deliver me?' of v.24, describing freedom through Christ and the Spirit.
- Galatians 5:16-25 — a related (though not identical) treatment of flesh warring against the Spirit/mind.
- 2 Nephi 4:15-35 (Psalm of Nephi) — shares the exact phrase 'O wretched man that I am!' (v.17); a thematic and phraseological echo, not established literary dependence.
