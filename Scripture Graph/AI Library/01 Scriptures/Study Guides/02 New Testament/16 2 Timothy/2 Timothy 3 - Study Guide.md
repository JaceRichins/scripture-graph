# 2 Timothy 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Timothy 3]] · My notes: [[2 Timothy 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Paul describes the apostasy and perilous times of the last days—The scriptures guide man to salvation.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Perilous character and counterfeit godliness (1–5):** a vice catalogue framed by misdirected love.

2. **Predatory teaching and resistance to truth (6–9):** vulnerable hearers are exploited; the opponents are compared with Jannes and Jambres.

3. **An embodied counterexample (10–13):** doctrine, conduct, virtues, persecution, and divine deliverance are placed against worsening deception.

4. **Continue in scripture-shaped formation (14–17):** Timothy is directed to reliable teachers, childhood sacred writings, faith in Christ, and scripture's power to equip for righteous action.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verses 15–17 hold together divine revelation, human formation, faith in Jesus Christ, and good works. The sacred writings can make a person wise unto [[Salvation]], but the verse explicitly locates salvation 'through faith which is in Christ Jesus.' Inspired scripture then teaches, reproves, corrects, and trains so that the servant of God is equipped to act. This resists both a merely informational view of scripture and a view that separates faith from transformed conduct.

Verse 12 also gives [[Endurance]] a sober setting: godly life is not promised freedom from persecution. The preceding testimony of deliverance should be read alongside the fact that the persecutions were genuinely endured.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV phrase 'given by inspiration of God' in verse 16 translates the Greek adjective *theopneustos*, often rendered 'God-breathed.' The expression emphasizes scripture's divine origin or character. The exact Greek syntax has generated a limited translation debate—whether the sense is 'all scripture is God-breathed and profitable' or 'every God-breathed scripture is also profitable'—but either reading treats the relevant scripture as beneficial for forming righteous life.

The KJV's 'throughly furnished' in verse 17 means thoroughly or fully equipped; it does not refer to moving 'through' something.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two turns marked by “But thou” organize the latter half of the chapter (verses 10 and 14). Against the opponents, Timothy is given an alternative pattern: a known life, endured suffering, trusted instruction, and sacred writings.

The vice catalogue is also framed by love-language: people love themselves at its beginning and love pleasures more than God at its conclusion. In the underlying Greek, the term translated “covetous” also means loving money. This supports a reading of the list as disordered devotion, though the text does not explicitly make every intervening vice a consequence of that single theme.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical-Scope Inference
> Because verse 15 identifies scriptures known from Timothy's childhood, the immediate referent of the following statement includes, and most clearly points to, the Jewish sacred writings. Verse 16 strongly supports a theology of divinely originated and useful scripture, but by itself it does not provide a table of contents for either Testament.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Broadly accepted immediate referent; wider application debated · [[Canonical-Scope Inference in 2 Timothy 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Extrabiblical Literary Parallel *(tentative)*
> Paul's naming of 'Jannes and Jambres' reflects a Jewish tradition external to the Exodus text itself (attested in sources such as the Damascus Document from Qumran, Targum Pseudo-Jonathan on Exodus, and later apocryphal writings), showing that some early Christian and Jewish teaching drew on named traditions about biblical figures beyond what is recorded in the Torah.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · General tradition accepted; source history requires qualification

> [!evidence] Doctrinal Interpretation Dispute *(tentative)*
> Verses 16-17 are frequently cited as biblical support for the Protestant doctrine of sola scriptura (scripture as the sole sufficient rule of faith), but the text itself only asserts that scripture is inspired and 'profitable' for teaching and correction, enabling the man of God to be 'throughly furnished unto all good works'; it does not explicitly state that scripture is the only channel of divine revelation or that continuing revelation is excluded.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · Theological application disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How can continual learning fail to become knowledge of the truth (verse 7)? What practices connect learning with repentance, faith, and action?
- How do verses 11–12 distinguish divine deliverance from exemption from suffering?
- What does the sequence in [[Acts 13]] and [[Acts 14]] add to the brief reference to Antioch, Iconium, and Lystra?
- What tradition lies behind the names Jannes and Jambres, and why might the audience have recognized them without explanation?
- Verse 15 points to sacred writings known from Timothy's childhood, while verse 16 speaks more broadly of “all scripture.” What does the passage establish about scripture, and what questions about the later canon does it leave unanswered?
- Does a community evaluate godliness chiefly by outward form, or also by the character and good works described in verses 10 and 17?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare 2 Timothy 3:8–9 with [[Exodus 7]] and [[Exodus 8]]. Exodus does not name Pharaoh's magicians, but it shows their initial imitation, eventual failure, and acknowledgment of God's power.

Read the locations in verse 11 alongside [[Acts 13]] and [[Acts 14]]. The order Antioch–Iconium–Lystra follows that mission narrative and gives specific content to the word 'persecutions.'

Compare the chapter's account of inspired scripture with [[2 Peter 1]], while allowing each passage to retain its own emphasis: 2 Timothy stresses formation and usefulness; 2 Peter stresses the divine source of prophetic speech.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
