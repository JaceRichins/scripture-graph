# Galatians 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Galatians 3]] · My notes: [[Galatians 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Galatians 3 argues that justification, the gift of the Spirit, and inheritance of the promise made to Abraham come through [[Faith]] rather than through the works of the [[Law of Moses]]. Paul appeals first to the Galatians' own experience of receiving the Spirit (vv.1-5), then to Abraham, who "believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness" (Galatians 3:6) before the law existed — so those who share Abraham's faith, not those who keep the law, are his true children and heirs (vv.7-9, 29). A chain of scriptural quotations argues that the law brings a curse on those who fail to keep all of it (v.10), that no one is justified by the law (v.11), and that Christ removed this curse by becoming a curse himself on the cross (v.13). Paul then argues that the Abrahamic promise, having come first, cannot be annulled by the law given 430 years later (vv.15-18), and explains the law's real but limited purpose: it was "added because of transgressions" until the promised seed (Christ) came, serving as a schoolmaster, or guardian, until faith was revealed (vv.19-25). He explicitly denies that the law opposes God's promises (v.21) even while insisting it cannot give life. The chapter closes with a declaration that all who are baptized into Christ — Jew or Greek, bond or free, male or female — are "one in Christ Jesus" and heirs of Abraham's promise (vv.26-29); this establishes shared covenantal standing and inheritance, though the passage itself does not spell out every social or institutional implication of that unity.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Experience of the Spirit (vv. 1–5):** Paul asks how the Galatians received the Spirit and challenges their movement from Spirit to flesh.

2. **Abraham, faith, and blessing (vv. 6–9):** Abraham's belief becomes the model for identifying his children.

3. **Curse, redemption, and promised Spirit (vv. 10–14):** Paul contrasts the law's curse with life by faith and Christ's redemptive work.

4. **Promise before law (vv. 15–22):** A prior covenant cannot be annulled by the later law; Paul then explains why the law was added.

5. **From custody to heirship (vv. 23–29):** The schoolmaster image gives way to faith, [[Baptism]], unity in Christ, and inheritance.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter closely joins [[Faith]], [[Justification and Sanctification]], and the promised Spirit, but it does not present faith as merely intellectual assent: the discussion culminates in belonging to Christ and being baptized into him (vv. 26–29). Its treatment of the [[Law of Moses]] is qualified. The law cannot give life or replace the promise (vv. 17–22), yet Paul denies that it is contrary to God's promises and assigns it a temporary purpose (vv. 19, 21, 24–25).

Galatians 3:28 places Jew and Greek, bond and free, and male and female within one shared identity in Christ. The immediate context concerns common status as God's children and Abraham's heirs; broader social applications require arguments beyond this verse alone.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"Hearing of faith" (vv.2, 5) can mean either the act of believing what is heard or the faith-producing message itself; the chapter does not settle which nuance Paul intends. "Flesh" in v.3 is broader than the physical body and functions here as the opposite of reliance on the Spirit. In v.16, Paul reads Abraham's singular "seed" (Hebrew zera / Greek sperma) as pointing to Christ; both terms are collective singular nouns that elsewhere denote many descendants (e.g., Genesis 22:17), so Paul's argument is better understood as a christological, typological reading than as a claim resting on strict Hebrew grammar. "Schoolmaster" (v.24, Greek paidagogos) translates a household guardian or attendant responsible for a child's custody and discipline until maturity, not primarily a teacher — the law's authority was temporary custody more than instruction, which clarifies "no longer under a schoolmaster" in v.25. The phrase "faith of Jesus Christ" (v.22) is grammatically ambiguous between faith in Christ and Christ's own faithfulness; v.26's "faith in Christ Jesus" favors the former within this chapter but does not end the wider scholarly debate.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The argument advances through rhetorical questions (vv. 1–5), a chain of scriptural citations (vv. 6–14), a human covenant analogy (vv. 15–18), and another question-and-answer sequence concerning the law (vv. 19–22). The final paragraph gathers the argument into identity language: children, baptism, clothing, oneness, seed, and heirs.

The schoolmaster image in verses 24–25 marks custody as temporary. The context supports a salvation-historical movement toward Christ more directly than a universal claim that every individual's spiritual development must follow precisely the same sequence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What exactly had the Galatians begun doing that Paul describes as seeking perfection "by the flesh" (v. 3)?

- Does "faith of Jesus Christ" in verse 22 emphasize human trust in Christ, Christ's own faithfulness, or both?

- How should Paul's singular interpretation of "seed" in verse 16 relate to scriptural passages where Abraham's seed plainly refers to many descendants?

- What continuing significance, if any, does Paul assign to the law after the arrival of faith?

- Which consequences of the oneness declared in verse 28 follow directly from this chapter, and which require evidence from elsewhere?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
