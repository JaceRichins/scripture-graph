# 1 Corinthians 2 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Corinthians 2]] · My notes: [[1 Corinthians 2 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
1 Corinthians 2 continues Paul's response to the divisions at Corinth addressed in 1 Corinthians 1, where some had aligned themselves with teachers prized for eloquence. Paul recalls that when he first came to Corinth, he deliberately avoided 'excellency of speech or of wisdom,' resolving to know nothing among them but 'Jesus Christ, and him crucified' (2:1-2). He contrasts the wisdom of this world — passing away, and blind even to 'the Lord of glory' — with God's 'hidden wisdom,' revealed not by human argument but 'by his Spirit' (2:6-10). The chapter closes with the claim that 'the natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God' because they are 'spiritually discerned,' while the spiritual person, having 'the mind of Christ,' rightly judges these things (2:14-16). Paul does not reject wisdom or reasoning outright — he affirms in verse 6 that 'we speak wisdom among them that are perfect' — but insists the source and content of that wisdom differ from what the world prizes. This makes the chapter a foundational New Testament text on [[Revelation]] as the basis of [[Faith]], received through the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]] rather than through persuasion alone.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The manner and basis of preaching (vv. 1-5):** Christ crucified, human weakness, and reliance on God's Spirit and power.

2. **God's wisdom contrasted with worldly wisdom (vv. 6-9):** hidden wisdom, the rulers' ignorance, and what God has prepared.

3. **The Spirit as revealer and teacher (vv. 10-13):** God's depths are known through God's Spirit, whom 'we' have received.

4. **Natural and spiritual discernment (vv. 14-16):** contrasting responses to spiritual things and the concluding claim to have the mind of Christ.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter teaches that faith is meant to rest 'in the power of God' rather than in the wisdom of men (2:5), and that God has a wisdom — 'ordained before the world unto our glory' — that was hidden from 'the princes of this world' but is now revealed to believers 'by his Spirit' (2:7-10). Central to this wisdom is Jesus Christ crucified (2:2, 8): the rulers who crucified 'the Lord of glory' did so in ignorance of what God was doing. Paul's explicit statement that 'we speak wisdom among them that are perfect' (2:6) rules out reading the chapter as a blanket rejection of reasoning or learning; the contrast is with wisdom that originates in and terminates on the world, not with wisdom or reason as such. Latter-day Saints have read this passage's teaching on Spirit-revealed knowledge as connected to the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]] and ongoing [[Revelation]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several phrases in this chapter resist confident, single-answer translation. 'Perfect' (v. 6) is not defined within the chapter and should not be read as sinless perfection. 'Princes of this world' (vv. 6, 8) may refer to human rulers, spiritual powers, or both — the text does not resolve which. 'Mystery' (Greek mysterion, v. 7) in Paul's usage typically denotes a truth once hidden but now disclosed by revelation, not something permanently unknowable. 'Comparing spiritual things with spiritual' (v. 13) is likewise genuinely ambiguous: proposed readings include expressing spiritual truths in Spirit-taught words, interpreting spiritual things spiritually, or explaining them to spiritual people. The chapter's doctrinal core does not depend on settling these questions.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 16's rhetorical question, 'who hath known the mind of the Lord,' echoes Isaiah 40:13 and is nearly identical to Paul's wording in Romans 11:34, suggesting Paul draws on a shared scriptural formula to close his argument with an authoritative flourish.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Textual-Rhetorical
> The linked contrasts in verses 1-5 provide strong textual evidence that Paul portrays his manner of preaching as a deliberate attempt to direct confidence away from the preacher's rhetorical prestige and toward God. This is evidence for Paul's stated communicative purpose, not proof that his preaching contained no rhetorical skill.

> [!evidence] Contextual Qualification
> Paul's explicit affirmation that he speaks wisdom, together with his contrast between divine and worldly wisdom, strongly supports reading the passage as a critique of wisdom defined apart from God rather than as a wholesale rejection of thought or learning.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · interpretive synthesis, not an independently sourced consensus · [[Contextual Qualification in 1 Corinthians 2|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Intertextual *(tentative)*
> Paul's rhetorical question in 1 Corinthians 2:16, 'For who hath known the mind of the Lord, that he may instruct him?' is widely recognized by biblical scholars as drawing on Isaiah 40:13 (as quoted in the Septuagint), showing Paul's use of Hebrew scripture to frame Christian revelation as continuous with prior prophetic tradition.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What characteristics distinguish the wisdom Paul rejects from the wisdom he says he teaches?

- Does 'demonstration of the Spirit and of power' refer principally to miracles, inward conviction, transformed lives, or the efficacy of the message? What evidence would be needed to decide?

- Who is included in Paul's repeated 'we' in verses 6-13: Paul and his coworkers, the apostles, mature believers, or Christians generally?

- How can a claim to spiritual discernment be tested without making human wisdom the final authority or making private impressions immune from correction?

- How does the ending, 'we have the mind of Christ,' qualify the meaning of being spiritual?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare 1 Corinthians 2:9-10 with D&C 76:10, which uses closely similar language ('things that eye has not seen, nor ear heard') in describing further mysteries revealed concerning the [[Kingdoms of Glory]]. Compare also with John 3 on the necessity of spiritual rebirth to perceive the kingdom of God, a parallel to this chapter's claim that the natural man cannot receive the things of the Spirit.
