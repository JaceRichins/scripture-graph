# 1 Corinthians 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Corinthians 4]] · My notes: [[1 Corinthians 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Christ’s ministers must be faithful—The Apostles suffer, minister, and keep the faith—The kingdom of God is not in word but in power.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Stewards accountable to the Lord (4:1-5):** faithfulness is required, and final disclosure of hidden matters belongs to the Lord.

2. **Factional boasting confronted (4:6-7):** Paul and Apollos serve as examples against elevating one person over another; received advantages provide no ground for boasting.

3. **Corinthian status versus apostolic suffering (4:8-13):** irony and antithesis contrast fullness, royalty, wisdom, and strength with apostolic deprivation, weakness, labor, and contempt.

4. **Paternal warning and imitation (4:14-17):** Paul explains his corrective purpose, appeals for imitation, and sends Timothy to recall his ways in Christ.

5. **The prospective visit (4:18-21):** Paul will examine power rather than speech and poses the rhetorical alternative of a rod or love and meekness.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Stewardship and accountability:** Ministers serve Christ and administer what belongs to God. Faithfulness is required, and the Lord—not reputation or self-assessment—gives the final verdict (4:1-5).
- **[[Final Judgment]]:** The Lord will disclose hidden things and the purposes of hearts (4:5). In context, this cautions against premature verdicts about servants and motives; it is not an absolute prohibition on every form of discernment.
- **[[Humility]]:** Whatever distinguishes or benefits a person has been received, so it cannot consistently become grounds for self-glory (4:7). The verse does not specify every kind of gift or advantage Paul has in view.
- **Costly ministry:** Apostolic service may include weakness, labor, mistreatment, and patient response (4:9-13). Suffering itself, however, does not automatically validate a person's teaching.
- **[[Kingdom of God]]:** God's kingdom is "not in word, but in power" (4:20). The context does not reject teaching, nor does it define the power exclusively as miracles, authority, conduct, or emotional effect.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV expression "I know nothing by myself" in 4:4 can be misunderstood by modern readers. Its immediate logic is that Paul is conscious of no charge against himself, yet he refuses to treat that self-assessment as justification; the Lord remains judge.

"Judge nothing before the time" in 4:5 concerns premature judgment of servants and hidden heart-purposes. [[1 Corinthians 5]] shows that Paul can still require communal moral judgment, so 4:5 should not be isolated into an absolute ban on discernment.

"Puffed up" links 4:6 and 4:18 and names the disposition attacked throughout the chapter: inflated status expressed through alignment with leaders, boasting in received advantages, and confidence in speech.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 8-13 form the chapter's rhetorical center. Repeated antitheses—rich and deprived, reigning and condemned, wise and foolish, strong and weak, honorable and despised—turn claims or appearances of Corinthian status inside out. The passage is strongly ironic, though Paul's wish that they truly reigned may also express sincere longing.

The chapter moves through several relational images: steward under a lord, condemned public spectacle, society's refuse, father of children, and a disciplinarian with a rod. These images portray apostolic authority as accountable and costly. The hardship catalogue resembles lists Paul uses elsewhere, but here it both depicts apostolic suffering and rebukes Corinthian self-exaltation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What kinds of judgment does 4:5 forbid, and what kinds does [[1 Corinthians 5]] require?

How does the admission that every advantage is received reshape competition, gratitude, and [[Pride]]?

Which features of verses 8–10 signal irony, and where might sincere aspiration remain within the ironic language?

In the immediate context, what observable "power" would distinguish God's work from impressive speech?

How do Paul's suffering and manual labor qualify his request that the Corinthians imitate him?
