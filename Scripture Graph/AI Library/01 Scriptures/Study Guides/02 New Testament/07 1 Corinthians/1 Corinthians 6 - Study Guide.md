# 1 Corinthians 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Corinthians 6]] · My notes: [[1 Corinthians 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Church members should not fight one another in the courts—The unrighteous will not be saved—True Saints are the temple of the Holy Ghost.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Disputes and communal failure (vv. 1-8):** Paul contrasts the saints’ anticipated judicial role with their inability to settle ordinary disputes and prefers accepting loss to wronging or defrauding a fellow believer.

2. **Warning and transformed identity (vv. 9-11):** A kingdom-inheritance warning leads to the contrast between what some Corinthians were and their having been washed, sanctified, and justified.

3. **Qualified freedom and the body’s destiny (vv. 12-14):** “All things are lawful” is immediately limited by benefit and freedom from mastery; the body is for the Lord and destined for resurrection.

4. **Theological meaning of bodily conduct (vv. 15-20):** Believers’ bodies are members of Christ; sexual union has one-flesh significance; the body is called a temple of the Holy Ghost; and divine purchase grounds the command to glorify God.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Verse 11 holds moral warning and grace together. “Such were some of you” distinguishes former conduct from the believers’ present standing, while “washed,” “sanctified,” and “justified” attributes that change to the Lord Jesus and the Spirit. This strongly supports [[Justification and Sanctification]], but the verse does not establish a complete chronology or make the three descriptions interchangeable.

Paul presents the body as belonging within God’s enduring purposes: it is “for the Lord” (v. 13), destined to be raised (v. 14), a member of Christ (v. 15), and a temple of the Holy Ghost (v. 19). Bodily holiness is consequently supported by several connected premises, including [[Resurrection]], union with Christ, divine indwelling, and purchase by God.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The six “know ye not” appeals occur in verses 2, 3, 9, 15, 16, and 19. This refrain repeatedly recalls truths the audience is expected to recognize and gives the chapter a corrective tone.

“All things are lawful” in verse 12 may be a Corinthian slogan, Paul’s own compressed formulation, or a saying shared by both parties. Its origin is uncertain, but its limitation is explicit: Paul tests it by benefit and by whether a practice exercises mastery.

The archaic KJV terms “effeminate” and “abusers of themselves with mankind” in verse 9 require lexical and historical care. Their precise scope and relationship to ancient social practices are disputed, and ancient behavior categories should not be mapped mechanically onto modern identity categories. That uncertainty does not make every proposed translation or historical reconstruction equally well supported.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 13-20 build a cumulative case: the body is for the Lord; God will raise it; believers’ bodies are members of Christ; sexual union makes two one flesh; union with the Lord is spiritual; the body is a temple; and believers have been bought with a price. The command “Flee fornication” therefore stands within a layered argument rather than as an isolated prohibition.

Verse 16 invokes the one-flesh language of [[Genesis 2]]. Paul uses it to deny that intercourse is an inconsequential bodily transaction. [[Ephesians 5]] applies the same scriptural language positively to marriage, but neither use by itself resolves every later question about marriage or sexual ethics.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Placement And Theological Argument
> The placement of bodily resurrection between the declaration that the body is for the Lord and the discussion of sexual union provides strong evidence that Paul's sexual ethic is grounded in God's enduring purpose for the body, not merely in concern about temporary physical consequences.

> [!evidence] Explicit Rhetorical Contrast
> The movement from "such were some of you" to "washed," "sanctified," and "justified" is strong textual evidence that the warning about unrighteous conduct is paired with the possibility of a decisive change in status and life through the Lord Jesus and the Spirit.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · strong explicit rhetorical contrast · [[Explicit Rhetorical Contrast in 1 Corinthians 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Scope Established By Repeated Textual Qualifiers
> The repeated descriptions "another," "brother," and "brethren" provide strong textual evidence that the immediate instruction concerns civil disputes within the Christian community. The passage therefore should not automatically be treated as a comprehensive prohibition on every possible appeal to public courts.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strong immediate-scope inference with necessary limits · [[Scope Established By Repeated Textual Qualifiers in 1 Corinthians 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Translation/Lexical-Historical *(tentative)*
> The KJV renderings 'effeminate' and 'abusers of themselves with mankind' (v.9) translate the Greek terms malakoi and arsenokoitai. Arsenokoitai is a rare compound, possibly coined by Paul, and modern scholarship is genuinely divided over the precise ancient referents of both terms — this is a live lexical/historical question, not a settled one, and should not be resolved dogmatically in either direction.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · lexical scope disputed; alternatives unevenly supported

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does it mean that the saints will judge the world and angels (vv. 2-3), and what details does Paul leave unexplained?

- Does verses 1-8 address every use of public courts, or specifically ordinary disputes in which believers wrong one another? How should verse 7’s preference for accepting loss be taken seriously without extending the passage beyond its stated setting?

- How do “washed,” “sanctified,” and “justified” relate without forcing them into a chronology the verse does not supply?

- Are the sayings in verses 12-13 Corinthian slogans, Pauline formulations, or shared language that Paul immediately qualifies?

- What distinction is Paul making when he says fornication is a sin against one’s own body (v. 18)?

- How does the bodily temple image compare with the more clearly communal temple language in [[1 Corinthians 3]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the freedom formula with [[1 Corinthians 10]], the members-of-Christ imagery with [[1 Corinthians 12]], and the continuation of sexual and marital questions in [[1 Corinthians 7]]. Study the one-flesh language alongside [[Genesis 2]], and compare Paul’s preference for suffering wrong with [[Matthew 5]]. These are substantive parallels, but their differing circumstances should be preserved rather than harmonized automatically.
