# 1 Corinthians 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Corinthians 3]] · My notes: [[1 Corinthians 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Paul treats division around admired teachers as evidence of spiritual immaturity. He then recasts teachers as servants: Paul plants, Apollos waters, and God gives the growth. The congregation is successively pictured as God's field, building, and holy temple. Christ alone is its foundation; builders remain accountable for the quality of their work. The final wisdom argument forbids boasting in leaders and places every relationship within a larger order: all things serve the Saints, the Saints belong to Christ, and Christ belongs to God.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Immaturity exposed (vv. 1-4):** milk, carnality, envy, strife, and partisan slogans.

2. **Servants in God's field (vv. 5-9):** different ministries, one purpose, individual accountability, and God-given growth.

3. **Builders on Christ's foundation (vv. 10-15):** grace-enabled labor, varying materials, testing by fire, reward, and loss.

4. **The building revealed as a temple (vv. 16-17):** God's Spirit dwells in a holy community that must not be corrupted.

5. **Wisdom and allegiance reversed (vv. 18-23):** worldly wisdom is humbled, boasting in people ends, and the community's belonging to Christ becomes decisive.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Paul presents ministry as both grace-enabled and accountable: he builds according to God's [[Grace]], yet every builder must take heed how he builds (1 Corinthians 3:10). Paul and Apollos perform distinct labors, but God gives the increase (1 Corinthians 3:5-9). [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] is the one laid foundation (1 Corinthians 3:11).

The fire imagery distinguishes the builder's work from the builder: work may endure and receive reward, or burn and bring loss, while the builder is nevertheless described as saved (1 Corinthians 3:12-15). This naturally relates to [[Final Judgment]], but the passage does not identify the materials, define the fire, or establish purgatory, universal salvation, degrees of glory, or a complete map of the afterlife.

In verses 16-17 the Corinthian community is addressed collectively as God's holy temple, indwelt by God's Spirit. This gives [[Temples]] a corporate dimension here. Factional division is the immediate context and may be one way of damaging the community, but the passage does not exhaustively define what it means to defile the temple.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated word “carnal” links spiritual immaturity to visible social behavior, especially envy and division. “Milk” and “meat” concern capacity to receive instruction; the text does not identify a fixed list of doctrines belonging permanently to either category.

The plural “ye” in verses 16-17 matters. Paul addresses the gathered community as one temple, continuing the singular building image from verse 9. This does not cancel individual temple applications elsewhere, including [[1 Corinthians 6]], but it guards against reducing this passage to individual health or bodily conduct alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter uses a connected sequence of everyday and sacred images. The Corinthians are infants receiving milk (1 Corinthians 3:1-2), then God's cultivated field and building (1 Corinthians 3:9), and finally God's holy temple (1 Corinthians 3:16-17). Paul likewise appears first as a planter and then as a masterbuilder. In both images his contribution is real, while growth and enabling grace come from God.

The conclusion reverses the opening factional logic. “I am of Paul” defines the believer by a teacher; “all things are yours” includes Paul, Apollos, and Cephas among what serves the community. The expansion is then bounded by “ye are Christ's; and Christ is God's” (1 Corinthians 3:23). This literary pattern is visible in the text without proving a formal rhetorical design.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Grammar And Literary Context
> The plural address 'ye' and the sustained building imagery provide strong textual evidence that the immediate temple referent is the Corinthian community collectively. The warning presents factional damage to a holy community as a serious offense against God.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · Corporate reading strongly supported · [[Grammar And Literary Context in 1 Corinthians 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Distinction
> The passage textually distinguishes the tested work from the worker: a work may burn and its builder suffer loss while the builder is nevertheless saved 'so as by fire.' This is strong evidence that reward, loss, and salvation are not treated as identical categories in this particular illustration.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · Observation accepted; broader theology disputed · [[Textual Distinction in 1 Corinthians 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Scriptural-Exegesis *(tentative)*
> Verse 15 distinguishes the fate of a person's 'work' from the fate of the person: even if a man's work is burned and he 'suffer[s] loss,' he himself is still said to be saved, 'yet so as by fire.' This is sometimes read as supporting differentiated post-judgment outcomes (e.g., degrees of reward/glory) rather than a strict saved/damned binary, but the verse itself only speaks of loss of reward for the individual builder, not of a general doctrine of graded kingdoms of glory.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.8 · Textual distinction accepted; theological significance disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What exactly are the gold, silver, stones, wood, hay, and stubble—teachings, converts, ministerial methods, moral deeds, or a deliberately broad image of work? The chapter does not specify.

Does “the day” mean the final judgment? That is a natural reading of disclosure, fire, reward, and loss, but the chapter does not explicitly name the event.

How does reading “ye are the temple” corporately change the application of verse 17? In context, could damaging Christian unity be one way of defiling the temple?

How should communities honor distinct contributions without reproducing the leader-centered boasting Paul rejects?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
