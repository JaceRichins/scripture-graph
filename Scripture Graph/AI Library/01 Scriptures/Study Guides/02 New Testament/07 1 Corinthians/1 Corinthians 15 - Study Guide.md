# 1 Corinthians 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Corinthians 15]] · My notes: [[1 Corinthians 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
[[1 Corinthians 15]] presents the resurrection as the load-bearing claim of Paul's gospel. Paul begins with a received proclamation of Christ's death, burial, resurrection, and appearances. He then argues that denying resurrection empties preaching, faith, forgiveness, hope, and sacrifice of their meaning. The second half answers questions about the resurrected body through images of seed, diverse created bodies, Adam and Christ, and mortality putting on immortality. The chapter ends where its practical argument has been heading: resurrection hope makes faithful labor meaningful.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The received gospel and its witnesses (vv. 1-11):** Christ died, was buried, rose, and appeared; Paul locates his own ministry within the common apostolic proclamation.
2. **Consequences of denying resurrection (vv. 12-19):** A tightly repeated conditional argument exposes what would follow if the dead do not rise.
3. **Christ's resurrection and the defeat of death (vv. 20-28):** Christ is firstfruits; Adam and Christ represent death and life; Christ reigns until every enemy is subdued.
4. **Practice and conduct in light of resurrection (vv. 29-34):** Baptism for the dead, apostolic danger, and moral seriousness are contrasted with fatalistic indulgence.
5. **The character of the resurrected body (vv. 35-49):** Seed, flesh, heavenly bodies, and the two Adams illustrate continuity, difference, and transformation.
6. **The last trumpet and victory (vv. 50-57):** Both living and dead must be changed as mortality puts on immortality.
7. **Ethical conclusion (v. 58):** Steadfast labor is not in vain.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter's doctrine of [[Resurrection]] is bodily but transformative. The body is sown and raised, yet corruption becomes incorruption, weakness becomes power, and the natural body becomes spiritual. This does not read naturally as mere resuscitation; nor does the phrase spiritual body mean that embodiment disappears.

Paul connects resurrection directly to the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]: if Christ is not raised, believers remain in their sins. He also holds [[Grace]] and effort together in his account of ministry—his labor is real, yet grace is its enabling source.

Verse 29 is important for [[Baptism for the Dead]], but interpretive restraint is essential. It indicates a recognizable practice or argument; it does not independently specify participants, procedure, authority, or full salvific meaning. [[D&C 128]] supplies a much fuller Restoration interpretation.

The sun, moon, stars, and celestial and terrestrial bodies invite comparison with [[D&C 76]] and [[Kingdoms of Glory]]. The literary relationship is meaningful, but Paul's immediate use of the imagery is an analogy for resurrection. The complete later taxonomy should not be attributed to this chapter alone.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV's recurring **vain/not in vain** language creates a semantic motif of futility and meaningfulness. Belief may be in vain, grace toward Paul was not in vain, preaching and faith would be vain without resurrection, and the chapter concludes that labor in the Lord is not in vain. This is a real pattern in the English text, although several different Greek expressions lie behind the KJV's repeated word.

**Sleep** serves as a metaphor for death while retaining the expectation of awakening, even as Paul calls death an enemy. **Firstfruits** presents Christ's resurrection as the beginning of a larger harvest. The paired descriptions **natural body** and **spiritual body** distinguish conditions or modes of embodiment; the noun body remains present, but the expressions do not by themselves define the raised body's material composition.

The repeated sown/raised contrasts make transformation central: corruption/incorruption, dishonor/glory, weakness/power, and natural/spiritual. Related contrasts include earthy/heavenly and mortality/immortality.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter moves from testimony to logical argument, from logical argument to analogy, and from analogy to prophetic celebration. Verses 12-19 form a reductio: Paul temporarily grants the denial of resurrection and traces its unacceptable consequences. Verses 35-44 answer an imagined objector through agricultural and cosmological comparisons. Verses 54-55 then transform prophetic language from [[Isaiah 25]] and [[Hosea 13]] into a taunt against death.

Scriptural echoes deepen the argument. [[Psalm 110]] and [[Psalm 8]] frame Christ's reign; [[Genesis 2]] supplies the first-Adam language; [[Isaiah 22]] supplies the fatalistic alternative; and Isaiah and Hosea supply the final victory imagery. The intertexts form a movement from creation and mortal dominion to renewed humanity and the destruction of death.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How broadly should all be made alive in verse 22 be read, especially in light of the ordering statement in verse 23?
- What can verse 29 establish about baptism for the dead, and what details remain unavailable without later sources such as [[D&C 128]]?
- What precise distinction does Paul intend between a natural body and a spiritual body?
- Is Paul's claim about fighting beasts at Ephesus literal, metaphorical, or conventional language for extreme opposition?
- How should the Son's final subjection in verses 27-28 be related to other New Testament claims about Christ's divine authority?
- Which elements of the glory imagery belong to Paul's immediate resurrection analogy, and which arise from its later development in [[D&C 76]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the Adam–Christ argument with [[Romans 5]] and the topic [[Fall of Adam and Eve]]. Read the living-soul language alongside [[Genesis 2]], and compare the enemies-underfoot argument with [[Psalm 110]] and [[Psalm 8]]. Trace the fatalistic saying and victory over death through [[Isaiah 22]], [[Isaiah 25]], and [[Hosea 13]].

For related trumpet and resurrection imagery, compare [[1 Thessalonians 4]]. Read verse 29 alongside [[D&C 128]] for the fuller Restoration development of [[Baptism for the Dead]]. Compare verses 40–42 with [[D&C 76]] and [[Kingdoms of Glory]], carefully distinguishing Paul's immediate analogy about resurrected bodies from the later three-kingdom taxonomy.
