# 1 Corinthians 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Corinthians 5]] · My notes: [[1 Corinthians 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Paul addresses both a reported sexual offense and the congregation's response to it. He orders severe communal discipline, but states a saving aim for the offender. He then frames the community's holiness through [[Passover]] imagery and clarifies that his association rule concerns a professed insider, not withdrawal from immoral people generally. The chapter therefore combines accountability, corporate identity, judgment, and the possibility of ultimate salvation without resolving every question about the disciplinary mechanism.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The reported offense and the congregation's failed response** (vv. 1–2)
2. **Paul's judgment and instructions for gathered discipline** (vv. 3–5)
3. **Leaven, Passover, and communal identity** (vv. 6–8)
4. **Clarification of the association rule** (vv. 9–11)
5. **Responsibility toward insiders and outsiders** (vv. 12–13)

The argument moves from a reported case to the image of leaven affecting a whole lump, then returns to a bounded rule for communal association. [[Passover]] imagery grounds the call to communal holiness, while the concluding removal command likely recalls a recurring Deuteronomic formula without identifying one certain source verse.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Communal accountability.** Paul treats the congregation's pride and inaction as part of the problem (vv. 2, 6). The leaven image depicts tolerated wrongdoing as a danger to the community, while the vice list prevents reducing the principle to sexual conduct alone.

**Severe discipline with a saving horizon.** Verse 5 is punitive and its mechanism is disputed, yet its stated purpose is that the offender's spirit may be saved. That purpose supports a restorative orientation but neither guarantees repentance nor makes the immediate action gentle.

**Holiness grounded in Christ.** The command to purge old leaven is grounded in the declaration that Christ is the Passover sacrifice and that the community is already unleavened (vv. 7–8). Ethical transformation is presented as conduct consistent with a Christ-given identity.

**Bounded communal judgment.** Verses 9–13 reject withdrawal from society and distinguish professed insiders from outsiders. The text assigns the community responsibility for its internal fellowship while affirming that God judges those outside. It does not thereby approve outside conduct, but neither does this passage authorize treating all outsiders as though they were under church discipline.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
"His father's wife" corresponds to wording in [[Leviticus 18]] and [[Deuteronomy 22]] and most naturally denotes a stepmother or another wife of the offender's father; the woman's precise status is not given.

"Deliver such an one unto Satan" and "destruction of the flesh" are difficult expressions. Exclusion from the gathered community plausibly describes the action, while bodily affliction, death, or destruction of flesh-governed conduct have been proposed as its expected effect. The saving purpose is clearer than the mechanism.

"Called a brother" marks professed membership as relevant to the association restriction. "No not to eat" clearly intensifies that restriction, but the wording alone does not conclusively decide whether Paul means ordinary meals, a sacred communal meal, or both.

Paul's claim that the conduct was not so much as named among Gentiles is his emphatic characterization of the scandal; it should not be treated without independent evidence as a comprehensive historical description of all Gentile societies.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 6–8 form a compact [[Passover]] pattern rather than an isolated metaphor. As leaven was removed for the feast in [[Exodus 12]], the congregation must purge old leaven because Christ its Passover has been sacrificed. Paul moralizes the food imagery: old leaven corresponds to malice and wickedness, while unleavened bread corresponds to sincerity and truth.

The clause "as ye are unleavened" creates a productive tension between identity and obligation: the community is commanded to become what Paul declares it already is. The repeated movement from the individual offender to the whole lump also makes the chapter's concern corporate without erasing individual responsibility.

The little-leaven proverb recurs in [[Galatians 5]], where the contextual threat is different. That comparison warns against assigning the metaphor a fixed referent apart from its setting.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What kind of pride led the congregation to remain "puffed up" rather than mourn?
- How should the explicit hope of salvation constrain interpretations and applications of verse 5?
- Does "destruction of the flesh" concern the body, sinful disposition, social status outside the congregation, or some combination?
- Does "no not to eat" include ordinary meals, the sacred communal meal, or both?
- What safeguards should accompany modern applications of ancient communal discipline?
- How does Paul's refusal to judge outsiders constrain attempts to apply this chapter to wider society?

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
### Open threads

- **Earlier writing.** In 5:9 Paul refers to having written about association with sexually immoral people. A separate, now-non-extant letter is the common interpretation, but an epistolary-aorist reading referring to the present communication remains possible. The verse securely preserves only a summary and clarification.
- **The prohibited relationship.** Compare 5:1 with [[Leviticus 18]] and [[Deuteronomy 22]]. These passages establish a close legal correspondence, although Paul does not explicitly cite either one and may also appeal to broader moral convention.
- **Passover and leaven.** Compare verses 6–8 with [[Exodus 12]] and the repeated proverb in [[Galatians 5]]. The former supplies the feast and leaven-removal setting; the latter shows that the proverb can be applied to different threats.
- **Delivery to Satan.** [[1 Timothy 1]] uses similar language with a corrective purpose. The parallel is useful, but neither passage explains the mechanism, and the authorship of 1 Timothy is disputed in critical scholarship.
- **The removal formula.** Verse 13 likely echoes Deuteronomy's recurring command to remove evil from the community. Because the formula occurs repeatedly, no single Deuteronomic verse should be treated as the certain source.
- **Other discipline passages.** [[Matthew 18]] presents a graduated process under different circumstances. [[2 Corinthians 2]] describes communal punishment followed by forgiveness and consolation, but the text does not securely identify that offender with the person in 1 Corinthians 5.
