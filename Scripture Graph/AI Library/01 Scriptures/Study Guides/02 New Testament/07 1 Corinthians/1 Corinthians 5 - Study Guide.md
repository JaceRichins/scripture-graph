## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The offense and the church's failure to respond** (vv1-2)
2. **Paul's judgment and instructions for discipline** (vv3-5)
3. **The leaven metaphor and the Passover rationale** (vv6-8)
4. **Clarifying an earlier instruction: judging insiders vs. outsiders** (vv9-13)

The chapter moves from a concrete case to a general principle (leaven) and back to concrete application (whom to exclude), unified by the underlying question of how holiness within a community relates to Christ's sacrifice.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Communal accountability.** Paul treats the congregation's response as part of the problem. The leaven metaphor presents tolerated wrongdoing as a danger to the whole community, while the vice list shows that the concern is not limited to sexual conduct.

**Severe discipline with a saving aim.** Verse 5 combines severe and disputed language with the stated hope that the offender's spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus. This supports an ultimately restorative aim without making the action merely gentle or nonpunitive. The chapter does not narrate the offender's later response.

**The scope of judgment.** Verses 9–13 distinguish responsibility for those inside the believing community from judgment of those outside it. This passage directly governs communal fellowship and discipline; it does not establish either moral approval of outside conduct or a universal prohibition on public moral testimony.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The expressions "deliver such an one unto Satan" and "destruction of the flesh" are difficult and disputed. Proposed readings include exclusion from the church's sphere of fellowship, bodily affliction or death, and destruction of sinful impulses. These possibilities are not necessarily alternatives at the same level: exclusion may describe the action, while affliction or destruction may describe its expected effect. The chapter establishes the stated hope—salvation in the day of the Lord Jesus—more clearly than it establishes the mechanism.

"Called a brother" limits the association rule to someone identified with the believing community. "With such an one no not to eat" clearly intensifies the restriction, but the verse does not settle whether ordinary meals, a sacred communal meal, or both are intended.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The central image is not an isolated comparison but a compact [[Passover]] pattern. As Israel removed leaven for the feast in [[Exodus 12]], the Corinthian community is told to purge old leaven because Christ its Passover has been sacrificed. Paul then moralizes the food imagery: old leaven becomes malice and wickedness, while unleavened bread becomes sincerity and truth. The same leaven proverb appears in [[Galatians 5]], where its application differs, showing that the maxim can describe more than one kind of corrupting influence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary Evidence
> The statement that Paul had already written 'in an epistle,' followed by a clarification of what that instruction meant, is strong internal evidence for correspondence with Corinth preceding this passage.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted inference with a minority alternative · [[Internal Literary Evidence in 1 Corinthians 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical Criticism *(tentative)*
> Verse 9's reference to an earlier letter Paul 'wrote... in an epistle' that gave the 'not to company with fornicators' instruction does not match any content in the canonical 1 Corinthians up to that point, which many New Testament scholars take as evidence of a now-lost earlier Pauline letter to Corinth, meaning canonical '1 Corinthians' is not actually the first letter Paul sent that congregation.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.8 · widely held inference, not certain from verse alone

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->

- How should the hope of salvation in verse 5 govern interpretations and applications of the severe language surrounding it?
- What safeguards are necessary when translating ancient communal discipline into modern institutional practice?
- Does "no not to eat" concern ordinary table fellowship, a sacred communal meal, or both?
- How does the explicit refusal to judge outsiders constrain applications of this chapter to wider society?
