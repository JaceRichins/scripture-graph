# 1 Corinthians 11 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Corinthians 11]] · My notes: [[1 Corinthians 11 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
1 Corinthians 11 addresses two very different problems in the Corinthian congregation: propriety in public worship (vv. 1-16) and disorder at the communal meal that included the sacrament (vv. 17-34). In the first half, Paul reasons from a chain of [[Marriage|headship]] (Christ, man, woman, God, v. 3) to instruct on head coverings in prayer and prophesying, while also balancing that hierarchy with a strong statement of mutual interdependence 'in the Lord' (vv. 11-12). In the second half, Paul rebukes the Corinthians for turning the Lord's supper into an occasion for factionalism and inequity, then delivers what may be the earliest surviving written account of the words of institution of the [[Sacrament]] (vv. 23-25), followed by a warning against partaking unworthily and a call to self-examination.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter divides into two loosely connected units: (1) vv. 1-16, instructions on head coverings grounded in headship and creation order, closing with an appeal to common church custom (v. 16); and (2) vv. 17-34, correction of abuses at the Lord's supper, framed by an opening and closing rebuke (vv. 17, 34) that bracket the words of institution (vv. 23-26) and the warning about unworthy partaking (vv. 27-32).
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Key doctrinal threads: a headship structure culminating in God as head of Christ (v. 3), tempered by mutual dependence of man and woman 'in the Lord' (vv. 11-12); the [[Ordinances|ordinance]] of the sacrament instituted 'in remembrance' of Christ's broken body and shed blood (vv. 24-25); the principle that divisions and heresies serve to make manifest 'they which are approved' (v. 19), a theme of testing echoed elsewhere in scripture; and the call to self-examination before partaking of the sacrament so as not to eat and drink 'unworthily' (vv. 28-29).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV renders the Greek exousia in verse 10 as 'power,' a translation many commentators read as denoting the woman's own authority rather than a symbol of the man's authority over her — a genuine lexical crux rather than a settled point. The phrase 'this do in remembrance of me' (vv. 24-25) closely parallels the wording of [[Luke 22]], while 'the new testament in my blood' (v. 25) is a near-verbatim echo of that same Synoptic tradition.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Paul's rhetorical method here is to state a principle, immediately qualify or balance it, and then decline to fully resolve the tension — most visibly in the movement from strict headship (vv. 3, 7-9) to explicit mutuality (vv. 11-12), and again in the deliberately open-ended close of verse 16 ('we have no such custom'). The chapter also functions as an early piece of Christian catechesis: verses 23-25 preserve fixed, formulaic liturgical language ('received... delivered,' 'in remembrance of me') distinct from Paul's surrounding argumentative prose, marking it as quoted tradition rather than original composition.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical-Critical Dating *(tentative)*
> 1 Corinthians 11:23-25 is widely dated by New Testament scholarship to a Pauline letter written earlier than the composition of the canonical Gospels, making it plausibly the earliest surviving written account of the words of institution of the Lord's supper.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · broadly held among critical scholars but dependent on contested dating

> [!evidence] Lexical/Translation *(tentative)*
> The KJV word 'power' in 1 Corinthians 11:10 translates the Greek exousia; many lexicographers and commentators read this as denoting the woman's own authority or right rather than a token of the man's authority over her, making the verse a well-known translation crux rather than a settled instruction.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed lexical/interpretive question

> [!evidence] Textual/Interpretive *(tentative)*
> The referent of 'such custom' in verse 16 is grammatically ambiguous and disputed: it may mean 'we have no custom of being contentious' or 'we have no custom of women praying uncovered,' and the choice materially affects whether verses 2-16 read as a fixed transcultural ordinance or a local Corinthian accommodation.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · genuinely disputed among commentators
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
What does 'because of the angels' mean in verse 10, and why does Paul leave it unexplained? Does verse 16 mean Corinth has no custom of contentiousness, or no custom of women praying uncovered — and how much should that ambiguity affect how the whole passage is applied today? Is the headship language of verse 3 about a fixed hierarchy of authority, or a 'source/origin' relationship that verses 11-12 are meant to clarify? What specifically triggered the illness and death mentioned in verse 30 — communal irreverence at the meal itself, unresolved sin, or something more particular to Corinth?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
