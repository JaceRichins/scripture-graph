# 1 Corinthians 16 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Corinthians 16]] · My notes: [[1 Corinthians 16 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
1 Corinthians 16 closes the letter with practical instructions and personal warmth rather than doctrinal argument. Paul organizes a relief collection for the poor saints in Jerusalem (vv. 1-4), lays out his travel plans through Macedonia toward Corinth while explaining why he must remain at Ephesus for now (vv. 5-9), commends his younger colleague Timothy and explains Apollos's delayed visit (vv. 10-12), issues five terse exhortations culminating in [[Charity]] (vv. 13-14), commends the household of Stephanas and other faithful helpers (vv. 15-18), passes along greetings from several churches and individuals (vv. 19-20), and ends with Paul's own handwritten salutation, a solemn warning, and a benediction (vv. 21-24).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter follows a recognizable Pauline letter-closing pattern also found in [[Romans 16]], [[Galatians 6]], and [[2 Thessalonians 3]]: (1) practical/financial business — the collection (vv. 1-4); (2) travel plans (vv. 5-9); (3) commendation of co-workers — Timothy and Apollos (vv. 10-12); (4) rapid-fire exhortations (vv. 13-14); (5) commendation of local leaders — Stephanas's household (vv. 15-18); (6) greetings from other churches and individuals (vv. 19-20); (7) autograph, curse-formula, and benediction (vv. 21-24). Recognizing this convention helps explain why the chapter reads as a list of discrete items rather than a continuous argument.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Although this chapter is largely administrative, it closes the letter's argument on a doctrinal note: 'let all your things be done with [[Charity]]' (v. 14) deliberately returns to the theme developed at length in 1 Corinthians 13, framing charity as the governing principle behind every other instruction in the letter, including the collection for the poor (vv. 1-4), which models practical [[Service]] and [[Consecration]] toward fellow believers regardless of geographic distance. The instruction to submit to those who 'addicted themselves to the ministry of the saints' (vv. 15-16) illustrates an early, service-based rather than title-based basis for recognizing local leadership.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Two phrases reward closer attention. 'Quit you like men' (v. 13) is archaic KJV idiom translating a Greek verb meaning 'act courageously' or 'be brave'; it is an idiom of courage, not a gender restriction. 'Anathema Maran-atha' (v. 22) fuses a Greek word for 'accursed' with an untranslated Aramaic acclamation, 'Maranatha,' meaning roughly 'our Lord, come' (or 'our Lord has come'). That Paul could drop an Aramaic phrase into a Greek letter to a Gentile congregation without explanation suggests it was already a recognizable Christian formula; the same phrase resurfaces later in a Eucharistic prayer in the Didache (10:6).
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 14's summons to charity functions as a structural bookend, closing the circle opened by the extended treatment of charity in 1 Corinthians 13 just before the letter ends. The closing itself is built from conventional epistolary building blocks — travel notice, commendation, greeting list, autograph, benediction — shared across Paul's letters, which is why so many verses here (vv. 19-23) have close verbal parallels in [[Romans 16]] and the Thessalonian correspondence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Linguistic/Historical *(tentative)*
> The presence of an untranslated Aramaic acclamation ('Maranatha,' roughly 'our Lord, come' or 'our Lord has come') inside an otherwise Greek letter to a Gentile, Greek-speaking congregation is evidence that some early Christian liturgical formulas originated in the Aramaic-speaking Palestinian church and were transmitted, untranslated, into Gentile communities; the same term later appears in a Eucharistic context in the Didache (10:6).
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · broadly accepted but Didache dating/relationship is not certain

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical *(tentative)*
> Paul's practice of adding a personal, autographed closing line recurs in near-identical language in 2 Thessalonians 3:17 ('The salutation of Paul with mine own hand, which is the token in every epistle') and in Galatians 6:11, and fits the scribal note in Romans 16:22 ('I Tertius, who wrote this epistle'), supporting the conclusion that Paul routinely dictated his letters and personally authenticated them with a final autograph, likely as a safeguard against forged letters (compare his warning in 2 Thessalonians 2:2).
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.7 · observation accepted, anti-forgery motive disputed

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical *(tentative)*
> Verse 2's instruction to set aside money 'upon the first day of the week' is sometimes cited as the earliest surviving hint that Sunday held special significance for early Christians, prior to explicit later evidence of Sunday worship.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why might Paul specify that the Corinthians treat Timothy 'without fear' (v. 10)? What does this suggest about Timothy's standing or temperament?
- What might explain Apollos's decision not to visit Corinth 'at this time' (v. 12), given the earlier factionalism recorded in 1 Corinthians 1 and 3?
- How does placing 'let all your things be done with charity' (v. 14) right after 'quit you like men, be strong' change how you read courage and strength in a Christian context?
- What does the instruction to 'submit yourselves' to those who serve (vv. 15-16) suggest about how the earliest churches recognized leadership, before any formal hierarchy existed?
- What might explain the presence of an untranslated Aramaic phrase, 'Maranatha,' in a letter written in Greek to a Gentile audience?
