# 1 Corinthians 10 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Corinthians 10]] · My notes: [[1 Corinthians 10 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Christ is the God of Israel and the spiritual Rock that guided them—Ancient Israel rebelled against Christ—Paul contrasts true and false sacraments.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Israel’s privileges and failure (vv. 1–5):** all shared the cloud, sea, food, and drink, yet many were overthrown.
2. **Israel as admonition (vv. 6–13):** idolatry, fornication, tempting Christ, and murmuring become warnings, followed by counsel about vigilance and temptation.
3. **Exclusive communion (vv. 14–22):** the Lord’s table is contrasted with sacrificial fellowship at pagan tables.
4. **Liberty disciplined by another’s good (vv. 23–30):** ordinary food may be eaten, but its public religious meaning and effects upon conscience matter.
5. **Controlling purpose (vv. 31–33):** glorify God and seek the profit and salvation of many.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Christ and Israel's wilderness story:** Paul identifies the spiritual Rock as Christ (v4), presenting Christ as integral to Israel's sacred history. The verse strongly supports Christ's pre-mortal significance, though it does not by itself define the precise manner in which the Rock "followed" Israel or prove every later systematic identification.
- **Privilege and perseverance:** Israel's shared cloud, sea, food, and drink did not prevent the overthrow of many (vv1–5). Paul applies this as a warning against presumption: "take heed lest he fall" (v12).
- **God's faithfulness in temptation:** Verse 13 teaches that temptation is not irresistible and that God provides a way through or out sufficient for endurance. It should not be expanded into a promise that disciples will face no hardship.
- **Communion and one body:** The cup and bread are called communion with Christ's blood and body, and common participation in one bread is related to the many being one body (vv16–17). This gives the [[Sacrament]] a clear communal dimension without resolving every later debate about sacramental metaphysics.
- **Liberty governed by another's good:** Lawfulness is not Paul's only standard; expediency, edification, conscience, God's glory, and the salvation of others also govern conduct (vv23–33).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated **all** in verses 1–4 creates a strong rhythm of shared privilege before the reversal, “But with many of them God was not well pleased.” The repeated prohibitions in verses 7–10 accumulate Israel’s failures. Later, **cannot** in verse 21 marks an incompatibility between rival fellowships, while **lawful**, **expedient**, and **edify** in verse 23 distinguish permission from wise, community-building action.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Paul’s use of Israel is explicitly interpretive rather than a merely incidental allusion: the events are called “examples” and “ensamples” written for admonition. The chapter also contains two identifiable scriptural connections. The golden-calf wording links verse 7 with [[Exodus 32]], while “the earth is the Lord’s, and the fulness thereof” links verses 26 and 28 with [[Psalm 24]]. The latter citation supports freedom to receive ordinary food as God’s creation, while the surrounding argument limits that freedom when eating communicates idolatrous participation or harms another’s conscience.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Numerical Intertextual Tension
> The KJV texts preserve a surface numerical difference: Paul reports that twenty-three thousand fell in one day, while Numbers reports twenty-four thousand deaths in the plague. The texts themselves do not explain the difference.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 1.0 · Difference accepted; explanation unresolved · [[Numerical Intertextual Tension in 1 Corinthians 10|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Explicit Literary Typology
> The explicit designation of Israel’s experiences as “examples” and “ensamples,” together with their application to the Corinthian audience, is strong textual evidence that Paul is deliberately reading Israel’s history as morally and theologically instructive for the church.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · Broadly accepted with scope qualification · [[Explicit Literary Typology in 1 Corinthians 10|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Theological Tension *(tentative)*
> Paul holds two claims about idols in close tension: that 'the idol is any thing' (i.e., nothing) and that 'the things which the Gentiles sacrifice, they sacrifice to devils, and not to God.' Taken together, this suggests idols themselves are ontologically empty, yet the sacrificial act still channels worship toward real spiritual powers Paul calls devils — a nuanced position rather than either pure monotheistic dismissal or belief that pagan gods are real deities.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · Juxtaposition accepted; ontology disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Paul stress that **all** received sacred privileges before saying that many were overthrown?
- How does “a way to escape” relate to the final phrase “able to bear it” in verse 13?
- What distinguishes eating ordinary food from participating in an idolatrous table?
- Why does another person’s disclosure about the food alter the recommended action in verses 28–29?
- How should the difference between the twenty-three thousand in verse 8 and the twenty-four thousand in Numbers 25:9 be evaluated without either concealing or exaggerating it?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[1 Corinthians 8]], [[1 Corinthians 9]], and [[1 Corinthians 10]] as a continuous treatment of knowledge, rights, self-restraint, idol-offered food, and concern for another person’s salvation. Compare the qualification of “all things are lawful” in [[1 Corinthians 6]]. For Paul’s scriptural foundations, compare 1 Corinthians 10:7 with Exodus 32:6 and 1 Corinthians 10:26, 28 with Psalm 24:1.
