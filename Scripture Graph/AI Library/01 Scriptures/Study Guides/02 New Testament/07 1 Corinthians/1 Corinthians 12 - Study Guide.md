<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. Verses 1–3: discerning speech associated with the Spirit.
2. Verses 4–11: diverse gifts, administrations, and operations from one divine source.
3. Verses 12–20: one body requires many differently functioning members.
4. Verses 21–26: no member is dispensable; mutual care opposes schism.

6. Verse 31: transition from gifts to the more excellent way of [[Charity]].

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins unity and diversity rather than treating them as rivals. Unity comes from one Spirit and membership in the body of Christ; diversity appears in gifts, functions, and members. A gift is not presented primarily as private spiritual distinction: verse 7 gives it a beneficial purpose, and verses 25–26 define the community by reciprocal care.

The chapter also resists ranking human worth by visibility. Members that seem feeble are called necessary, and the part lacking honor receives greater honor. This supports a demanding account of [[Service]] and communal solidarity, though the anatomical metaphor should not be forced into a detailed institutional blueprint.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repetition of “same” in verses 4–6 and “one” in verses 11–13 supplies the verbal backbone of the argument. Against that repeated unity stand “diversities,” “differences,” “many,” and “severally.” Paul therefore does not argue for sameness of function; he argues that differentiated activity can arise from and serve a common divine source.

The rhetorical questions in verses 15–17 and 21 expose faulty conclusions through imagined body parts. Those in verses 29–30 expect negative answers, underscoring that no single role or manifestation belongs to everyone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Paul builds the passage with recognizable rhetorical devices: a tricolon in vv4-6 (diversities/differences/diversities paired with same Spirit/Lord/God); anaphoric "to another... to another" repetition structuring the gift list (vv8-10); a diatribe-style personification of body parts arguing with one another (vv15-21); and a rapid-fire series of rhetorical questions in vv29-30 that expect the answer "no." Structurally the chapter moves from a catalogue of gifts, through the extended body metaphor, back to a catalogue of church functions — the first list naming manifestations of the Spirit, the metaphor explaining why differentiated gifts are necessary, and the final list locating those functions within the church. Verse 31 functions not as a return to the chapter's opening but as a deliberate pivot, closing the gifts discourse while opening onto the charity discourse of [[1 Corinthians 13]].
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How does verse 7's purpose clause constrain the way spiritual manifestations should be evaluated?
2. Which danger receives more attention in the body metaphor: feeling unnecessary, treating others as unnecessary, or both?
3. What practices would follow from giving greater honor to members who appear weaker?
4. Does “covet earnestly the best gifts” invite comparison among gifts, and how does the ensuing treatment of [[Charity]] qualify such comparison?
5. How should verse 3 be applied without reducing discernment to the mere utterance of a formula?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
