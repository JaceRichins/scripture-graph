# 1 Corinthians 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Corinthians 14]] · My notes: [[1 Corinthians 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> People should desire spiritual gifts—Tongues and prophecy are compared—Prophecy is the greater gift—Paul says, You may all prophesy; covet to prophesy.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Gifts that edify others (vv. 1-5):** prophecy directly benefits the church; tongues require interpretation for congregational edification.
2. **Why intelligibility matters (vv. 6-19):** instruments, a battle trumpet, mutually unintelligible voices, prayer, singing, and the congregational Amen illustrate the argument.
3. **Signs and outsiders (vv. 20-25):** an adaptation of [[Isaiah 28]] introduces a difficult contrast. Mass tongues cause an outsider to think the church is mad, whereas prophecy may disclose the heart and lead to worship.
4. **Order in the assembly (vv. 26-33):** varied contributions are permitted but limited, interpreted, evaluated, and given in sequence.
5. **Silence, authority, and conclusion (vv. 34-40):** disputed instructions concerning women's speech are followed by Paul's assertion of authority and a closing balance—desire prophecy, do not forbid tongues, and act decently and in order.

The similar exhortations in verses 1 and 39 may be read as a literary frame, though the repetition does not by itself establish a deliberately constructed inclusio.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents edification as a governing purpose of [[Spiritual Gifts]]. A manifestation may be meaningful between the speaker and God yet remain unfruitful for a congregation that cannot understand it. Public [[Revelation]] is also joined to responsibility: prophetic speakers can yield, prophetic speech can be judged, and claimed spirituality does not exempt anyone from order. The outsider scene in verses 24-25 makes intelligible worship a form of witness: its desired result is not fascination with the speaker but recognition that God is among the people.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated vocabulary of understanding, meaning, interpretation, teaching, learning, and edification creates a sustained contrast between sound and communicated meaning. Paul's sequence moves from 'speaking into the air' (v. 9) to another person being able to say Amen (v. 16), to five understandable words teaching others (v. 19). The numerical contrast between five and ten thousand makes the priority memorable without requiring the numbers to be read as a literal rule.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is framed by nearly matching exhortations to desire prophecy (vv. 1, 39). Inside that frame, Paul accumulates analogies before turning to concrete meeting rules. The pipe, harp, and trumpet show that sound without distinction cannot guide action; mutually unintelligible voices estrange speaker and hearer; the Amen example then places the same principle inside worship. This progression turns an abstract dispute over gifts into the practical question of whether another person can recognize, answer, learn, and be comforted.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary And Social Evidence
> The cumulative instructions provide strong textual evidence that the Corinthian assembly known to Paul included varied verbal contributions and that he sought to regulate them through intelligibility, discernment, turn-taking, and communal edification.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted internal social evidence · [[Internal Literary And Social Evidence in 1 Corinthians 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Cumulative Textual Argument
> The repeated contrast between unintelligible and intelligible speech strongly supports the conclusion that, in this chapter, a claimed spiritual origin does not by itself make public speech beneficial; benefit to the church requires communicable meaning.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted cumulative textual argument · [[Cumulative Textual Argument in 1 Corinthians 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism / Manuscript Transmission *(tentative)*
> In some manuscripts of the Western textual tradition, verses 34-35 appear after verse 40 rather than in their usual location; a number of textual critics cite this floating placement as evidence the verses may be a later marginal gloss incorporated at different points by different scribal traditions, rather than part of Paul's original text.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · real textual-critical dispute; documentation incomplete

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does 'sign' in verse 22 mean a converting sign, a judicial sign, or something else in view of [[Isaiah 28]] and the negative reaction in verse 23?
- Who are 'the other' who judge prophetic speech in verse 29, and what criteria does Paul expect them to use?
- What specific kind of speech is restricted in verses 34-35, given the reference to women praying or prophesying in [[1 Corinthians 11]]?
- Which principles are explicit and transferable—edification, intelligibility, discernment, peace, and order—and which meeting details may address Corinth's particular circumstances?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[1 Corinthians 12]], [[1 Corinthians 13]], and [[1 Corinthians 14]] as one argument: diversity of gifts, the supremacy of charity, and the orderly use of gifts for others. Compare Acts 2:4-11 with 1 Corinthians 14:2-13 rather than assuming that every New Testament use of tongues describes an identical phenomenon. Compare D&C 46:22-25 on tongues and interpretation, D&C 50:17-24 on mutual edification, and D&C 88:122 on speaking one at a time.
