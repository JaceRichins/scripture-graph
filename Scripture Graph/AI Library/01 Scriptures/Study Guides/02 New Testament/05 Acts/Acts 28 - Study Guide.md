# Acts 28 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 28]] · My notes: [[Acts 28 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Paul is unharmed by a viper’s bite—He heals the sick in Melita—He preaches in Rome, first to the Jews and then to the Gentiles.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Melita: hospitality, danger, and healing (vv. 1–10).** The islanders receive the survivors; the viper incident produces a dramatic reversal in their judgment of Paul; healings are followed by material support for the voyage.

2. **The road to Rome (vv. 11–16).** A place-by-place itinerary narrows toward Rome. Fellow believers come out to meet Paul, and their welcome gives him courage.

3. **Paul and Rome's Jewish leaders (vv. 17–29).** Paul explains his imprisonment, presents the hope of Israel, and spends a day reasoning from the law and prophets. The audience divides, leading to the citation of [[Isaiah 6]] and an announcement concerning the Gentiles.

4. **A prisoner yet unhindered (vv. 30–31).** Two years are summarized in an open-door ministry from Paul's rented house.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds continuity and expansion together. Paul describes his message as the hope of Israel and argues from the [[Law of Moses]] and the prophets, while also declaring that God's [[Salvation]] is sent to the Gentiles. The text therefore does not frame Gentile inclusion as a message detached from Israel's scriptures.

The healing of Publius's father joins [[Prayer]], laying on of hands, and healing, but the brief account does not define a complete ordinance or explain the mechanism of the miracle. The final verses also portray [[Missionary Work]] as possible under severe constraint: Paul cannot move freely, yet he can welcome, teach, and testify.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The phrase “hope of Israel” compresses Paul's larger defense: elsewhere in Acts he connects Israel's promised hope with resurrection. “This sect” is the Roman Jewish leaders' description of the Christian movement, not Paul's preferred self-designation.

The KJV's “barbarous people” should not automatically be heard as a modern moral condemnation. Within the chapter itself, these islanders are characterized by exceptional kindness, hospitality, and generosity. Whatever the older label's linguistic background, the narrative's moral portrayal is strongly positive.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Two reversals shape the chapter. The islanders move from calling Paul a murderer to calling him a god, illustrating how the same event can be forced into opposite interpretations. Later, the hearing motif from [[Isaiah 6]] turns on a more consequential division: some hear and believe, while others hear without accepting.

The ending is also ironic. Paul is chained and guarded, but the final words emphasize confident teaching with “no man forbidding him.” Acts leaves Paul's legal fate unresolved and instead gives narrative closure to the movement of the witness toward Rome. This makes the progress of the message—not Paul's personal safety—the final focus.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Narrative Perspective
> First-person plural language continues through the Melita and travel narratives, so the narrator presents these scenes from the perspective of someone within Paul's traveling company. This is meaningful internal evidence for a participant perspective, but only limited evidence for actual eyewitness authorship.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.9 · accepted perspective evidence / disputed historical inference · [[Narrative Perspective in Acts 28|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical/Nautical Detail *(tentative)*
> The chapter's incidental geographic and nautical details (the Alexandrian grain ship's figurehead of Castor and Pollux, the stops at Syracuse, Rhegium, and Puteoli, and the named Via Appia waystations Appii Forum and The Three Taverns) are cited by some scholars as evidence of the author's familiarity with genuine first-century Mediterranean travel, and are used to argue for the narrative's historical grounding.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · details verified; historical inference modest and disputed

> [!evidence] Internal/Textual *(tentative)*
> The continuous 'we'-narration in Acts 28 (part of a pattern beginning at Acts 16:10) is commonly cited as internal evidence that the author drew on, or was, an eyewitness travel companion of Paul.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · accepted observation / disputed authorship inference

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What assumptions lead the islanders to interpret first the bite and then Paul's survival as direct evidence of his identity?

- Why does Paul describe his imprisonment as being for the “hope of Israel”?

- How does the statement that some Jews believed qualify interpretations of Acts 28:28?

- What is achieved by ending Acts with ongoing proclamation rather than a verdict in Paul's trial?

- Which features of the chapter are observations available to all readers, and which conclusions depend on accepting a miraculous interpretation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the mistaken divine acclamation in [[Acts 14]] with Acts 28:6. Read the Isaiah citation alongside [[Isaiah 6]], [[Matthew 13]], and [[2 Nephi 16]], noting both context and wording. Compare “hope of Israel” with Paul's defenses in [[Acts 23]], [[Acts 24]], and [[Acts 26]]. Finally, trace the paired themes of Israel and the Gentiles from [[Luke 2]] through [[Acts 13]] to this chapter.
