# Acts 5 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 5]] · My notes: [[Acts 5 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Acts 5 pairs two very different demonstrations of what it costs to belong to the young Jerusalem church. Ananias and Sapphira misrepresent the proceeds of a sale to the community and die when confronted with their deceit (vv.1-11); soon after, the apostles are arrested for preaching Christ, delivered from prison by an angel, and beaten for continuing anyway — yet leave "rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer shame for his name" (v.41). Between these episodes stands a summary of apostolic signs and healing so vivid that people sought out even Peter's shadow (vv.12-16). The chapter closes with a Pharisee named Gamaliel counseling the council toward restraint rather than execution (vv.34-39) — a rare moment where an opponent's own reasoning becomes part of the narrative's argument.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. Ananias and Sapphira's deception and deaths (vv.1-11)
2. Summary of apostolic signs, wonders, and growth (vv.12-16)
3. Arrest, imprisonment, and angelic deliverance (vv.17-21)
4. Confusion of the officers and recapture without force (vv.22-26)
5. Trial before the council; Peter's declaration and doctrinal speech (vv.27-32)
6. Gamaliel's intervention and counsel (vv.33-39)
7. Beating, release, and continued daily teaching (vv.40-42)

The chapter alternates between an internal crisis (deception judged) and an external crisis (persecution overcome), with the healing-ministry summary (vv.12-16) serving as a hinge between them — a plausible reading of the sequence rather than an explicit statement by the narrator.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Voluntary [[Consecration]] and [[Honesty]]:** Peter's words to Ananias — "was it not thine own? ... was it not in thine own power?" (5:4) — show that the property and its proceeds remained under Ananias's own control throughout. The sin was not withholding funds but claiming to have given what was actually kept back: honesty toward God, not the amount given, is the point at issue.

**Obedience and revelation:** Peter's declaration "We ought to obey God rather than men" (5:29) sets a claimed divine command (the angel's instruction in v.20) above a specific human prohibition; it is best read within that concrete conflict rather than as an unrestricted license to resist authority in general. Verse 32 ties reception of the Holy Ghost's confirming witness to "them that obey him," linking [[Obedience]] to the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]].

**Christ's exalted role:** Jesus is described as exalted "to be a Prince and a Saviour, for to give repentance to Israel, and forgiveness of sins" (5:31), tying his exaltation directly to the offer of [[Repentance and Forgiveness]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Three descriptions sharpen the opening accusation: Ananias lies "to the Holy Ghost" (v. 3), lies "unto God" (v. 4), and the couple tempt "the Spirit of the Lord" (v. 9). Their proximity supports a close association between the Spirit and God, while leaving later systematic formulations unstated.

The phrase "all the words of this life" (v. 20) is not defined within the chapter. In context it appears to designate the message the apostles teach about Jesus, but whether "life" has a specialized technical sense here cannot be settled from Acts 5 alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The Ananias and Sapphira episode uses repetition with variation: each spouse is confronted about the same deception, falls dead, and is carried out by the young men, with "great fear" reported after each death (vv.5, 11) and escalating from individual hearers to "all the church."

The prison scene turns on irony: the council sends for prisoners it believes securely confined, only to learn the prison is still shut and guarded while the men are openly teaching in the temple. A second irony closes the chapter — beating and prohibition do not silence the apostles but are followed by rejoicing and daily instruction (vv.40-42).

The chapter's judgment on deceit from within (vv.1-11) and deliverance from persecution without (vv.17-21) invite a structural contrast between internal integrity and external boldness, though this pairing is an interpretive reading of the sequence, not an explicit narrator statement. Gamaliel's citation of two failed popular leaders, Theudas and Judas of Galilee (vv.36-37), supplies the chapter's only explicit link to datable secular history; Josephus places a Theudas revolt under the procurator Fadus (c. AD 44-46) and Judas's revolt at the census (c. AD 6) — the reverse order, and roughly a decade after Gamaliel's speech is narratively set — a long-noted, disputed chronological difficulty rather than settled apologetics either way.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Behavioral And Narrative Evidence
> The apostles' continued teaching after imprisonment, beating, and prohibition strongly supports the narrative theme of courageous endurance. Their persistence by itself, however, is not logically sufficient to prove that their religious claims were true.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation · [[Behavioral And Narrative Evidence in Acts 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Logical/Apologetic
> Gamaliel's maxim—that a movement 'of men' will come to nought while one 'of God' cannot be overthrown (5:38-39)—is sometimes cited apologetically as evidence for the divine origin of the church based on its subsequent survival, but the text presents it as one Pharisee's prudential political advice, not a divine oracle; as a general inference rule it is logically weak, since institutional endurance can result from social, political, or cultural causes unrelated to divine origin.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · affirmed (matches B:persistence-not-proof / gamaliel-conditional-counsel) · [[Logical/Apologetic in Acts 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Biblical-Theological
> Peter's parallel accusations — that Ananias lied 'to the Holy Ghost' (v.3) and 'unto God' (v.4) — treat lying to the Holy Ghost and lying to God as equivalent offenses, which functions as textual evidence for an identification of the Holy Ghost with God within Luke's theology in Acts.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.8 · accepted observation / disputed doctrinal weight · [[Biblical-Theological in Acts 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical *(tentative)*
> Gamaliel cites two failed popular leaders, Theudas and Judas of Galilee, as cautionary precedents. Judas's revolt is explicitly tied to a census ('the days of the taxing'), matching Josephus's account (Antiquities 18.1.1) of Judas the Galilean's uprising against the census of Quirinius, c. AD 6. However, Josephus (Antiquities 20.5.1) also records a rebel named Theudas active under the procurator Cuspius Fadus, c. AD 44-46—roughly a decade after Gamaliel's speech is narratively set (mid-30s AD)—and Acts places Theudas chronologically before Judas, the reverse of Josephus's dates for these two men. This is a long-noted, disputed chronological difficulty in comparing Acts with Josephus.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed — genuine tension with Josephus

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Acts 5:4 affirms that Ananias retained control over the property and its price. What was the lie actually about, and what does that imply about the nature of the offense against "the Holy Ghost"?
- Does the text attribute Ananias's and Sapphira's deaths directly to divine action, or does it chiefly invite that inference through timing and context, leaving the mechanism unstated?
- Verse 15 reports the crowd's hope concerning Peter's shadow, and verse 16 reports healings among a separate group. How can a careful reader avoid turning that hope into a claim the text itself does not make?
- What are the strengths and limits of Gamaliel's "if it be of God, ye cannot overthrow it" (5:38-39) as a test for evaluating a religious movement — does Luke present it as an endorsement of the apostles, or only as one leader's pragmatic caution?
- How should the chronological tension between Acts 5:36-37 and Josephus's dating of Theudas and Judas be weighed when evaluating Luke's historical reliability?
- What safeguards keep "we ought to obey God rather than men" (5:29) from being generalized into an all-purpose license to resist religious or civil authority?
