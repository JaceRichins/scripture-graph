# Acts 3 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 3]] · My notes: [[Acts 3 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Peter and John heal a man lame since birth—Peter preaches repentance—He also speaks of the age of restoration preceding the Second Coming—He identifies Christ as the prophet of whom Moses spoke.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter divides cleanly into a narrative unit and a sermon unit:

- **vv.1-11 — The healing.** Setting (v.1), the lame man's daily situation (v.2), the encounter and command (vv.3-8), and the crowd's astonished recognition (vv.9-11).
- **vv.12-26 — The sermon**, itself structured in movements:
  - vv.12-16: Peter deflects credit from himself to Christ, whose name and faith in that name effected the healing.
  - vv.13-15: A rehearsal of the Passion — delivered up, denied before Pilate, the murderer preferred, the "Prince of life" killed and raised.
  - v.17: A pivot — the people's guilt is reframed as ignorance.
  - vv.18-21: Christ's suffering and future return are framed as the fulfillment of "all his prophets," tied to a coming "times of refreshing" and "times of restitution of all things."
  - vv.22-25: Scriptural proof from Moses, Samuel, "all the prophets," and the Abrahamic covenant.
  - v.26: A closing summary — God sent his Son "to bless you, in turning away every one of you from his iniquities," echoing v.13's "his Son Jesus" and forming a loose inclusio around the sermon.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter links [[Miracles]] with testimony rather than celebrity: Peter refuses to treat the healing as proof of his personal holiness (Acts 3:12). It links [[Faith]] to the name of Jesus (Acts 3:16), though the verse does not settle whose faith is principally in view. It also presents [[Repentance and Forgiveness]] as an offered response even after severe wrongdoing: sins may be blotted out, and the covenant blessing includes turning people from iniquity (Acts 3:19, 26).

Acts 3:21 is important to [[Restoration of the Gospel]], but careful study should distinguish the verse's observation from later interpretation. The text anticipates a “restitution of all things”; identifying its complete historical or institutional contents requires additional argument and comparison with other scripture.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The sermon's rhetorical movement — accusation (vv.13-15), mitigation via appeal to ignorance (v.17), and invitation to repent (v.19ff.) — is a recognizable prophetic pattern rather than an accusation left standing alone. The healing narrative carries its own reversal: the man moves from sitting to walking, from outside the gate into the temple, while the crowd's attention is deliberately redirected by Peter away from the apostles and onto Jesus (Acts 3:12-16). A second reversal frames the sermon's center: those who killed the "Prince of life" did so to the one whom "God hath raised from the dead" (Acts 3:15). The triadic "God of Abraham, and of Isaac, and of Jacob" (v.13) is a fixed covenantal formula, not incidental phrasing, anchoring the argument in the patriarchal narratives before it turns to Moses and the prophets. The repetition of "his Son Jesus" near the sermon's start (v.13) and end (v.26) can be read as a loose envelope around the speech, though this rests partly on the KJV's rendering of the underlying Greek (*pais*, "child/servant") and shouldn't be treated as a rigorously demonstrated structure. Note also that while the healing lets the man enter the temple he had only approached from outside, the text never states that a purity law had previously barred him.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Linguistic/Lexical *(tentative)*
> The Greek term underlying 'his Son Jesus' (vv.13, 26) is 'pais,' which can mean either 'child/son' or 'servant.' Many scholars read this as an echo of the Isaianic 'servant of the LORD' (Isaiah 42, 52-53, LXX uses 'pais'), making Peter's speech an early application of Suffering Servant imagery to Jesus. This is an accepted lexical observation with a disputed theological extension.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.6 · accepted lexical fact / disputed theological extension

> [!evidence] Interpretive/Theological *(tentative)*
> Latter-day Saint interpretive tradition reads 'the times of restitution of all things' (Greek apokatastasis) as a prophecy of an institutional Restoration of doctrine, priesthood, and ordinances in the latter days. Mainstream New Testament scholarship more commonly reads the same phrase as a general eschatological renewal/cosmic restoration accompanying Christ's return, without necessarily implying a specific future institutional restoration event. Both readings are compatible with the Greek term's semantic range, but they are not the same claim.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · believing scholarship vs. mainstream critical reading, both stated
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Whose faith is emphasized in Acts 3:16 — the healed man's, the apostles', or faith made possible through Christ? The Greek syntax does not clearly resolve this.
- Why does Peter pair an acknowledgment of the people's "ignorance" (v.17) with an undiminished call to repent (v.19) — pastoral softening, a theological account of how prophecy could be fulfilled through wrongdoing, or both?
- How do "times of refreshing," the sending of Jesus, and "the times of restitution of all things" (vv.19-21) relate to one another chronologically, and what would justify reading "restitution" as one specific future restoration rather than a more general eschatological renewal?
- In what sense does Acts 3:26 define the Abrahamic covenant's blessing as "turning away every one of you from his iniquities" — as its whole content, or as its first effect on this audience?
- What should be made of the close verbal overlap between Acts 3:22-25 and [[3 Nephi 20]]'s citation of the same prophecies? Live possibilities include independent record-keeping of the same teaching, deliberate reuse of Peter's own words by a later speaker, or the Book of Mormon's translation drawing on the King James wording of Acts — the parallel itself doesn't settle which.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Acts 3]] with [[Deuteronomy 18]] on the prophet like Moses and with [[Acts 7]], where Stephen also invokes that same Mosaic promise. [[3 Nephi 20]] is an especially close parallel, since it places the same Moses-prophecy and Abrahamic-covenant language in Christ's own mouth. For the Abrahamic covenant formula in Acts 3:25, compare [[Genesis 22]] and [[Genesis 12]]. For the temple setting and its aftermath, continue into [[Acts 4]], where the same healing leads to the apostles' arrest before the Sanhedrin.
