# Acts 16 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 16]] · My notes: [[Acts 16 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Paul is directed in a vision to preach in Macedonia—He casts an evil spirit out of a woman—He and Silas are imprisoned, and they convert the jailor—They admonish all to believe on the Lord Jesus and be saved.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Timothy and the Jerusalem decrees (vv. 1–5):** Timothy joins the mission, is circumcised because of local Jewish concerns, and helps carry the apostles' decrees.

2. **Redirection to Macedonia (vv. 6–12):** Two blocked routes are followed by a vision, communal discernment, and travel to Philippi.

3. **Lydia and her household (vv. 13–15):** A Sabbath gathering by the river becomes the setting for the first narrated Philippian response.

4. **Exorcism and prosecution (vv. 16–24):** Liberation of an enslaved woman destroys her masters' expected income and precipitates public punishment.

5. **Prison deliverance and the jailer (vv. 25–34):** Worship, earthquake, preserved life, gospel instruction, baptism, care, and rejoicing form one continuous scene.

6. **Public accountability and departure (vv. 35–40):** Paul challenges the secret release, the magistrates respond fearfully, and the missionaries encourage the believers before leaving.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Revelation]] as both restraint and direction: the Spirit prevents travel into Asia and Bithynia, while the Macedonian vision opens a new field — but the travelers still have to interpret the vision and act on it (vv. 6-10).

The jailor's question about [[Salvation]] receives the direct answer to believe on the Lord Jesus Christ (v. 31), but the scene continues with instruction in the word, [[Baptism]], care for the wounded, hospitality, and rejoicing (vv. 32-34) — belief here is embedded in a larger response rather than an isolated verbal act.

Both Lydia's household (v. 15) and the jailor's household (v. 33) are baptized together with them. This pattern has been read in different ways in later debates over [[Infant Baptism (rejected doctrine)|infant baptism]]; the text itself gives no ages for household members and cannot by itself settle the question either way.

Lydia's conversion attributes both human hearing and divine initiative to the same event: she hears Paul, while "the Lord opened" her heart (v. 14). The text does not resolve the relationship between grace and agency, but holds both together in its account of conversion.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV's "spirit of divination" (v. 16) translates a Greek phrase built on "python" (pythōn), a term historically associated with the serpent said to guard the oracle at Delphi. This background is well attested for the word itself, though the text does not signal a deliberate literary allusion to the myth — it may simply be the ordinary contemporary label for a divining spirit. Either way, the term marks the girl as a professional medium whose oracular speech her owners exploited for profit, sharpening the contrast with Paul's authority over the spirit itself.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly turns apparent interruption into a fresh occasion for witness: closed routes lead to Macedonia; an exorcism leads to trial; imprisonment leads to the jailor's conversion; a proposed quiet release leads to public acknowledgment by the magistrates. This is a feature of the narrated sequence, not a promise that hardship always ends so visibly.

Within the Philippi conflict specifically, the house of Lydia brackets the persecution narrative — the missionaries are staying there when trouble begins and return there immediately after their release (v. 40) — though this framing applies to the conflict narrative (vv. 16-40), not the whole Philippi account, since Lydia's house isn't introduced until after the group's arrival and her own conversion (vv. 11-15).

Acts 16:10 begins a notable first-person-plural section, shifting from "they" to "we." This is significant narrative evidence, but by itself it does not prove any particular theory of authorship, eyewitness participation, or source composition.

The girl's own words about Paul's mission ("the way of salvation," v. 17) are difficult to fault, yet Paul silences rather than welcomes them — an ironic note the text does not explain.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does Paul circumcise Timothy immediately before delivering the Jerusalem council's decrees exempting Gentile converts from circumcision? The narrator gives a local, pragmatic reason (v. 3), not a general rule.
- Why does Paul tolerate the divining spirit's true-sounding proclamation for many days before expelling it, and why does he act when he does?
- What does Paul's demand for a public, in-person release by the magistrates (vv. 35-39) suggest about how the earliest church related to civil authority and legal protection?
- What might explain the abrupt shift to first-person narration beginning at verse 10?
- Why do the other prisoners remain in place after the earthquake looses their bonds and opens the doors?
