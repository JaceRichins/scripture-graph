## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Growth and grievance (6:1):** multiplication of disciples exposes unequal treatment in the daily ministration.

2. **Discernment and delegation (6:2–6):** the Twelve propose qualifications and differentiated responsibilities; the multitude chooses seven; the apostles pray and lay hands on them.

3. **Growth summary (6:7):** the word increases, disciples multiply, and many priests obey the faith.

4. **Stephen's witness and opposition (6:8–10):** his miracles and Spirit-given wisdom cannot be successfully resisted in debate.

5. **Accusation and arrest (6:11–15):** opponents recruit witnesses, raise charges about Moses, the law, and the holy place, and bring Stephen before the council. His angel-like face closes the chapter and prepares for [[Acts 7]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents spiritual gifts and practical care as mutually connected. Reputation, wisdom, and fullness of the Holy Ghost qualify people to oversee material needs (6:3), while the apostles preserve sustained attention to [[Prayer]] and proclamation (6:4). Stephen subsequently demonstrates that a practical assignment does not confine spiritual ministry: he teaches and performs [[Miracles]] (6:8–10).

The commissioning in verse 6 is relevant to [[Priesthood]] and [[Ordinances]], but caution is necessary. Acts 6 records prayer and laying on of hands without naming a priesthood office or calling the seven deacons. Later institutional categories should not simply be read back into the passage.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV term “Grecians” in verse 1 is commonly understood here to mean Hellenists—Greek-speaking Jewish disciples—contrasted with Jewish disciples associated with Hebrew or Aramaic language and culture. On this standard reading, the complaint reflects tension within the believing Jewish community, not a straightforward dispute between Jews and Gentiles.

Verse 7 says that “the word of God increased,” a compact expression describing the message's continued spread as the disciples multiplied. The phrase “obedient to the faith” portrays the priests' response as active adherence, though “the faith” may refer either to belief or to the emerging Christian movement.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verse 7 closes the first scene with a growth summary before the narrative turns to Stephen. The repeated multiplication language in verses 1 and 7 frames that scene, though it does not prove that the appointment of the seven caused the subsequent growth.

Stephen's characterization accumulates: he is full of faith and the Holy Ghost (6:5), full of faith and power (6:8), and speaks with wisdom and spirit that his opponents cannot resist (6:10). Opposition likewise escalates from disputation to recruited accusers, agitation, arrest, and false testimony.

The temple-related accusation resembles the false testimony at Jesus' hearing in [[Mark 14]], supporting a literary parallel between Stephen's rejection and Jesus' rejection. Stephen's “face as it had been the face of an angel,” placed after accusations concerning Moses, has also been associated with Moses' shining face in [[Exodus 34]]. That association is possible but less secure: Acts uses angel imagery rather than saying that Stephen's face shone.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Ecclesiological Textual Evidence
> Acts 6 describes the seven as appointed over a particular business but never calls them deacons. The episode can inform later discussions of church offices, yet identifying it as the formal institution of the diaconate goes beyond what this chapter explicitly states.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · accepted textual restraint · [[Ecclesiological Textual Evidence in Acts 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual And Narrative-Pattern Evidence
> Stephen's hearing parallels the trial tradition concerning Jesus: hostile witnesses present a charge involving the destruction of the holy place or temple. Together with the narrator's designation of Stephen's accusers as false witnesses, this supports a literary presentation of Stephen as following the pattern of Jesus' rejection.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · strong narrative parallel; degree of design debated · [[Intertextual And Narrative-Pattern Evidence in Acts 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary And Intertextual Evidence *(tentative)*
> Immediately after witnesses accuse Stephen of speaking against Moses, the council sees his face as the face of an angel. This juxtaposition plausibly recalls Moses' transformed face after speaking with God and creates narrative irony: the accused opponent of Moses is visually presented with a sign resembling Moses' divine authorization.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · juxtaposition accepted; specific allusion disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does verse 1 establish intentional discrimination, or only actual neglect perceived along cultural-linguistic lines?

- What is gained by having the multitude choose the seven while the apostles appoint them?

- Why are spiritual fullness, wisdom, and reputation required for administering material assistance?

- Does the sequence from reorganization to growth in verse 7 imply causation, or simply narrative succession?

- Since the narrator calls Stephen's accusers false witnesses, which parts of their report might be invention, distortion, or decontextualized versions of his teaching?

- How does the description of Stephen's face prepare the reader for his speech and vision in [[Acts 7]]?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the welfare arrangements in [[Acts 4]] with the breakdown and response in Acts 6. [[Exodus 18]] offers an earlier model of delegation under expanding leadership demands, while [[Numbers 27]] supplies an important commissioning scene involving the laying on of hands. Read [[Mark 14]] alongside Acts 6:11–14 to assess the trial parallel, and [[Exodus 34]] alongside Acts 6:15 to evaluate the proposed Moses echo. [[Acts 8]] follows the consequences of Stephen's death and the ministry of Philip.
