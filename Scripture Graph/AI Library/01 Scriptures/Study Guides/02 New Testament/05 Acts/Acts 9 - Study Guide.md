# Acts 9 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 9]] · My notes: [[Acts 9 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Acts 9 interweaves two apostolic story lines. Saul travels to Damascus as a persecutor but encounters Jesus, receives help from Ananias, is baptized, and begins proclaiming the one whose disciples he had sought to arrest. His former reputation makes both opponents and disciples react with understandable suspicion or hostility; Barnabas helps secure his reception in Jerusalem. The focus then returns to Peter, whose ministry at Lydda and Joppa includes the healing of Æneas and the restoration of Tabitha. Across both story lines, Jesus remains the source and object of the work: Saul bears his name, and Peter explicitly attributes healing to Jesus Christ.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Saul approaches Damascus and encounters Jesus (9:1–9).** The intended captor becomes blind and must be led by the hand.
2. **Ananias receives and baptizes Saul (9:10–19).** Coordinated visions bring a fearful disciple and the former persecutor together.
3. **Saul preaches and faces opposition (9:20–30).** His reversal produces amazement, disputed preaching, escape, suspicion in Jerusalem, and another threat to his life.
4. **A summary of church growth (9:31).** Rest, edification, reverence, the Holy Ghost's comfort, and multiplication are joined in one transition.
5. **Peter ministers at Lydda and Joppa (9:32–43).** The healing of Æneas and restoration of Tabitha are followed by reports of people turning to or believing in the Lord.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Conversion]] as both divine encounter and entry into a community. Saul receives direction from Jesus, but Ananias must enter the house, lay hands on him, call him brother, and participate in his reception. Barnabas later performs another communal role by vouching for him before the apostles.

Saul's calling joins [[Missionary Work]] with [[Adversity]]. He is chosen to bear Jesus' name before an unusually broad audience, yet suffering for that name is part of the commission rather than evidence that it has failed. His prompt [[Baptism]] follows the restoration of his sight, while Ananias also identifies being filled with the Holy Ghost as part of the purpose for which he was sent. The passage should not be pressed into a complete chronology of initiation rites because it does not explicitly narrate every moment of that process.

Peter's words to Æneas keep the agent of the healing clear: "Jesus Christ maketh thee whole." The miracles consequently direct attention toward [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] rather than Peter's independent power.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Acts 9:2 calls the Christian movement "this way," an early designation that depicts discipleship as a manner or path of life rather than merely a set of propositions. The chapter also repeatedly uses the language of Jesus' "name": believers call on it, Saul is to bear it and suffer for it, and he preaches boldly in it. Within the narrative, the name represents allegiance, authority, proclamation, and the cause for which suffering occurs.

The image of something falling from Saul's eyes "as it had been scales" is explicitly a comparison. It communicates the visible or felt recovery of sight but does not warrant a confident retrospective medical diagnosis.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Saul's story is built on reversal: he arrives intending to lead disciples bound to Jerusalem but is himself led by the hand into Damascus; he comes to silence those who call on Jesus' name and soon proclaims that name himself, becoming a hunted man saved by fellow disciples.

Tabitha is characterized before her death by her effects on others — the widows' grief and the garments they display make her service concrete. Peter's clearing the room, prayer, and command "Tabitha, arise" recall Jesus raising Jairus's daughter in [[Mark 5]] (shared elements: privacy, physical contact, a brief command). The parallel is suggestive rather than proven — Tabitha is presented as the woman's own name, and similar clearing-the-room and command patterns also appear in Elijah's and Elisha's resuscitation accounts, so this may reflect a broader biblical type-scene rather than deliberate imitation of one Gospel episode.

Peter's lodging with "Simon a tanner" (v.43) sets geography for the vision in the next chapter, though claims that tanning was a marked ritually-unclean occupation should be held loosely without a clear primary source.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Evidence
> Within Acts 9, the Aeneas and Tabitha episodes explicitly connect publicly known restorations with turning to or believing in the Lord. This is strong evidence for the narrator's theological presentation of miracles as signs supporting faith, but only internal evidence for the events' historical occurrence and effects.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 1.0 · well-labeled internal evidence · [[Internal Narrative Evidence in Acts 9|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary Characterization
> The contrast between Saul's intended arrests, his immediate proclamation of Jesus, and subsequent threats against his own life provides strong textual evidence that Acts portrays a radical reversal of allegiance and vocation.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · strong, appropriately hedged · [[Literary Characterization in Acts 9|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary/Redactional Parallel *(tentative)*
> The raising of Tabitha closely mirrors the structure and even the sound of Jesus' raising of Jairus's daughter: both accounts have the healer put mourners/others out of the room, take the dead person's hand, and issue a short command to arise ("Talitha cumi" / "Tabitha, arise"), a resemblance long noted by commentators as a deliberate Lukan literary technique of patterning apostolic ministry on the ministry of Jesus.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · suggestive parallel, disputed, no citation

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the Lord employ Ananias rather than completing Saul's restoration without human mediation?
- What does Barnabas's intervention suggest about the relationship between justified caution, testimony, and communal trust?
- Is "conversion" the most precise description of Saul's experience, or does "prophetic call and commission" preserve aspects that the modern term can obscure?
- Why does the narrative pair Saul's commission with an explicit prediction of suffering?
- How do Tabitha's remembered works of service contribute to the meaning of her restoration?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Saul's three retellings — this chapter, [[Acts 22]] (his defense before the Jerusalem crowd), and [[Acts 26]] (before Agrippa) — noting what each adds, omits, or adjusts, including the disputed detail of what his companions heard or saw (Acts 9:7 vs. 22:9). Compare the Damascus wall/basket escape with Paul's own account in [[2 Corinthians 11]], and consult [[Galatians 1]] for the chronology of his early travels, which Acts 9 alone does not resolve. For Peter's raising of Tabitha, compare the sequence and wording with Jesus raising Jairus's daughter ([[Mark 5]]; [[Luke 8]]) and with Elijah's and Elisha's raisings of the widow's and Shunammite's sons (1 Kings 17:17-24; 2 Kings 4:32-37), a broader pattern of prophetic and apostolic resuscitation miracles.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
