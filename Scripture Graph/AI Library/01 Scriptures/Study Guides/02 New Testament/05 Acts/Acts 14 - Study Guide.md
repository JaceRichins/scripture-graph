# Acts 14 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 14]] · My notes: [[Acts 14 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Persecution attends the spread of the gospel—Paul heals a crippled man; Paul and Barnabas are hailed as gods—Paul is stoned and revived; he preaches—Elders are ordained.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Iconium—belief, division, and flight (vv. 1–7):** Jews and Greeks believe; opposition intensifies; the missionaries flee a threat of abuse and stoning.
2. **Lystra—healing and mistaken worship (vv. 8–18):** A man unable to walk is healed; the crowd calls Barnabas and Paul gods; the missionaries redirect them to the Creator.
3. **Lystra and Derbe—violence and continued preaching (vv. 19–21):** Paul is stoned and presumed dead, rises, and continues to Derbe.
4. **Return journey—strengthening congregations (vv. 21–25):** The missionaries retrace their route, exhort disciples, and appoint elders with prayer and fasting.
5. **Antioch—completion and report (vv. 26–28):** They return to the sending church and report God's work among the Gentiles.

The Antioch in verse 21 is Pisidian Antioch; the Antioch in verses 26–28 is the Syrian sending church identified by the wider itinerary in [[Acts 13]].
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **[[Grace]] and divine witness:** The Lord bears witness to the word of grace through signs (v. 3), and the missionaries later return to the church that had commended them to God's grace (v. 26).
- **[[Faith]] and healing:** The man's faith has an explicit narrative role in his healing (vv. 8–10), but the passage does not teach that faith guarantees physical healing in every case.
- **[[Creation]] and witness:** The missionaries present rain, fruitful seasons, food, and gladness as witnesses of the living Creator's goodness (vv. 15–17).
- **[[Adversity]] and the [[Kingdom of God]]:** Tribulation is presented as an expected part of continuing in faith toward God's kingdom, not as payment that earns entrance (v. 22).
- **Community formation:** Mission includes strengthening believers, appointing elders, prayer, [[Fasting]], and commending congregations to the Lord (vv. 21–23). The passage does not define a complete hierarchy or the elders' precise authority.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
In context, “men of like passions” emphasizes the missionaries' shared humanity and denies the crowd's identification of them as gods (v. 15). “Vanities” characterizes the inadequate worship from which the crowd is urged to turn toward the living Creator. The “door of faith” is a metaphor portraying Gentile access to belief as something God opened (v. 27). The mention of the Lycaonian language may help explain why the apostles recognized the intended sacrifice only later, but the text never says they were unable to understand the crowd.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly juxtaposes success and opposition. At Iconium, many believe, yet the city divides and violence threatens (vv. 1–6). At Lystra, the narrative moves sharply from attempted sacrifice to Paul being stoned and presumed dead (vv. 11–19), though it does not establish that every would-be worshiper became an attacker. The geographical movement also reverses: after reaching Derbe, Paul and Barnabas retrace their route through places of danger to strengthen the new disciples (vv. 20–23). The Creator formula in verse 15 closely resembles [[Acts 4]], but this may reflect shared biblical language rather than a uniquely Lukan expression.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Narrative And Institutional Pattern
> The coordinated sequence of evangelizing, revisiting converts, strengthening them, appointing elders, and reporting to the sending church strongly supports the interpretation that Acts presents mission as durable community formation rather than preaching alone.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · Strong narrative pattern; affirmed · [[Narrative And Institutional Pattern in Acts 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary-Rhetorical Pattern
> The contrast between the creation-and-providence appeal in Acts 14 and the scriptural-history address in Acts 13 is substantial evidence that Acts intentionally portrays missionary speech as adapted to an audience's religious background.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Strong literary evidence; historical scope qualified · [[Literary-Rhetorical Pattern in Acts 14|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Classical Literary/Historical Background *(tentative)*
> Commentators often cite an ancient regional legend (preserved in Ovid, Metamorphoses 8) in which Zeus and Hermes visit Phrygia in human disguise and are received by an elderly couple, as cultural background that could help explain why a Lycaonian crowd would default to identifying a healer's companion as Zeus/Jupiter and the chief speaker as Hermes/Mercury specifically.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · Background tradition real; specific relevance disputed

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Biographical Inference *(tentative)*
> Paul's stoning and apparent left-for-dead recovery near Lystra (Acts 14:19-20) is commonly identified by commentators as the event Paul later references autobiographically ('once was I stoned') in his catalog of sufferings, since Acts records only one occasion of Paul being stoned.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · Natural identification, not textually demonstrated
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Why does the narrative place a true [[Miracles|miracle]] beside the crowd's false interpretation of it? What does this suggest about the limits of signs without sound teaching?
2. What distinguishes prudent flight in verses 5–6 from courageous return in verses 20–22?
3. How do the appeals to [[Creation]] and providence in verses 15–17 adapt the message to this audience?
4. What practices in verses 21–27 turn initial converts into enduring congregations?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the healing in [[Acts 3]] with Acts 14:8–10, noting both the close narrative correspondences and the different public reactions. Compare Acts 14:15–17 with [[Acts 17]], where a Creator-centered appeal again addresses Gentile hearers without a sustained scriptural-history argument. Read [[Acts 13]] for the mission's commissioning and outward journey, then [[Acts 15]] for the controversy following the reported opening of faith to the Gentiles. Paul's statement that he was once stoned appears in 2 Corinthians 11:25; identifying that event with Lystra is plausible but not explicit.
