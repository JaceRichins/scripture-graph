# Acts 21 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 21]] · My notes: [[Acts 21 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Acts 21 moves from an orderly voyage into the prolonged custody that shapes the remainder of Acts. Along the journey, Spirit-associated warnings and Agabus's enacted prediction anticipate suffering, while Paul declares himself ready for imprisonment or death. In Jerusalem, James and the elders celebrate God's work among the Gentiles but address concern among law-zealous Jewish believers. Paul follows their purification proposal, yet opponents from Asia accuse him at the temple, partly on a supposition about Trophimus. Roman intervention saves Paul from the crowd while also placing him in chains. The chapter repeatedly raises tensions between revelation and human response, attempted conciliation and conflict, and accusation and verified knowledge.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter has four main movements:

1. **Journey and community farewells (Acts 21:1–9):** Paul's company travels through Tyre to Caesarea, meeting disciples and praying together on the shore.
2. **Warnings and resolve (Acts 21:10–16):** Agabus enacts his prediction, the companions plead with Paul, and Paul continues toward Jerusalem.
3. **Jerusalem consultation and purification (Acts 21:17–26):** Paul reports his Gentile ministry; James and the elders address reports about his relationship to the [[Law of Moses]] and recall the decision in [[Acts 15]].
4. **Temple violence and Roman custody (Acts 21:27–40):** Opponents accuse Paul, Roman soldiers stop the beating and bind him, and Paul receives permission to address the crowd.

The geography narrows from sea travel and Christian households to Jerusalem, the temple, and finally the stairs of the Roman castle.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter presents [[Revelation]] and [[Prophecy]] without making discernment mechanically simple. Acts 21:4 attributes the Tyrian disciples' prohibition to speech through the Spirit; Agabus predicts Paul's treatment in Jerusalem; Paul nevertheless continues; and the companions finally say, “The will of the Lord be done.” The text does not explicitly reconcile these elements. A disclosure-of-danger followed by human inference is possible, but it is an interpretation rather than an explicit distinction in the verse.

Acts 21:20–26 should be read alongside [[Acts 15]]. The Jerusalem elders report that many Jewish believers are zealous for the [[Law of Moses]], while recalling the previously written requirements for Gentile believers. This documents differentiated practice and a difficult pastoral situation, but it does not by itself provide a complete or permanent system governing every Jewish and Gentile Christian.

Paul's stated willingness to suffer for the name of Jesus exemplifies [[Endurance]]. The narrative does not glorify violence: fellow believers try to protect him, and Roman intervention prevents his immediate death even as it places him in custody.

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Agabus turns his warning into an enacted image by binding his own hands and feet with Paul's girdle. The subsequent arrest broadly corresponds to the prediction, but not exactly: Jewish opponents seize Paul and seek his death, while Roman soldiers rescue him, take custody of him, and perform the narrated binding.

The chapter repeatedly contrasts reports with established knowledge. The elders discuss circulating reports about Paul's teaching; the temple accusers suppose that Paul brought Trophimus inside; contradictory shouting prevents the commander from learning “the certainty”; and the commander tentatively associates Paul with an Egyptian insurgent. The text directly exposes the supposition behind the Trophimus charge, not necessarily every broader allegation about Paul.

There is also narrative irony in the temple episode. Paul enters under a plan meant to demonstrate orderly regard for Jewish practice, yet the temple becomes the setting for accusations that he opposes the people, law, and sacred place. A comparison with Jesus's Jerusalem suffering in Luke is possible, but it remains a limited thematic proposal rather than an established structural pattern.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does Acts 21:4 present a divine prohibition, or could the disciples' prohibition be their response to Spirit-given knowledge of danger? What does the wording support, and what must be inferred?

- Why does the narrator mention that Philip's daughters prophesied but preserve none of their words, while recording Agabus's prophecy in detail?

- Does Paul's purification reflect personal Jewish observance, pastoral accommodation, rebuttal of a damaging report, or mixed motives?

- How does the narrator's distinction between what the accusers saw and what they merely supposed affect the specific temple-pollution charge?

- What does the commander's inability to learn “the certainty” contribute to the chapter's treatment of accusation and violence?

- What evidence would be needed to establish a deliberate literary parallel between Paul's journey toward suffering and Jesus's passion?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
