# Acts 13 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 13]] · My notes: [[Acts 13 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Saul and Barnabas are called to missionary service—Saul, now called Paul, curses a sorcerer—Christ is a descendant of David—Paul offers the gospel to Israel, then to the Gentiles.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
The chapter divides into two major movements:

1. **Cyprus mission (13:4-12).** Barnabas and Saul preach in the synagogues of Salamis, then confront the sorcerer Elymas/Bar-jesus at Paphos, culminating in his temporary blindness and the proconsul Sergius Paulus's belief.
2. **Pisidian Antioch (13:13-52).** After John Mark's departure at Perga, Paul delivers an extended synagogue sermon (13:16-41), which itself has a three-part shape:
   - Israel's national history from Egypt to David (13:17-22)
   - Jesus as David's promised heir, His death, and resurrection proved from Psalms (13:23-37)
   - An application offering forgiveness/[[Justification and Sanctification|justification]] and a prophetic warning (13:38-41)

The sermon's positive reception among Gentiles the following sabbath, followed by Jewish opposition, triggers the pivotal declaration 'lo, we turn to the Gentiles' (13:46) and the company's expulsion to Iconium.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects several doctrines without reducing them to isolated propositions. [[Missionary Work]] begins in communal worship, [[Fasting]], [[Prayer]], and guidance from the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]] (13:1–4). Paul's sermon presents the [[Resurrection]] as God's reversal of Jesus' condemnation and death (13:27–37). It then joins [[Repentance and Forgiveness]] with [[Justification and Sanctification]], declaring forgiveness through Jesus and justification for believers where the [[Law of Moses]] could not justify (13:38–39).

The turn toward Gentile hearers belongs to the chapter's treatment of [[Jews and Gentiles]], but it should not be read as though every Jew rejected the message. Verse 43 says that many Jews and religious proselytes followed Paul and Barnabas; the opposition narrated in verses 45 and 50 is substantial but not universal.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The designation “Saul, (who also is called Paul,)” in 13:9 presents two names rather than narrating a divine renaming. Claims that this verse records Saul receiving a new Christian name go beyond its wording.

“Ordained to eternal life” in 13:48 has played a large role in later debates over predestination and human response. Within this chapter, the expression describes those who believe, but the verse alone does not settle how divine appointment, foreknowledge, agency, and conversion relate.

The recurring vocabulary of sending is significant: the Antioch community sends Barnabas and Saul after the Spirit's call (13:3), while the next verse says they were sent by the Holy Ghost (13:4). The narrative places communal action and divine direction together.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Paul's sermon repeatedly makes God the subject of the action: God chooses the fathers, exalts the people, delivers them from Egypt, gives judges and kings, raises up David, and finally raises Jesus from the dead (13:17-37). This pattern of divine initiative gives the speech its coherence, reinforced by contrasts: Jerusalem's rulers condemn Jesus, but God raises him (13:27-30); David saw corruption, but the risen Jesus did not (13:35-37); some hearers reject the word while Gentiles rejoice and receive it (13:45-48).

The Isaiah 49:6 'light of the Gentiles' language Paul and Barnabas quote to justify the turn to Gentile hearers (13:47) also appears in Simeon's blessing over the infant Jesus in [[Luke 2]], suggesting Luke uses this text as a thread connecting his two-volume narrative from Jesus' infancy to the apostolic mission. A further, more speculative echo: Paul, once struck blind on the road to Damascus ([[Acts 9]]), now pronounces temporary blindness on an opponent of the faith (13:11)—a possible but unconfirmed authorial parallel, since the text draws no explicit connection between the two episodes.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Parallel *(tentative)*
> The parallel between Paul's own blindness (Acts 9) and the blindness he inflicts on Elymas (Acts 13:11) is a plausible instance of Lukan literary patterning, but blindness as divine judgment is a common biblical motif independent of this specific pairing (e.g., Genesis 19:11; 2 Kings 6:18), so the echo could be coincidental rather than an intentional callback.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · speculative, honestly hedged

> [!evidence] Literary/Onomastic *(tentative)*
> The Saul-to-Paul naming shift at 13:9 is sometimes read as theologically significant, but the evidence for authorial intent behind the timing is weak.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, appropriately hedged

> [!evidence] Theological/Compositional *(tentative)*
> Paul's statement that believers 'are justified from all things, from which ye could not be justified by the law of Moses' is an early, explicit articulation of justification through Christ apart from Torah-observance, anticipating themes developed at length in the Pauline epistles (e.g., Galatians, Romans).
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation

> [!evidence] Lexical/Translation Dispute *(tentative)*
> The clause 'as many as were ordained to eternal life believed' has been a focal point of translation and theological dispute: the underlying Greek participle can be read as passive ('ordained'/'appointed' by God) or in a more reflexive/dispositional sense ('as many as were disposed toward eternal life'), and interpreters have long divided along predestinarian versus free-will lines on this basis.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, but not evenly balanced
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What practices in 13:1-3 precede the missionary call, and which details (such as how the Holy Ghost spoke, or the exact nature of the laying on of hands) does the text leave unexplained?
- Why does Paul begin his proclamation of Jesus with a recital of Israel's history rather than an abstract doctrinal statement?
- Does 'ordained to eternal life' (13:48) describe God's prior appointment of believers or their own disposition toward belief? The more natural reading of the Greek participle is passive ('appointed'), though the verse alone cannot settle the larger question of predestination versus agency.
- What does the mixed response in 13:43-50—some Jews following, others opposing—caution against saying about either Jewish or Gentile hearers as a whole?
- Why does Luke leave John's departure at Perga (13:13) unexplained, given how much weight it carries later in [[Acts 15]]?
- How does the joy of the disciples in 13:52 coexist with the persecution and expulsion immediately preceding it?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the mission to “the ends of the earth” in [[Acts 1]] with the citation of [[Isaiah 49]] in Acts 13:47. Compare Saul's temporary blindness in [[Acts 9]] with Elymas's blindness here, while keeping the proposed parallel tentative because the narrator does not explain it. John's departure in 13:13 should also be read beside [[Acts 15]], where it becomes the stated cause of disagreement between Paul and Barnabas. Paul's use of [[Psalm 2]] and [[Psalm 16]] is especially important for studying how Acts interprets Israel's scriptures in relation to Jesus.
