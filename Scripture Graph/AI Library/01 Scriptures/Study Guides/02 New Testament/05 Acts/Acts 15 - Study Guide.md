# Acts 15 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 15]] · My notes: [[Acts 15 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **The controversy reaches Jerusalem (15:1-5):** Teachers demand circumcision and law observance for Gentile converts.
2. **Deliberation, testimony, and scripture (15:6-18):** Peter recalls God's witness through the Spirit; Barnabas and Paul recount signs among Gentiles; James appeals to [[Amos 9]].
3. **Judgment and letter (15:19-29):** Gentiles are not subjected to the disputed broader burden, but receive four specified requirements.
4. **Reception at Antioch (15:30-35):** The letter brings consolation, and Judas and Silas strengthen the congregation.
5. **A second contention (15:36-41):** Paul and Barnabas disagree over Mark and depart into different mission fields.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Peter places Jewish and Gentile believers on one footing: God knows their hearts, gives the Holy Ghost without making the disputed distinction, purifies by [[Faith]], and saves through the [[Grace]] of Jesus Christ (15:8-11). In context, this strongly rejects circumcision and comprehensive Gentile law observance as prerequisites for salvation. It does not say that grace makes conduct irrelevant; the letter still gives four concrete requirements, though their precise rationale and intended duration are not explained.

The chapter portrays discernment through debate, testimony about divine action, interpretation of scripture, corporate judgment, and claimed guidance from the Holy Ghost. The phrase "the Holy Ghost, and to us" joins divine guidance with human deliberation, but does not by itself establish the infallibility of every council or prove that every participant agreed at every stage.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Peter says, "we shall be saved, even as they" (15:11). The wording makes Jewish believers the compared group and places both Jews and Gentiles on the same basis of Christ's grace. It may carry rhetorical force, but the word order alone does not prove a deliberate reversal of an expected comparison.

James's citation in 15:16-17 reflects the Greek textual tradition of [[Amos 9]] more closely than the Masoretic/KJV wording. The KJV of Amos speaks of possessing "the remnant of Edom," whereas Acts speaks of "the residue of men" seeking the Lord. This difference involves more than Edom versus humanity; the verbs "possess" and "seek," possible underlying Hebrew variants, and adaptation of the Greek citation must also be considered. The passage does not establish which textual form is earliest or recover James's exact spoken wording.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The central deliberation moves through three converging forms of testimony: Peter recounts the Holy Ghost's witness, Barnabas and Paul report God's works among Gentiles, and James appeals to prophetic scripture. Acts presents these as mutually supportive within the narrative.

The chapter also places two conflicts in striking proximity. The public controversy moves through dispute toward corporate action and consolation, while the disagreement over Mark becomes so sharp that Paul and Barnabas separate. Acts neither conceals the rupture nor says that the conflict was good, divinely intended, or a reopening of the circumcision question. Reading the two conflicts as deliberate literary bookends is plausible but remains interpretation.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Textual Criticism *(tentative)*
> James's quotation in Acts 15:16-17 matches the wording of the Greek Septuagint of Amos 9:11-12, which differs materially from the Hebrew Masoretic Text: the MT speaks of Israel possessing 'the remnant of Edom,' while the Greek (followed here) speaks of 'the residue of men' seeking the Lord — a divergence widely attributed to the LXX translator reading the Hebrew consonants for 'Edom' (אדום) as 'mankind' (אדם).
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.6 · broad textual distinction accepted; explanation disputed

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Legal Background *(tentative)*
> The four prohibitions in the decree thematically resemble restrictions Leviticus 17-18 places on non-Israelite 'strangers' residing among Israel (improper sacrifice/idolatry, eating blood, eating improperly killed animals, sexual immorality), suggesting the council may have drawn on that legal framework as a minimal standard for Gentiles rather than requiring full Torah observance.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · plausible but disputed reconstruction

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why were the four prohibitions in Acts 15:20 and 15:29 selected? Proposed backgrounds include resident-alien laws in Leviticus 17-18, rejection of pagan cultic practices, conditions for Jewish-Gentile table fellowship, a combination of Torah passages, and universal-law traditions sometimes compared with later Noahide formulations. Acts does not explicitly choose among them.

- How does Acts 15:21 function in James's reasoning? Does it explain Jewish sensitivities in mixed communities, point to the widespread availability of Mosaic instruction, or provide another rationale?

- How should Peter's warning against imposing an unbearable "yoke" be related to the four requirements the council retains?

- Is the Jerusalem meeting in Acts 15 the same visit discussed in [[Galatians 2]]? Shared issues make comparison important, but chronology, participants, and details complicate identification.

- What does the unresolved Paul-Barnabas separation contribute to a chapter that has just narrated corporate agreement?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
