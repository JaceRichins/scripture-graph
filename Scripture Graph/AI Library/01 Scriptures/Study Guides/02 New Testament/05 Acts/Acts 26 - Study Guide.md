# Acts 26 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 26]] · My notes: [[Acts 26 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Paul recounts his former persecution of the Saints as a Pharisee—He testifies of the appearance of Jesus on the Damascus road—Paul bears his testimony to King Agrippa.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Opening appeal (vv. 1–3):** Paul asks Agrippa, whom he regards as knowledgeable about Jewish questions, to hear patiently.
2. **Shared Jewish ground (vv. 4–8):** His Pharisaic past and hope in God's promise frame the resurrection question.
3. **Persecutor and witness (vv. 9–18):** Paul confesses his campaign against Jesus' followers and recounts the heavenly appearance and commission.
4. **Obedient mission (vv. 19–23):** His preaching of repentance, resurrection, and light explains both his arrest and his continuing testimony.
5. **Competing responses (vv. 24–29):** Festus calls him mad; Paul answers calmly and directs the prophetic question to Agrippa.
6. **Private verdict (vv. 30–32):** The rulers find no capital or imprisonable offense, although the appeal to Caesar remains decisive.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter integrates several teachings rather than treating them in isolation. [[Revelation]] creates an obligation that Paul calls obedience; [[Conversion]] redirects both allegiance and vocation; [[Faith]] in Christ is associated with forgiveness, inheritance, and sanctification; and [[Repentance]] includes conduct appropriate to a genuine turning toward God. Above all, [[Resurrection]] is presented as the promised hope toward which Israel's scriptures point.

Acts 26:18 also gives a compact account of deliverance: darkness gives way to light, Satan's power to God's rule, guilt to forgiveness, and exclusion to inheritance. These paired movements illuminate the chapter's presentation of [[Salvation]] without implying that this single verse exhausts the doctrine.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The commission in verse 18 is constructed from antitheses: **darkness/light** and **Satan/God**. These contrasts turn Paul's personal reversal into a model for the mission he is commanded to undertake. The vocabulary of opened eyes and light to Gentiles also recalls Isaiah 42:6–7 and 49:6, though whether Acts is directly quoting a particular passage or drawing on a broader prophetic complex remains open.

Verse 20 balances turning and doing: hearers should repent, turn to God, and perform works appropriate to repentance. The works are described as fitting repentance, not as a substitute for the faith and forgiveness named in verse 18.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The speech advances through four kinds of persuasion: Paul's established identity as a Pharisee, candid admission of his hostile past, narration of a divine commission, and an appeal to Moses and the prophets. It then becomes personal when Paul asks Agrippa whether he believes the prophets. The alternating judgments—Festus's charge of madness, Paul's claim to truth and sobriety, Agrippa's response, and the rulers' innocence verdict—let the narrative display sharply different reactions to the same testimony.

Comparison with [[Acts 9]] and [[Acts 22]] is especially useful. The accounts share a stable narrative core but vary in setting, selection, and emphasis. Acts 26 stresses prophetic fulfillment, Gentile mission, and Paul's obedience because those themes directly serve the defense before Agrippa.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Intertext
> The language of opening eyes, turning from darkness to light, and bringing light to Gentiles closely aligns Paul's commission with Isaiah's servant-mission imagery. This supports reading the Gentile mission as presented in continuity with Israel's prophetic scriptures.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · accepted intertext; precise derivation disputed · [[Literary Intertext in Acts 26|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Repeated Narrative Motif
> The declaration of Paul's innocence in Acts 26 continues a sequence of official findings in Acts that the dispute concerns religious questions rather than a capital Roman offense. This supports the interpretation that Acts has a sustained apologetic interest in distinguishing Christian proclamation from punishable political crime.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · repeated motif accepted; purpose interpretive · [[Repeated Narrative Motif in Acts 26|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Translation
> Agrippa's reply in v.28 is a well-known translation crux. The KJV rendering 'Almost thou persuadest me to be a Christian' is often popularly read as near-conversion, but the underlying Greek is genuinely ambiguous and has been rendered by various translators/commentators as sarcastic deflection, a statement about the brevity of Paul's argument ('in a short time...'), or a noncommittal courtly reply — not unambiguously a sincere near-conversion.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · translation ambiguity broadly recognized · [[Textual/Translation in Acts 26|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Narrative-Thematic Coherence
> Paul's summary that the Christ would suffer, rise, and proclaim light to Jews and Gentiles substantially parallels the risen Jesus' summary that the Christ would suffer, rise, and have repentance and remission preached among all nations. The convergence supports a coherent fulfillment-and-mission theme across Luke-Acts.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · strong narrative-thematic parallel · [[Narrative-Thematic Coherence in Acts 26|full dossier]]

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What does Paul gain rhetorically by beginning with Israel's promised hope before recounting his vision?
- Why does the speech include such an unsparing account of Paul's former violence?
- How do forgiveness, sanctification, inheritance, faith, repentance, and works relate within verses 18–20?
- Does Agrippa's statement in verse 28 express near-conversion, irony, resistance, or another nuance? The English word “almost” should not by itself settle the question.
- How does the private innocence verdict affect the interpretation of Paul's continuing journey to Caesar?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Acts 9]], [[Acts 22]], and Acts 26 together, listing what remains constant and what each setting emphasizes. Then compare Acts 26:17–18, 23 with Isaiah 42:6–7 and 49:6, and Acts 26:22–23 with Luke 24:46–47. These comparisons clarify how Acts relates Paul's mission to prophetic scripture while also exposing the limits of claims about exact historical wording.
