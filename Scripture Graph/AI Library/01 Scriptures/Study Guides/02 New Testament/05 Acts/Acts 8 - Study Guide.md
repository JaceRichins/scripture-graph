# Acts 8 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 8]] · My notes: [[Acts 8 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Saul persecutes the Church—Philip’s ministry in Samaria is described—Philip performs miracles and baptizes men and women—Peter and John come to Samaria and confer the gift of the Holy Ghost by the laying on of hands—Simon seeks to buy this gift and is rebuked by Peter—Philip preaches about Christ and baptizes an Ethiopian eunuch.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Persecution and dispersal (Acts 8:1-4):** Saul attacks the church; scattered believers preach the word.
2. **Philip in Samaria (Acts 8:5-13):** preaching, miracles, communal response, baptism, and Simon's initially narrated belief.
3. **Peter, John, and Simon (Acts 8:14-25):** baptized Samaritans receive the Holy Ghost through prayer and laying on of hands; Simon's attempted purchase is condemned.
4. **Philip and the Ethiopian (Acts 8:26-40):** divine direction, interpretation of Isaiah, baptism, the traveler's joy, Philip's sudden removal, and further coastal preaching.

The geographical movement into Judaea and Samaria closely advances the mission program announced in [[Acts 1]], without completing it.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The Samaritan episode is important for studying [[Baptism]], the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]], and [[Ordinances]]. In this case, people are baptized before Peter and John pray and lay hands on them, after which they receive the Holy Ghost (Acts 8:12-17). The sequence is explicit, but Acts 8 does not explain the delay or establish by itself that every conversion must follow identical timing.

Simon's rebuke connects [[Repentance]], [[Prayer]], and questions of spiritual authority. Peter rejects the idea that God's gift can be purchased, says Simon's heart is not right, and directs him to repent and pray. Simon then requests intercession, but the chapter does not disclose his sincerity, repentance, or later fate. His earlier belief and baptism should neither be declared fraudulent nor treated as proof of mature faith.

Philip's encounter with the Ethiopian joins [[Scripture Study]] with [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]. Philip listens to the reader's question and begins from the passage already before him. Acts identifies the traveler as an Ethiopian eunuch and worshiper but does not settle whether he was Jewish, a proselyte, a God-fearer, or a Gentile.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“Esaias” is the New Testament form of the name Isaiah. “Candace” is generally understood as a Kushite/Meroitic royal title rather than the ruler's personal name, though Acts does not identify the particular monarch.

The Samaritan account repeatedly describes attention shifting from Simon to Philip. The people had heeded Simon and regarded him because of his sorceries (Acts 8:10-11), but they later heed Philip and believe his preaching about the kingdom of God and Jesus Christ (Acts 8:6, 12). Simon presents himself as someone great; Philip directs attention to Christ.

Acts 8:37 appears in the supplied canonical text, but important early Greek witnesses omit it, and modern textual critics generally consider it secondary. Its contrast between wholehearted belief and Simon's disordered heart may be studied as a feature of the received form of the chapter, but it should not be confidently attributed to Luke's earliest recoverable text.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Acts 8 alternates public and personal mission. Samaria responds through crowds, miracles, and apostolic visitation; the Gaza-road scene narrows to one traveler, one text, and one conversation. In both settings Philip keeps Jesus central while responding to different circumstances.

The chapter's geography widens from Jerusalem into Judaea and Samaria, then toward Gaza, Azotus, and Caesarea. This movement supports the larger expansion announced in [[Acts 1]]. Joy occurs in both principal mission episodes (Acts 8:8, 39), creating a possible linkage between them, although joy is common throughout Luke-Acts and the repetition does not prove a deliberate chapter-level frame.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Ordinance Sequence
> In the Samaritan episode, baptism in the name of the Lord Jesus and reception of the Holy Ghost are narrated as distinguishable events. Peter and John pray and lay hands upon people who have already been baptized, after which they receive the Holy Ghost.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · observation accepted; universal interpretation disputed · [[Ordinance Sequence in Acts 8|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Narrative-Geographical Structure
> The movement from Jerusalem into Judaea and Samaria is strong literary evidence that Acts 8 develops the geographical mission announced in Acts 1:8. It is a partial realization of that program rather than its completion, since the mission to the uttermost part of the earth continues through the rest of Acts.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 0.9 · broadly accepted literary reading · [[Narrative-Geographical Structure in Acts 8|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Doctrinal/Liturgical Pattern
> The two-stage sequence in vv.12-17 (baptism, then a separate later laying-on-of-hands for the Holy Ghost) is often read as scriptural precedent for a distinct confirmation ordinance following baptism. This is a genuine textual pattern, but its status as a universal template — rather than a one-time narrative device authenticating the new, ethnically distinct Samaritan mission to the Jerusalem apostles — is disputed.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · pattern accepted; universal application disputed · [[Doctrinal/Liturgical Pattern in Acts 8|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] New Testament Textual Criticism
> Acts 8:37, including the Ethiopian's confession that Jesus Christ is the Son of God, is present in the canonical text supplied here but absent from several of the earliest and strongest Greek witnesses. Textual critics generally regard it as a later expansion reflecting an early baptismal confession; interpretation of the chapter should therefore avoid depending on this verse alone.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broad textual-critical consensus · [[New Testament Textual Criticism in Acts 8|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Intertextual/Thematic Inference *(tentative)*
> Some readers connect the eunuch's unrestricted welcome and baptism in Acts 8 to Isaiah 56:3-5, where a eunuch is promised 'a place and a name' in God's house better than sons and daughters. This is a plausible thematic resonance, but Acts 8 never quotes or alludes to Isaiah 56 — the eunuch is reading Isaiah 53 — so the connection is a reader/scholarly inference about narrative resonance, not something Luke states.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · speculative thematic resonance
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Is the Samaritan delay in receiving the Holy Ghost a general ordinance pattern, an exceptional authentication of Samaritan inclusion, or some combination of both?
- What does Acts actually establish about Simon's initial belief, later motives, and response to correction? Where does the text remain silent?
- What was the Ethiopian's religious status? Why should his journey to worship at Jerusalem not be treated as decisive evidence?
- How much interpretive weight should be placed on Acts 8:37, given its presence in the supplied canonical text and weak support in the earliest Greek manuscript tradition?
- Does the Spirit's catching away of Philip describe miraculous transportation, forceful divine redirection, or a mechanism the narrative leaves unexplained?
- How does Philip's question-led use of Isaiah model the relationship between personal reading and guided interpretation?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Acts 8 with [[Acts 1]] for the geographical mission program and with [[Acts 19]] for another episode involving baptism, laying on of hands, and reception of the Holy Ghost; note the important differences between the two accounts. Read the Ethiopian episode alongside [[Isaiah 53]], remembering that Acts follows Greek-scripture wording that differs from the KJV wording of Isaiah. [[Isaiah 56]] offers a possible eunuch-inclusion resonance, but Acts does not cite or clearly allude to it. [[Luke 24]] provides a broader comparison for interpreting Israel's scriptures in relation to Jesus.

The index also reports limited text overlap with [[1 Nephi 11]]. No substantive literary or doctrinal connection has been demonstrated in these proposals, so that overlap should be treated as a search lead rather than an accepted parallel. Text-critical study of Acts 8:37 is worthwhile because the received text and modern critical editions differ at that point.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
