# Acts 17 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Acts 17]] · My notes: [[Acts 17 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Paul and Silas preach and are persecuted in Thessalonica and in Berea—Paul, in Athens, preaches from Mars’ Hill about the unknown god—He says, We are the offspring of God.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Thessalonica (vv. 1–9):** scriptural reasoning, conversions, organized opposition, and a political accusation.

2. **Berea (vv. 10–15):** ready reception, daily examination of scripture, belief, and renewed opposition.

3. **Athens before the speech (vv. 16–21):** distress over idolatry, discussion in synagogue and marketplace, and an invitation to the Areopagus.

4. **Areopagus address (vv. 22–31):** contact through an altar, proclamation of the Creator, common human origin and divine nearness, critique of idols, and a summons to repentance before judgment.

5. **Athens after the speech (vv. 32–34):** mockery, delayed interest, and belief.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter binds [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] to suffering, [[Resurrection]], kingship, and future judgment. Its doctrine of [[Creation]] emphasizes both divine transcendence and human dependence: God cannot be contained or sustained by human construction, while every life and breath depends upon him. The unity of the nations and God's nearness provide the foundation for a universal summons to [[Repentance]] and accountability at the [[Final Judgment]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV's “too superstitious” in Acts 17:22 translates a Greek expression that can carry a range from “very religious” to “superstitious.” The opening may therefore be deliberately tactful or ambiguous; Paul's following argument supplies the criticism more clearly than the opening adjective alone. Likewise, “God winked at” in Acts 17:30 is an older English idiom for overlooking or passing over former ignorance, not an assertion that God approved it. The KJV's “of one blood” in verse 26 reflects its textual tradition; many modern editions render the underlying phrase as “from one,” while retaining the central claim of common human origin.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter repeatedly pairs proclamation with examination and divided response. Thessalonica moves from scriptural argument to belief and violence; Berea from eager reception to verification and belief; Athens from curiosity to a threefold response of mockery, postponement, and faith. The Areopagus speech itself moves from what the audience recognizes to what challenges it. Paul cites an altar and Greek poetry, yet uses those points of contact to reject divine need, handmade representations, continued ignorance, and immunity from judgment.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary/Intertextual
> The line Paul cites ('we are also his offspring') closely matches a phrase from the Hellenistic poet Aratus's Phaenomena ('for we are also his offspring'), and a similar idea appears in Cleanthes' Hymn to Zeus; this is a widely noted point in New Testament commentary on Acts 17.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · Widely accepted verbal parallel; exact dependence unresolved · [[Literary/Intertextual in Acts 17|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary-Historical Intertext
> The wording about humanity being God's offspring closely corresponds to a line in Aratus's Greek poem Phaenomena. Together with Paul's explicit reference to “your own poets,” this is strong evidence that the speech, as narrated, deliberately uses Greek cultural language as a point of contact.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · Established parallel; exact literary dependence disputed · [[Literary-Historical Intertext in Acts 17|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Canonical Historical Convergence *(tentative)*
> Acts 17 and 1 Thessalonians independently present Thessalonian conversion in a setting of opposition. The letter also recalls receiving the word in affliction, turning from idols, and awaiting Jesus whom God raised. This convergence supports the broad historical setting more strongly than it supports every narrative detail in Acts.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · Broad convergence accepted; independence and force disputed
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Why does the narrator call the Bereans noble for both receiving and testing the message?

- Does the accusation about “another king” accurately describe Paul's preaching, strategically distort it, or do both at once?

- Which parts of the Areopagus speech establish common ground, and at what point does the speech become confrontational?

- Why does resurrection, rather than the earlier teaching about a Creator, provoke the sharpest recorded division?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the Gentile address in [[Acts 14]] with the Areopagus speech: both appeal to the Creator and providence, but Acts 17 adds a developed argument about human unity, idolatry, repentance, judgment, and resurrection. Compare the handmade-temple language with [[Acts 7]], and the creation-to-idolatry argument with [[Romans 1]]. Finally, read [[1 Thessalonians 1]] alongside Acts 17 for Paul's own description of Thessalonian conversion, affliction, departure from idols, and hope in the risen Jesus.
<!-- SG:END further-study -->
