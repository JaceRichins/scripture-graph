# Acts 20 — Study Guide

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Paul raises Eutychus from death—Paul is free from the blood of all men—He predicts apostasy from within the Church—He reveals a teaching from Jesus, It is more blessed to give than to receive.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Journey through Macedonia and Greece (vv. 1–6):** Paul exhorts believers, avoids a plot, and travels with a geographically diverse company.
2. **Gathering at Troas (vv. 7–12):** teaching and breaking bread frame Eutychus's fall and restoration.
3. **Journey to Miletus (vv. 13–17):** the tightly paced itinerary emphasizes Paul's haste to reach Jerusalem.
4. **Retrospective testimony (vv. 18–21):** Paul recalls humility, suffering, comprehensive teaching, [[Repentance]], and [[Faith]].
5. **Present resolve (vv. 22–27):** foreknown affliction does not displace his commitment to finish his ministry.
6. **Future warning and charge (vv. 28–35):** elders must watch themselves and the flock, resist corrupt teachers, trust divine [[Grace]], and support the weak.
7. **Farewell (vv. 36–38):** prayer, grief, affection, and accompaniment to the ship conclude the scene.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Paul's missionary summary joins “repentance toward God” with “faith toward our Lord Jesus Christ” (Acts 20:21), connecting [[Repentance]], [[Faith]], and [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]]. He describes his ministry as testimony to the “gospel of the grace of God” (Acts 20:24), while the word of [[Grace]] can build believers and give them an inheritance among the sanctified (Acts 20:32).

The elders are charged to watch themselves and care for God's flock. The Holy Ghost has made them overseers, and the church's costly purchase grounds their responsibility (Acts 20:28). The KJV reads “the church of God, which he hath purchased with his own blood,” but the underlying verse has important textual and syntactic questions; it should not alone bear a precise conclusion about the relationship of the Father and the Son. The warning in Acts 20:29-30 supports study of [[Apostasy]] in the immediate sense of destructive teaching and division threatening this community, not proof by itself of a total or universal apostasy.

Paul also makes self-supporting labor and care for the weak part of faithful [[Service]] (Acts 20:33-35).

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
“I go bound in the spirit” (Acts 20:22) can describe Paul's inward resolve, direction by the Holy Ghost, or an overlap between them; Acts 20:23 supports divine guidance but does not eliminate the ambiguity.

“Pure from the blood of all men” (Acts 20:26-27) is illuminated by the watchman principle in [[Ezekiel 3]] and [[Ezekiel 33]]: faithful warning releases the watchman from responsibility for another person's blood. Acts does not mark this as a direct quotation, so shared Jewish moral language remains possible.

“Flock,” “overseers,” “feed,” and “wolves” form a sustained pastoral metaphor in Acts 20:28-30. The KJV's “church of God…his own blood” occurs at a recognized textual crux: manuscripts differ over “God” and “Lord,” and interpreters dispute whether the construction can mean the blood of God's “own one” or Son. Doctrine should therefore be drawn from this wording with appropriate caution.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The farewell address moves through past, present, and future: Paul recalls his conduct among the elders (Acts 20:18-21), explains his journey into foreknown affliction (Acts 20:22-27), and prepares them for dangers after his departure (Acts 20:28-35). Repeated appeals to what they know make his visible conduct part of his testimony.

First-person plural narration in Acts 20:5-6 and 13-15 presents portions of the journey from a participant's perspective. This may preserve eyewitness or participant-shaped itinerary material, but it does not by itself identify the final author; incorporation of a source or a literary convention remains possible.

The Eutychus account is framed by teaching and breaking bread. His restoration has plausible analogues in [[Acts 9]], [[Luke 7]], [[1 Kings 17]], and [[2 Kings 4]], but these comparisons do not establish deliberate allusion. Acts 20:35 also preserves a saying attributed to Jesus that is absent from the four canonical Gospels, providing evidence for Jesus tradition beyond sayings incorporated into those narratives while leaving its transmission route unknown.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Early Christian Gathering Practice
> Acts 20 supplies strong evidence that at least one early Christian community met on the first day of the week for teaching and breaking bread. It is weaker evidence for a universal weekly rule, a formal transfer of Sabbath status, or the exact ritual meaning of the meal.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · Single first-day practice accepted; broader rule disputed · [[Early Christian Gathering Practice in Acts 20|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Historical
> This unrecorded saying is one of the NT's clearest 'agrapha' (sayings of Jesus preserved outside the Gospels), suggesting that early Christian communities retained oral traditions of Jesus' teaching beyond what the Gospel writers chose to include.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.8 · Broadly accepted with transmission route uncertain · [[Textual/Historical in Acts 20|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Extra-Gospel Jesus Tradition
> Acts preserves the saying attributed to Jesus, "It is more blessed to give than to receive," although that saying is not otherwise recorded in the four canonical Gospels. This is meaningful evidence that sayings of Jesus circulated beyond those incorporated into the Gospel narratives.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · Broadly accepted; transmission mechanism uncertain · [[Extra-Gospel Jesus Tradition in Acts 20|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Internal Narrative Evidence
> The first-person plural narration presents part of the journey from a participant's perspective and includes concrete travel stages and durations. This is meaningful internal evidence for access to an itinerary or participant-shaped travel tradition, but it does not by itself prove that the final author personally witnessed the journey.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.9 · Pronoun evidence accepted; explanation disputed · [[Internal Narrative Evidence in Acts 20|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Historical Practice *(tentative)*
> Acts 20:7 is often cited as early evidence that first-generation Christians gathered, including a shared meal, on the first day of the week (Sunday) rather than the Jewish Sabbath.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does “bound in the spirit” in verse 22 describe Paul's inward resolve, direction from the Holy Ghost, or both? Verse 23 supports divine guidance but does not completely remove the grammatical ambiguity.
- How should Paul's statement “his life is in him” be related to the narrator's statements that Eutychus was taken up dead and later brought alive?
- Was the first-day gathering a regular weekly pattern or an exceptional farewell meeting? What can verses 7 and 11 establish, and what remains unstated?
- How does Paul's command that overseers first watch themselves affect readings of leadership and accountability?
- Why does Paul treat drawing disciples after oneself, rather than merely teaching an error, as a defining feature of the danger?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare Paul's innocence-from-blood declaration with the watchman commission in [[Ezekiel 33]]. Read the warning about wolves alongside [[Matthew 7]], and compare Paul's labor and exemplary conduct with [[1 Thessalonians 2]] and [[2 Thessalonians 3]]. The continuation into [[Acts 21]] shows how the predicted bonds and afflictions become increasingly concrete as Paul approaches Jerusalem.
