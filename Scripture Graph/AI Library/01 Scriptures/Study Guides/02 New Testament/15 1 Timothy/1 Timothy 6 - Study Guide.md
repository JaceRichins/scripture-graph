# 1 Timothy 6 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Timothy 6]] · My notes: [[1 Timothy 6 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter moves from conduct within an unequal social order to false teaching, contentment, the dangers of pursuing wealth, Timothy's personal charge, and duties imposed on the rich. Its economic teaching is more precise than the slogan often drawn from verse 10: love, pursuit, pride, misplaced trust, and selfish use of wealth are explicitly condemned. Rich believers are directed toward generosity and good works, while both they and Timothy are told to lay hold on [[Eternal Life]]. The chapter closes by connecting fidelity to [[Truth]] with guarding an entrusted teaching.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
One plausible outline is:

1. **Servants and believing masters (vv. 1–2):** conduct, service, and the reputation of the doctrine.
2. **Contrary teaching and false gain (vv. 3–10):** contentious speech, corrupt motives, contentment, and the dangers of desiring riches.
3. **Charge to Timothy (vv. 11–16):** virtues to pursue, the fight of [[Faith]], public confession, Christ's appearing, and doxology.
4. **Charge to the rich (vv. 17–19):** humility, trust in God, generosity, and future treasure.
5. **Final custody of the trust (vv. 20–21):** avoidance of empty speech and falsely named knowledge.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Godliness is paired with contentment rather than material gain. The chapter neither calls wealth intrinsically secure nor treats it as the proper ground of trust; riches are explicitly “uncertain,” whereas God is described as the giver of things to enjoy. Those possessing wealth are accountable to do good and share it. This supports links to [[Humility]], [[Service]], and [[Eternal Life]], but it does not supply a complete theory of ownership, poverty, or economic justice.

Timothy's charge also joins human perseverance with divine calling. He must pursue virtue, fight, keep the commandment, and guard his trust, yet eternal life is also that to which he has been called. The horizon of this endurance is the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Verse 10 says that **the love of money** is the root of all evil; replacing its subject with “money” changes the statement. Even so, the scope of “all evil” should not be settled merely by paraphrase without further lexical study.

The expressions “will be rich” and “rich in this world” identify different groups: aspiring accumulators in verses 9–10 and existing wealthy believers in verses 17–19. The latter are not told here simply to admire generosity but to become “rich in good works.” In verse 18, the parallel between being ready to distribute and willing to communicate makes sharing the contextual sense of “communicate.”

“Science falsely so called” should not automatically be equated with modern science. The immediate target is knowledge falsely named, paired with profane babbling and oppositions that have drawn some away from the faith.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The sharpest verbal pattern is the reversal of **gain**: false teachers treat gain as godliness, while the response calls godliness with contentment great gain. Another repetition links the chapter's two principal exhortations: Timothy is to “lay hold on eternal life” in verse 12, and the rich receive the same final imperative in verse 19. The intervening duties differ, but the repeated destination unifies them.

The chapter also uses energetic metaphors: Timothy must flee, pursue, fight, and lay hold; the rich lay up a foundation for the future. These images portray faithful life as directed action rather than passive assent.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should readers distinguish pastoral accommodation to an existing master-servant system from moral approval of that system?
- Does “the root of all evil” mean every evil without exception, or is it a forceful description of money-love's broad destructive capacity? The English chapter alone does not settle the lexical question.
- What precisely is “this commandment” in verse 14: the immediately preceding charge, Timothy's whole commission, or a larger body of instruction?
- Who is the grammatical subject of the doxology in verses 15–16, and how does that identification affect its Christology?
- What was committed to Timothy's trust in verse 20, and how much of its content can be reconstructed from this chapter?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the teaching about treasure and masters in [[Matthew 6]], the rich fool in [[Luke 12]], and contentment in [[Hebrews 13]]. [[Job 1]] supplies a close conceptual parallel to bringing nothing into and taking nothing out of mortal life. Timothy's command to fight anticipates the retrospective language of [[2 Timothy 4]]. For the confession before Pilate, compare [[John 18]]. These parallels clarify themes but do not erase the particular pastoral setting or ethical difficulties of 1 Timothy 6.
