# 1 Timothy 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 Timothy 1]] · My notes: [[1 Timothy 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter opens with Paul's greeting and Timothy's assignment to resist speculative and contrary teaching at Ephesus. It evaluates teaching partly by its fruit: [[Charity]], a pure heart, a good conscience, and sincere [[Faith]]. After explaining the lawful use of law, the speaker presents himself as the central example of the chapter's message—Christ saves sinners and displays longsuffering toward them. A doxology follows, and the chapter closes by renewing Timothy's charge and warning that rejecting faith and conscience can end in spiritual shipwreck.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Greeting and commission (vv. 1–4):** Paul addresses Timothy and recalls his charge to remain at Ephesus and oppose other doctrine.

2. **Goal of the charge and misuse of law (vv. 5–11):** Love, conscience, and sincere [[Faith]] are contrasted with empty speculation; the law is called good when used lawfully.

3. **Mercy displayed in the speaker (vv. 12–17):** A former persecutor receives [[Grace]], enters ministry, and becomes a pattern of Christ's longsuffering; the unit closes in praise.

4. **Charge and warning renewed (vv. 18–20):** Timothy is told to hold faith and a good conscience, while Hymenaeus and Alexander illustrate shipwrecked faith and corrective discipline.

The charge language near the beginning and end may frame the intervening discussion as preparation for Timothy's pastoral task, though this could also reflect ordinary repetition in pastoral instruction rather than deliberate literary design.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter tightly connects doctrine and moral formation. Its stated aim is not speculation but [[Charity]] grounded in integrity and sincere faith (vv. 4–7). Law is called good, but only under the condition of lawful use (vv. 8–11), cautioning against both dismissing law and claiming expertise without understanding.

The theological center is the saying that Christ came to save sinners (v. 15). The speaker's own past makes [[Justice and Mercy]] concrete: serious wrongdoing is not minimized, yet abundant grace makes ministry and [[Eternal Life]] possible (vv. 12–16). The text specifically makes this mercy a pattern of Christ's longsuffering, not merely a private experience.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
In the KJV, 'charity' (v.5) denotes love, not merely almsgiving. Paul's phrase 'the end of the commandment' uses 'end' (Greek telos) to mean goal or purpose, most plausibly the aim of Timothy's charge rather than the whole gospel or law.

'This is a faithful saying' (v.15) is a formula repeated across the Pastoral Epistles (compare 1 Timothy 3:1; 4:9; 2 Timothy 2:11; Titus 3:8); it plausibly marks a quoted early confession, though this remains a scholarly inference rather than a certainty.

The chapter contrasts productive teaching with 'fables,' 'endless genealogies,' and 'vain jangling,' without identifying the disputed content precisely. The closing images are forceful metaphors, not literal description: Timothy must 'war a good warfare,' while others have made 'shipwreck' concerning faith.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What criteria does verse 5 provide for evaluating religious teaching beyond whether it sounds learned?

- What distinction does the phrase "use it lawfully" require between the law itself and a teacher's use of it?

- How does the speaker's refusal to minimize his former conduct shape the chapter's account of [[Grace]]?

- What is explicit, and what remains unstated, about delivering Hymenaeus and Alexander unto Satan?

- How should ignorance in verse 13 be related to responsibility, mercy, and unbelief without treating it as a complete excuse?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the warning about teachers at Ephesus with [[Acts 20]], especially Acts 20:17–32. Compare the speaker's account of former persecution and subsequent grace with [[Acts 22]], [[Acts 26]], and [[Galatians 1]]. For recurring warnings about false teaching and fables within the same letter, continue with [[1 Timothy 4]]. These parallels clarify themes but should not be treated as independent proof of authorship.
