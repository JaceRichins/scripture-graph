## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful four-part outline is:

1. **Life with Christ (3:1-4):** resurrection identity, hidden life, and future glory.
2. **Put off the old life (3:5-11):** disordered desire, destructive anger and speech, falsehood, and the old man.
3. **Put on the new life (3:12-17):** mercy, kindness, humility, patience, forgiveness, charity, peace, the word of Christ, worship, and thanksgiving.
4. **Household instructions (3:18-25):** commands to wives and husbands, children and fathers, and servants. The corresponding command to masters occurs in [[Colossians 4]]:1, showing that the literary unit crosses the later chapter boundary.

This outline follows clear transitions in the text but is not the only defensible division.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Present identity and future hope:** believers are described as risen with Christ, while their appearing with him in glory remains future (3:1-4), connecting [[Resurrection]] with the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]].
- **Renewal of the new man:** the old man has been put off, and the new man is being renewed in knowledge after the Creator's image (3:9-10). This plausibly evokes [[Creation]], though the precise Genesis allusion is not explicit.
- **Forgiveness and [[Charity]]:** believers are to forgive as Christ forgave them, and charity is placed above the other virtues as the bond of perfectness (3:13-14).
- **Discipleship within unequal relationships:** marriage, parenting, and servitude are brought under allegiance to the Lord (3:18-25). The text imposes duties on multiple parties but does not explicitly abolish the hierarchy or institution of servitude.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter uses two controlling images. First, **above** and **earth** contrast the believers' heavenly allegiance with sinful patterns associated with the earth (3:1-5). Second, **put off** and **put on** portray moral transformation as a change of clothing (3:8-14). In verses 15-17, peace is to rule, the word of Christ is to dwell richly, and every word or deed is to be done in the Lord's name. Thanksgiving is explicit in verses 15 and 17; verse 16 speaks of singing with grace in the heart, which is thematically compatible but not identical wording.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Colossians 3 has substantial parallels with [[Ephesians 4]], [[Ephesians 5]], and [[Ephesians 6]]: old and new humanity, psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, thanksgiving, and household instructions appear in a comparable sequence. The supplied index verifies direct textual overlap with Ephesians 5; the Ephesians 4 and 6 connections are strong thematic and structural parallels. The cumulative correspondence supports literary affinity or shared instructional tradition but does not establish priority, direct copying, or authorship. Colossians 3:11 also parallels [[Galatians 3]], though Galatians includes male and female while Colossians includes circumcision and uncircumcision, Barbarian, and Scythian.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary And Thematic Parallel
> Colossians 3 and Ephesians contain a concentrated sequence of parallels: putting off the old man and putting on the new, psalms and hymns and spiritual songs, thanksgiving in Christ's name, and instructions to wives, husbands, children, fathers, servants, and masters. The cumulative correspondence strongly supports a literary relationship or shared instructional tradition.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Broad affinity accepted; literary mechanism disputed · [[Literary And Thematic Parallel in Colossians 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary/Theological Comparison Across Epistles *(tentative)*
> Colossians 3:11's unity formula structurally parallels Galatians 3:28 but differs in content: it omits 'male nor female' (present in Galatians) and adds 'Barbarian, Scythian.' This difference is notable because Colossians 3:18-19, just a few verses later, retains a gendered household hierarchy (wives submit, husbands love) rather than dissolving it the way ethnic/status categories are dissolved in v.11.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Textual comparison accepted; implications disputed

> [!evidence] Comparative Social/Historical Context *(tentative)*
> Unlike a purely one-directional household code, vv.18-19 place an ethical demand on the socially dominant party as well as the subordinate one: wives are told to submit, but husbands are commanded to love their wives and specifically 'not [be] bitter against them.' Scholars of Greco-Roman household codes (Haustafeln) have noted that this kind of explicit constraint on the household head, while not unique, goes beyond many surrounding codes that address only the subordinate party's duties.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · Textual reciprocity accepted; historical comparison unsupported
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How does seeking things above reshape conduct on earth rather than encourage withdrawal from earthly responsibilities?
- Why does the text call covetousness idolatry?
- How do the put-off and put-on images describe moral transformation?
- Does the difference between the unity lists in Colossians 3:11 and Galatians 3:28 carry theological significance, or might it reflect their different contexts?
- How should verse 11's treatment of bond and free be read alongside the servant-master relationship retained in 3:22-4:1?
- What does impartial judgment in verse 25 imply, and does its immediate warning address servants specifically or every party in the household unit?
