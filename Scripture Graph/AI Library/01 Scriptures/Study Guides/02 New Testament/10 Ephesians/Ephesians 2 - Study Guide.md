# Ephesians 2 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ephesians 2]] · My notes: [[Ephesians 2 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Ephesians 2 develops two closely related reversals. Verses 1–10 move from death in sin to life with Christ, grounding [[Salvation]] in divine mercy and [[Grace]] received through [[Faith]], while presenting good works as the purpose rather than the boastful basis of the new life. Verses 11–22 move from Gentile alienation to reconciliation: through Christ’s blood and cross, those who were far away are brought near, the divided groups become one body, and former strangers become members of God’s household. The chapter ends with a corporate temple image founded on [[Apostles]] and [[Prophets]], with Jesus Christ as the chief cornerstone.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Former death and present life (2:1–7):** a “time past” dominated by sin is reversed by God’s mercy, love, and action in Christ.

2. **Gift and vocation (2:8–10):** salvation is God’s gift, not grounds for boasting; the newly created life is directed toward good works.

3. **Former distance and present nearness (2:11–18):** Gentile exclusion gives way to peace, one new humanity, reconciliation to God, and shared access to the Father.

4. **Former strangers and present dwelling (2:19–22):** citizenship and household images develop into a building and [[Temples|temple]] image.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds divine initiative and transformed conduct together. God makes alive, raises, saves, brings near, reconciles, and builds; the recipients contribute no grounds for boasting. Yet verse 10 immediately gives the renewed community a vocation of good works. This sequence cautions against treating either grace or discipleship as dispensable.

Reconciliation is simultaneously Godward and communal. The cross reconciles “both unto God” and also ends the enmity separating the two groups. Thus the chapter’s account of the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] includes the formation of a shared people, not only isolated individual rescue.

The closing image is corporate: believers are being built together as God’s habitation through the Spirit. Verse 18 similarly places access to the Father through Christ and by the Spirit, making the passage relevant to study of [[The Godhead]] without by itself resolving later technical formulations about divine nature.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
- **'Quickened' (2:1, 2:5)**: the verb (Greek *zōopoieō*, 'made alive') appears explicitly in 2:5 as *synezōopoiēsen*, 'made alive together with'; in 2:1 English translations supply the same verb by ellipsis from the later clause, so the parallel between the verses is real but translational rather than a repeated Greek verb in both.
- **'Workmanship' (2:10)**: Greek *poiēma*, 'a thing made' (root of English 'poem') — it signals God's authorship of the new creation but shouldn't be over-read as implying artistic craftsmanship specifically.
- **'That' (2:8)**: the demonstrative in 'and that not of yourselves' is grammatically debated — it may refer narrowly to faith or broadly to the whole preceding statement of salvation by grace through faith. The verse secures 'salvation as God's gift' regardless of which reading is preferred.
- **'Ordinances' (2:15)**: Greek *dogmasin* (dative plural of *dogma*), 'decrees/regulations' — the legal-ceremonial requirements of the Law of Moses. This is a different concept from the technical Restoration use of 'ordinances' for saving ordinances (baptism, sacrament, temple rites); the shared English word should not be read as identifying the two. The precise scope of what is abolished (specific boundary-marking regulations vs. the Mosaic law more generally) is debated among interpreters.
- **'Middle wall of partition' (2:14)**: Greek *mesotoichon tou phragmou*, a dividing barrier. Often connected with the physical barrier (the *soreg*) in Herod's Temple separating the Court of the Gentiles from the inner courts, attested by Josephus and by surviving warning inscriptions — but the text does not explicitly name this structure, so the identification remains a plausible historical inference rather than a certainty.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter is woven together by repeated corporate language: believers are made alive, raised, and seated 'together' with Christ (2:5-6); Christ makes 'both' groups 'one' and reconciles them in 'one body' (2:14-16); 'both' have access by 'one Spirit' (2:18); and they are 'builded together' into a habitation of God (2:21-22). This accumulation gives the chapter a coherent movement from shared death-and-life in Christ to a reconciled people and shared divine dwelling, though the repetition may partly reflect the unavoidable vocabulary of describing two groups made one rather than a fully deliberate literary architecture.

Two parallel adversative turns — 'But God, who is rich in mercy...' (2:4) and 'But now in Christ Jesus...' (2:13) — mark reversals within each half of the chapter (2:1-10 and 2:11-22 respectively), pivoting from a hopeless prior state to divine intervention; this parallel is a reasonable literary observation, though calling it 'deliberate' authorial design is an inference rather than a certainty.

The language of being 'far off' made 'nigh' (2:13, 2:17) plausibly echoes Isaiah's promise of 'peace...to him that is far off, and to him that is near' (Isaiah 57:19); the close clustering of 'peace,' 'far,' and 'near' makes wholly independent phrasing less economical, though whether this reflects a deliberate literary allusion versus shared prophetic idiom cannot be settled with certainty. The chapter's closing metaphors escalate in intimacy — fellow citizens, then household members, then constituent stones of the place where God dwells (2:19-22) — culminating in a growing 'holy temple... an habitation of God through the Spirit' (2:21-22).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary Pattern
> Repeated collective terms—“together,” “both,” “one,” and “builded together”—give the chapter a coherent movement from shared life in Christ to a reconciled people and shared divine habitation. This repetition is substantial evidence for deliberate thematic unity within the chapter.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · accurate lexical pattern, modest claim scope · [[Literary Pattern in Ephesians 2|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual Ecclesiology
> Ephesians 2 explicitly assigns apostles and prophets a foundational place in its building metaphor while reserving the decisive cornerstone role for Jesus Christ. This strongly supports the limited claim that these witnesses and Christ are constitutive of the community portrayed here.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · textually secure, model-quality hedging · [[Textual Ecclesiology in Ephesians 2|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Archaeological/Historical Background *(tentative)*
> Herod's Temple in Jerusalem had a real stone barrier (the soreg) separating the Court of the Gentiles from the inner courts, marked by inscriptions threatening death to any Gentile who passed beyond it (attested by Josephus and by two surviving inscribed stones found in 1871 and 1935); some interpreters see this literal wall as background imagery for Paul's 'middle wall of partition,' while others read the phrase as a metaphor for the Mosaic law generally.
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.6 · disputed, citations under-specified

> [!evidence] Theological/Interpretive Dispute *(tentative)*
> Ephesians 2:20 is often paired with Ephesians 4:11-14 (apostles, prophets, evangelists, pastors, and teachers given 'for the perfecting of the saints... till we all come in the unity of the faith') to argue for the ongoing necessity of apostolic and prophetic church leadership; but a widely held alternative reading takes 'foundation of the apostles and prophets' as describing a unique, closed, once-for-all foundational role belonging specifically to the first-century apostles and New Testament prophets.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.6 · genuinely disputed cessationist/continuationist question

> [!evidence] Doctrinal/Textual Comparison *(tentative)*
> Historically, 'by nature the children of wrath' (2:3) has been read by much of Christian theology as support for inherited original sin or total depravity; this sits in tension with the Latter-day Saint doctrine that the Fall's consequences do not include inherited guilt (compare the 'natural man' language of Mosiah 3:19, which locates the problem in an unredeemed disposition rather than transmitted legal guilt).
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How do 'by grace...not of works' (2:8-9) and 'created...unto good works' (2:10) fit together without contradiction? What role do good works play if they do not save?
- What is the antecedent of 'that' in 'and that not of yourselves' (2:8) — faith specifically, or the whole preceding statement of salvation by grace through faith?
- What is the precise scope of 'the law of commandments contained in ordinances' abolished in 2:15 — specific boundary-marking regulations, or the Mosaic law more broadly?
- In what sense, if any, is the 'middle wall of partition' (2:14) still meaningful today — as a literal historical reference, an ongoing metaphor for division, or both?
- Does 'foundation of the apostles and prophets' (2:20) describe a completed, past event or an ongoing pattern of church leadership — and does 'prophets' here mean early Christian prophets, the prophets of the Hebrew Bible, or both?
- How does the corporate temple image of 2:21-22 relate to other New Testament temple/building metaphors, such as [[1 Corinthians 3]] and 1 Peter 2?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
For related material within Ephesians, see [[Ephesians 1]] (shared vocabulary of grace and being 'in Christ') and [[Ephesians 4]] (apostles and prophets as gifted roles for the unity of the body). [[Colossians 2]] offers a close parallel on being made alive together with Christ and the 'ordinances' language; [[Colossians 1]] parallels the reconciliation-through-blood/cross language. For the building/temple imagery of 2:20-22, compare [[1 Corinthians 3]] and 1 Peter 2 (believers as God's temple and living stones on Christ the cornerstone), alongside the canonical topics [[Apostles]], [[Prophets]], and [[Temples]].

For the grace/works question of 2:8-10, compare Romans 3 (boasting excluded from justification) and James 2 (faith and works) — but ask carefully what each author means by 'works' before assuming the passages address an identical question. A popular comparison to 2 Nephi 25:23 ('saved by grace...after all we can do') is genuinely contested: the two passages may not be making an identical claim about the grace/effort relationship. Galatians 3 shares a Jew-Gentile-unity theme with 2:14-18 but argues from a different premise and should be read as a thematic echo rather than a tight parallel.
