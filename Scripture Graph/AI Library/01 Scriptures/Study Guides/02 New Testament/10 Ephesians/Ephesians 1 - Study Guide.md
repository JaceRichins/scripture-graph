# Ephesians 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Ephesians 1]] · My notes: [[Ephesians 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Ephesians 1 moves from greeting (vv. 1–2), to an extended blessing for God's work in Christ (vv. 3–14), to thanksgiving and prayer for the readers (vv. 15–23). Its central themes include [[Grace]], redemption through Christ, adoption, inheritance, revelation, the Spirit's seal, and Christ's resurrection and supremacy. Verse 10 describes God's purpose to gather all things in Christ. The official chapter heading reads this through [[Restoration of the Gospel]] and latter-day fulfillment; the verse itself names the “dispensation of the fulness of times” but does not independently define its date or explicitly say that the gospel will be restored.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Greeting (1:1–2):** Paul identifies himself and blesses the readers with grace and peace.

2. **Blessing and divine purpose (1:3–14):** Election, adoption, redemption, forgiveness, revealed purpose, inheritance, and the Spirit's seal are repeatedly located in relation to Christ. Praise refrains in verses 6, 12, and 14 help organize the unit.

3. **Thanksgiving and prayer (1:15–19):** Paul gives thanks for the readers' faith and love and asks that their understanding be enlightened.

4. **Christ's exaltation and the church (1:20–23):** The prayer culminates in Christ's resurrection, enthronement, supremacy, and headship over the church.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter repeatedly emphasizes divine initiative: God chooses, predestinates, adopts, redeems, reveals, seals, raises, and exalts (vv. 3-23). Election and predestination (vv. 4-5, 11-12) are defined by their stated ends — holiness, adoption, inheritance, and praise — rather than by any systematic account of individual versus corporate election; the chapter does not by itself settle later Calvinist/Arminian-style disputes over [[Agency]] and predestination.

[[Grace]] encompasses more than pardon: acceptance, redemption, and forgiveness of sins are grounded specifically in Christ's blood (v. 7), tying the passage to the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. Verse 4's language of being chosen 'before the foundation of the world' is the same idiom the official chapter heading reads through foreordination in [[Premortal Life]]; the verse's plain sense is corporate/eternal-plan language, so that connection is best held as an LDS theological reading rather than an explicit textual claim.

Sealing by the Spirit and the Spirit as 'earnest' of inheritance (vv. 13-14) present present possession as a pledge toward a still-future redemption, which the chapter heading connects to the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]] as a seal of promise. Paul's prayer for 'the spirit of wisdom and revelation' (v. 17) ties ongoing [[Revelation]] to knowledge of God.

Verse 10's purpose to 'gather together in one all things in Christ' 'in the dispensation of the fulness of times' is what the official chapter heading applies to a latter-day [[Restoration of the Gospel]]; the verse itself does not independently date or describe a nineteenth-century restoration. The chapter closes with Christ's [[Resurrection]] and exaltation 'far above all principality, and power' as head over the church, 'which is his body' (vv. 20-23).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated expressions “in Christ,” “in him,” and “in whom” make union or participation in Christ the passage's dominant relational frame. The benefits listed are not described as independent possessions; they are consistently located in him.

“Earnest” in verse 14 functions as the image of an initial pledge associated with a future inheritance. The surrounding language—“until the redemption”—guards against reading the present seal as the entire completed inheritance.

The move from “we” to “ye also” in verses 12–13 deserves attention. It may distinguish two historical groups, but the chapter does not explicitly label them Jews and Gentiles. That identification should therefore remain an interpretation rather than an observation.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 3-14 form one continuous sentence in Greek, obscured by the KJV's sentence breaks, opening with an extended blessing ('Blessed be the God and Father...') in the same berakah/eulogy pattern found in other New Testament openings (compare 2 Corinthians 1:3; 1 Peter 1:3). Three parallel refrains — 'to the praise of the glory of his grace' (v. 6), 'to the praise of his glory' (v. 12), and 'unto the praise of his glory' (v. 14) — give the passage a loose three-part rhythm often associated with the work of Father, Son, and Spirit; the persons and actions overlap enough that this should be read as a discernible pattern rather than a rigid outline the text itself labels.

The prayer of vv. 15-23 builds to a climax of its own: enlightened understanding leads to knowledge of hope and inheritance, which is illustrated by God's power at work in Christ's resurrection, enthronement, and headship over the church.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Literary Structure
> The related praise refrains in verses 6, 12, and 14, together with the sustained attention to the Father, Christ, and the Spirit, provide substantial internal evidence that verses 3–14 form a deliberately organized unit. The pattern supports literary and theological coherence but does not prove a rigid three-part scheme.

> [!evidence] Textual/Theological *(tentative)*
> Verses 5 and 11 use 'predestinated,' language that in historic Reformed theology supports unconditional election, while Latter-day Saint and Arminian readings interpret the same Greek (proorizo, 'to determine beforehand') as God's foreordained plan operating alongside human agency and foreknowledge rather than deterministic predestination.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What stated purposes accompany choosing and predestination in verses 4–12, and which later theological questions does the passage leave unanswered?

- How does the future-oriented language of inheritance and redemption qualify the present experience of being sealed in verses 13–14?

- Does the shift from “we” to “ye also” identify distinct communities, or can it be explained as a rhetorical turn to the readers?

- What does Paul ask believers to know in verses 17–19, and why does he answer that prayer by recounting Christ's resurrection and exaltation?

- In verse 10, what can be concluded from “gather together in one all things in Christ,” and what conclusions about universal salvation or latter-day chronology would require additional texts?
