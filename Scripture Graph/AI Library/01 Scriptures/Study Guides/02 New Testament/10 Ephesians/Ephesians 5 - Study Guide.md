<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Walk in love (vv. 1–7):** Imitate God through Christlike love; reject sexual impurity, covetousness, and deceptive rationalization.

2. **Walk as children of light (vv. 8–14):** Live out a changed identity through goodness, righteousness, truth, discernment, and exposure of darkness.

3. **Walk wisely (vv. 15–21):** Use time well, understand the Lord's will, seek fullness of the Spirit, worship, give thanks, and submit to one another.

4. **Christlike marriage (vv. 22–33):** The church's relation to Christ frames differentiated instructions to wives and husbands; the creation text about one flesh is interpreted in relation to Christ and the church.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter joins [[Sacrifice]], holiness, and love: Christ gives himself for believers and for the church, and that costly action becomes an ethical pattern (vv. 2, 25). Sanctification is depicted as Christ cleansing and preparing a holy people (vv. 26–27), imagery relevant to [[Justification and Sanctification]] and possibly [[Baptism]].

Its teaching on [[Marriage]] should retain both parts of the text. Wives receive explicit instruction to submit and reverence (vv. 22–24, 33), while husbands receive repeated commands to love through self-giving, bodily care, nourishment, and cherishing (vv. 25, 28–29, 33). Verse 21's mutual submission supplies important context, but the precise relationship between that statement and the differentiated marital commands remains debated.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The repeated verb **walk** supplies the chapter's clearest organizing motif. Each occurrence advances the argument: love gives the pattern, light names the transformed identity, and wisdom governs discernment and time.

Several contrasts reinforce the movement: darkness/light, unfruitful/fruitful, foolish/wise, drunkenness/Spirit-filled life, and corrupt speech/thanksgiving and song. The repetition of Christ's self-giving in verses 2 and 25 binds the general exhortation to love to the marriage instruction.

The one-flesh saying in verse 31 connects the argument with [[Genesis 2]], while verse 32 rereads that marital language through Christ and the church. The text therefore moves in both directions: marriage illustrates the Christ-church relationship, and Christ's treatment of the church supplies the moral standard for marriage.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Literary And Contextual Evidence
> The placement of mutual submission immediately before the instructions to wives and husbands is substantial textual evidence that the household instructions should be read within a wider ethic of deference to others under God.
> — strength 0.7 · confidence 0.9 · accepted placement; disputed scope · [[Literary And Contextual Evidence in Ephesians 5|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Doctrinal Application *(tentative)*
> The 'great mystery' language of v.32, applied to Christ and the church via the marriage text, has historically been used (e.g., in Catholic sacramental theology and in LDS teaching on eternal marriage) as scriptural grounding for viewing marriage as sacred and enduring; however, Paul's immediate argument is a typological illustration about Christ and the church's unity, not an explicit statement about marriage continuing beyond mortality.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.5 · later reception partly established; LDS historical claim unsourced

> [!evidence] Source-Critical *(tentative)*
> Verse 14 is introduced with 'Wherefore he saith' (a formula elsewhere used to introduce a citation), but the words that follow—'Awake thou that sleepest, and arise from the dead, and Christ shall give thee light'—do not match any known Old Testament passage verbatim; the source is disputed, with an early Christian hymn or baptismal exhortation being a common (but unproven) proposal.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.7 · source disputed

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How do the three commands to walk—love, light, and wisdom—interpret one another?

2. Why does the chapter place thanksgiving opposite both corrupt speech (v. 4) and drunkenness (vv. 18–20)?

3. What practical conduct follows from being called light rather than merely being told to seek light?

4. How should verse 21's mutual submission shape the reading of verses 22–33 without erasing the different commands addressed to wives and husbands?

5. In what ways does Christ's self-giving challenge attempts to use marital language as a warrant for domination or neglect?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
