# Ephesians 6 — Study Guide

## Overview

<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Children and fathers (6:1-4):** obedience, honor, restraint, and nurture.
2. **Servants and masters (6:5-9):** sincere service, divine recompense, nonthreatening authority, and impartial judgment.
3. **The whole armor of God (6:10-17):** strength in the Lord, the spiritual character of the conflict, and armor identified with gospel realities.
4. **Prayer and bold witness (6:18-20):** persevering intercession for all saints and for the imprisoned ambassador.
5. **Tychicus and benediction (6:21-24):** news, comfort, peace, love, faith, and grace.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The family instructions join [[Obedience]] with reciprocal responsibility. Children are addressed as moral agents, but fathers are also restrained from provoking them and charged with nurture (6:1-4). In the servant-master section, sincere [[Service]] matters to God regardless of bond or free status, while masters are reminded that heaven's Master is impartial (6:5-9). This establishes shared accountability without explicitly abolishing the social hierarchy being addressed.

The armor passage portrays resistance to evil through [[Truth]], righteousness, the gospel of peace, [[Faith]], [[Salvation]], God's word, and persistent [[Prayer]] (6:10-18). Because the struggle is "not against flesh and blood," the text discourages identifying human beings as the ultimate enemy. The request for bold utterance while in bonds also connects spiritual endurance with [[Missionary Work]] rather than with physical domination (6:19-20).
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The repetition of “whole armour” in verses 11 and 13 and the repeated commands to stand or withstand in verses 11, 13, and 14 organize the passage around steadfast resistance. The text itself maps the equipment to truth, righteousness, preparation associated with the gospel of peace, faith, salvation, and God's word, so speculative symbolism based on incidental details of armor is unnecessary.

[[Isaiah 59]] provides the strongest explicit scriptural parallel: righteousness is a breastplate and salvation a helmet. [[Isaiah 52]] more loosely connects feet, good news, and peace. These correspondences support participation in prophetic divine-warrior imagery but do not establish that Isaiah was the only background; ordinary military experience and other ancient virtue-as-armor traditions may also have contributed. [[D&C 27]] later closely reuses and expands the armor sequence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Ethical/Historical
> Ephesians 6:5-9 regulates conduct within the master-servant relationship (mutual accountability, no threats, no partiality before God) but does not call for the abolition of the institution of slavery itself.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 1.0 · Broadly accepted with ethical implications disputed · [[Ethical/Historical in Ephesians 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Literary Intertextuality
> The paired correspondences of righteousness with a breastplate and salvation with a helmet provide strong textual evidence that Ephesians 6 participates in the prophetic armor imagery of Isaiah 59:17; the association of feet, good news, and peace also recalls Isaiah 52:7.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · Isaiah 59 strong; Isaiah 52 supporting but looser · [[Literary Intertextuality in Ephesians 6|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Philological/Authorship *(tentative)*
> The closing benediction (6:23-24) is phrased in the third person ('Peace be to the brethren... Grace be with all them that love...') rather than the second-person 'grace be with you' typical of Paul's undisputed letters; this is one of several stylistic features critical scholars cite in the ongoing dispute over whether Ephesians was written by Paul himself or a later disciple writing in his name.
> — strength 0.2 · confidence 0.6 · Authorship disputed; proposed diagnostic weakly supported

> [!evidence] Exegetical/Textual *(tentative)*
> 'First commandment with promise' most plausibly means the first commandment (among those governing human relationships, the 'second table') to carry an attached reward, not the first commandment in the Decalogue's overall sequence, since the Sabbath command precedes it in standard enumerations.
> — strength 0.3 · confidence 0.4 · Disputed interpretation
<!-- SG:END evidence -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Does "in the Lord" in verse 1 qualify the scope of obedience, describe its manner, or do both?
2. In what sense is honoring parents "the first commandment with promise," given the form and ordering of the Decalogue?
3. How does the heavenly Master's impartiality challenge masters while leaving the chapter's bond/free distinction in place?
4. What practices follow from treating flesh-and-blood people as not being the ultimate enemy?
5. Is prayer in verse 18 another item of armor, or the continuing activity through which the entire exhortation is lived?
6. Why does the imprisoned ambassador request bold speech rather than first requesting freedom?
<!-- SG:END questions -->
