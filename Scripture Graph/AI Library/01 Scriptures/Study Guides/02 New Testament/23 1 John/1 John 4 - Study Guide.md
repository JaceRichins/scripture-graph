# 1 John 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[1 John 4]] · My notes: [[1 John 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
The chapter has two closely connected movements: testing spiritual claims (verses 1–6) and knowing God through divinely initiated love (verses 7–21). Confession that Jesus Christ came in the flesh is an explicit test, while testimony, the Spirit, and embodied love provide related signs of fidelity and divine indwelling. The text does not formally present these as an exhaustive or mechanically equal checklist.

“God is love” occurs in verses 8 and 16. Its immediate context describes that love through the Father's sending of the Son and through the resulting obligation to love one another, connecting [[Charity]], [[Testimony]], [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], and [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]].
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
One useful reading arrangement is:

1. **Verses 1–6 — Testing spirits:** false prophets, confession of Christ come in the flesh, God versus the world, hearing or refusing the author's group, and truth versus error.
2. **Verses 7–12 — The source and pattern of love:** love is from God; God manifests love by sending the Son; recipients of that love ought to love one another.
3. **Verses 13–16 — Assurance and mutual indwelling:** the Spirit, testimony, confession of the Son, and abiding love appear as related assurances.
4. **Verses 17–21 — Love perfected:** love gives boldness before [[Final Judgment]], casts out tormenting fear, and makes hatred of a brother incompatible with professed love for God.

This outline is textually useful but is not the only possible literary division.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
God's love is prior to human love: “he first loved us” (verse 19). The chapter manifests that love through the Father's sending of the Son to give life, save the world, and be the propitiation for sins (verses 9–10, 14), directly relating the passage to the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]].

Father, Son, and Spirit have distinguishable roles: the Father sends, the Son saves, and the Spirit is given as assurance of mutual indwelling (verses 9–14). This makes the chapter relevant to [[The Godhead]], while leaving later technical questions about divine ontology unstated.

Love for one's brother is not an optional supplement to professed love for God. Verses 20–21 declare that hatred of the visible brother contradicts a claim to love the unseen God. The chapter's direct wording concerns “one another” and “brother”; extending its scope to every social relationship is a further ethical inference.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The chapter repeatedly uses epistemic language: “try,” “know,” “confess,” “seen,” “testify,” and “believed.” This makes love more than sentiment; it participates in the chapter's account of discernment and assurance. The repeated expressions “Hereby know” (verses 2, 6, 13) reinforce that concern, although repetition alone does not prove a formal literary design.

“God is love” should be read in its immediate context. The chapter describes God's love through sending the Son and commands reciprocal love; it does not say that every desire or action called love is therefore divine.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The argument repeatedly uses antitheses: God and the world, truth and error, love and hatred, boldness and fear, confession and denial. Recurring expressions such as “of God,” “in us,” “dwell,” “love,” and “hereby know” help connect the chapter's movements.

There is also a suggestive progression from testing professed spiritual authority in verse 1 to testing the profession “I love God” in verse 20. This supports a thematic relationship between truthful confession and lived allegiance, but it does not by itself establish a formally designed system of tests.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Historical Inference From Polemical Language
> The insistence that Jesus Christ came in the flesh is strong internal evidence that the writer was opposing some denial or inadequate confession of Jesus' embodied coming. The text does not identify the opponents precisely enough to equate them confidently with a fully developed later movement such as second-century Docetism or Gnosticism.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. Is confession that Jesus Christ came in the flesh a complete test, or one prominent test among related signs involving testimony, the Spirit, and love?

2. Who is the “we/us” of verses 6 and 14, and what grounds its claim to be heard? The chapter asserts its authority but does not fully identify the group within these verses.

3. How do the Spirit, testimony, confession, love, and mutual indwelling relate in verses 13–16 without turning them into a mechanical checklist?

4. Does fear in verse 18 refer principally to judgment and punishment, given verse 17, or does the saying also invite broader application?

5. How broadly should “brother” and “one another” be understood? The immediate language concerns communal relationships, while wider application requires further argument.
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[2 John 1]], especially verse 7, for the closely parallel warning about deceivers who deny that Jesus Christ came in the flesh. Read [[1 John 3]] and [[1 John 5]] for neighboring discussions of belief, love, the Spirit, divine birth, indwelling, and overcoming the world. Compare [[John 3]] for God's love manifested in sending the Son, and John 13:34–35 for the command to love one another.
