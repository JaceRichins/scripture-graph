## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Christ as advocate and propitiation** (vv. 1-2): John pairs two images — Christ pleading the believer's cause with the Father, and Christ as the propitiation covering sin, extended explicitly to 'the whole world.' This assurance sits alongside, not against, the stated aim that readers 'sin not': the letter holds together divine provision for sin and a demanding account of discipleship.

**Obedience and conduct as evidence** (vv. 3-6): 'Hereby we do know that we know him, if we keep his commandments' makes obedience central to the test of knowing Christ; a claim without corresponding conduct is called a lie.

**Brotherly love as a test of light** (vv. 9-11): loving or hating one's brother functions as an observable test of abiding in the light, tying [[Charity]] directly to Johannine light/darkness imagery.

**Worldliness named specifically** (vv. 15-17): John supplies a threefold taxonomy — lust of the flesh, lust of the eyes, and [[Pride|pride]] of life — as a lens for what 'is not of the Father.'

**The anointing** (vv. 20, 27): believers are described as having received a teaching anointing from 'the Holy One' that abides in them. Read in its immediate setting, 'ye need not that any man teach you' is best understood as confidence against the seducers just described (v. 26), not a categorical rejection of teaching — the author is himself teaching them and appeals to what they were taught 'from the beginning.'

**Eternal life as the named promise** (v. 25): John explicitly identifies eternal life as 'the promise that he hath promised us.' The chapter closes by connecting doing righteousness with being 'born of him' (v. 29), without fully specifying whether conduct is cause, consequence, or evidence of that spiritual birth.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several repeated terms organize the argument: "know," "abide" or "continue," "from the beginning," "truth" and "lie," and "light" and "darkness." These repetitions connect conduct, doctrine, and perseverance.

"World" should be interpreted contextually. In verse 2, "the whole world" expresses the broad scope of Christ's propitiation. In verses 15–17, "the world" is characterized through disordered desire and pride. Treating the word as having exactly the same referent in both settings would create an avoidable overreading.

"Antichrist" is not used only for a single expected figure: verse 18 also speaks of "many antichrists," and verse 22 applies the term through a doctrinal test. Likewise, "the last time" signals the author's eschatological assessment but supplies no calendar for the coming mentioned in verse 28.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
John repeatedly tests claims against evidence rather than accepting professions at face value: claiming to know Christ is tested by commandment-keeping; claiming to be in the light is tested by love of a brother; claiming truth is tested by confession of the Son. These paired contrasts — truth and lie, light and darkness, love and hatred, abiding and passing away, confidence and shame — give the chapter unusual cohesion and echo the light/darkness imagery found elsewhere in Johannine literature.

Verses 12-14 interrupt this testing with a rhythmic, sixfold address: three addressees ('little children,' 'fathers,' 'young men') are named twice, first in the present tense ('I write') and then in the perfect tense ('I have written'), each paired with a reassuring reason — forgiveness, knowledge of him from the beginning, and victory over the wicked one.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. In what sense can the commandment be both old and new (verses 7–8), and how decisively do verses 9–11 identify it with brotherly love?
2. Does "from the beginning" refer to the beginning of the readers' Christian instruction, to Christ's ministry, or to a broader theological beginning?
3. How should "ye need not that any man teach you" (verse 27) be limited by the immediate warning about seducers and by the fact that the author is teaching the readers?
4. Are the people who departed in verse 19 necessarily identical with every Christ-denier described in verses 22–23, or does the argument combine related tests without supplying a complete historical profile?
5. In verses 27–29, does each occurrence of "him" refer unambiguously to Christ, to the Father, or does the language permit some referential fluidity?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[1 John 1]] alongside verses 1–2 to see how confession, forgiveness, and the command not to sin are held together. John 13:34 is the clearest comparison for the "new commandment," while John 15:4–10 provides a close verbal and conceptual comparison for abiding, love, and commandment keeping. [[1 John 3]] develops several themes continued from this chapter, including spiritual birth, righteous conduct, love, sin, and the appearing of Christ.
