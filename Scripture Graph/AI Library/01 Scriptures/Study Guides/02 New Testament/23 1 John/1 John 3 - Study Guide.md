## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A useful, text-grounded outline is:

1. **God's children now and at Christ's appearance** — identity, future likeness, and purification (vv. 1-3)
2. **Divine birth opposed to sin** — Christ removes sin and destroys the devil's works (vv. 4-10)
3. **Cain and Christ as opposing patterns** — hatred, death, sacrifice, and material compassion (vv. 11-18)
4. **Assurance and abiding** — the heart before God, prayer, the command to believe and love, and the witness of the Spirit (vv. 19-24)

The transition in verse 10 is especially important: failure to do righteousness and failure to love one's brother are placed together, preparing the sustained treatment of love in verses 11-18.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter holds future transformation and present discipleship together. Believers are God's children now, while their future likeness to Christ remains connected with his appearance, seeing him, and the purifying hope described in verses 2-3. These themes support links to [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]], [[Hope]], and [[Justification and Sanctification]], without defining the full extent of future likeness.

Verses 5, 8, and 16 present complementary aspects of Christ's work: he takes away sins, destroys the devil's works, and lays down his life. His self-giving then becomes a pattern for [[Service]] and concrete love (3:16-18).

The statements that one abiding in Christ or born of God does not or cannot sin (3:6,9) are deliberately severe, but their precise scope is disputed. They should be read beside [[1 John 1]], which rejects claims of sinlessness, and [[1 John 2]], which anticipates that a believer may sin and need an advocate. These surrounding passages constrain, but do not completely resolve, the interpretation.

Verse 23 joins belief in Jesus Christ with love for one another under one commandment. Verse 24 likewise joins [[Obedience]], reciprocal abiding, and knowledge by the [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Repeated vocabulary helps carry the argument. **Manifest** language connects Christ's mission to take away sins and destroy the devil's works (1 John 3:5,8) with the disclosure of the two opposed identities in verse 10. **Abide** or remaining language appears in relation to Christ, God's seed, death, eternal life, and reciprocal divine-human indwelling (3:6,9,14-15,24). Forms of **know** connect recognition, moral discernment, and assurance.

The severe wording of verses 6 and 9 should be interpreted within the whole letter. [[1 John 1]] denies that believers may truthfully claim to have no sin, while [[1 John 2]] anticipates that a believer may sin and need an advocate. A settled or characteristic-practice reading is one leading harmonization, but tense alone does not establish it; idealized identity and differing rhetorical targets remain serious alternatives.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter develops several sharp oppositions: children of God and children of the devil (1 John 3:10), love and hatred, life and death, and word or tongue versus deed and truth (3:14-18). Its strongest narrative contrast is Cain and Christ. Cain kills his brother because his works are evil (3:12); hatred retains a murderous orientation (3:15); Christ instead lays down his life for others (3:16). The argument then brings extraordinary self-sacrifice into ordinary material life by asking how divine love can abide in someone who closes compassion against a needy brother (3:17).

The wording of 1 John 3:1-3 has a close English parallel in [[Moroni 7]], especially Moroni 7:48. Both connect divine sonship, Christ's appearance, seeing and becoming like him, hope, and purification. This is a strong textual observation. It does not establish ancient authorship, independence, direction of dependence, or a particular ancient source. At the level of the English texts, dependence on or deliberate echo of King James Bible language is the most economical historical explanation, though the overlap alone does not specify the finer mechanism.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Internal-Textual-Tension *(tentative)*

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How do 1 John 3:6 and 3:9 relate to the acknowledgment of believers' sins in 1 John 1:8-10 and 2:1?
- Why does the author move immediately from laying down one's life to sharing ordinary material goods?
- Does "God is greater than our heart" chiefly reassure an overly condemning conscience, warn that God knows guilt the heart overlooks, or intentionally permit both applications?
- In what sense is love evidence that one has passed from death to life rather than a means of earning life?
- How does verse 23 prevent belief in Christ and love of neighbor from being treated as competing alternatives?
