## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Remembrance (vv. 1–2):** Recall prophetic words and apostolic teaching.
2. **The scoffers' challenge (vv. 3–4):** Apparent continuity is used against the promise of Christ's coming.
3. **The response (vv. 5–10):** Creation, the flood, future judgment, God's relation to time, and divine longsuffering answer the objection.
4. **Ethical implications (vv. 11–14):** Future expectation calls for godliness, diligence, peace, and blamelessness.
5. **Scripture and stability (vv. 15–18):** Paul's writings can be difficult and distorted; informed readers must remain steadfast and grow in grace.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter connects divine longsuffering with [[Repentance]] and [[Salvation]] (vv. 9, 15). Its treatment of the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]] is primarily moral rather than chronological: because the day is unexpected, the operative question is what kind of people believers ought to be (vv. 10–14). The expected new heavens and new earth place righteousness, rather than catastrophe itself, at the center of the promised future (v. 13).

Verse 9 expresses God's unwillingness that any perish, but it does not specify that every person will ultimately repent. Claims either for or against universal salvation require evidence beyond this sentence.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
In the KJV, "holy conversation" in verse 11 refers to holy manner of life or conduct, not merely speech. To "wrest" writings in verse 16 is to twist or distort them. Verse 12's "hasting unto" can raise a genuine interpretive question: the chapter clearly commands expectant conduct, but this wording alone should not bear an elaborate theory about whether human action changes the date of the coming.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The argument moves from the world "of old" (v. 5), through the heavens and earth "which are now" (v. 7), to the promised new heavens and earth (v. 13). Water and fire provide contrasting images of judgment across these stages. Repeated terms—"promise," "day," "look for," and "beloved"—bind the polemical discussion to pastoral exhortation. The close parallel between 2 Peter 3:2–3 and [[Jude 1]] is evident, but the text alone cannot determine which writing used the other or whether both drew on common tradition.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Internal Textual Evidence
> Second Peter 3:15-16 is strong internal evidence that the literary setting represented by the epistle knew a plurality of Pauline letters and accorded them scriptural significance. It is evidence about the status of Paul's writings in that setting, not sufficient proof of a closed New Testament canon or of the epistle's date and authorship.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · accepted observation / disputed interpretation, well-calibrated · [[Internal Textual Evidence in 2 Peter 3|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Theological/Doctrinal *(tentative)*
> Verse 9's language that God is "not willing that any should perish, but that all should come to repentance" is sometimes cited as evidence for a universal divine salvific will that is conditioned on human agency/repentance rather than unconditional election, in tension with strict predestinarian readings of other New Testament texts.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · disputed, appropriately labeled

> [!evidence] Historical-Critical/Authorship *(tentative)*
> 2 Peter 3:16's treatment of a collection of Pauline epistles as authoritative "scripture" is cited by many scholars as evidence that 2 Peter was composed later than Peter's traditional lifetime (i.e., after a body of Paul's letters had circulated and gained scriptural status), while other scholars defend Petrine authorship and an earlier composition on the grounds that some of Paul's letters were already written and known within the apostolic circle before Peter's death (c. AD 64-68).
> — strength 0.5 · confidence 0.5 · disputed; presentation understates critical-scholarship majority view

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does the scoffers' appeal in vv.3-4 to observable continuity challenge the certainty of Christ's coming, its timing, or both?
- Is "one day...as a thousand years" (3:8) a literal ratio, a poetic device for divine timelessness, or something else — and how far should that language be pressed as a chronological formula?
- What does Peter mean by "the elements" (Greek *stoicheia*, v.10) — basic physical matter, heavenly bodies, or spiritual powers, as the term is used variously elsewhere in ancient literature?
- Why does an eschatological objection provoke an answer chiefly about present conduct (vv.11-14) rather than a chronological rebuttal?
- In what sense are believers "hasting unto" the day of God (3:12) — eager anticipation, or something that implies human conduct affects the timing?
- What practices help readers engage difficult scripture, including Paul's letters, without becoming "unlearned" or "unstable" (v.16)?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the time-language of 2 Peter 3:8 with [[Psalm 90]], the thief imagery of verse 10 with [[1 Thessalonians 5]], and the new-creation promise of verse 13 with [[Isaiah 65]] and [[Revelation 21]]. [[Mormon 9]] shares the chapter's cosmic judgment imagery of the elements melting with fervent heat. For the danger of distorting scripture and the need for remembrance, compare [[Jude 1]] and [[2 Peter 1]].
