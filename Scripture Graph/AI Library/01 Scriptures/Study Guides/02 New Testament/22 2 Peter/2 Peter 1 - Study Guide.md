# 2 Peter 1 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[2 Peter 1]] · My notes: [[2 Peter 1 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Peter, writing as he anticipates his own death (2 Peter 1:14), addresses believers who share 'like precious faith' with the apostles (2 Peter 1:1) and urges them toward Christian maturity. The chapter moves through three related concerns: divine gift paired with disciplined growth (vv. 1–11), remembrance and the preservation of his teaching in view of his approaching death (vv. 12–15), and the grounds for trusting the apostolic proclamation about Christ — his own eyewitness testimony of Christ's majesty on the holy mount (vv. 16–18) and the divinely inspired [[Prophecy]] of scripture (vv. 19–21). Believers are called to add virtue upon virtue, from [[Faith]] to [[Charity]] (vv. 5–7), so they may become 'partakers of the divine nature' (v. 4) and 'make [their] calling and election sure' (v. 10).
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Greeting and divine provision (vv. 1–4):** Grace, knowledge, promises, and participation in the divine nature.

2. **Diligent growth and its outcome (vv. 5–11):** A linked sequence from faith to [[Charity]], contrasted with blindness and forgetfulness; diligence is connected with stability and entrance into Christ's kingdom.

3. **Remembrance in view of death (vv. 12–15):** The speaker intends to reinforce known truth and preserve it beyond his death.

4. **Witness and prophetic word (vv. 16–21):** The proclamation rests rhetorically on witnessed majesty, the heavenly voice also reported in [[Matthew 17]], and prophecy produced through the Holy Ghost.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
- **Divine initiative and human response**: God's power supplies what pertains to life and godliness (v. 3) before any virtue is commanded, and believers are then called to add virtue with diligence (vv. 5, 10) — the chapter resists reducing growth to either unaided effort or passive receipt.
- **Partakers of the divine nature** (v. 4): a striking expression, unique in this exact wording in the New Testament, describing real participation in God's nature that is morally framed by escaping worldly corruption. Full-strength claims about what this participation entails metaphysically (theosis, eternal progression) go beyond what this verse alone establishes.
- **Calling and election made sure** (v. 10): a phrase unique to this verse in the New Testament, tied in its own context to sustained diligence ('if ye do these things, ye shall never fall,' vv. 10–11); later doctrinal elaborations of the phrase go beyond what is explicit here.
- **Prophetic inspiration** (vv. 20–21): scripture originates not in human will but as 'holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost' — a foundational statement on the nature of [[Revelation]] and [[Prophets]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated word **knowledge** links the greeting, divine provision, moral growth, and fruitfulness (vv. 2, 3, 5, 8). In context, knowledge cannot easily be reduced to information: it is associated with grace, godliness, cultivated virtue, and fruitfulness.

The **tabernacle** imagery in verses 13–14 treats the body or mortal life as a temporary dwelling that the speaker expects to put off. Verse 19's images of light, dawn, and the day star communicate increasing illumination, but the precise referent of the day star is not explicitly identified in this chapter.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Verses 5–7 form a *sorites* (a linked chain in which each named quality connects to the last), giving the passage cumulative rhetorical movement; a similar linked-chain technique appears in Romans 5:3–4, though this likely reflects a shared paraenetic style rather than demonstrated dependence. The chain's rhetorical form does not by itself prove a fixed developmental sequence — it may equally describe mutually reinforcing qualities. Verses 12–15 repeat forms of 'remembrance,' reflecting Peter's concern that his teaching outlast his death. Verses 16–21 then place two grounds for trust side by side: witnessed majesty (vv. 16–18) and the [[Prophecy|prophetic word]] (vv. 19–21), presented as complementary rather than competing.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Authorship / Historical-Critical Scholarship *(tentative)*
> Peter's internal claim to eyewitness authority (2 Peter 1:16-18) sits inside a long-running scholarly dispute over the letter's authorship: mainstream critical scholarship widely regards 2 Peter as the most disputed authorship case in the New Testament, often dated after Peter's death, based on Greek style differing from 1 Peter, apparent literary dependence on Jude, and reflection on a 'delay of the parousia' in chapter 3; traditional and many believing scholars maintain Petrine authorship, appealing partly to these very eyewitness claims.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
1. How do divine provision in verses 3–4 and human diligence in verses 5 and 10 qualify one another?

2. Does the sequence in verses 5–7 describe fixed stages, mutually reinforcing virtues, or primarily a rhetorical progression?

3. What exactly is made "more sure" in verse 19: prophecy itself, the readers' confidence in it, or the apostolic proclamation?

4. Does the explanation beginning with "For" in verse 21 indicate that verse 20 is chiefly about prophecy's origin rather than every reader's interpretation?

5. What safeguards are needed when moving from the chapter's internal eyewitness claim to larger historical conclusions?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare 2 Peter 1:16–18 with [[Matthew 17]] and the divine declaration recorded in [[3 Nephi 11]], noting both shared wording and distinctive details. For a thematic study, trace [[Faith]], [[Charity]], and [[Endurance]] through the virtue chain in verses 5–11, and compare how verses 19–21 describe [[Prophecy]] with the broader question of how [[Records and Sacred Writings]] originate. John 21 offers a plausible, though not certain, background for Peter's expectation of death referenced in verse 14.
