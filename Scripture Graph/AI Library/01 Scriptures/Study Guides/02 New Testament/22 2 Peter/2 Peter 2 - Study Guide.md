## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **False teachers and their effects (vv. 1–3):** hidden destructive teaching, followers, public damage to truth, and exploitation.

2. **Precedents of judgment and deliverance (vv. 4–9):** sinful angels, Noah and the flood, and Sodom, Gomorrha, and Lot lead to the conclusion in verse 9.

3. **Character and conduct of the corrupt teachers (vv. 10–19):** arrogance, appetite, greed, deceptive fellowship, Balaam's way, empty promises, and bondage to corruption.

4. **The danger of returning to corruption (vv. 20–22):** renewed entanglement is described as worse than the former condition and illustrated by two degrading proverbs.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**Agency and accountability.** The false teachers' judgment is repeatedly tied to deliberate choice — they are "selfwilled" (v. 10) and have "forsaken the right way" (v. 15) — connecting [[Agency]] directly to consequence.

**Judgment and deliverance together.** The chapter joins justice and mercy without treating them as opposites: the same argument that judges the ungodly also preserves Noah and Lot (vv. 5-9). This should not be overread as a promise that the faithful escape all adversity — Lot himself is described as deeply vexed by the evil around him (v. 8) even while being delivered from its destruction.

**Apostasy after real knowledge.** Verses 20-21 describe people who had escaped the world's pollutions "through the knowledge of the Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ" and became entangled again, ending up worse than before. This raises, without fully resolving, hard questions about accountability for received light — bearing on [[Apostasy]] and [[Repentance and Forgiveness]] — but the text does not by itself settle later theological debates about whether such persons were ever fully converted.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
Several KJV expressions can mislead modern readers. In verse 7, “conversation” refers to conduct or manner of life, not merely speech. “Make merchandise of you” in verse 3 depicts exploitation. “Noah the eighth person” in verse 5 is most naturally read as Noah being preserved with seven others, not as a chronological claim that Noah was the eighth human being. The precise referents of “government” and “dignities” in verses 10–11 are less transparent and should not be confidently identified from the English wording alone.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
**Relationship to Jude.** 2 Peter 2 shares a dense cluster of parallel material with [[Jude 1]] — sinning angels 'reserved' in darkness (compare 2 Peter 2:4 with Jude 1:6), the overthrow of Sodom and Gomorrha (Jude 1:7), contempt for authority (Jude 1:8), and the Balaam episode. Scholars are divided on the direction of dependence — whether 2 Peter used Jude, Jude used 2 Peter, or both drew on shared tradition — and the comparison alone cannot settle it. (Jude does not include the Noah/flood example that 2 Peter adds.)

**Proverbial closing (v. 22).** The chapter ends with two proverbial images: the dog returning to its vomit, which quotes Proverbs 26:11 nearly verbatim, and the washed sow returning to the mire, which has no identified Old Testament source.

**Vivid, cumulative denunciation.** Verses 12-19 stack metaphor upon metaphor — brute beasts, waterless wells, storm-driven clouds, spots and blemishes — intensifying the polemic against the false teachers rather than arguing point by point.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> [!evidence] Internal Thematic Evidence

> [!evidence] Textual/Literary-Critical *(tentative)*
> The close verbal and structural overlap between 2 Peter 2 and Jude 1:4-16 (same sequence of angels, flood/Sodom, Balaam, and similar denunciatory vocabulary) is widely treated by biblical scholars as evidence of a direct literary relationship between the two texts.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.6 · disputed

> [!evidence] Textual/Contextual *(tentative)*
> Reading verse 4's "angels that sinned" as the Enochic Watcher tradition (rather than a pre-mortal war in heaven) is supported by the verse's immediate literary context (flood judgment in v. 5) and by the same association in the parallel passage Jude 1:6, but this does not settle which reading the author of 2 Peter himself intended, since he does not name a source.
> — strength 0.4 · confidence 0.5 · weakly supported

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Which accusations in the chapter concern teaching, and which concern conduct or motive?

- How does verse 9 interpret the examples in verses 4–8?

- Does “they” in verses 20–21 refer to the false teachers, their converts, or both? What in the immediate context supports each possibility?

- How does verse 19 redefine a promise of [[Freedom and Liberty]] as bondage?

- What conclusions about repentance or final salvation would go beyond what verses 20–22 explicitly say?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare the flood and Lot narratives referenced in verses 5-8 with [[Genesis 6]] and [[Genesis 19]]. For the Balaam episode referenced in verses 15-16, see [[Numbers 22]]. For the proverb quoted in verse 22, see Proverbs 26:11. For the closely parallel material on false teachers, see [[Jude 1]] — a side-by-side reading shows extensive overlap while leaving the question of literary priority open.
