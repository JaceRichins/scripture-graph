## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
- **1:1** — Salutation to 'the twelve tribes...scattered abroad'
- **1:2-4** — Trials produce patience; patience produces completeness
- **1:5-8** — Ask God for wisdom in undoubting faith
- **1:9-11** — Reversal: the poor exalted, the rich made low (grass/flower image)
- **1:12** — Blessing on the one who endures temptation (a hinge verse bridging trial and temptation)
- **1:13-15** — Temptation does not come from God; it arises from one's own lust and issues in sin, then death
- **1:16-18** — Every good gift comes from the unchanging 'Father of lights'; believers begotten by 'the word of truth'
- **1:19-21** — Swift to hear, slow to speak and to wrath; receive the 'engrafted word'
- **1:22-25** — The mirror analogy: hearers versus doers of the word
- **1:26-27** — Bridled tongue and care for the fatherless and widows define 'pure religion'

This division is a reasonable reading aid, not an outline the chapter itself announces.

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
James portrays God as a generous and unwavering giver, not the author of enticement to evil (vv. 5, 13, 17). Human desire can develop into [[Sin]] and death (vv. 14–15), while God's word is described as able to save (v. 21). The chapter does not treat faithful hearing as sufficient by itself: the hearer must continue and act (vv. 22–25). Verse 27 makes care for afflicted fatherless children and widows inseparable from the chapter's account of pure religion.

Within Latter-day Saint reception, James 1:5 is directly connected with the [[First Vision]] because [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] identifies it as the passage that moved Joseph Smith to ask God. That explicit literary connection should be distinguished from broader historical or theological conclusions, which the intertext alone cannot prove.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The KJV uses 'temptation' in two senses across the chapter: testing that can be endured (vv. 2, 12) and enticement rooted in one's own lust (vv. 13–15) — the same English word covering both. The word 'perfect' also recurs, describing patience's completed work (v. 4), the gifts that come from above (v. 17), and the law of liberty (v. 25); the repetition creates thematic coherence without implying the three phrases mean exactly the same thing. 'Glass' in 1:23 refers to a polished metal mirror, the ordinary mirror of the ancient world, which gave a dimmer, less exact reflection than a modern glass mirror. 'Engrafted word' (1:21) renders a Greek term meaning 'implanted,' picturing the word as something planted within a person rather than merely heard. 'Divers temptations' (1:2) uses 'divers' in its older sense of 'various' or 'of many kinds.'
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
James uses compact images to expose unstable or ineffective discipleship: the wavering petitioner resembles a wind-tossed wave (v. 6), wealth fades like a scorched flower (vv. 10–11), and the inactive hearer resembles someone who immediately forgets his reflected appearance (vv. 23–24).

A particularly strong contrast appears in the chapter's birth imagery. Lust conceives and brings forth sin, which brings forth death (v. 15); God, by contrast, begets a people through the word of truth as firstfruits (v. 18). Later commands to receive and do that word develop the life-giving side of this contrast (vv. 21–25).
<!-- SG:END literary -->

> Authorship of James is traditionally attributed to James the brother of Jesus, leader of the Jerusalem church (identified in the salutation only as 'James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ'), but the epistle's polished, idiomatic Greek and its Hellenistic wisdom-literature style lead a significant portion of biblical scholarship to regard direct authorship by a Galilean brother of Jesus as uncertain, proposing instead a scribe/amanuensis, later compilation of his teaching, or pseudonymous composition under his authority.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What distinguishes 'asking in faith, nothing wavering' (1:6) from being 'double minded' (1:8) — ordinary uncertainty, or divided allegiance to God?
- In what sense is a trial itself 'joy' (1:2), versus the joy belonging to what enduring it eventually produces?
- How should 1:13's denial that God tempts anyone shape the way a reader talks about the source of personal struggles?
- What is the 'perfect law of liberty' (1:25) — the gospel generally, Jesus' teaching, or something else? The chapter does not define it.
- Is 1:27's pairing of caring for the fatherless and widows with remaining unspotted from the world meant as an exhaustive definition of pure religion, or a concentrated example of its fruits?
