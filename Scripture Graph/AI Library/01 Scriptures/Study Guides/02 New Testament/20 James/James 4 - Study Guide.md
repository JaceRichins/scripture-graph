# James 4 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[James 4]] · My notes: [[James 4 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
> [!info] Chapter heading (official)
> Wars are born of lusts—The friends of the world are the enemies of God—Sin is failure to walk in the light we have received.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Conflict diagnosed (4:1-5):** desires generate fighting, distort prayer, and divide allegiance between God and the world.
2. **Grace and repentance (4:6-10):** the promise of greater grace leads to a sequence of commands and reciprocal promises.
3. **Judging a brother (4:11-12):** speaking evil places a person in the posture of judging the law, although there is one lawgiver.
4. **Boasting about tomorrow (4:13-16):** detailed commercial plans are exposed as presumptuous because life is brief and contingent on the Lord's will.
5. **Known good omitted (4:17):** responsibility includes acting on moral knowledge.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
**[[Grace]], [[Humility]], and [[Repentance]] (vv. 6-10):** God gives grace to the humble, and James answers that promise with commands to submit, resist the devil, draw near, cleanse, purify, mourn, and humble oneself. The passage joins dependence on God with active repentance.

**Motive in [[Prayer]] (vv. 2-3):** James distinguishes failure to ask from asking for self-indulgent purposes. This identifies one reason these readers do not receive; it is not an exhaustive explanation of every unanswered prayer.

**Submission and resistance (v. 7):** Resistance to [[Satan and Temptation]] is placed within prior submission to God rather than presented as autonomous self-mastery.

**Culpable omission (v. 17):** Knowing a good action and failing to perform it is identified as [[Sin]].
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The canonical English text repeatedly uses desire language—“lusts,” “lust,” and “lusteth”—across verses 1-5, but the occurrences should not automatically be treated as one technical term. The rhetoric nevertheless creates a sustained picture of desire turned inward: it wars within people, fuels conflict, shapes requests, and competes with loyalty to God.

“Friendship of the world” in verse 4 is best read in context as allegiance to a value system opposed to God, not as a declaration that creation, ordinary human relationships, or all participation in society is evil. “If the Lord will” in verse 15 expresses dependence rather than functioning merely as a verbal formula.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
James uses several reversals and contrasts: frustrated desire does not obtain what it seeks; friendship with the world produces enmity with God; God resists the proud but gives grace to the humble; and those who humble themselves are promised that the Lord will lift them up. These patterns reinforce the chapter's opposition to proud self-assertion, though they need not constitute a formal literary design.

The transition from [[James 3]] to [[James 4]] is especially important. Envy and strife in James 3:14-16 lead directly into the question about wars and fightings in James 4:1, revealing continuity across the chapter boundary. The warning about future commerce also invites a thematic comparison with [[Luke 12]], where confident economic plans are interrupted by mortality; this does not establish direct dependence.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Explicit Scriptural Quotation And Verbal Parallel
> James 4:6 introduces the saying about God opposing the proud and giving grace to the humble as scriptural authority. It corresponds to Proverbs 3:34 and is repeated in nearly the same form in 1 Peter 5:5, providing strong evidence for a shared early Christian use of this wisdom saying.
> — strength 0.9 · confidence 1.0 · broadly accepted · [[Explicit Scriptural Quotation And Verbal Parallel in James 4|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual And Intertextual Ambiguity
> The words introduced in James 4:5 as what scripture says do not reproduce any readily identifiable Old Testament verse. The sentence's subject and sense are also debated, making detailed doctrine built solely on this verse unusually uncertain.
> — strength 0.8 · confidence 0.9 · obscurity broadly recognized; interpretations disputed · [[Textual And Intertextual Ambiguity in James 4|full dossier]]

> [!evidence] Textual/Translation History *(tentative)*
> The wording of James 4:6 is commonly explained in NT scholarship as following the Greek Septuagint rendering of Proverbs 3:34 rather than the Hebrew-based wording preserved in the KJV Old Testament translation, consistent with the broader pattern of NT writers quoting the LXX. This is a claim about textual/translation history, not about the underlying Hebrew text's meaning.
> — strength 0.6 · confidence 0.7 · standard explanation but unsupported here by a cited source

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- Does “ye kill” in verse 2 describe actual violence, severe communal hostility, or deliberate rhetorical intensification? The chapter alone does not decide the question.
- In verse 5, is the spirit human, the divinely given human spirit, or the Holy Spirit? How does each reading affect the meaning of “lusteth to envy”?
- Does “speak not evil” prohibit all unfavorable moral evaluation, or more specifically slanderous and condemnatory speech that assumes God's judicial place?
- How can planning remain diligent while honestly acknowledging the uncertainty described in verses 13-16?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[James 1]] alongside verses 2-3 and 8 for the letter's treatment of asking and double-mindedness. Read [[James 3]] with verses 1-2 to follow the movement from envy and strife to open conflict. Compare the humility saying with [[Proverbs 3]] and [[1 Peter 5]], and compare the warning to merchants with [[Proverbs 27]] and [[Luke 12]]. These parallels illuminate shared themes without by themselves proving direct literary dependence.
