# Richard Richins, The Seed from Which the Richins Saints grew

_A story of [[Richard Richins (LLQX-X98)|Richard Richins]]._

## The story as it was written

Richard Richins was born on March 26, 1800. He was married at the age of 17 to his wife Priscilla, in the year 1817, and had 11 children. He lived in England his whole life, and died at the age of 48 in 1848. Less than half of these children lived to marry, most of them dying as infants, and the others dying from tuberculosis. The two married young, but were only able to share 25 years together. They both died young, Priscilla being only 42 when she died of childbirth complications, and Richard at 48. The poor man began to develop a serious drinking problem, perhaps because of his wife’s death. It could also have been his father’s death from gangrene, after which he went to live with his mother, either seeking comfort, or wanting to help his mother.
His first daughter was Hannah Richins. When she was 18, she fell victim to the outbreak of tuberculosis in England. They didn’t know how to treat or prevent it at the time, since it was the 1800s and medicinal knowledge was very limited. She died at the age of 18 in 1838, for which her father was present.
His son John and Daughter Mary Ann were both unfortunately very short lived. John was named after Richard’s great-grandfather, but only had the name for 2 months before he passed away. His daughter Mary Ann was able to stay with them for 16 months, during which her parents became enamored with her. Her funeral was a very tear-filled one.
After their previous heartbreaks, Richard persisted, and had his fourth child. A son named George. He was born in the winter of 1825, and made it through his infancy without trouble. Much like me, he had his first job before he was fifteen years old. During this, he lived with his relatives Jeremiah and Esther Wager, and their children Jeremiah Jr. and Mary. According to the Richins Surname Organization, which apparently exists, he was married around 1847 to Ann Pulum. He had two daughters, Hannah and Mary Ann, presumably named after his late sisters. He is listed as a laborer in England, and immigrated to Canada after he had at least 3 children in England. Immigration back then was akin to death, because George would never be able to see his family again. However, the move was likely due to the 1850-1860 depression in an attempt to find work.
Funny enough, Thomas Richins, my next direct ancestor, was a twin as the 5th child. Much like Andrew and Olivia! However, unlike Olivia, his twin sister Charlotte Priscilla assumedly died at birth.
The next child of Richard and Priscilla was named Charles in 1828. There aren’t a ton of records to suggest he was ever christened, mainly because the reverend was sick back then. Charles was literate, and living at home in 1841 at 12. When he was 13, his mother Priscilla tragically died. Whether or not he continued to live with his Father thereafter is unclear. He joined the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints on December 26, 1849, making him the first of my ancestors to be a member. He was also the first of my ancestors to move to Utah, for whatever reason. He was part of the scouts with Brigham Young to colonize Heneferville on the Weber River, and later to do the same in Mexico.
Charles’ younger brother, Edwin, unfortunately died at 10 months. Apparently, Edwin’s birth was when Richard Richins was last listed as a Hallier, but it may have also been a problem with drinking, because at the christening of his next child, he was listed as a laborer.
His younger Brothers, John and Edward, lived with the same family friend as George Richins after Priscilla’s death. John’s wife was a member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, who encouraged her husband to be baptized in 1854. They left for America in 1856, and lived in Utah for the remainder of their lives.
As for Edward Richins, he was baptized in 1853 at the age of 19. He gathered with the rest of the Saints in Utah in 1855.
The last child to be born before Priscilla’s death was William Richins in 1840. The poor boy unfortunately died of tuberculosis at nine years old. It’s likely that he lived with his uncle Joseph after his mother died, since the person listed as present at his death is one J. Richins, and the other two J. Richins’ were too young to have a home at the time of William’s death.
It is thought that Richard was a mischievous and headstrong young man, meeting many of his trials head on without much backing him up. It may have been seen as foolish and immature back then, but that was simply his teenage years. After his marriage, he grew into a kind young man. The sort of person who would have been a very fun grandfather in today’s world. Even though he developed a bad drinking habit, he never once became angry or abusive, willing to stay by his daughter, Hannah’s side as she took her final breath, and by his wife Priscilla’s side as she torturously spasmed. The sorrow he felt as his family was torn apart by death was, in his mind, most easily battled with alcohol.
