# Goshen Centennial History by Louie S. Steele Jensen Part I

_A story of [[Søren Christensen (KWJ5-DCW)|Søren Christensen]]._

## The story as it was written

Goshen Centennial History
1857-1957
I was born in Goshen in what was called the bend. My maternal grandparents Hugh and Julia McKee were among the first settlers in the Old Fort, my mother was born in Goshen. My father came to Goshen with his parents, Samuel and Nancy Steele in May 1862.
Our neighbors in the bend were from these early-day pioneers: Mikkel Powelson, Hannah Burraston, Chesta Sorensen, William Baxter and his wife, James Gardner Sr. and William Page Sr., were frequent visitors. They would gather at our home for an evening and talk about the early day problems. Indians and mountain lions were their main subject. Oh yes and the Ghosts, there were so many Ghosts in their stories, as children, we were afraid to go outside at night or even when it started to get dark, if we tried to venture out one of them would yell look out perhaps an Indian of a lion will grab you, or you might even see a ghost. As we grew older we knew most of these people lived with superstition.
They would tell much of the early-day happenings. I was much interested in these stories. When I asked then why don’t you write them down so you can remember them, they would say, we know them we lived them we won’t forget them, we don’t need them. Some of the early day people had left, whenever you asked about them, all they would say was. Oh, they moved away we don’t remember what they did.
Many happenings I learned from father and mother and my grandparents. When I was fourteen I was given a project in Mutual to find some of the first things of early days. This started me gathering these happenings. I wrote them down so not to forget them. Through the years I have kept this information, and added to it as time went by. It was hard to get a list of all the early day people, for as they would say they moved away. But this is what these early day pioneers told me.
In May 1857, a little band of pioneers looking for a stream of water came into the Goshen Valley, about one mile north of the present town site. Here they found two streams of water, that seemed good enough for them. They made camp, while some started to dig dugouts, for these would be their homes. Others explored the valley. On the west side along the creeks and washes they found a little band of Indians. They had dubouts dug into the banks. Their leader said his name was Joe: that they had been here a long time, and they were of the Goshute tribe. He could talk fairly good English. They told him they wanted to build their homes on the east side of the valley, he said that would be alright, for they were peaceable Indians and would cause no trouble.
Word was sent to Brigham Young of their findings and of their desire to make them a home. They would like to call their new home Goshen for we had found Goshute Indians here and they were peaceful. Brigham Young sent Phineas Cook down to colonize them and be presiding Elder over the branch.
These are the names of the first settlers of the Fort as far as these people could remember. Thomas Job, who settled just below the fort didn’t want to live with the Mormons, he was a Josephine Bill Teeples, Bill Larkin, Robert Till, George Eakins, John Finch, Hugh McKee, Mikkel Powelson, Hannah Powelson, Mr. Andreason, Chris Nelson, Paul Gourley, Mr. Jeffs, Zeak Oleman, Mr. Johnson, Soren Sorensen, their wives and children, Phineas Cook, his wife and four children, James Gardner, Hans Powelson, Alex Gourley, Robert Gourley, Tom Morgan, with their sisters and little brother.
When Phineas Cook arrived they had their dugouts completed, they soon found they were right in the middle of Indian territory, the Indians coming from the south to hunt, they didn’t like white people using their ground, then the Indians from the south down in Sanpete would cross Goshen going to Tooele to make war on the Indian tribe there. Goshen was now on the cross roads.
Phineas Cook had the people build a fort, to protect their cattle and families. This fort was made of rocks, posts and mortor. A watch tower was built high on the wall and a guard put in it night and day, he would call out every hour of the night, but if he saw Indians he would call Indians, and every one was awake getting their families inside the fort, also their livestock. The pigs and chickens could run in and out of the fort at all times so they didn’t have to worry about them. The large gate was closed, sometimes they had quite a battle before the Indians would leave, sometimes they had to stay in the fort for four or five days, some cattle that they wouldn’t get into the fort before they had to close the door the Indians would drive off. This was a great loss to them, for perhaps it was their milch cow on which they depended for their only source of food many times.
That first winter was a hard one. It was very cold, they never had much bedding to keep them warm, not much to eat. Then having no come of their dugouts at any hour of night or day, they really suffered with the cold. Very little was grown that first summer, brush had to be grubbed, ditches made. A one leg room was built inside the fort for Phineas Cook to live in. Some of the settlers went to Spanish Fork, for the winter coming back in the spring.
During the summer of 1857 they found out the water wasn’t so good to drink so along with their other work they dug a long pit, then when winter came they cut ice from the creek, put a layer of ice a layer of grass and rushes and then another and another until the pit was filled, then a top was put on of posts, poles, willows and dirt. This kept the ice so the next summer they had ice water to drink also ice to keep the milk and butter. Also during the summer of 1858 Bishop Cook had the women and children gather cattails and make pillows of them. Then they gathered cane tops and made ticks of them, then that winter they had better beds.
In that first year they didn’t forget to prepare for Independence Day. A bowery was built, some benches made by sawing logs in half, putting pegs in the underside for legs. Then a piece of ground was leveled and scraped clean. Then the task of keeping it hard and smooth was given to the girls. Rabbit Brush was used for brooms. The girls would first sweep it, then pour wate on it. This was carried on for a number of days until finally it was hard and smooth. Then some of the boys went to the canyon west of the town and cut pinon pine limbs, these were stuck into the ground at different places around this dance floor. At night they would light these sticks and as they burned they gave off a good light. When the fourth of July arrived they were prepared. First a program of singing, readings, toasts and sentiments were given. Then when they went to dance some had shoes, some didn’t. The shoes scratched the floor and cut the feet to the bare foot ones. It was decided everyone had to dance bare footed. Mr. Sorensen played the violin for these dances.
Some corn was grown this first year beside some garden vegetables. When the pioneers left Nauvoo Brigham Young advised everyone to take their coffee mills with them. How foolish this must have seemed to them to haul a coffee mill weighing from 2 to 6 pounds when they could have taken that much more food, besides there was no coffee where they were going. But they never questioned their leader but listened to all he said.
Now this first year in Goshen their confidence was paying off, they would grind this corn in this coffee mill to make it into meal, how thoughtful of their leader to have foreseen this, truly he must have been inspired of the Lord. To make a change from just corn meal, they would first parch some corn then grind it and pour milk on it to eat.
During the second year (1858) quite a bit of wheat was grown. Hugh McKee brought a little thresher from Spanish Fork to thresh this wheat. It was a box affair with a dash in the middle, a sweap project out on which they hitched a horse, he went around in a circle pulling this dasher around the wheat put in the top, it was ground up by this churning process. Then wheat and chaff came out of the bottom. This was placed on a canvas and shook until the shaff came to the top and was lifted off or blown away.
When the threshing was over it was decided to take a chance of getting it to mill in Manti. The next thing was to decide who would undertake this job, for everyone knew there was a chance they would be killed by Indians that roamed over the Sanpete area, they were robbers and killers. Then the bishop said every man and boy would be given a number, they would be put in a box and the number that was marked to go, when they drew numbers, would be the one. Jim Gardner, a young man drew the number, he was the poorest teamster of any of them, but he was quite a wit so they considered him alright. This is the story of the trip as told to me by Jim Gardner himself.
The wheat was loaded into a wagon. I was given one of the best teams and I was on my way. I was really afraid for I didn’t know too much about horses or wagons but I had been called and it was my duty to go. The road if it could be called a road across the bottoms southeast and over the hill into York then on down the line. I made the trip down alright, it was slow going, then had to wait a few days for my turn to get my grist. Then I started for home with my flour feeling fine for a trip well done. After the first day and night I see I was being followed by some Indians. I just kept on my way as if I knew nothing about them. Then I made camp at night. I unhitched my team, watered them from a little stream, then hobbled then so they could pick something to eat. Then I got a bucket of water, then some branches and made a fire and prepared my supper.
All the time I could see Indians watching me from the bush. After I had eaten I gathered more and more brush and as it began to get dark I made a big fire and lay down, soon the Indians left and I knew I was safe until morning. Soon as they were a safe distance I caught my horses, hitched them to the wagon, then I put more brush on the fire and started for home. I traveled all night. The story as I found out later was the Indians came upon a man camped same as I had been but closer to Manti, they killed him, took his horse and flour and set fire to his outfit. Just my calm thinking had saved my life. It had been nearly three weeks since I had left with the wheat. The people had given up hope that I had got through. They seemed to think I was dead. Also there would be no bread that winter.
For some Indians had brought news of the killing of a man with a load of flour, so there was much sorrow in the fort. One evening just as the sun was going down a dust was seen across the valley, everyone was out to see who it could be. Imagine the joy when it got close enough to see it was Jim and the flour safe and sound.
During the summer of 1858 a man named Friel came to the fort. He made so much trouble they ran him out of the town boundary. So he set up a distillery just outside of the town, here the Indians came to get whisky. Getting so drunk, even some of the men of the town started drinking, so began a reign of terror. A double watch had to be put on the wall of the fort, night and day. Ground cherries grew abundantly all over the fields, these were gathered and dried. The people grew a little pink beet, this was boiled down until it formed a syrup. This was used to sweeten their food and to sweeten the ground cherries when they stewed them in the winter. Sugar was scarce and sold for a dollar a pound. But the dollar was more scarce than the sugar.
During the summer and winter they had made friends with some of Joe’s tribe, these Indians learned the settlers how to talk indian also how to trap for some of the game that was around, that was a big help to the people. Among these friendly Indians, besides the leader Joe, was Queant Ponywatts, Jack Mooneye, these were lasting friends as long as they lived.
Some very mean Indians were Sour Dick and Chief Showian. They were always stealing and trying to kill. In a battle old Chief Showian was killed just about where the beet dump now stands, he was taken up the creek by the old mill and there buried in a ravine. This was on Current Creek.
The men and boys had to go to the hills to get posts and poles, leaving women and children with only one or two men to keep a look out. Many a time they had an exciting chase getting the cattle inside the fort and keeping the Indians out. There was one friendly Indian lived right with them his name was Jock. It seems while some of these people were living at Spanish Fork. They had found this Indian very ill they had taken him home and cared for him until he was well again. He had followed them here and here he lived with them helping to care for the children and the cattle. One day as he and some of the children were herding the cattle south of town in the tall brush.
They saw some Indians on horses watching the cattle. Jock told the children to run home and get some of the men to come help get the cattle back before the Indians could drive them off. He told the children to hurry and he would try to keep the Indians off until help came. The children hurried away and Jock just went along in the brush until he came to the Indians. They asked him what he was doing there, he said, just walking around. Then he asked them what they were doing, they said they were waiting for the cattle to get a little closer to them then they would run them off. Joe offered to scare the cattle for them so they waited for him to get around them. But instead of Jock going around them he started them for home fast as he could make them go. They made a big dust and the Indians waiting thought they were coming toward them so they never bothered to see. Soon Jock met the men coming from the Fort to get the cattle, they scared the Indians off. They were very grateful to Jock for his help but he said you helped me when I was sick, now I help you.
Babies were arriving in the fort, the first was Robert Till Jr., and Elias Johnson, both being born in September 1857.
One day a soldier arrived at the fort in his uniform and brass buttons, soon all the girls had a crush on him. But he chose Agnes Gardner and it wasn’t long until they wanted to get married, his name was Bill Gardner, but no relation of Agnes, her brothers and mother were against her marrying this soldier. No one knew anything about him or his people. But when they couldn’t get her to give up this man the mother gave her consent and Bishop Cook married them, this was fall of 1857, the first marriage. All had a good time dancing and singing. After the wedding they left the fort and went elsewhere to live.
More brush cleared from the land and more crops planted. Some of the people lived on one side of the creek, some on the other. One cold winter night a boy came to Mrs. Gardner’s home for help.
There were a number of his mother’s children sick. She needed some help, so Mrs. Gardner went to help. They had to cross the creek by walking on a pole for it was quite wide and six foot deep. After staying most of the night she started home. When half-way across the creek she slipped and fell off the pole into the water. The ice was not heavy enough at this time to hold her up. As she struggled to get back out the ice kept breaking. When at last she got on to some firm ice she had to crawl quite a distance before the bank sloped enough for her to get out. Her clothes were froze on her. After this experience she had rheumatic fever, and this left her hands and feet crippled the rest of her life.
After all the hardships and hard work they found the soil was not very good and didn’t produce very good crops. So during the winter of 1860 they decided to move their town site a mile, a little south of west of the fort. Here was plenty of sand.
Presiding Elder Phenias Cook was recalled to Salt Lake, and on February 15, 1860, William Price was sent down to be their bishop, having been set apart to this position before coming down to Goshen.
Here was another stream of water. Again ground was cleared and planted. It was so sandy everything dried out so fast then when the wind blew everything was covered with sand, there just wasn’t enough water to water it all, so Mr. Till went up along the stream to where it had widened out, here he had the men help him to put in a large dam to hold the water back. When this was finished the water stored for two or three days then let loose it came down so fast it washed out whosever garden was being watered. So it was proposed to hold the water for about a week everyone getting ready to water their gardens at once then when it came down it went into several different directions and didn’t wash anything away. The dam was made up where Del Jensen’s farm is. It was always called Till dam. Many fish came down this canyon stream and stopped in this dam. It made fishing for many boys and girls in the early days of Goshen. With new grain growing and the new location and grain plant still around the fort made it necessary to get a better thresher.
A Mr. Coziar came from Nephi with a tread mill thresher. When the grain was threshed out it was put into a box affair that had a fan inside it. The men took turns turning this fan by means of a wooden handle, this blew the chaff out of the top while the grain dropped to the bottom where it could be gathered up. George Pierce from Nephi ran the machine. More people began coming to Goshen.
So many years had passed since that time until I began gathering this information it was hard for these old pioneers to remember all the names, but these are ones they could still recall. These men with their wives and children: Peter Borup, John Rouse, Samuel Steele, John B. Johnson, William Page, George Taylor, Benjamin Cole, Mr. Peterson, Bill Keeler, Peter Nebel, Mr. Hunsaker, Ole Christensen, Pete Blaylock, Arthur Higginsen, John Morgan, J. C. Christensen, Elizabeth Weach, David Baxter, Joshua Ferris, Rufus Lewis, Tinker Jones, Bishop William Price. These came in 1860-1862.
They began building one log rooms but still kept their dugouts to sleep in for they were warm in the winter and cool in the summer. Some of the people didn’t like the sand, some drifted to the east and some went south to where there was a bend in the creek. Hugh McKee, Soren Sorensen, Mikkel Powelson, John Finch, Peter Nebel came into this bend and it is still called the bend after all these years. Hans Jasperson, Samuel Steele, Richard Johnson went a little way east. Brigham Young advised the people to try to get fruit trees to plant.
The men went to the hills, brought currant bushes, choke cherry and elderberry trees and planted. A little later these men in the bend and the ones east of town planted orchards. There was a need for a school house so the men got logs from the hills and built a one room school house. Here the children could go to school. Also they held church in this building. From time to time the Indians were so bad they would have to discontinue church and school. Had to have a scout out all the time watching for Indians. The first babies born here were Annie Till and Mary Ann Gardner. Annie was born in August, Mary Ann in September 1862. The first wedding was for William Page and Ellen Orton Steele. Bishop Price performed the ceremony, this was in 1865.
William Page Sr. had come from Salt Lake he had a suit of store clothes his father had a general store in the city. It was quite a novelty to see a man in a suit of clothes. Nearly every man and boy wore pants and jackets of jean cloth or buckskin. For a wedding present William’s father sent them six chairs from the store. They were the envy of all the young folks.
Everyone else had home-made chairs which Mr. Joshua Ferris made of wood and rawhide. These chairs were very strong and lasted many years. They were also very comfortable to sit on. Till 1865 every one slept on the floor. But now Mr. Ferris started making bed steads. This was done by making four posts then fastening boards from one post to another, then boring holes in the sides and lacing rawhide or rope from side to side in a criss-cross shape. Then their ticks were placed on it and they really had a bed. They called these beds the four posters.
Another dance hall had been made at Sandtown like the old at the old Fort. Mr. Soren Sorensen continued to play for the dances, but now he had help for Mikkel Powelsen, Zeke Olman and Albert Steele helped him. They would play as long as even one string lasted on their violins.
No one had a stove, all cooking was done on a fire in the fireplace. Bread was baked in a iron bake oven, this had a lid on. It was placed on hot coals, then hot coals were banked all around it and over the lid. Soups, meat, potatoes, and greens were placed in a pot or kettle and hung on a rod in the fireplace. Times were hard and there were times when there was no bread or potatoes or meat then they had to live on greens. These greens were wild spinach, wild lettuce, sheep sorrel, dandelions, thistle, grasewood sprouts, pig weeds, sego roots, mustard and milkweeds. Long after food was more plentiful these pioneers still gathered greens, said they were good for you, that they were full of iron.
None of them would destroy any of these weeds. It was after the turn of the century before the people began getting rid of these weeds.
In 1902 the first sugar beets were raised here in Goshen, the people began eating the beet tops for greens and leaving the weeks alone. Even when the beet tops were full grown in the fall when the beets were topped the tenderest leaves were sorted out to eat. When the beets were plowed out of the ground, the children gathered them up, put into piles, then the toppers sat on a box with a board on their laps to cut the tops of the beets with a butcher knife. So they could easily find the tender ones and put to one side for use as greens.
A good many people developed sore mouth and loose teeth. Samuel Steele had lived among the Indians of Grantsville since 1849, so could talk Indian language. One day he was talking to an Indian about their sore mouths and loose teeth. The Indian said you need salt, but there was no salt. Samuel knew they needed salt but how to get it was another thing. He asked the Indian what they did for salt. He said we gather the little tender sprouts from greaswoods, these we cook and eat, some we keep and put into whatever we cook and that salts it. The people began eating greasewood greens, putting it into their bread, soups, and potatoes and all meats, soon their sore mouths were cured.
Every bit of clean fat was saved from pigs, beef, deer. The unclean fat was made into soap, this was done by using ashes from the greasewood. They would clean off a piece of ground, burn the brush, then gather up the ashes. This ashes was put into a barrel of water and churned up good. Then the water from this was put with the fat and boiled into soap.
There was no light in the dugout or room, only from the fireplace. So they would put a little fat into a saucer, put a rag into it with one piece hanging out, this they would light and as the grease went into the rag this would make a light. They called this a “bitch” but it was only used in emergencies as it took too much fat. Some grew a few squash, then began the task of making a few candles. A long narrow hole was cut into a squash, hot grease or tallow poured into it, as it started to harden they would take ravelings from cloth, press this into the hardening tallow, then they had a candle. This still was used to on special occasions as they couldn’t spare too much tallow.
They had no matches to light a fire. To start a fire they use a flint and steel, rubbing them together to produce a spark, it seemed a hard thing for the women to do. Sometimes the men would shoot their guns into the fireplace where some dry kindling had been placed. This would also start a fire. But mostly they would bank their fires at night with ashes then in the morning they would uncover these ashes and in the middle there would be a spark of fire safe and sound. But sometimes if you weren’t careful this spark went out.
During the winter months the men and older boys would go into the hills to cut posts and logs, this left the women to care for the fire banking.
William Steele and when he was seven years old his father and older brothers had gone to the hills and their fire went out. His mother told him to take a bucket and go to the neighbors and borrow some fire. It had snowed nearly all night and was very cold. He never had any shoes so he didn’t want to go in the snow. He told his mother he could start the fire with the shot gun like he had seen his father do. At first his mother wouldn’t listen, but as he coaxed so hard to try, rather than go in the snow she gave in. He arranged his kindling as he had seen his father do, then he got the gun, it had such a long barrel he had to lean over backward far as he could in order to hold it and point it into the fire. Finally he was all set. He pulled the trigger, the gun went off, the fire started, but he was kicked right out through the door. He never tried that again but willingly went with the bucket to borrow fire.
While living at Sandtown the people bought a bell and put on top of the school house. Then when the pickets or as we would say, now the scouts would see Indians skulking among the bush they would hurry and ring the bell, this would bring all the men from the fields. Sometimes they were just friendly Indians, just passing through, but they couldn’t take any chance of a raid. For the holidays they would have jumping contests both high jump and broad jump, horse-shoe pitching horse pulling, also horse racing.
A Mr. Hunsaker owned a beautiful white horse, it could really run. He kept practicing it so to be able to run on the 4th of July. All the Indians came to Sandtown wanted to trade for it. But Mr. Hunsaker wouldn’t listen to them, he wanted it for himself. The Indians had made several attempts to steal it. So Hunsaker built a high corral that a horse couldn’t jump over then he put a padlock on the gate.
He always let his young son go with him when he was practicing the horse to run. The boy would ride another horse to run with the white horse.
One day when Hunsaker had to be in the field some distance from home. He told the boy to watch the white horse and under no circumstances was he to take the horse out of the corral for he had seen some Indians hiding around in the brush.
After his father had gone he climbed up on the corral and looked around but he couldn’t see any Indians. His sister came out to talk to him. He began coaxing her to help him to exercise the white horse. She didn’t want to for she said father saw some Indians, but the boy kept telling her he couldn’t see any Indians anywhere, finally she gave in. She rode the other horse and the boy rode the white horse. The race track was about a half mile from their home. Soon they were riding around the track.
All at once there were a dozen or more Indians all on horses riding at them and screaming we want the white horse. The children kept riding around the track trying to see a break so they could go home. Finally the boy told his sister to make a break for home and ring the bell and he would ride south and if she would hurry the men could catch up with him and save the horse. The horse could really run, first a few would ride hard after him, then they would drop back and another group would ride up. This kept their horses from becoming winded, but the boy pushed the horse faster and faster. As they ran through the bend the Indians were screaming and making a horrible noise. The people rushed out to see what was wrong. Then had to go to the corrals to get horses to follow and try to save the boy. On went the boy up the road past the bend then up the sand hill up past the Partridge farm. There were a number of people living up along the side of the hill by the creek. When they heard this awful noise they rushed out of their homes and from the fields just in time to see this white horse streak by, followed by these Indians. They all ran after them, some had a shovel, some a fork or rake. But just as the boy got to the ford in the creek the white horse dropped dead.
The boy ran into some squawberry bushes and hid. The Indians got off their horses and looked at the white horse lying dead. Then they did make an awful noise. Again this time the people living along the hill and creek came upon them. The Indians began looking for the boy, no one dare make a move for fear they would find the boy. They were all on their horses but one, ready to give up the hunt as this one was coming to his horse to go he happened upon the boy in the squawberry bush. He dragged him out, they all got off their horses and made a circle with the boy in the middle. The people tried to talk the Indians into letting the boy go, but the leader was so mad to think the boy would run the horse to death, the horse he wanted so bad, that when he heard the people from Sandtown coming he pulled out his tomahawk and scalped the boy, then rode away as fast as they could go. The boy only lived a few minutes.
The people as yet had no cemetery when anyone passed away they were most all taken to Spanish Fork for burial. His father and mother were so broken hearted they felt like they couldn’t stand to live here any longer they moved away. Whenever any of the old pioneers talked about this incident they could call it the story of the white horse.
More people bagan coming into this Sandtown district. But like the others they soon became discouraged with the sand, so discontent was among everyone.
Samuel Steele, with his family had come here in this year 1862. He was a good veterinary also fairly good doctor, also pulled teeth. While living in the east he ad worked for a Veterinarian as they would go from place to place they found many sick. They would beg them to help them so this Veterinary purchased a set of medical books and they commenced to study medicine and broken bones. It wasn’t very long until they could take care of the sick people or set their broken bones. Next they bought a set of forceps for each one, then they could pull teeth. Armed with all this knowledge Samuel was a big help for these people. He went among tthe white people and the Indians helping them all, never charging anything for his service.
This more than anyone other thing made friends with the Indians that lived around here.
Having so much discontent among the people made it hard to govern the town. So the bishop proposed they all move a mile farther north. In the fall and winter of 1863-1864 they moved the townsite. Some that had moved to the Bend and the ones that moved east of Sandtown stayed, so began a new townsite. Trees were planted, gardens and flower gardens. One man, Tinker Jones, planted a hollyhock garden behind his home.
The water only came down the ditch once a week. Everyone would dip up enough water to last ‘till the next week.’ Tinker Jones had dipped up some tubs of water they just covered a burlap over them to keep the dirt out. One of his little children fell into a tub and was drowned. This child was buried behind the house in this hollyhock garden for Mr. Jones said, “I know this will be the townsite and I will donate the cemetery plot.” The next year he had another child drown in the tub. Again this child was buried by the other one in the hollyhock garden. These graves were never moved. No one else was every buried there. The two little ones lie there fogotten.
Men began driving sheep down around west side of Utah Lake to feed. A shearing corral was built at Pelican Point. This furnished a little money for anyone that could shear a sheep. Chesty Sorensen, and Mary Nebel used to walk to Pelican Point on Monday morning, shear sheep all week, then walk home on Saturday night to see to the welfare of their families. Then after shearing was over they would go out among the brush and gather wool that was pulled from the sheep while they were being held close to the shearing corrals. This the women brought home, washed and dried it, then corded it, then spun it into yarn, then knit it into stocks and jackets for their men and children. There was not idle people around.
The ground around the new townsite was the worst they had yet tried, no matter what they planted the salaratus turned it yellow and it just dried up. Now the people at the Bend were growing good crops. This made the other more discontent. They told Bishop Price they were all going to move to the Bend. He was to come out and settle the question whether to move to the Bend or stay.
When Brigham looked the situation over he said that is your best farming ground, don’t take it for a townsite. But use it to grow your food, then he called the men together and had them come with him to look over the new townsite. They chose the present townsite. Then he dedicated it for a permanent town.
Then in the fall and winter of 1867-1868 the people moved into the new town. They moved the old school house and when they put the roof back on the shingled it, aslo put a floor in it. Then the old place were referred to as the Old Fort, Sandtown and Old Goshen. Brigham Young advised the people to take their time in moving so to give themselves time to build a log room or an adobe room and do away with their dugout homes. But people were anxious to get settled and couldn’t wait for adobe to be made or logs cut and hauled, so many just moved from their old dugouts to new ones. South and west of town they found beds of white clay. This they began making into adobes, and finally the people were replacing their dugouts with adobe houses.
This was a desolate strip of ground on which they settled. Sage brush, rabbit brush everywhere. Some were discontent, for they had left trees and some flowers around their old homes. It just seemed like they would only get started to make it look like home then have to move again. They were afraid that when they had another home started it would be moved again. It was no wonder they felt discontent, but the new homeland has stayed.
There was no stream of water here, wells had to be dug to get water for man and animals. Some wells were rocked up to keep from caving in. Some just dug the well and when it caved in they dug another. Some just had some boards laid across it. Some had a curbing around it, then a pole with a bucket on one end and a large rock tied to the other end for balance was mounted on a post. It was easy to let the bucket down into the well and the weight of the rock helped to pull the bucket back up.
The first winter of 1867-1868 clay was hauled and with the first warm days of spring adobes were made, and a four room house was erected for the Bishop. This was the first adobe house in the new town.
Most of these pioneers only had oxen to work. It was slow and hard breaking up the ground getting it ready to plant again. They were mostly men with families that had come here first. Now another ten years were added onto them -no wonder they were discouraged with so many moving. No wonder they looked back with longing at their old attempts at home building. The words of the poet express some of their feelings:
The old house sits by the orchard trees.
Gray and silent and alone!
Throught its dim windows only sees
The desolation time has strewn.
Old house why do you sit and wait
For those long gone who come no more?
Do you hear them open the broken gate,
Do you see them pass through the long closed door,
Dreary day after empty night.
Till coldly steals the morning light
Fading the shadows of dark away.
Long are the days and long the year.
For you, old house, as you set and dream.
Some day I’ll wander back again,
To where that old home stands,
Beneath the old tree down the lane,
To that home that first we planned.
The people that settled in the bend began making adobes to make better homes. John Finch built one of seven rooms on his farm. Then across the road on another small piece of ground he built a two room home. Mikkel Powelson built a two room home. Hugh McKee built a three room home. Soren Sorensen built a four room home. These were all built close together, this way they were company one for another. In later years more homes were built in this bend. But today there is only one still standing.
Hearing Brigham Young had dedicated a townsite, more people came into the valley. Some homesteaded: some used squatters’ rights. Some bought a piece of ground from the ones that had already homesteaded.
One man named Bill Keeler that came was a miller. When he seen how hard it was for the people to get flour he built a mill to grind their grain and corn. This was called a burr mill. The wheat and sun flowers grew together. When it was milled it was all ground together. It made gray streaks in the flour and the bread was none to light. It was more on the soggy side. But every crust was saved no matter how hard it was. These crusts were browned, then put into a kettle in water until it came to a boil, then it was put into cups, a little milk or cream added, then they had crust coffee.
Imagine us today grinding our wheat or corn in a coffee mill to get a little bread, or use flour that was part sunflowers and having to eat it. We had the very best of everything and still we are not content like our pioneer parents. They were so thankful to have any kind of bread that they were content with their lot, and truly thankful to their heavenly father for all blessings.
With more grain growing and a mill to mill it, they needed a better thresher than what they had. Bishop Price heard of a thresher for sale that was run by horse power, at San Bernardino, California. He hired Alex Gourley, a young man to go to San Bernardino and get it. Said he would pay him fifty dollars for the trip, he rode one horse, tied his bed roll and camping equipment on the other three and was on his way. This trip took him six weeds -today that trip can be made in 18 to 20 hours. Now with a good thresher and mill the people had much better bread and mush. There were no stoves, just the old bake oven. This was a great novelty to the people and Indians to see this mill grind flour from wheat. Old Chief Showian and some of his followers were always at the mill watching and stealing a little flour whenever they could. All their time was spent here except the days they were on the war path.
Grain was cut with cradle. This was slow work. Some were better with a cradle than others. It was said Albert Steele, Sr., could cut three acres in a day. That was more than anyone else could do. After it was cut it was raked into piles with a hand rake that the people made from a board, boring holes into it and wooden pegs drove in for teeth, then a good straight willow for a handle. Then it was bound in bundles by twisting a piece of grain around the bundle. This went on for a number of years then John Burraston bought a binder in Salt Lake. This binder cut the grain, bound it in bundles with a wire around it to tie it. But some one had to walk along and cut the wire. This was slow work, people were waiting to get their grain cut found their grain getting too ripe, so had to use the cradle.
Peter Roberts bought a self rake this cut the grain and dropped it into little piles so all you had to do was tie it. Then Peter Borup bought a binder that would cut the grain, bind it and drop it. Then Sam Stansfield bought another self-rake, between them all they go the grain harvested with-out the cradle.
The women went into the fields to help bind the grain for if it lay too long it would shell out. Maren Jensen could bind as much as any man in a day. She was a little woman, weighing about 90 pounds, but she could move fast, also move her hands fast.
Brigham Young advised the people to plant fruit trees. Nearly everyone in this new town had fruit trees. Apples, peach, pears, red and blue plums, apricots, greengages, rhubarb and asparagus. The men went to the hills, bringing home cottonwood trees for shade, currant bushes, choke cherries, elderberries. There are still a few of these elderberry and choke cherry bushes growing where these early pioneers first planted them.
This was a great blessing for the people. They would dry the fruit so to have for winter. If the apple crop was a failure some years, then the women went to Payson where they had more and larger apple orchards. They would peel and cut the apples for half. But the owner of the orchards usually had his own children lay his share to dry. These women would stay a week at a time, camping in the orchards sending their share home every other day to be laid out to dry.
Hugh McKee was a good mason, learning this in his old home in Pennsylvania. He was kept busy laying up houses for as Brigham Young said, get out of your dugouts soon as every you can. Many of these first adobe homes had poles and canes for the roof, the white clay put over it. This made good, warm homes. Soon as the people could they would buy shingles and put a new roof on. Some lived in this dirt roof until they moved or passed away. Some of these old buildings built in the 1870s to 1890s are still standing, a testimony of good workmanship of these people.
In May 1871 Hans Jensen came into the valley to live. He was a native of Denmark. His father was a weaver and tailor. From him he learned the tailor trade, but on arriving in Salt Lake valley went to work for Brigham Young, boiling molasses from sugar cane.
When he left Salt Lake he brought molasses with him, the people were really glad to get a little of this molasses for they had no sweets, only a little syrup boiled from a pink beet. This was hard to get boiled out of the beet. It didn’t take him long to get the people interested in growing sugar cane. Then he got Bill Ercanbrack interested in buying the molasses mill. When the cane was cut in the fall it was hauled up on current creek where Bill lived, here he supervised the grinding. Hans took care of the boiling. Soon everyone had molasses. They mostly had to take molasses for pay for boiling, for hardly anyone had money. But they took it and peddled it up at Homansville and Silver City.
What a blessing this molasses was. The stewed fruit could now be sweetened. They had it on their bread and many a happy evening was spent making candy. Everyone had candy pulls.
Hay had to be cut with a cycle or a scythe, then piled with a fork to dry. This was slow work but like everything else the people did it thankfully and joyfully for this also was going towards their permanent homes. Then one day George Higginsen went to Salt Lake and came back with a mowing machine. What a sight that was to see hay cut so easy.
Some ground was spaded up, some plowed with a hand plow. You had to walk behind this plow and hold to the handles to keep it in the ground. Hans Jensen was a cripple and couldn’t keep up with the plow, so he took the front wheels from his wagon and fastened the plow under neath then he could sit on the hounds of the wagon and hold the plow. One time when he went to Salt Lake to conference he saw a riding plow. He bought it and brought it home with him, now he could really plow. Soon others were buying one. While some thought it didn’t turn the ground as good as the hand plow. This new plow they called a sulky. Everyone took turns borrowing it and trying it out.
As time moved on the old thresher began wearing out. You see there were as many sunflowers as wheat growing together. Threshing so many sun flowers was hard on the machine. Then Tom Morgan bought another one. Of course the sun flower seeds were threshed out into the wheat and growing up together, so you just about had gray bread.
Everyone paid their tithing from the fruits of the field. A tithing office was built on the northwest corner of the public square next to the log school house. This was an adobe two story with basement. The basement was used to store potatoes and garden vegetables. The upper story was used for dry fruit and cured meat which hung from the rafters. The ground floor was made into bins of wheat, oats, barley, and corn. In one corner was the desk, where the recipes were made out.
A barn was built east of this tithing office where the hay was stacked. Many Indians traveled through Goshen going to and from Tooele County and the Tintic district. Always they stopped for a few days to go begging. Their horses were tied along the tithing yard fence and most generously fed on tithing hay.
The younger folks always were glad to see these Indians for the young folks were used to Indian Joes children and often ran races or jumped with them. Then when these other Indians came the boys were there to challenge them to races and jumping matches. This even afforded fun for the older people as well as the other Indians.
More land was being cleared so more water was needed for irrigation. More settlers kept coming. Some to homesteaded. They would sell a portion of their ground for whatever the buyer had to give. They called this kind of buying, chips and whet stones. Some people just used squatters rights.
It was slow work drawing water from wells to water their garden, but the gardens they had to have, no matter how slow and hard the work, for people had to live. A town council was held and each expressed their views on how to get water. Finally in 1871 a committee was chosen to go west of town to look over the site where the waters that came down Current creek. It seemed possible to dam this water off and made a dam large enough to hold back the early waters so to divert them into ditches for irrigation purposes. When this was explained to the rest of the people and met with the approval of the bishop, work was started.
This was no easy task. First they hauled rock from the nearby hills. Then cut posts to form a bridge. These posts were layed long wise, then cross wise. Small posts put in between, dirt and gravel poured over every layer to fill all cracks. Then rocks were put in front of these posts and dirt poured over all again.
The dam depth was dug mostly with shovels and dirt being thrown into wagons and hauled along the banks of the dam. This was hard work. Into the one channel a large headgate was built. Today we call it Job Creek. This creek watered the meadows running nearly full all year round, but when the water was dammed of it didn’t reach over to the east meadow where the Jobs had settled so Mr. Job dug a ditch from the west channel to east turning what water came down over to his holdings, so the people gave it the name of Job creek. Still holds today. Headgates were put to the east of this dam that was filled with posts, of course rocks were put all the way across. A headgate was put west of this dam also. Then a large long gap had to be cut through a sand hill in order to get water to go east to town. Here another headgate was constructed. This dam was dug considerably deeper than it is today. It has filled with silt over the years. Ditches were dug from these outlets down through the fields so to cover all farms. A ditch was dug into the town. Where the upper sect and lower sect were dug they found the one sect would have to cross the other to reach higher ground. Another large bridge had to be constructed over the top. Hans Jensen was awarded this contract, he built it of posts, gravel and dirt, forming a ditch on the top. It didn’t look as if it could stand to carry water very long. The company that had been formed to take care of this work refused to pay him unless he would guarantee it would last five years. He had to sign a contract to this effect. He said I built this solid and sound. I will guarantee it to last as long as I live, and it surely did. When he passed away in 1910 the bridge still stood with its ditch on top.
Men, boys and women worked on digging ditches. After a summer, fall and winter of work there was water being stored in early spring. But the trouble had just begun, the water would seep between the rocks, loosen the gravel in the posts and get through. Men had to watch day and night to keep their dam from going out. They used the dam for a road thinking perhaps traveling over it would help it become more firm and settled. But with irrigation they began growing crops through the fields and plenty of gardens in town.
Orchards were planted on nearly every lot. Men went up Current creek and dug currant bushes, planting them along the fence that fenced their yards from their neighbors. Even giving bushes to the widows. Soon everyone had currants to eat. But the trouble still persisted at the dam. It was then decided to put a board bulk head clear across the fill. This took time and labor, for the lumber had to be hauled from Santaquin canyon where they had a saw mill. But these pioneer souls were determined to win. So with this in mind it was repaired and made stronger. But still from time to time it would break and we who lived in the field were really frightened when the water would come down the ditch overflowing its banks and come into our homes. This dam furnished much entertainment for the people. In the winter they would skate on it and in the summer they would boat ride.
One day when a group of young boys were out in their boat it tipped over and Johnie Freckelton was drowned. This was the only drowning in the dam.
By the spring of 1872 the dam was in use it was registered as the Goshen Irrigation and Canal Co. Had a good water right, maintained its own overhead and today still carries a good water right and is independent. Thanks to our dear pioneers who labored so hard and uncomplainingly, to leave us security.
When the people first moved up here in 1867-1868, each one tried to do a little better than the other in beautifying their surroundings. Joshua Ferris went to the hills south and west of town to get a shade tree, there was a place filled with cottonwood trees. They called this cottonwood canyon. He dug a beautiful tree, bringing it down and planting it in front of his home. This tree he had to water with a bucket with water from the well he dug. It grew to great height and stood as a monument of his love and labor for (82) eighty-two years. After the ditches were dug along the streets this tree was directly on the bank. So much water running past it undermined its roots, and during a terrific wind storm in 1950 it blew over. The old timers that were left felt sad at its going.
Now there was water aplenty the citizens all turned out to bring shade trees from the canyon, planting them along ditch banks. It wasn’t long until the town took on a gala look. Orchards were planted, nearly everyone had a few fruit trees. Now the trees began to bear fruit. All kinds were dried and hung from the rafters to keep for winter use. Squash was dried. It was cut in circles, the peeling or rind removed, then hung to dry. Corn was cut from the cob after cooking and laid to dry. This was the hardest of all dried stuff to cook. You had to soak it all night then boil three hours.
Now having the dam and ditches complete they needed a schedule to water by. It was put to vote and Hugh McKee was chosen because he was a fast walker. He made a schedule for himself. Going from ditch to ditch, even into town. When it was time for a man to take the water Hugh had to turn it to him. When his time was up Hugh turned it to the next one. From town to the far fields was three and one-half miles. Walking to turn it to each one was a fulltime job day and night. Each man was to pay him for this service.
When he would go to collect most everyone wanted to pay with butter. Some butter was so stale it was impossible to eat. The stores wouldn’t take it. As the Bishop governed everything he also governed the water. Hugh went to the Bishop to see what he could do about it. He told him to make it into soap. This work was full time, spring, summer, fall, all gone with nothing much but bad butter. He had a family to keep so refused to work the next summer. No one else would have the job. But people just couldn’t let their crops die. Everyone was taking water whenever he was ready. This caused confusion and some quarles all summer. A meeting was called to try and straighten out affairs for another year. A schedule was made for each ditch and a water master to turn the water to each one as their time came. This worked for a while. Then it was decided to make each man a schedule, then he could take the water himself as his turn came. This worked out better. This kept friends among the farmers. For they now knew what they were doing. Ever since that time the Irrigation Company has kept to this individual schedule.
Many of the people built upstairs in their homes as it was called in the early days. Today we say two stories high. Rufus Lewis, John White, John Burraston, Hans Jesperson, E. L. Thomas homes still stand as they were first built with their upstairs. Samuel Steele, Mrs. Hyrum Weach, John Rouse and Bishop Price homes have been remodeled, top story taken down. John B. Johnson, Jim Gardner, John L. Jenkins, James H. Jenkins, Hy Weach Jr., Sam Rudd, Robert Gourley, Peter Roberts, J. C. Christensen old homes still stand and people are living in them. They are made from clay adobes -cool in summer and warm in winter. Hugh McKee was a good brick mason and built the most of the homes.
Having no meeting house and only a one log room school house, the Relief Society had to hold their meetings in each other’s homes. At a meeting they decided to build a Relief Society hall. With the help of the Priesthood to do the heavy work, hauling rock, hauling clay for the adobes. Hugh McKee laid the rock foundation and then the adobes. The men of the ward did the carpenter work and bade the pulpit, also the benches. Elizer Edwards, Peter Okelberry, J. C. Christensen, John L. Jenkins, were some of the carpenters. Dan Atherly made the adobes. It was decided that every sister had had chickens donate the Sunday eggs towards funding to buy the lumber. This with parties and suppers brought enough to buy the lumber. After years of struggle the Relief Society had their own building to meet in.
Church had to be held in that one log room school house. With the exception of fast day. As this came on Thursday, it was held in the Bishop’s home. Now the Bishop transferred all church meetings to the Relief Society hall. Funerals were held here also.
A transient came to Goshen during the fall harvest. Working for something to eat and a bed to sleep in, said he had no home or family. He was taken sick and finally died. No one knew from where he came. Up till now when anyone had died they were taken to Spanish Fork for burial. At this time there was no burial ground except the two little graves at Lower Goshen, and one little grave near Yeagers home by the lake. Bishop Price suggested making a cemetery on the west side of his property, so this unknown man was buried there. The people wondered if this would be a good idea having a burial ground so close to their homes.
The young people of the ward were really roused up about this, for just across the street from the Bishop’s home was the school house where all their parties were held. Getting together they decided to do something about it. There was to me no more burials in city limits.
They went to the Bishop and complained so hard he had to promise to put the cemetery some other place and move the body to the new grounds.
The next burial was out west of town the other side of Job creek. Many graves were dug here. But they never got around to move the transient out there. His body still lays where first buried.
One day when Brigham Young came to town he went with Bishop Price to see the cemetery. Standing there he said someday this will be all farmed, the graves will all be flattened, a highway will run just west of this. I think you had better have another cemetery. He then went with Bishop Price south of town on the side of the sand hill. He said this is where you must put your cemetery, have all the bodies exhumed and brought here from the old cemetery. Some of the people didn’t like this idea and a few are still buried in the old cemetery. After a few graves were made in the new cemetery, people began bringing their dead and reburring them in the new cemetery. Some of the old roads have been closed and planted to grass. The other roads have been graveled.
Having to use the Relief Society hall so much made plenty of cleaning and fires to be made. A heating stove had been bought and put in the middle of the room. One night I was talking to Walt Jensen and Will Burraston about their activities as boys. This is what they said. Will was president of the Deacons, Walt first counselor. A meeting was held at the home of Bishop Price every Wednesday or Thursday night and a schedule blocked out for the weekend. This was the set-up to clean the house. Two deacons were to sweep it out and dust on Saturday night; two deacons to chop enough wood to keep it warm for Sunday School and meeting, (there was no coal).; one deacon to keep filling the stove with wood; one deacon to hold the door; two deacons to get the bread and water for sacrament. After meeting two deacons to sweep and dust again so it would be clean for the Relief Society ladies. This program carried through all the weeks, just changing deacons.
Then the deacons were to go around town and chop wood for the widows. The Bishop had set aside a piece of meadow ground just north and west of town for a community pasture for milch cows. Every morning when you milked your cow you turned her out into the road, in a few minutes a deacon was there to drive your cow to the pasture, then return her a chore time at night. Then the deacons gathered fast offerings, these were paid in whatever you had. So the deacons had a full time job. As William Burraston said as deacons were the Bishop’s right hand man. The years before the Relief Society hall was built the deacons had to clean that old log school room just the same only they didn’t have to chop the wood so small for in the school room was a fire place.
The first store was owned by Mikkel Powelson in one room of his home, this was 1868. He sold pins, needles, some stockings, pieces of cloth, a few shoes, candy, tobacco and sometimes he had tea and coffee. He would go to Salt Lake to get these things. After the move in 1869 Louise Taylor set up a notion store in one room of her home. This she operated for about twenty-five years. Here you could buy pins, needles, thread, lace, collars, ribbons, gloves, peppermints, thimbles, black combs, cloves (sometimes every woman carried in he pocket and kept one in her mouth while at church, entertainments and dances), also she sold pictures of the President and his counselors, the Twelve Apostles, the President of Seventy, also Joseph and Hyrum Smith. These were all in little red velvet frames with a little wire stand on. Nearly everyone had some of these pictures to stand on their mantel shelves. They were prize possessions.
In 1870 Bishop Price put up a General Merchandise store. Here you could trade eggs, butter, grain, corn, for store goods. This the people really needed. Then A. A. Noon built a three room home where Ted Okelberry has a house for rent. After building this home he decided to put a store in one room. Then he put a saloon in another room. He lived in the other room. This house was three room long so the store was between his home and saloon. Then Ole Hansen set up a tailor shop in one corner of the saloon. This became a busy center. The mail was left at his store. Ore was being hauled from Iron King and North Lily to Santiquin. A number of Goshen men and boys got jobs hauling, some had four horse teams, some had four oxen. This was a long hard haul especially in bad weather. The roads were so poor. It was decided to build a smelter at Goshen Dam site. In 1872 the smelter was built along with a boarding house.
A number of people built lumber homes out around the smelter to be closer to work. One ore was smelted with wood. Seems like the night shift firemen liked to play cards, the fire would go down. The ore that had started to heat good would cool in the pots, no amount of heat could then dissolve it. Then they would have to dig it out of the pots and put more in it. Much ore was lost this way.
When pay day came the smelter men and teamsters would go to the superintendent of the smelter for their pay. He would say the head boss lives at Santaquin, go there for your pay. When they would get there he would inform them they would have to take their pay in (White Mule). This was a poor brand or whisky that he had some of his men distill for him. There was no sale for it for everyone that worked to the mine, or smelter or teamsters had to take this for pay, they couldn’t live on it. One after the other quit. They could never keep a crew working. The smelter was closed down and left to rust and decay.
Tom Morgan bought the boarding house and moved it on his farm. People who had built around it moved their houses away. All that is left today is a little red dirt and one tin that has a little room in the bottom and a little room in top. The top slopes around on top. Part of this north slope is gone.
In 1870 the mines in Eureka were discovered. This provided work for many people. Houses had to be built, as well as the mines.
Many of the Goshen men and boys went to Eureka to get work. For money was scarce in this little community. About the only money anyone had was at Homansville where they had coke overs. When the dam was completed the people could raise about anything. With the mines going they needed vegetables, so haw and grain, for among the many other things they had a livery stable. Most all mine bosses kept a riding horse to ride over the hills.
Soon the peddler wagons were on the road boing with their surplus eggs, butter, vegetables. These peddled from door to door. This way they got a little money they would never have had. Payson, Santiquin, as well as Goshen peddlers were on the road.
Sam Rudd built a saloon on the corner of Curley’s store. There Ole Hansen moved his tailor shop into one corner of Rudd’s Saloon, figuring it was more centrally located.
Whenever the school notice had to be tacked up, it was supposed to be in three prominent places. The school minutes read; We have posted said notices in three prominent places as instructed, namely Goshen Coop (known as Prices Store). One on A. A. Noons Store. One on Ol Hansen’t Tailor Shop, better known as Rudd’s Saloon.
Nearly all the women knit their families stockings and jackets, so whenever a sheep herd came up in the brush from Pelican Point west of town the women and children went wool gathering taking every little bit that had pulled off on the weeds. Even if the store had stockings to sell they didn’t do too good at business. The women could knit stockings, keep what money they had or whatever they had to trade for more essential things.
In 1862 a dramatic club was formed with Old Christensen as dramatic leader. They had a fine choir, had some fine singing along with their dramatics. The people were organized to have a little reception. Then on Saturday at noon everyone stopped work and gathered at the school house where they ran races, hugh jumped, board jumped, played horse shoes, wrestled and pulled horses. Then at night the dramatic committee took over. Then on Sunday there were no sports or games. All could honor the Sabbath by going to Church.
