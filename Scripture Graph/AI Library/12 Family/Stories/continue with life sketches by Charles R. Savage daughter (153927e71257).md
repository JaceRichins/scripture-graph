# continue with life sketches by Charles R. Savage daughter

_A story of [[William Budge (KWC8-8X9)|William Budge]]._

## The story as it was written

At the age of 15 he first heard a Mormon Elder, Thomas B.H. Stenhouse preach, and this Mormon sermon made a deep impression on Charles, who had a religious nature but was not satisfied with the teaching of the different sects he had visited. The teaching of the Latter-Day Saints satisfied his cravings and gave him something new to work for.
Among his new church acquaintances he met Brother William Eddington, who had a bookstore in Portsmouth. He offered Charles a position in his store.
Charles was baptized May 21, 1848, by John Lewis, who later was his neighbor in Salt Lake City.
In the fall of 1852 Charles was ordained to the ministry, and sent on a mission to Switzerland, where he labored for three years. He traveled many miles on foot staying with Saints. He soon acquired the French language also a little German. He was imprisoned with Elder William Budge in the city of Zurich for preaching Mormonism.
After he returned to England, he traveled as a missionary in the Derbyshire Conference with Elder Israel Evans. On the 11th of December, because of his knowledge of French, he was appointed as an interpreter for a company of Italian and Swiss Saints who were migrating to America.
During Charles' labors in England, he became acquainted with Annie Adkins, of Leighton Buzzard, Bedfordshire County, and they soon became engaged to marry. Circumstances prevented their marriage, however, before Charles left for New York, but in a few months, she joined him there. He sailed on the ship, John J. Boyd, December 12, 1856, arriving in New York February 15, 1857. The voyage was a stormy one; and a number of passengers died at sea. The long trip used up their provisions, so they endured many hardships.
After his arrival in New York, Charles was asked to assist in the transfer of other immigrant
companies at Castle Gardens. He found employment in Samuel Booth's printing office in New York, where he worked for two years.
Charles passed through the panic of 1857.
He took charge of the New York Branch choir, where the Saints had crowded meetings and had to rent large halls to accommodate the new converts.
Within four months after Charles reached America, he married Annie, who had arrived on the ship Thornton, from Liverpool. They were married in Brooklyn Long Island, at the home of Henry Sadler, by Elder John Taylor. The marriage was witnessed by Elders Miles, Orson Pratt, Ezra T. Benson and George A. Smith.
It was in Carles' early residence in New York that he became interested in photography. This appealed to his artistic nature, and his old friend Stenhouse had a photograph gallery in Brooklyn at this time. Mr. Stenhouse had brought a camera from England, and it is said he also introduced into America the first stereoscopic camera.
In 1859 Charales was sent by George Q. Cannon on a special business to Council Bluffs. He took along with him a camera, an old gray blanket for a background, and a tea chest, which was his dark room.
Thus, the longing to come to Utah, the Zion both he and Annie had had for years, was partly satisfied. Here Charles worked hard in his primitive photograph gallery to earn money enough to get oxen and a wagon so they could finish their journey to Salt Lake.
By the Spring of 1860 he was able to join the company of Captain Brown. He took his camera along and made pictures on the plains. From his collections of views some on the route, we have some of our earliest Pioneer pictures today.
After six weeks of life on the plains, Charles, Annie and their two little ones arrived in Salt Lake August 27, 1860. They went to their old friend William Eddington, who had befriended Charles when he was a penniless boy.
Soon after his arrival Charles joined Marsene Cannon, and opened a photograph gallery in the upper room of a house located on Main Street, next to the old Salt Lake House. The faithful oxen that carried Charles and his family across the plains were traded for a 10 by 10 lot on what was then called the east bench. the ground was covered with sagebrush, and there were few people living in that neighborhood.
In those early days Charles distinguished himself as a military man, and served for a number of years as Lieutenant and Captain in a company of the first battalion, third regiment of infantry of the Nauvoo Legion. In 1870, together with seven others, he was imprisoned for two days for having turned out to a harmless muster of the Nauvoo Legion, for violating Governor Schaffer's orders forbidding the asembly of troops in Utah. this affair is known in history as the "Wooden Gun Rebellion."
In 1870 Charles accompanied Brigham Young and others on an exploring expedition through the upper Rio Virgin country, including what is now known as Little Zion Canyon. The views he took at this time were the first ever taken of this Little Zion.
Brother Savage was ordained a High Priest at President Brigham Young's office May 9, 1873, and set apart to act as a member of the High Council of Salt Lake Stake. He held this position until 1874.
In 1875 he organized the idea of Old Folks Excursion. A committee was appointed, with Presiding Bishop Hunter, Elder George Goddard, and others, to arrange for this yearly excursion of all old people over seventy in the city. this idea has been successfully carried on each year ever since.
For many years Elder Savage labored as a home missionary of Salt Lake Stake. He also delivered a number of illustrated lectures on the scenic beauties of Utah and other places of interest. He was deeply interested in science and delivered lectures on astronomy and the history of photography. He contributed many letters of his travels to the Deseret News, and to local magazines. He was also a very popular speaker and was much sought after because of his wit and homely philosophy.
In 1861 he became a charter member of the Tabernacle Choir, and continued with this organization until his death.
In 1883 his store on 12 and 14 South on Main Street--which he had built was one of the leading art centers in Salt Lake--was burned to the ground. He lost his entire stock of negatives--a loss which could never be replaced. His undaunted courage never failed him. Many businessmen and a host of kind friends came to his assistance, and in a short time he built a larger and better store (which building still stands on Main Street), called The Savage Art Bazaar.
Elder Savage was a leader wherever he lived. He was original and was always promoting unique entertainments in the Twentieth Ward. He was foremost in developing every new device pertaining to photography. He was official photographer for all the western railroads. He traveled extensively and made views of all points of interest in the west. He discovered and named the royal Gorge, which is seen by thousands of tourists yearly. He won first prize on his exhibits in photography at the World's Expositions in Chicago, St. Louis, San Francisco, and Portland, besides winning first prize yearly in our own Fair exhibits.
He was a true friend to the unfortunate, and he and his wife found their greates joy in assisting the widows and orphans.
His wife Annie died November 23, 1893. He was the father of eleven sons and daughters, nine of whom lived to maturity. His first son, Charles Stenhouse, died in New York; Roscoe Eddiinton was also born there in 1858. Ralph Graham was born at Council Bluffs in 1860. Annie Amelia, George Lewis, Fannie Maude, Enos Hoge, Luacine Annetta, Ida May, Lennie Louise and Ray Thomas were born after they came to Salt Lake.
In 1876 Brother Savage married Emma Fowler. She died in 1881, having had no children. In 1878 Brother Savage married Ellen Fenn; she bore him a son and a daughter, Arley F., and Emma Jane. He later married Annie Smith Clowes.
On February 3, 1909, Brother Savage died, after an illness of three days. His death came as a great shock to his many friends, as he was always blessed with good health. He had friends among all classes of people, beloved by both the rich and the poor. He was a devoted lover of art and was of great assistance to the struggling Utah artists in the early days.
His funeral was held in the Assembly Hall, which was filled to capacity with mourning friends. He is buried in the City Ceme.
The Old Folks of Utah contributed money for the erection of a drinking fountain to his memory, which stands at the corner of Main and South Temple Streets near his Art Bazaar.
This monument will forever bear witness of his love for the old and unfortunate--a love which dominated his entire life.
