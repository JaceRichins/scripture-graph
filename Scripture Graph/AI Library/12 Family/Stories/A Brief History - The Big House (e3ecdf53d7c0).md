# A Brief History - The Big House

_A story of [[Charles Wager Richins (KVP1-SVD)|Charles Wager Richins]]._

## The story as it was written

A Brief History of Charles Wager and Agnes Mary Willmott Richins
(Based on "Our Pioneer Heritage" by Kate B. Carter. p. 254.)
Charles and Louisa Shill Richins were pioneers of 1853. They were converted to the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints in England. They crossed the plains in Joseph Young's oxteam company, arriving in Salt Lake City October 10, 1853. (Another account says he came alone and his wife and child came later with the child dying on the way.) At that time they were the parents of one child, Hannah Louisa. She died in 1854.
The family lived in Salt Lake City for seven years. Then in 1860 Charles received a call from Brigham Young to go to a little valley situated on the Weber River to help settle that area and to organize a branch of the church. Charles made the trip alone to Heneferville to make a survey of the country. In 1861 he brought Louisa and his new wife, Esther (married 9 Mar 1861), to the sagebrush covered valley to make their home.
Charles pitched two tents on the banks of the Weber River. This was home to the Richins families for quite some time. Soon he began to make plans for better living quarters for his families and a great deal of hard labor was spent in digging three dugouts in the hill side. These, when finished, were quite roomy and much more comfortable than the tents although they had only dirt floors and no windows. The doors were made from heavy planks held by crude strap hinges. These dugouts served as their homes for one year.
The following year Charles began to build what eventually became known as Bishop Richins' "Big House". The house was known to more people than any other between Echo Canyon and Salt Lake City. The first year Charles built two rooms over the dugouts and continued over the years to add more rooms until, when it was finished, it was a two-story dwelling with fifteen rooms. A large porch graced three sides of the home.
The "Big House" was a mansion compared to the tents and dugouts of earlier years...There was a well at the back of the house. It was lined with rock and covered over by a little roof. A windlass and bucket were used to draw up the water. The well was also used as a refrigerator for perishable foods.
In 1878 Charles married Agnes Mary Willmott, a beautiful little English girl. She was twenty, and by now Charles was over fifty years of age. So the third bride came to live in the Big House. One spring three new babies came to bless the Big House and those who dwelt therein. All growing up together.
One can well imagine the huge washings done in large wooden tubs and on wooden wash boards. Esther's oldest daughter (Hannah Edith) tells how she and Prudence, daughter of Louisa, each had a tub and the clothes were rubbed in three different waters. Water carried from the well was heated on the stoves. Then using their handmade soap, they would rub away for hours.
Louisa, a quiet, stately woman with small, beautiful hands, was an herb doctor and a midwife. She attended many of the women in the community at the birth of their children. She received no pay, but served only for the love of serving. Occasionally she was asked to help in the care of sick animals which she willingly did. One room of the Big House was used solely by Louisa to mix and store her medicines. No one else was allowed to enter the room.
Each wife had private sleeping quarter for herself and family, but there was just one kitchen and the families all ate together as one, and usually included anyone else who happened to come along.
Since Henefer was the first Mormon settlement reached after coming down Echo Canyon, it was natural that many immigrants would stop at the Richins Home. Charles, being the bishop, took anyone in who needed food or a place to sleep. As time passed, he became a successful farmer, rancher, and also dealt in real estate, so he was in a position to furnish work to those who desired it. Many of the early settlers worked for Charles, and became attached to the settlement and stayed to build homes. Since there was little money at this time, Charles paid his help in commodities such as meat, Milk, cheese, butter, and even vinegar which he made from apples grown in his orchard in Pleasant Grove.
Both men and women found work at the Big House. The men cut the grain with cradles while the women followed behind and tied the grain into bundles. Many were the tasks in the home--churning, cheese making, sewing clothing, moulded candles used for lighting, curing and storing meat in the brine in the dark cellar, and many other tasks too numerous to memtion which had to be done by these hard-working pioneer women.
The growing boys in the family were kept busy milking a large herd of cows. The milk was set in shallow pans on the shelves in the cellar. Then it was skimmed and the cream was made into sweet butter. The skimming of the milk was quite a job in itself.
Parley T., son of Esther, remembers an incident in connection with the churn. One time his pet cat was lost, and after a long search, they found her in a large churn in the cellar. She had jumped into it and given birth to her family; then was unable to get out. They were all dead when discovered. Needless to say, it took plenty of hot water, lye, and soap to put the churn back into condition for use again.
The Big House was always bustling with activity. One room was used to repair clocks. Leonard Richins became quite skilled at this craft repairing clocks for people all over the valley. On occasion the back porch would become a dentist office where many a tooth was extracted with crude instruments. Or a barber shop.
The Parlor was used to lay out the bodies of those who had passed away. (The coffins, made locally, were in the form of the body. They were usually lined with white muslin and covered with black velvet if at all available.
Charles entertained many of the Church authorities in the Big House. It was used for meeting purposes, both religious and business, for parties and receptions.
