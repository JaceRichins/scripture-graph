# AUTOBIOGRAPHY OF JAMES MCBRIDE

_A story of [[James John McBride (KWJ5-DHB)|James John McBride]], [[Olive Mehitable Cheney (LYB9-K1C)|Olive Mehitable Cheney]]._

## The story as it was written

AUTOBIOGRAPHY OF JAMES MC BRIDE
(1818-1881)
My Dear Children:
 As time speeds swiftly on, and age creeps over me, I am caused to reflect on the necessity of handing down to you a short history of my life. I have, there-for, made an effort to furnish you such truths as I yet retain in my memory. While this may be considered a very brief sketch of the past part of my life, it is never-the-less, a truth.
 The genealogy, though not so complete as it should be, I trust you will study with interest, and lend a helping hand, in behalf of our forefathers and relatives, in the Latter-Day work.
 Many of the accounts I write, of my own life, are no more than common occurrences with many who are cast upon this stage of action and subject to its changes.
 Others picture plainly the unusual, unjust, unmerciful, and almost indescribable, evils heaped upon the Latter-Day-Saints. With whom I have been a sufferer, not because I was ever convicted of a crime, but on account of my religious beliefs. I do not write with a desire to excite feelings, strife I detest. I would gladly suffer evils, as long as evils are sufferable, I would willingly forbear, so long as there is virtue in forbearance, but since we, God’s children, have been driven from our home repeatedly, at the hands of a ruthless mob backed in their infamous crimes by acting officials, I have no hesitance what-every in giving vent to my feelings and exposing, in my feeble way, such facts as are stamped on the very tablets of my memory, and which time can-not erase.
 I greatly regret not having kept a journal of my life from my boyhood, but I did not then realize the necessity of doing so, consequently, a great deal is forgotten that might have been of interest, to you.
 I now ask you, my children, to continue my history from this time, should I not be abele to, and I enjoin upon you, as a duty you owe to your posterity, to commence early in life and to keep your history, to hand down to them.
 As I have said, this you may think a very short history from me, but if I were in possession of as much, wrote by my father, what a valuable book I would think it, what a precious gem! Often which my fore-parents passed.
 I hope, my dear children,, that you will fully appreciate the value of the little history that I leave you.
 May God bless you, my children.
Your Father
 James Mcbride
Grantsville City, Utah
August A. D., 1874
CHAPTER ONE
 My grandfather’s name was James McBride. He was born in the state of Virginia, in which state he died, at seventy or seventy five years of age.
 My grandmother’s maiden name was Mary White. After her marriage to grandfather, she gave birth to John, James, Philip, and Thomas and of the girls there were Isabelle, Mary, Sarah and I believe another girl whose name I cannot tell (1) but was the wife of ( ) McDougal.
 Perhaps the names are not given regular (1) but are as nearly so as I can remember having heard them.
 I believe my grandfather was of Irish descent (3) his wife’s name was Ruth ( ), and after her marriage to grandfather John, she gave birth to James, and a son who was scalded and died when quite young and whose name I cannot give of the girls, Sarah, Elizabeth, Hannah, and Catherine (my mother), are all I have any information of.
 Thomas McBride, my father, was born March, 1776 in Loudoun County, (4) Virginia. At the age of about eighteen he was married to Catherine John (5). At the time she was about sixteen (6) years of age, I thing they were married in Han**** County (7) Virginia, at least grandfather John was living in that county (8) at that time.
 To my mother were born, Rebecca, Ruth, Amos, Mary, Hannah, Elizabeth, Susan, Thomas, Sarah, Isabelle, James, Catherine, and Dorcas. There were two other girls born in the family that died when infants and I think they were not named. The first seven above named were born in the state of Virginia, in which state Susan died.
1810
 My father left Virginia, in the spring of 1810 and moved to New Lancaster, Fairfield County (9) Ohio, a journey of about three hundred miles, taking with him all his children living except Ruth, who was living with her grandfather and grandmother John, and remained in Virginia for sometime after.
 In the autumn of 1810, Hannah died. She was, then, about three years old (10).
1813
 In the autumn of 1813 my father returned to Virginia and brought Ruth home with him. I will now give you a short account of my father’s trip.
 He rode a pacing mare, (11) called “Snap”, and not being able to furnish a better mode of conveyance, my sister Ruth, who was about fourteen years old was obliged to ride and walk, in turn with father, a trip as you already understand, of about three hundred miles, from Virginia to Lancaster, Ohio.
 Rebecca and Mary were both married in Fairfield County, Ohio. Thomas, Sarah, Isabelle, and my self, were born in the same county.
1820
 In March, 1820 my father moved from Fairfield County to Wayne County, (12) a distance of about one hundred ten miles, taking with him Ruth, Amos, Elizabeth, Thomas, Sarah, Isabelle and myself. There he took a lease on a school section of land, for fifteen years, which was situated on one of the tributaries of the Mohegan River called the “Red Haw’. (13)
 The conditions on which the lease was taken were that my father was to clear not less than twenty five acres of heavily timbered land. The clearing was to be divided into fields of not more than seven en acres each, lawfully fenced. He was also to put out an orchard of not less than twenty-five apple trees and twenty-five peach trees; a log house and a log double barn (14) were to be built.
 My father’s circumstances were very poor. He had but little stock when he took the lease, and unfortunately, lost part of that.
 Bone and sinew were put to chopping and grubbing (15) the younger hands to gathering brush, whether boys or girls it mattered not, the clearing must be done.
 The first year about five acres were cleared and put to corn. Heavy frost destroyed the crop, so that first year there was no income from the lease. In three years, however, the eight acres were about cleared and a gradual income was realized.
 Catherine and Dorcas were born in Wayne County. Ruth, my eldest sister remaining at home, was here married to Perry Durfee, a few years after my father took the lease.
1828
 Amos McBride married Keziah McBride a daughter of Robert McBride, but no relation to my father’s family. Elizabeth afterward married a man by the name of James McMillian. My sister Susan died about the year 1828.

CHAPTER TWO
 Go! Those whose eyes peruse these pages in search of the particular history of my life. Let my say. Go back with me a dew years and I will endeavor to give you a plain account of my life from childhood..
1818
 I was born on the ninth day of May A. D. 1818 in the county of Fairfield, State of Ohio.
 I was but two years old when my father moved to Wayne County.
 Of the early part of my life, you have already read something in the first chapter of this work, and, perhaps, I cold not say much more than I have already said that would be of interest to you, about it.
 While my father lived on the Red Haw Branch of the Mohegan on the lease of which I have already given an account, came first to us the sound of the Ever lasting Gospel as revealed to man in these last days. It was the gospel of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, proclaimed by two elders. Thomas Tripp and Harvey Green. I was, then, about eleven years old. My father, who previously had not felt to join any Christian denomination now opened his house and welcomed the elders into his home.
1831
 The first sermons preached on the Red Haw by elders of this church, were preached in my father’s house in April, 1831, by the above named older. Soon after my father, mother and sister Isabelle were baptized and confirmed members of the church by the same elders.
Auguat 1833
 My father sold the lease, and in August, 1833, accompanied by broth Amos and his family and James McMillan and family, started to Jackson County, (1) Missouri, to join with the church. The season (2) being well advanced, he was not able to get further than Richland County, (3) Ohio, that season.
1834
 While there on March 18 Isabelle and Thomas married.
 My father stayed in Richland County until the spring of 1834. When accompanied by Amos and family. James McMillan and family, Isabelle and husband, James Daley and family, he started to Missouri.
June 1834
 Having traveled about two months with ox teams, in the latter part of June, 1834, we arrived in Pike County, (4) Missouri. The church being very much scattered and unsettled. We remained in Pike County about two years.
1836
 In the spring of 1836 the company above mentioned moved to Ray County, (5) and were joined with a branch of the church. We stopped there about three months during which time we suffered a great deal from ague and fever. (6)
 The howling of the mob were heard on every side, and it was decided that we should move to Caldwell County. (7)
 In September, my father, taking with him what of his children yet remaining at home and accompanied by James Daley and wife, moved to Coldwell County and settled, about three-forths of a mile from Haunt’s mill, (8) on Shoal Creek. There my father entered, from the government, eight acres of land, (9) and began to make a home.
 A branch of the church was organized at Haun’s Mill, presided over by David Evans, ans.
1838
 I was baptized into the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, by David Evans, in June, 1838. At the same time James Haun and Isaac Laney were baptized.
 Though many of the followers of the prophet Joseph Smith had been beaten, tarred and feathered, (10) driven from their homes, and their property confiscated for the use of mobocrats, their persecutions were yet to come. Threats were made against the Mormons, their rights of citizenship denied them. The little few, now fully realizing the dangerous situation in which they were placed, decided to adopt measures to defend themselves against the raids of the mob. It was decided that a guard would be posted at the mill.

CHAPTER THREE
October 30, 1838
 One beautiful afternoon, October 3, 1838, my father came home from a meeting with brethren at the mill. He talked with me and told me of the arrangements made, and that he was called to help from the guard.
 I was sick at the time, with the every-other-day-ague. Father said that on my well day I should take his place on the guard, and that he would guard on the day that I was sick, that with himself and me, he wished to fill one man’s place.
 You will remember that my father was then in his sixty-third year. During the summer he had been very sick, but having recovered appeared to be feeling very well, in fact, I think he looked better than I had ever before seen him.
 My sister Catherine was living at the mill, with Haun’s family, leaving only me and my youngest sister, Dorcas, at home with father and mother.
 My father was in good spirits and his countenance wore a cheerful expression. Having shaved himself in this usual style, leaving side beards, and taking with him his gun and blankets, he started on his return to the mill to join the rest of the guard.
 Mother, with sister Dorcas, started to visit a neighbor woman living about a quarter mile distant from father’s place.
 This being the day on which I was sick, the next day I should have taken father’s place on the guard. The day was gradually passing, evening was coming on, the large, red sun, so characteristic of an Indian Sumer, (1) shone through the smoky atmosphere. All was still.
 My father had but little more than got to the mill; in fact not more than thirty minutes had elapsed from the time he left the house, when a gun was heard, and another, followed by the deadly crack of musketry, which told too sell, the fate of all who fell prey to the blood=thirsty mob.
 Perhaps not more than six minutes had passed, from the firing of the first gun, until the massacre was accomplished. The bloody deed was done, the firing ceased. The screams of mothers, daughters, and the wounded told the dreadful tale.
 The bloody picture! In the Book of Time, may it every stamp, with stigma, the brow of that government that offered not a protesting hand to those who were ruthlessly cut down, wounded, or made widows and orphans at the Haun’s Mill Massacre.
 The sun slowly sank beneath the western horizon, and darkness sped its broad mantle over the universe.
 Without a single exception, the dead were left lying where they fell; in fact, there were none left that were able to take care of them. Whether dead, or alive, all feared alike.
 All was uncertainty. All was pain, and sorrow. In vain did the affectionate wife, with aching heart and streaming eyes watch through the long, long night for the return of her husband.
October 31, 1838
 The 31st dawned, and again, the rays of the morning sun kissed the landscape. As yet, the extent of the massacre was not known.
 Brother Amos, having been detailed on the previous day to get word for the families, was on his way to the mill when he was told there had been serious trouble there. His home was about three miles from the mill, and as he was not detailed on guard, he was not at the mill at the time of the slaughter.
 He went on, and passing the mill a short distance, came to Haun’s house. The first object that met his eyes, in human form, was the mangled body of my murdered father lying in the dooryard. He had been shot with his own gun, after having given it into the mob’s possession. He had been cut down, his body disfigured with a corn-cutter (1) and left lying in the creek. Some of the women had dragged him from the creek into the dooryard and left him there. One of his ears was almost cut from his head, deep gashes were cut in his shoulders, and some of his fingers were cut ‘til they would almost drop from his hand.
 On further examination, it was, found that fifteen were murdered, and fifteen were wounded, one of which was a woman.
 Mary Stedwell, who in trying to escape, was shot through the hand, and fell behind a log. Several bullet holes were found in the log, directly opposite of where she lay.
 Allan Smith, and I believe, one (________) Merrick were the only would children yet alive. Of the wounded men, three afterward died, making eighteen dead, in all.
 Isaac Laney, a young man that was baptized into church at the same time as I, was in the blacksmith shop (3) when the mob (4) began to fire on them. His gun-stock (5) was shot to pieces in his hands. He then escaped from the shop, ran to the mill, and climbed down one of the mill timbers into the creek, that being the quickest way for him to escape danger. From there he went into the house where sister Catherine, Mrs. Haun, Mrs. Merrill, and some other women were, They administered to Isaac and put him under the floor. He had received eleven bullet marks in his body.
 I knew Issac Laney well, and helped to take care of him until he recovered. He told me that when trying to escape from the mob, the blood gushing from his mouth would almost strangle him. While he was under the floor, he said he suffered a great deal from want of water; the women not daring to venture out to get water until they felt sure the mob was entirely gone. Isaac recovered, and lived thirty-five years from the day of the Haun’s Mill Massacre.
CHAPTER FOUR
 A FEW RODS (1) SOSUTH OF THE BLACKSMITH SHOP WAS AN UNFINISHED WELL, ABOUT EIGHT TO TWELVE FEET DEEP, BUT NO WATER WAS IN IT. This made the sepulcher (2) for the dead. Fifteen murdered person, including my father, were carried on a board, one at a time, and dropped into the well by brother Amos McBride, James Daley, and Jacob Myers, the only three able bodied men that were present.
 It was now plainly shown that there was no mercy for us. What few men and boys of much age yet alive, were under necessity of hiding away to escape danger.
 About the first day of November, being tired lying out I the wood, I concluded to venture a trip to the mill. I was anxious to see the ground on which the slaughter took place, and learn, if possible, the general situation of affairs. Accordingly, with feelings that I cannot here describe, I slowly wound my way to the spot.
 I walked over the ground noticing here and there the blood socked earth, and seriously reflecting on our, then, sorrowful situation.
 On the outside the logs of the shop were defaced with bullet marks, and on the inside of the shop the ground was scarcely visible for blood.
 I traced the blood from the dead bodies of those who were carried and buried in the well. I went to that place and stood at the edge of the silent tomb of my beloved father. A silent prayer I offered to god, and turned away.
 I went to a house in which lived a widow woman by name of Mear. Her husband was a victim of the massacre. She was yet there with her family. She advised my to be careful, lest the mob come upon me and kill me.
 Having spent a few minutes at the house I went into the mill to look, once again, through it.
 While there a noise attracted my attention. I saw the woman of whom I was speaking, running and beckoning to me in a frightened manner. I sprang to the doorway and was about thirty rods distant, a posse of men coming in the direction of the mill. I did not feel right in trusting myself in their hands, but rather therefore waked from the mill to the dam crossed it and quietly walked on until I was out of sight. Why they did not fire at me I cannot tell.
 A few days after a company of men commanded by Neamiah Comstock, took possession of the mill.
 In that company was a man by the name of ( ) Mopin for whom my father, who was then a magistrate (3) had collected a judgment amounting to ten dollars ten cents just before his death. Mopin now threatened that my mother’s house would be burned down over us if the money was not forth coming.
 I heard the threats made, and after reflecting for a time started to chance my fate with the mob.
 In as bold a manner as I could assume I went among them. They did not bother me and I soon thought my self quite safe. I found Mopin and presented him with the money. He took it and seemed somewhat affected on learning of the situation of my father’s family. To remunerate me for my trouble, he gave me ten cents.
 I was quite small for my age, was smooth faced and very sickly, which perhaps, in part, accounted for my being allowed to leave in peace.
 Having ventured thus far, I decided that I would again return, and act as a spy. One day, having worked my way back into their midst, I discovered that a man named Robert White, who was a member of the church, had turned traitor and given the enemy all the information he could about the Moron families and their situations.
 The captain, who was aside instructing his men, I overheard mention my brother, Amos, as one having a gun, which he said was hid in a hollow tree and if he refused to give it up when called for, they were to shoot him down, without further ceremony.
 As soon as the chance presented itself, I left the camp and as soon as I was out of their sight, made my way across the hills to where Amos lived and told him what I had heard.
 I advised him to go get his gun, and when demanded of him, to give it to them as we were betrayed, and if he tried to keep his gun, he would lose his life, Amos did as I advised him.
 A few days after, brother Amos, James Daley, and David Lewis were taken prisoners. They were kept a few days, harassed and tormented then set at liberty.
 While Comstock’s Company remained at the mill, they used it to do their grinding. They would shoot down our cattle and hogs, not caring how much they were needed by the widows and children that had been left to care for themselves. This was indeed, hard to bear, but to resist was death.
 The Governor of Missouri, Boggs, (4) not being satisfied with the suffering already borne by the Latter Day Saints, issued orders requiring them to surrender their firearms, give up their principal leaders, and leave the state at a given time.
 The suffering caused by that exterminating order of Boogs” could hardly be described. Families were turned out of their homes, and widows and orphans found themselves cast, helplessly, upon the mercy of the church. Some were without teams, and almost destitute of food and clothing. Thus exposed, to the storms of winter, to travel a journey of more than two-hundred miles.
 It was now necessary to get rid of our house at the mill, in the best way we could. If we could get something for it, well and good; if not we were to leave it anyway. The place was worth about one-thousand dollars.
February, 1839
 In February, 1839, the place was sold for an old vehicle worth forty dollars, and an old horse worth about twenty dollars, and a mare worth forty dollars. In all, we did not get more than one-hundred dollars for the place.
 With the horse and mare hitched to the carry-all, (5) and a yoke of oxen (6) to a two-wheeled cart, our traveling outfit was made up. Dependent on this was my mother, my two youngest sister, myself, my brother Amos and family, James Dayley and family; also Harrison Severe, a young man who afterward married my sister Dorcas; numbering, in all, about seventeen persons.

February 24, 1839
 We left Haun’s mill on the 24th day of February, on our way to Illinois.
 On the first day, we traveled about nine miles, and then camped in a house which had been vacated by one of the brethren. The day had turned extremely cold and we decided to remain at the place ‘til weather turned more favorable for traveling.
 While there camped, we were informed that our guns, which had been taken from the Saints at Haun’s Mill, and at the surrender of arms at Far West, had been taken to Richmond in Ray County, and that we could get them by first describing them, swearing to the description, and paying a fee of sixty cents for each gun. James Daley and myself decided to ascertain the truth of the matter, and if possible, get our guns. My main object was, however, to get possession of father’s gun, with which you may remember, father was shot.
February 27, 1839
 Accordingly, about the 27th, we started to Richmond. At the same time, the main body of our company started on their journey to Illinois.
 We had not horses to ride, no teams were traveling in that direction, consequently, we were compelled to go on foot.
 Now let me say, this was the beginning of the hardest days, of suffering from fatigue, that I every experienced.
 The first day, about one hour before sunset, we arrived in Richmond, and after describing our guns, taking the oath and paying the required fees, we were directed into a room in which was stored several hundred guns, all of which justly belonged to our people, but of which they had been unwarrantedly deprived.
 My father’s gun was not to be found, by us, but fortunately, I got my brother Amos’ gun and James Daley got his.
 Having done all we could, we turned our faces in the direction of our company.
 Having traveled about ten miles on our way, at a late hour we stopped for the night at the home of Brother Pleasant Ewell who had been a good friend to many of the Saints, and who gave e us lodging, supper, and breakfast.
 At an early hour of the morning we were again traveling. After a hard day’s trip, and just before sunset, we came to a place where, we were informed, our company had passed about eleven that day. We were now on the road our company had traveled which made us anxious to push on.
 We had been without eating since early breakfast, so we arranged with the man of the place for our dinners, for which we paid twelve=and one-half cents each.
 A few miles ahead commenced a prairie through which we would have to travel about eighteen miles. The country through which we were traveling was a new country and it was not thought strange there, to travel ten or more miles without seeing a house; but with the hope that our company had camped at the edge of the prairie and that we might overtake the, we traveled on. Darkness came upon us. We reached the prairie but found no one there.
 The wolves were howling around us in almost every direction. We were indeed tired, but to lie down in the cold and to trust ourselves to the hungry appetites of the howling wolves, seemed hopeless and we still traveled on. Repeatedly I proposed to my traveling companion to stop, but he would not consent to this proposal at this critical time.
 Slowly we trudged on, till at a late hour, we saw to our right and about a mile distant, a fire in the timber. We left the road and went in the direction of the fire. When we got to the place we gathered wood to keep a fire and there camped for the rest of the night.
 Before day downed the shrill clarion of the dung-hill **** (7) informed us we were then but a short distance from a house. We went to the house and got our breakfast, which were very acceptable to hungry and weary foot travelers.
 About eleven that day we overtook our company. We had traveled and average of about forty miles each day, and you are left to judge the good feeling we enjoyed at again meeting our friends.
After a torturous journey and a great deal of exposure, from which many died, we arrived in Adams County, Illinois, (8) at which place I built a house.
 I rented a piece of land which I farmed on season.
 At that place, Dorcas married Harrison Severe.
April, 1840
 In the spring of 1840, about the last day of April, I started to Nauvoo, Han**** County, (9) taking with me mother and sister Catherine, and accompanied by Amos and family, James Daley and family, Harrison Severe and wife, also a young man by the name of William Pope, who afterward married Catherine.
 At Nauvoo I got in with a man named Cramer from who I got a small place and raised a crop of corn the first year.
 The gathering of the church was at Nauvoo.
1841
 In the year 1841, I rented a portion of a farm from a man named John Eagle, which I cultivated.
July 26-28, 1841
 My circumstances were very good and all went well, until about July when my mother was taken with the King’s Evil, (10) from which she died about the 26th of July, 1841.
 At that place Catherine married William Pope.
 In the autumn of ‘41, I moved north about two miles, and built another house in which I remained about a year-and-one-half, then disposed of.
1842 - March 7, 1844
 In the spring of 1843 I bought a piece of land about one mile still north, on which I built a home and made some improvements.
 While on that place I married Olive Mehetable Cheney (1) on the evening of the seventh day of March 1844 (12).
 Olive Meherable Cheney, daughter of Arron Cheny and Mehetable Wells (13) was born on the fifteenth day of March 1817 (14) in Bloomfield Cattaraugus County (15) State of New York.
June 27, 1844
 On the 27th day of June 1844 the prophet Joseph Smith and patriarch Hyrum Smith were murdered at Carthage jail Illinois.

CHAPTER FIVE
 The hand of the assassin was not yet stayed. The persecutions of the Saints were very severe, and having long suffered the gross impositions of all who wished us evil, it was discovered that we would, again, have to move.
January 28, 1846
 On the 28th day of January, 1846, the breaking up of the church at Nauvoo began.
February 6, 1846
My first son, Brigham McBride, was born at Nauvoo, Han**** County, Illinois on the 6th day of February, 1846.
 In order to keep affairs in the best possible condition it was necessary to organize the church into companies. First, headed by a captain, would be a company of one hundred families, divided into two companies of fifty families each, and over each of these, a captain. There fifties were divided into companies consisting of ten families, each ten having a captain. Of the ten of which I was a member, James Daley was caption.
April, 1846
 In April 1846 we left Nauvoo, directing ourselves westward.
 A span of horses, of very inferior quality, to a small wagon, and a yoke of oxen to a wagon made up the traveling outfit for our entire company, to which was also added a widow woman and family.
 The first day we moved to the banks of the Mississippi, taking first a part of the company, their bedding, clothing, and provisions, and unloading it at the river, then returning with the teams for those remaining behind, and so on ‘til a evening, the company was all together. This was the way we traveled each day and by this method, we traveled about twenty five miles in ten days, all camping together every night.
 We stopped at Farmington, Iowa. (1) There the company took a job grubbing at which we worked during the summer, our wages amounting to about twenty five cents per day, each.
September, 1846
 In September 1846 we left Farmington and moved down the Des Moines River, to Tom’s mill, where we stopped.
 There was a deal of sickness in our company during the fall and winter, causing the deaths of son Brigham McBride, then aged about eleven months; Wyat Pope son of William Pope and Catherine McBride; and an infant son of James McMillan and Elizabeth McBride.
 I was the only man in the company able to work, for about ten days, though previous to that time my health had been very poor worse than the health of the rest of the men of the company.
 The company shared alike in all that was earned; if part were sick they shared equally in the earnings of the rest and I when I worked alone, all I earned was equally divided.
March 1847
During the winter brother Thomas was very sick but his health was now gradually improving.
 About the first of March 1847 he went to Farmington and while peaceable sitting there in a store, he was seized by a mob and hanged to the porch timbers of the building. While hanging there, his toes only allowed to touch the ground, the mob seated around laughing and looking on, a man by name of Isaac Gray came up and cut the rope. Thomas fell to the ground, his face black and life almost gone.
One Ike Gray as he was usually called was not a Mormon but sympathized with the persecuted. Though a rich man, he wore an old buckskin (1) coat and was always ready for the worst. He said a man could not be treated in that style while he was around, land the mob not desirous of getting into difficulty with so rough a fellow as Old Ike, concluded to quit the job till a more suitable chance presented itself.
 Thomas recovered so as to go home but about three weeks afterward, he died, and though we must not censure too readily, it altogether, the cause of his death.
May, 1847
About the first of May the Company with the exception of three families, resumed the journey west.
Having made some additions to our teams we started from Tom’s mill with three yoke of oxen and three wagons.
Though we could now travel with much less trouble than on our previous journey, we were not able to haul provisions sufficient to do the company any length of time.
 We traveled about sixty miles, and stopped in Appanoose County Iowa. (3) In fact it was now impossible to proceed any further. Our Teams were not sufficient to draw our families, and provisions to do them across the plains and after leaving that settlement, we would have but little chance to obtain provisions. The Great Plaines, extending then to the Pacific coast were before us.
 We did not know, yet, where we were to make our final home. The orders were, “move west”.
 Many companies had already gone ahead, but we did not. We did not know where they would stop.
 The frontier settlers among whom we now found ourselves, were filled with hatred toward the Mormons. Bitter threats were made against us and we were watched on every hand.
 A council was held by the little company.
 It was evident we could not, at the time, go further. What should we do?
 We must stop. It was necessary to court the friendship of our worst enemies.
 Camp was made. Some commenced to improve land, while others sought work of the settlers, in order to get means to buy provisions for our families.
 We had not been there long ‘til we gained a very good name. The work we bargained to do was done well. We could get work anywhere, and consequently, were able to make ourselves quite comfortable.
 No time was lost in idleness. Cabins were built, to winter in, and the next spring, we commenced to farm our own places, which we had improved. Our object was to gain means with which to travel west, and the sooner this could be accomplished, the better.
July 10, 1848
 Aaron McBride, my second son, was born in Appanoose County, Iowa, on the tenth day of July, 1948.
January 2, 1850
 My son Amos McBride, was born in Appanoose County, Iowa, on the second day of January, 1850.
 During our stay in Appanoose County, our company was considerably reduced in number.

CHAPTER SIX
May 17, 1850
 On the seventeenth day of May, 1850, having disposed of our house in Appanoose County, we again start west. In company with myself were Captain James Daley, Harrison Severe, William Pope, and their families; also my sister Mary and her children.
 Having traveled about three-hundred miles, we came to Bluff City, then called Kanesville. We stayed there a few days, during with time it was decided that the entire company could not, safely, venture to cross the plains that season.
 A council was held. Two families could go on, but the rest would have to stay there ‘til they could obtain more means.
 Harrison Severe, having a better traveling outfit than either of the men of the company, was determined to cross the plains that season. One family could go with him. Who should it be? It was, finally, decided that I should go.
 James Daley, William Pope, with their families, and my sister Mary Biddelcome and her children were left, to follow on as soon as possible.
 Harrison Severe, myself, and families, again started westward.
 Having traveled about fifty miles, we go to the ferry on the Missouri River. There we joined a company of fifty families, under Captain Gardner Snow. Joseph Young, a brother of President Brigham Young, was president of the company. Thomas Rich was captain of the ten in which we were included.
June 3, 1850
 The organization and all the necessary arrangements having been made, we crossed the river on the third day of June, 1850, on our way to Salt Lake, a distance of more than one-thousand miles over an uncivilized country.
 About the first day’s travel after we crossed the river, there was a case of cholera in the company, which proved fatal. In all, there were eight cases of cholera in our company during the first month. Seven of that number died.
 While traveling up the Platte River: much might be said that would be of interest, but I will pass over the principals and only make mention of a few incidents which transpired.
 One forenoon, as the company was quietly traveling along, the front teams started, and in an instant, the first teams were stampeded.
 Women and children were in the wagons, but most of the drivers were on the ground. (1) For the drivers to keep up with the teams was impossible. Away went the teams!
 Those who have not seen the like can hardly realize the excitement of the moment.
 For perhaps forty rods or more, wheels were whirling and wagon sheets (2) were cracking in the wind, when almost as simultaneous as was the start, the teams, principally in abreast, stopped.
 Some of the oxen had been dragged to the ground. Chains were wrapped around yokes, and some of the oxen were astraddle of chains.
 The stampede was as equally unaccounted for as was the sudden stop of so many frightened teams.
 The drivers, hurrying up and putting their teams in order, were anxious to know if anyone was hurt.
 It was found that Lyman Severe, son of Dorcas and Harrison Severe, at the time the teams started, was riding on the tongue of my wagon, which was very common for children to do, crossing the plains. In trying to jump from the wagon, he fell, and was run over by the next wagon coming up. He was no so badly hurt but that he soon recovered.
 There were many narrow escapes by drivers who, in trying to follow their teams, were crowded between oxen and wagons, while being overtaken and passed by them. None, however, were much hurt.
 From the time we left the Missouri River, near Bluff City. There were no white inhabitants, until we got to Fort Kearney (3) at Grand Island, a distance of one-hundred-fifty miles, or more.
 After traveling up the Platte River a distance of twenty-five miles, we fond buffalo as numerous as a well collected herd of cattle. To estimate their number would be impossible, by us. We could see them, on each side of the road, for a great distance. It was necessary that someone should be kept ahead to turn them out of the road, in order that the teams could pass with safety. As fast as the wagons would pass, the buffalo would close in, thickly, behind us.
 Over a vast space of county where, today, fields of golden grain are dotting the land, the hum of busy laborers is heard, and the Iron Horse, protected by the hand of civilization, whirls thousands of people across unmolested, then roamed only the Indians and wild beast. Indeed, the scenes on the plains, in those days, were wild and romantic.
 Among the many curiosities that attracted the gaze of the traveler, was one called “The Chimney Rock”, which was about two-hundred miles from Fort Kearney. It was not a rock, but a kind of body of clay.
 We traveled in sight of the chimney Rock about two days before we got to it. From great distances it looked very much like a flag pole, but gradually grew larger until we camped, at the nearest point to it from the road. We were, then, about two miles off.
 Harrison Severe and myself went to examine the curiosity.
 It was a column, more than one-hundred feet high and about twenty feet squire, which was standing on a hill perhaps three-hundred feet above the level of the plain. The column was covered, with names and dates, as high as men could reach.
August 5, 1850
 On a fragment laying near the column, I cut my name. This was the fifth day of August, 1850.
 Having climbed the main hill, a distance to the south, we could see, on top of the column, what we supposed to be raspberry briars growing.
 I have since heard that the Chimney Rock has crumbled down.
 Perhaps a hundred=and -fifty miles more brought us to Fort Laramie.. (4) We were yet on the Platte River.
 From Fort Laramie we traveled over a wild, mountainous country about four-hundred miles. We then came to a place called Fort Bridger. (5) A white man, fleeing from justice, with changed name, had sought refuge among the Indians, and had built a home there.
October 4, 1850
 From Bridger, a distance of about one-hundred-and thirteen miles brought us to the city of Salt Lake, the destined place for the Latter-Day-Saints. (6) We arrived in Salt Lake City on the 4th of October, 1850.

CHAPTER SEVEN
October 10, 1850
 We must now find a place to settle and decided to move to Tooele. (1) Having gotten to the place where Tooele City now stands, and not being satisfied with that place, Harrison Severe and myself concluded to move to a more favorable appearing place, twelve miles northwest of Tooele, now called Grantsville City. We arrived at this place on the tenth day of October, 1850.
 One white man, living in a wagon-bed, and herding stock,, was the only inhabitant of the place at the time.
 We built the first house to be built in Grantsville, did the first plowing in the place, and were, consequently, the first settlers.
 We had little means with which to support our families, and our nearest market was Salt Lake City, thirty-seven miles east of Grantsville, During the winter, we made several trips to Salt Lake City hauling coal, by which we managed to make a living.
 We did what farming we could, when not otherwise employed.
March 20, 1851
 About the 20th of March, 1851, on going out in the morning to get our cattle, we could not find them. Indian tracks were found and it was evident that they had driven off our cattle. Only two were found Harrison Sever’s, and all of mine were gone but one cow.
 We went to the South Cedars, where several parties were engaged in burning coal. With what few herders that could be gathered, amounting to eight in all, we started in pursuit of the Indians.
 We followed them west into Skull Valley, and on to the Cedar Mountains where we found one of Sever’s oxen lying dead.
 Our provisions were now nearly exhausted, in fact, we had but four biscuits to each man, and were sixty miles, on foot from home.
 The Indians were now routed, and at their camp, we found several of our cattle killed. We took that meat we wished and, as the Indians had gotten out of our reach and there was not prospect of recovering any livestock, turned toward home.
 We were now left with out teams sufficient to do our work and there was no one from whom we could get work, so we concluded to move east, across the valley, to a small settlement formed at Pine Canyon.
 From a coal burner at South Cedars we get an old ox, very much the worse from his hard usage.
 We hitched the two yoke of animals to Sever’s wagon and started. He went as far as the Six Mile Springs, and leaving his wagon and family, went back for me, Later the same day, I got as far as the dam at Benson’s Sawmill, now know as Richville, and there I camped, Severe remaining at the Six Mile Springs.
 The second day I went back and got his wagon, leaving mine at the mill. That night we got our wagons together and camped about four miles from Pine Canyon.
 Our teams were very much fatigued, the road was very muddy and we were traveling uphill.
 I stayed with the families while Harrison Severe went to Pine Canyon and got a man and a yoke of oxen to help us.
 While there we worked during the summer, principally in the canyon getting out timber and house logs.
 We paid ten dollars per hundred for flour, and a proportionate price for goods and groceries.
 We farmed some land at that place, from which we got about twenty bushels of wheat, and what potatoes and beets we wished for our own use.
 Notwithstanding the high prices we paid for all that we consumed, we saved, and invested three-hundred-and eighty dollars in work oxen.
December 1, 1851
 About the first of December we moved back to Grantsville, in company with five other families, and began to improve our places what we could, and put in our crop.
1852
 In the season of 1852, Severe and I raised about five=hundred bushels of wheat.
 A branch of the church was organized, presided over by Benjamin Baker and consisted of seven families.
September 5, 1852
 James Orson McBride was born in Grantsville, Tooele County, Utah, on the fifth day of September, 1852.
1853
 In 1853, in all we numbered about thirty families, presided over by Thomas Henry Clark who had been sent, to preside at Grantsville, in the autumn of 1852.
 The Indians were very bad, continually driving off and killing stock. They were then doing a deal of mischief among the southern settlers of the territory. The Walker Indians, then, appeared to be the worst in their raids upon them. We were compelled to guard our cattle by day, and corral them at night.
 It might be of interest to you to know that the spot on which my orchard now stands is the spot on which our cattle were then corralled.
 In the spring of 1853, we were instructed to build a fort in order to secure ourselves against danger.
 We decided to move our houses closer together, and when we could, to build a wall around them of sufficient height and thickness to make our families safe. We accordingly, began our work, our minds filled with care and anxiety. Houses were torn down and hurried to the appointed spot.
 During the autumn of 1853, or company was strengthened by quite a company, who had crossed the plains that season.
 Having been busily engaged in moving houses and doing other work which we could not, properly, leave undone, as yet we had not built our fort wall, but were all living closely together.
1853-1854
 In the winter of 1853-54, 23 commenced and built the fort. The north, west, and part of the south sides of the wall were built with dirt dig from outside of the walls and closely packed by beating with hand malls. (1) The wall was five feet thick at the bottom, twelve feet high, and eighteen inches thick at the top. The east side, and remaining part of the south side, were built of adobe, under which was a rock foundation.
 The wall made of adobe was three feet thick at the bottom, twelve feet high, and eighteen inches thick at the top, covering an area of thirty rods square, making one-hundred-and-twenty rods of wall, which was made at a cost of twenty-five dollars per rod, amounting to three-thousand dollars. Had the gates been put up and all of the top, of the last wall, been completed, the cost would have been much more.
February 18, 1854
 Elam Wells McBride (3) was born in Grantsville, Tooele County, Utah , on the eighteenth day of February, 1854.
 North Willow Creek, which now furnishes water sufficient to yearly irrigate hundreds of acres of grain, was at that time, a very small stream.
 The settlers had undertaken to farm a great amount of land that year. The season was a dry one, and grasshoppers came in great numbers, cutting down and eating the greater amount of what vegetation there was yet growing. However, we were not to starve, a kind hand was stretched forth in our behalf.
 That year I raised two-hundred-and-three bushels of wheat, the largest amount of grain harvested by anyone, that season. The settlers raised but very little grain, some none at all.
 A few of the brothers went north of Salt Lake City, to Ogden and other places, and obtained some wheat. Others, who were not able to but, must chance themselves with other sufferers.
1854-1855
 The winter of 1854-55 passed, and with spring commenced the suffering among the families of the little settlement. Their scanty supply of provisions, gathered the past season, was nearly exhausted. Some were, in fact, already without bread, except as those who yet had divided with them.
 As the season advanced we gathered and used grease-wood greens. In fact, some almost lived on these and other greens and roots which they gathered. We had quite a sufficient quantity of milk, but even that did not satisfy the hungry appetites of families. Men staggered with weakness as they went to and from their labors.
 I sold what wheat I had to spare for two dollars per bushel at home, among my neighbors, but was, at the same time, offered four dollars per bushel by men from other places. I afterward divided my flour, ‘til, I think, I was three day without bread.
1855
 John H. Cooley harvested the first grain on the place, in 1855, which was barley. Of that, each family was allowed about one-half of a bushel, which we would grind, and make into a thickened milk.
 In this way, we managed until we began to gather our wheat crops, which were not very good, but as soon as it was fit to use, families would boil and eat it with milk. Some was ground, on coffee mills, to make bread until threshing was done.
