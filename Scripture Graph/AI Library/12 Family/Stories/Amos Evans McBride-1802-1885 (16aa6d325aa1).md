# Amos Evans McBride-1802-1885

_A story of [[Thomas White McBride (KWJR-J45)|Thomas White McBride]]._

## The story as it was written

AMOS EVANS MCBRIDE
* Amos Evans McBride is the 2nd great Grandfather of the thirteen children of Jesse S. and Lilith P. Wilson
GLEANINGS FROM THE LIFE OF AMOS MCBRIDE, written by Eunice Cope, Sept. 29, 1962. Information taken from the Autobiography of James McBride and the memories of my mother Rebecca Wilson who was a granddaughter of Amos McBride.
 Amos Evans McBride was born 20 March, 1802 in Laudon County, Virginia the third child and the first son in the family of children born to Thomas McBride and Catherine John. He was one of 15 children. In 1810 when Amos was but a boy of 8, his father moved from Virginia to New Lancaster, Fairfield Co. Ohio, a journey of 300 miles. When Amos was a youth of 18 his family moved to Wayne Co. Ohio. Here they took a lease on a school section, on the Red Haw river. They agreed to pay for the lease by clearing virgin land. They suffered poverty and hardship. By the time Amos was 21 years old, they had cleared 80 acres of land. Amos undoubtedly played a big part in this work. On the 23rd of March 1825 he married Keziah McBride. His brother James states "a daughter of Robert McBride but no relation to my father's family." Their union was of short duration. A ragged page found in a family Bible written by Amos, gives us the information that Keziah bore him three daughters. [The accuracy of these dates is questioned. See Research report of Virginia Wilson and Marba Lazenby, 1992]
 Lucinda b 11 June 1825 d June 1826
 Evaline b 11 March 1826 d July 1826
 Rebecca b 28 April 1828
His wife Keziah who was born 2 February 1803, died 15 July 1828, leaving Amos at the age of 28 a widower with his baby Rebecca three months old. His mother Catherine John McBride cared for the baby. The tie between Amos and his father's family seems always to have been strong.
 Keziah had been dead about 33 months when Amos first heard of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints, from Elders Thomas Tripp and Harvey Green, who came to his father's home on the Red Haw River. Many of the McBride family soon joined the church but we have not yet proof that Amos was among the number but it is most likely that he was.
 Some time after Keziah's death Amos married a woman Hannah______. We do not know the exact date of this marriage. Likely it had taken place before August 1833 when his brother James states that his father "accompanied by brother Amos and his family" left Wayne Co. Ohio and started to join the church members at Jackson Co. Mo. During this journey they had to stop in Richland Co. Mo., Pike Co. Mo., and Ray Co. Mo, then to Caldwell Co. near Hauns mill. During all this time the suffering from mob violence was most severe. The story of the Hauns Mill massacre, too well known in Church History to be repeated here, was a tragedy that would have completely shaken a family less founded in the Gospel. Little Rebecca was a child in her 11th year. She often told of hoeing in a field with her father when they heard the shots at the mill, and of how her father Amos dropped the hoe and left her.
 The following description of the part played by Amos in this heart breaking tragedy is here quoted from the writing of his brother James.
 "One beautiful afternoon on the 30th day of October 1838, my father came home from a meeting with the brethren at the Mill. He talked with me, and told me of the arrangements made. He was called to help form a guard. I was sick at the time with the every-other-day ague, and father said on my well day, I should take his place with the guard, and that he would guard on the day that I was sick. That with himself and me, he wished to fill one man's place. You will remember, my father was then in his sixty-third year. During the summer he had been very sick--but having recovered appeared to feel very well; in fact, I think he looked better than I ever saw him.
 Father was in good spirits, and his countenance wore a cheerful expression. Having shaved himself with his usual style, leaving side beards, and taking with him his gun and blankets, started on his return to the mill to join the rest of the guard.
 My father had little more than got to the Mill--in fact not more than thirty minutes had elapsed from the time he left the house, when a gun was heard, and another, followed by the deadly crack of musketry, which told too well the fate of all who fell prey to the bloodthirsty mob.
 Perhaps not more than six minutes had passed from the firing of the first gun till the massacre was accomplished. The bloody deed was done.
 The firing ceased,--the screams of mothers, daughters and the wounded told the dreadful tale.
 With a single exception the dead were left lying where they fell---in fact there was none left that were able to take care of them. Whether dead or alive, all fared alike, all was uncertainty- all was pain and sorrow.
 The 31st day dawned, and again the rays of the morning sun kissed the landscape. And yet the extent of the massacre was not know.
 Brother Amos, having been detailed the previous day to get wood for the families, was on his way to the mill when he was told there had been serious trouble there. His home was about three miles from the mill and he was detailed on guard, was not at the mill at the time of the slaughter.
 He went on and passing the mill a short distance came to Haun's house. The first object that met his eyes in human form was the mangled form of his (my) murdered father, lying in the door yard.
 He had been shot with his own gun, after having given it into the mob's possession, was cut down and badly disfigured with a corn cutter, and left lying in the creek.
 Some women had dragged him from the creek into the door yard and left him there. One of his ears was almost cut from his head, deep gashes were cut in his shoulder, and some of his fingers were cut until they would almost drop from his hand.
 A few rods south of the blacksmith was an unfinished well about eight or twelve feet deep, but no water was in it. This made the sepulcher for the dead. Fifteen murdered persons, including my father were carried on a board, one at a time, and dropped into the well by brother Amos McBride, James Daley, and Jacob Meyers, the only three able bodied men who were present. Also Harrison Severe helped bury them." [Quoted from the Autobiography of James McBride]
 Following the massacre Gov. Boggs issued his famous "extermination order", requiring all Mormons to leave. The suffering and persecution was severe. Much of the time the property was stolen. At one time the mob threatened to kill Amos if he did not give up his gun. He gave the gun. James says, "A few days later, brother Amos, James Daley and David Lewis were taken prisoners. They were kept a few days, harassed and tortured and finally set at liberty. Amos carried the scars of this mob violence to his grave.
 Quoting again from James-
 "The- our place was worth about one thousand dollars. In February 1839 the place was sold for an old vehicle worth forty dollars, an old horse, worth about twenty dollars and a mare worth fifty dollars. In all we did not get more than $100.00 for the place. With the horse and mare to the carry-all and a yoke of oxen to a two wheeled cart, our traveling outfit was made up. Dependent on this was my mother, my two youngest sisters and myself, my brother Amos and his family, James Dayley and family, also Harrison Severe, a young man who later married my sister Dorcus, numbering in all about 17 persons."
 We left Hauns Mill on the 24th of February 1839 on the way to Ill. The group had a journey filled with suffering, stayed some months in Adams Co. Ill, where we farmed for one season.
 Quoting again from James-
 "In the spring of 1840, about the first of April, I started to Nauvoo, Ill, (Hancock, Co), taking with me my mother and Sister Catherine, accompanied by Amos and family, James Dayley and family and Harrison Sevier and wife, also a young man William Pope, who afterward married my sister Catherine. My mother was taken with the Kings Evil, from which she died 26 or 28 of July 1841."
 The family group left Nauvoo in April 1846 for the journey westward, about the first of May 1847 three families were left behind and did not move westward that year. Amos and his family undoubtedly remained behind. We have little record of Amos and his family during this period. Hannah had at least 2 children, Thomas and Catherine. The sorrow Rebecca felt at being taken from her loved grandmother Catherine John McBride, was partly compensated by her love for this half brother and sister. She had much of the care and responsibility of them since Hannah's mind failed her. Hannah died, date unknown.
 Amos married Matilda ________, likely back in the states.
 Rebecca came to Utah in 1864 with her husband Wellington Paul Wilson and his first wife, Elizabeth Boardman Smith. In 1869 they were living on the Muddy. Her husband was called to go on a mission to Vermont. Rebecca went to Grantsville to live, taking with her three unmarried daughters, Catherine, Allie and my mother Rebecca then less than one year old. The move was made that she might be near the McBride relatives there. Her father Amos and his wife Matilda, her uncle James and her Aunts Dorcus Sevier and Catherine Pope. Some of them had settled in Grantsville in 1850. Others came later. We do not know the date. We do know that her half-brother Thomas McBride met her in Salt Lake City and moved her to Grantsville, when her husband went on his mission. As Thomas was then in Grantsville more than likely Amos had also arrived before that time. If not he came soon as mother had many memories of seeing him there. Amos and Matilda had no children. Matilda had a granddaughter, Celia, a girl a little older than mother who became one of her best playmates. Celia taught her to sing songs. Mother remembered Amos and Matilda as being very happy together in Grantsville. Amos played the violin and sang. They each smoked a pipe. One day she and her mother visited them in their home. Matilda sent mother to borrow some soda from a neighbor and then she cooked a good dinner for them, hot biscuits and mushrooms. This was the first time mother had eaten mushrooms. When she was a small girl Amos took her on his lap and asked her who she liked best, him or Grandpa Baker. Grandpa Baker was an elderly gentleman her mother kept house for. She replied that she liked Grandpa Baker best. She was old enough to feel guilty knowing that she should have said that she like her own grandfather best. She remembered when Amos moved to a home near his son Thomas and his wife Lovisa Higsley, and the nice relationship that existed there.
 One day she and Celia were walking in some brush and willows and upon hearing a strange sound mother asked "What was that?" Celia answered, "Oh, that's grandma, She's praying", and they slipped quietly away.
 Mother remembered Matilda's death in Grantsville. She had not been sick for long. Celia was completely broken up at her grandmothers death. They went to the house where Matilda's corpse was "laid out", and were told by people on the porch not to go inside because the smell was bad. Celia cried and felt so terrible. Later Celia was taken away someplace and Amos was again alone. She remembered going to see him and he asked her to sweep up for him. The work was new and very hard for her, she was so young.
 Many of the McBride people moved from Grantsville to Oakley Idaho. At the age of 83 Amos died there. Aaron McBride, son of James McBride wrote me the following regarding his death. "Your grandfather, Amos, I helped to wash him and bury him here in Oakley Idaho, in the year 1885. There were stripes all over his back where the mob whipped him as wide as my finger." Agnes McBride Dummer told me she attended the funeral of Amos in Oakley.
 Amos McBride 1802-1885, A Mormon Pioneer.
