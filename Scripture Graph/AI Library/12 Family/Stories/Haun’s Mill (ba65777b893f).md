# Haun’s Mill

_A story of [[Thomas White McBride (KWJR-J45)|Thomas White McBride]], [[Catherine Ruth John (LLQX-D8P)|Catherine Ruth John]]._

## The story as it was written

Haun’s Mill
Thomas and Catherine McBride listened to the Elders as they taught them of the Restored Gospel of Jesus Christ. They accepted it willingly and soon they were on their way with their family from their home in Ohio, to Missouri to live with the Saints. They moved to an eighty-acre homestead on Shoal Creek, just three-fourths of a mile from Haun's Mill. Little did they realize at that time what they were about to face.
Northern Missouri was in an uproar the last week of October 1838, as mobs were heard in every direction. The mobs burned houses and crops, rustled cattle, detained prisoners, and threatened the Saints with death. There had been a small battle on the banks of the Crooked River involving the State army which had attacked the homes of the Saints and taken three Mormon men prisoners. On hearing the report, Judge Higbee, the first judge of the county, ordered the officer in command in the Mormon settlement of Far West to send out a company to disperse the mob and retake the prisoners.
The Militia of Far West was called and rode out to meet the mob. A battle ensued as the mob was lying in wait in the bushes of the river, and sprang into ambush as the Mormon Militia rode in. One Mormon man was killed and several on both sides were seriously wounded, including Apostle David W. Patten who later died.
Relying solely upon the false reports that the Mormons were hostile and rebellious, Governor Boggs wrote what became known as the Extermination Order, saying “The Mormons must be treated as enemies and must be exterminated or driven from the state, if necessary for the public good.”
Jacob Haun was a convert from Wisconsin. He had moved to Shoal Creek in 1835, hoping to avoid the persecutions his fellow Saints were experiencing elsewhere in Missouri. Haun’s Mill consisted of a mill, a blacksmith shop, a few houses, and a population of about twenty to thirty families at the mill itself and one hundred families in the greater neighborhood.
Immediately after the battle of Crooked River, the Prophet Joseph Smith advised all Saints in the outlying areas to move to Far West or Adam-ondi-Ahman. Unwilling to abandon his property, Jacob Haun disregarded the Prophet’s counsel and instructed the small community to remain. This unwise decision proved fatal.
The fateful day Of October 30th started peaceful enough. Nine wagons with immigrants from Kirtland had arrived at Haun’s Mill and had decided to rest a few days before traveling to Far West. There were many of the Saints gathered at the mill, including Thomas McBride. The banks of Shoal Creek on either side were full of children playing while their mothers were nearby preparing food and washing clothes. Some of the men and fathers were guarding the mills and other property, while others were gathering their crops for their winter consumption. The weather was very pleasant; the sun shone clear; all was tranquil.
All that changed when late in the afternoon approximately 240 men approached Haun’s Mill. The women and children fled into the woods, while the men sought protection in the blacksmith shop which they had previously decided could be used as a fort. David Evans, the military leader of the Saints, swung his hat and cried for peace. The sound of a hundred rifles answered him, most of them aimed at the blacksmith shop. The mob shot mercilessly at everyone in sight, including women, elderly men, and children. Thomas McBride was at the mill during the onslaught and was shot in the chest and then killed with a corn knife.
By the time the mob finished their dastardly deed and rode away, at least seventeen people had been killed and about thirteen more were wounded. Jacob Haun was among the wounded, but he recovered. Years later the Prophet remarked, “At Haun's’ Mill the brethren went contrary to my counsel; if they had not, their lives would have been spared.”
The survivors hid throughout the evening and night, fearing another attack. The next day a few able-bodied men buried the dead in a dry hole that had been dug for a well.
Amos McBride, the son of Thomas and Catherine, lived about three miles from the Mill. After the attack at Haun’s Mill, a mob came to his house in the night. When Amos came to the door with his gun, they grabbed it and dragged him in his nightshirt and bare feet about a mile and a half into the woods, where they tied his arms around a tree, and gave him eighteen lashes with a long hickory stick. Finally they turned him loose calling out threats as he stumbled back to his home.
Another son of Thomas and Catherine McBride was also ruffed up by a mob. Thomas Jr. was at a store near his home, when he was seized by an unruly hoard. They put a rope around his neck and hung him on the porch. A Mr. Gray happened by just in time and jumped through the crowd and cut the rope saying, “I will not see a human being hung that way.” Thomas Jr., limp and helpless, fell to the ground black in the face. In a few minutes he recovered enough to get home, but he died three weeks later, leaving his wife and child to mourn.
James McBride was the third son in the family, but he was spared because he was sick at home the day of the attack at Haun’s Mill.
During the main ambush at Haun’s mill, the rabble entered the blacksmith shop and found ten-year-old Sardius Smith hiding under the blacksmith’s bellows. One of the ruffian’s shot him dead. Alma Smith, Sardius’ seven-year-old brother, witnessed the murder of his father and brother. He, himself, was shot in the hip. Amanda Smith was the mother of little seven-year-old Alma. Now her young son Alma lay pale on the straw with a large hole in his hip, and she felt the weight of the world upon her. During the night, she crept into the cornfield to pray aloud. In her words, “It was as the temple of the Lord to me at that moment. I prayed aloud and most fervently. When I emerged from the corn a voice spoke to me. It was a voice as plain as I ever heard one. It was no silent, strong impression of the spirit, but a voice, repeating a verse of the Saints’ hymn:
‘That soul who on Jesus
hath leaned for repose
I will not, I cannot,
desert to his foes;
That soul, though all hell
should endeavor to shake,
I’ll never,
no never,
no never forsake!
“From that moment I had no more fear. I felt that nothing could hurt me.”
Amanda received revelation that instructed her how to fix Alma’s hip. She carefully followed the promptings of the Spirit and tended to her son’s wounds. Through the mercy of the Lord, Alma grew cartilage where the hip bone had been and completely recovered.
Through faith in the Lord Jesus Christ, women such as Amanda Smith, and Catherine McBride gathered those left in their families and went forward, crossing the state of Missouri to live in Nauvoo, and finally crossing the plains to live in the shadow of the everlasting hills with the Saints of God.
Thomas and Catherine McBride are the parents of James McBride who is the father of Olive Deseret McBride Barrus who was the first wife of Owen Henry Barrus. She died leaving six children to care for, and Owen Henry married Mary Ann Hunter. Owen Henry and Mary Ann Hunter Barrus are the parents of Edith Marian Barrus who is the mother of Deloris Dew, Milo LeRoy Dew, LaMar Owen Dew, and Maxine Dew.
