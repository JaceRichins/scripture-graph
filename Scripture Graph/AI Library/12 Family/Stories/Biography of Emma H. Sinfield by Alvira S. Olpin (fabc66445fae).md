# Biography of Emma H. Sinfield by Alvira S. Olpin

_A story of [[Emma Hannah Sinfield (KWJC-G7W)|Emma Hannah Sinfield]]._

## The story as it was written

In writing a story of the life of my mother I wish to, in the first place, pay tribute to her example of industry and her desire to render service, of her love and devotion to her husband and family. her ideals were of the highest type and the example of loyalty she set for others to follow was of the highest.
Emma Hannah Sinfield was born in Bedford, Bedfordshire, England January 6, 1844 daughter of Samual Sinfield, born March 8, 1820 in Eversholt, Bedfordshire, England and Phebe Cross, born May 13, 1816 in Condicotte Glous, England. Her father and mother had made all preparations to leave England for America when her mother died. This did not change her father's plans, he and his two children, Eliza, eight, and Emma, six, left soon after for America.
The only ships in those days were sailing vessels, so it took them six weeks to cross the ocean. They landed at New Orleans and came up the Mississippi to Council Bluffs. Her father got a chance to drive a church team across the plains. In that way, he and his two children reached Utah. They walked most of the way. When the children became tired, their father would set them on the wagon to ride, but the captain would order them set down. Their feet became so sore they were forced to crawl many times. Emma told of the great herds of buffalo they saw and the dust they made. Often the travellors had to stop while these herds passed.
After arriving in Utah, they lived for some time in Ogden. Emma called it Ogden Hole. For a home, her father dug a hole in the side of the hill and boarded up the front with slabs.
One day while her father was away, an old Indian came to their home. Emma ran inside, pulling the latch string inside. The Indian was angry and beat the door with his rope which was made of buckskin. After a while the Indian left. Emma said she never prayed harder in her life. At that time her sister Eliza was very sick for a long time with some kind of fever that left her quite lame. While living in Ogden they went without bread for six weeks. At the harvest time Emma helped her father glean wheat and at night he would beat the wheat out of the chaff, grind the wheat in the coffee mill and make hotcakes. Emma said it was the sweetest bread she ever ate.
Her father married a widow, named Mrs. Hannah Middleton Hawky, with two children. From this union three children were born.
After this marriage, family life was not so pleasant, so Emma left home at the age of twelve years to earn her own living. She continued to work out until she was married. At the time of Brigham Young married Amelia Folsom, she was the hired girl in the Folsom home. Emma told of the move south when the Johnson Army came to Utah and how the people laid fires all ready to start if their homes were taken. As a young woman, Emma was considered very good looking and she retained much of her beauty throughout her life.
In the year 1864 when Emma and her husband came to Pleasant Grove, they began house keeping with very few pieces of furniture. She tells of gathering cat tails from the swamps and stripping off the heads to make a mattress. And for many years the only kind of a mattress they owned was a straw bed. Emma being a good housekeeper and seamstress soon made their little home cozy and comfortable.
Emma had to work hard in those days as everything had to be prepared in the home. Fruits, vegetables and meat must be stored for winter use. All the clothing must be bade in the home. In early married life she washed, corded and spun the wool into yarn then knitted it into stockings. She even made her husband's clothes. Soap must be made, potatoes grated and the starch washed out of them and dried to be used later in the laundry.
When the United Order was established in Pleasant Grove, she and her husband joined.
Emma was always faithful and true to the religion she had accepted when a child. She was a wonderful helpmate to her husband. She stood by him through sorrow, trouble and adversity. She was the mother of eleven children, six girls and five boys. Her greatest desire was to see her children become useful God fearing men and women.
Emma was always ready and willing to assist with the sick and afflicted. Many homes were blessed by her in times of sickness and death.
Emma was an active worker in the Relief Society. Always trying to visit her teachers district each moth. In those days each pair of teachers carried a basket and received donations which were later used to help a less fortunate person. Emma was a widow for fifteen years. All of her children had the opportunity to attend schools of higher learning and two of her sons filled missions in foreign lands.
She died January 1, 1913.
We were just a common farm family of Pleasant Grove. About 1870 my father had bought the squatters right to a piece of land, about twelve acres, and built a two roomed hard rock house on it. Here the family lived and all but three of the eleven children were born there.
It was necessary for him to raise everything we had to eat. As he developed the land, wheat, corn, potatoes and vegetables were grown. Later came strawberries, raspberries and fruit trees. He believed in having enough work to keep his children employed at home. Just one or two incidents... When the railroad came through Pleasant Grove we used to pick our raspberries, putting them in pans ten to twenty quarts to a pan. Father would meet the train as it came through and would sell the berries to the conductors, engineers, and brakeman. They would bring the pans back the next day with more orders for berries. In that way he found a ready market for berries and other fruits as they came along.
