# Life Story of Sarah Ellen Richins Calderwood

_A story of [[Edward Richins (KW8L-N6R)|Edward Richins]], [[Alma Ether Richins (LLC5-YZB)|Alma Ether Richins]]._

## The story as it was written

The following story is lovingly dedicated to the memory of our mother, Sarah Ellen Richins Calderwood. She was the daughter of Edward and Sarah Beard Richins and was born October 7, 1868.
Their home was about one and a half miles north of Echo Canyon, Utah on the "Pioneer Trail" and near what is known as the "Witch Rocks." The land was purchased by Edward soon after his coming to Utah. He was pleased with the location and built his home there. The house was made of logs which had been cut and hauled in from the canyon. It consisted of three rooms. It had a dirt roof, board floors, and was a comfortable home at that time; and Edward was proud of it.
He had come from England to Utah in 1855. A few years later he met and married Ellen Tipper, who had come from England also. Two little boys came to bless their home, Albert Edward born February 11, 1865 and Alma Ether born December 3, 1866. Little Albert died when about one year old; and after the second baby was born, the mother's health began to fail and continued to do so until she passed away February 15, 1867, thus leaving Edward alone with his infant son, Alma. With the love and help of good relatives and friends, they were all well cared for.
It was in the following summer that Edward met Sarah who also had come from England just previous to this time. After a short courtship, they were married June 16, 1867; and she (Sarah) was happy to care for Ellen's baby son. The following year their first baby, Ellen was born; and she became our mother.
At the time of Mother's birth, there were no doctors close by; and when they were called, they had to travel on horseback. So the doctor didn't get there in time for her birth; but a good neighbor woman known as a "midwife" assisted in the absence of the doctor, who came later.
She was the oldest of twelve children born to them, and she was a healthy baby. The name given to her was after her mother and grandmother, Sarah Ellen. As she grew older, she had plenty of energy and needed someone to be watching her constantly.
As soon as she could walk, she was seen going towards the railroad track which had just been built down Echo Canyon, on past their home, and on down through Weber Canyon. It was not too far from their home. When her mother saw her, she ran quickly to get her off the track as she could hear a train coming around the point of the mountain. She got her off just in time to save her life. The strong breeze from the train blew her clothes around her.
Mother had some unusual experiences in her childhood days. While she was still very small, her mother was doing the regular family wash and using "lye" as a water softener. She put the can of liquid lye on the table and went about her work. Baby Ellen was watching this and wanted the can, so she got on a chair, then on to the table, and tried to drink the lye. In doing this she spilled some of it on the table and tried to lick that up also. This burned her mouth, tongue, and stomach very badly; and she was a very sick little girl. She was given olive oil and cream for about three months to heal her mouth and stomach. When she was healed from this, her mouth was somewhat smaller and also her tongue a little shorter, but this did not affect her speech. Later in her life she had a slight operation on her mouth before she could have dental work done.
At about the age of four or five years she enjoyed being with her brothers as they did their daily chores on the farm. She had gone several times with them to take the calves to the pasture in a canyon a short distance away. One day she decided to go to the canyon alone, only the dog went with her. Her mother missed her and a search began. While trying to find her, the dog came from the canyon wagging his tail and soon started back to the canyon. The boys and a close girlfriend and neighbor, Charlotte Fawcett, followed the dog and found Ellen unharmed.
During her childhood days she became acquainted with Indian children, and they were interesting to her. They would often come and camp on their farm for a short time and ask for produce such as potatoes, flour, etc. Our grandfather always said it was much better to feed the Indians than to fight them.
Mother was very closely associated with Alma, her older brother, and her younger brother, Heber, and loved to be with them. She loved Alma as her own full brother, and this love lasted throughout their lives.
She also associated with a young girl about her age who lived about one fourth mile from their home, and she loved her very much. One day this girl became suddenly very sick with diphtheria, and in a few days she died. Mother felt very badly over her death.
Some time later Mother had a dream which impressed her very much. She dreamed she saw a small white cloud in the sky; and as she watched it, it came closer to her until it settled to the ground just in front of her. As it settled, it seemed to open up; and this little girl was standing there with several other girls and their Savior was there in their midst. The little girl stepped forward and said, "Ellen, we have come to get you." She ran into the house to ask her mother if she could go and her mother said, "No, she couldn't spare her." She ran back and told the little girl she couldn't go. The girl stepped back into the cloud and ascended into the sky while Mother stood watching. She never did forget this, but it was only a dream.
It was one day in early summer when she was about six years old, that she and Alma were strolling through the meadow fields near the Weber River which flowed along the border of the fields. Their dog was with them; and as they got to the edge of the river, they began throwing sticks into the water to see the dog swim in and bring them back to them. Mother got a larger and longer stick' and as one end went into the stream of raging water, she became overbalanced and fell into the stream. (It was the springtime of the year when the river was overflowing its banks in many places from the melting snow in the canyons.) She was washed down the stream for quite some distance. As she was washed to the edge of a bank, she saw some roots of a tree and held on to them keeping her head above the water, while Alma ran to the house to tell her mother. A young man by the name of Bill (or William) Paskett, who was working for them, ran quickly to the river and rescued her. He said it was a hard struggle and the water was deep. She was very weak, chilled, and almost exhausted. Bill carried her home where she received the tender loving care of a worried mother. Throughout her life she was always grateful to Bill Paskett and always said, "He saved my life." All were thankful that her life was spared.
Mother told of her grandfather, Thomas Beard, who came to stay with them occasionally. He liked "homemade" beer and made it himself. One day he was making it outside near their milk cellar which was near the edge of a bank. Mother was watching him and probably getting in his way. He became angry with her and picked up a large brass bucket and hit her on the hip with it. She rolled down the hill into a bunch of wild rose bushes and rocks until her mother went to her rescue. He was happy when playing his violin.
As she was growing older, she was taught to work in the house and also to do outside chores. At about eight years old she was a great help to her mother and brothers; and as she was the oldest girl, they probably expected much of her.
As time passed by, the family increased in numbers as a new baby came to them about every two years until there were twelve children. Of these there were three girls and six boys. There was work for the boys also as they were growing older and helping with the farm work. Each year a vegetable garden was planted and well cared for. There was a large patch of prize-winning potatoes grown also, and many of these were shipped out of state.
The hay and grain fields were very good also with plenty of wheat for flour. Their meat was beef, mutton, and pork, with chickens for meat and eggs. There was plenty of milk, butter, and cheese. A few bushes of gooseberry, currants, and dewberries provided fruit for the family. So we see that most of their food was grown on their own farm land, and they grew to be strong and healthy children with good substantial meals.
Mother was a healthy girl and endured many hardships even in her younger years, but she learned to assume responsibilities in the home. She had enjoyment, too, with her brothers and neighbors who lived a short distance away. One of her closest girlfriends was Charlotte Fawcett (Beard), who often stayed with her several days at a time. They loved to gather wild flowers and fruits, such as currants, strawberries, etc. Occasionally the girls and boys would walk to Henefer (a distance of about three miles) to Sunday School or on holidays when there was something special to go to.
They also enjoyed going to the Weber River to catch fish. The river flowed through the lowlands of their property, and very often they could see wild animals out in the bushes. On one occasion when Mother and Heber were gathering wild strawberries, they saw a wolverine coming towards them. It didn't try to harm them but sat up and looked at them until the dog ran after it, then it ran back into the brush.
The clothing for the family was all very plain. Shoes were very expensive so the children went without shoes most of the summer except for the best.
When Mother was about ten years old, she went with her brother, Heber, to drive cows out of the grain field; and as they were coming back along the railroad track, her foot began to sting on the heel and was bleeding from two tiny holes. (She had no shoes on.) She began to feel sick before they got home. Her mother thought she may have been bitten by a rattlesnake. As they went back along the track, there were traces of blood along the wooden ties, and the snake was hidden under one of them. She was very sick, and they contacted a doctor at Wanship who couldn't come to their home. He told them to cut the wound open and pour ammonia into it and give her whiskey to drink until she was dizzy. This would counteract the poison of the snake. Her leg was very dark to her hip, and she was very sick. After about three weeks, she was much improved and soon well from the snake bite.
It was not unusual for the group of Washakie Indians to camp on the Richins' property asking for potatoes, flour, etc. The buck Indians (men) killed wild animals and brought them in for the squaws (women) to cut up and prepare for food. One day a young squaw was seen going out into the bushes with a blanket. A short time later, she came back with a little papoose (baby) in her arms wrapped in the blanket. This was all interesting to the Richins family.
The members of this family were always encouraged to attend church. On Sunday morning they would walk to Henefer to Sunday School or meeting in the afternoon. They also went to Henefer on holidays or other occasions if there was something special. They had to walk most of the time.
Mother has told us about the enjoyment they had at the children's dances which were always held on holidays in the afternoon. One Christmas day there was a dance at Henefer. It was a very cold day, below zero, but Mother and Heber wanted to go so they walked down the railroad track. Before they got to town, they were both so cold they couldn't keep the tears back. They didn't go to the dance but went to the home of their Uncle Stephen Beard where they could get warm. Their uncle and aunt comforted them and found their feet and legs were frostbitten which was very painful for a few days, but they were soon well again.
In the summertime they would often hear or see wild animals in the trees as they were walking to town. It was a nice day in summer that Mother and her brother, Alma, had gone for a walk toward Henefer. As they went along the road, they came to a large water tank near the railroad track that was full of water for the engines of the trains that came along the track. It was at the mouth of a canyon (later called Tank Canyon). As the trains came along the track, they stopped there to get water in the engine before going up Echo Canyon. When they got to the tank, Alma was curious to know how it worked so he climbed up the long ladder and pulled on a chain that let a big pipe come down; and the water came gushing out. Well he tried hard to get it back, but, of course, this was impossible. They both were frightened, realizing what they had done, so they both began to run and didn't lose any time getting out of sight of anyone. It's probable that no one ever knew who did it.
Another time when Mother was alone walking along the track, she could hear a train. But as she went on, she saw a porcupine had been wounded badly by a train and was making a pitiful noise.
As Mother grew older, her responsibilities increased. By the time she was thirteen or fourteen years old, her father had increased the number of his cattle, especially the milk cows. In the summertime the boys were busy helping their father in the fields of hay, grain, etc. There were about thirteen cows to be milked night and morning and almost that many calves to be fed; and this big responsibility was given to Mother. She had a hard time during the harvest time with so much work and no sister old enough to help much. Many times she got up about four o'clock in the morning to start milking. (It was all done by hand, and the calves were fed also.) The milk all had to be strained into pans and put on shelves or a table in the milk cellar. When the cream raised to the top of the milk, it was taken off with a milk skimmer and put into a big jar or crock to be made into butter. She made about 20 pounds of butter each week in a big wooden churn. It stood on four legs with a handle on one end and was turned by hand. The butter was put in salt brine in large barrels until the fall of the year. By that time there would be three or four barrels of butter to be molded into one pound size squares. This was to be sold at 35 cents per pound. If sold in summer, it would be 10 cents per pound. Most of it was shipped out of state.
In her busy life Mother didn't get time for much relaxation or fun, but occasionally she went to the neighbors to be with a girlfriend, Charlotte; and they enjoyed being together; or they could play ball with her brothers or just entertain themselves.
It was about this time in her life that her mother had twin baby boys named Willard and Wilford. These little boys were not very strong at birth, and their mother wasn't very well either. She remained in her room with the babies for about three months. By this time the babies were still very poorly; and about a month later one of these precious little boys, Wilford, passed away.
Some time later on the other little boy, Willard, met with a very sad accident. He wasn't yet two years old when he was playing around the kitchen and accidentally fell backward into a bucket of hot water. He was scalded so badly that no medication or treatment helped him, and he died about a month later. Mother has told us of this very sad experience which they could never forget.
For a family this size (and having sickness, too), there was plenty of washing to be done, mostly by hand. Mother usually did the washing in the evening or nighttime. She said she always tried to get all the white clothes on the line by the time the passenger train went past their home at 11 p.m.
She usually scrubbed the board floor on hands and knees after the family had gone to bed on Saturday night. She has told us that many times she would be so tired at night that she has practically thrown herself onto the bed and slept all night with her clothes on.
Mother has never said much about her school days, only that they were very limited. She remembered going for about three months to school in Echo. The school at that time was held in one room of the big Asper House with Mary Jane Asper (later Weaver) as the teacher. She had attended college and probably taught reading and writing during the short time Mother and other members of the family attended school. From then on she was self-educated.
About the age of sixteen she was beginning to take time out to fix herself up and look nice; and she was getting to be nicer looking, too. She decided there was something else in this world to be interested in besides work, but she still did her work.
She knew a young boy who was George Wilmot. He was very attractive and nice to her, and she thought he was a nice boy. It wasn't long until she had her first date with him. Some time after this she had a date with Arthur Richins. He was always called "Swift" probably because he was so slow. He took her to a dance at Henefer and other places until she was rather "sweet on him" (as the saying goes). Later on he told her he was "just a friend". This was a "let down" she said as she really liked him and thought he was her "Beau" (meaning boyfriend).
Some time later in the fall of the year a fine looking young man came to the Richins' home to inquire if they would like to have their grain threshed. It was harvest time, and there weren't many threshing machines in that area. He was riding a horse and said his name was Alex Calderwood, and he came from Coalville. Edward was glad of the offer to get the grain threshed out. It seemed that Alex and his brothers owned a threshing machine and were doing custom work in the valley. A few days later, they came to do the job which took two or three days to do it.
During this time Alex became very well acquainted with Ellen as they ate their meals there while on the job. After finishing the Richins' grain they were asked to thresh for other farmers, so Alex made his trips down quite often always making a short stop at the Richins' home.
He was the son of Alexander and Margaret Salmon Calderwood and was born July 8, 1864. He was the oldest of eleven children. His parents came from Scotland and settled in the Coalville, Utah area.
Mother had found a place in his heart; and she liked him, too. At that time they didn't have vehicles for transportation, so he came on horseback to see her on Sundays. Sometimes they walked to Henefer to church and strolled slowly back again. Mother has told us that one time he came down to take her to Coalville to a dance. He was riding a horse; and she rode the horse also; and when they got there, she was so stiff and sore that she couldn't dance (as much as she loved dancing). So they spent the evening chatting with friends and having a good visit themselves. This gave them an opportunity of knowing each other better, and they found they had much in common in their lives. Some time later Alex had better ways of traveling, and they went to Coalville, Henefer, or Echo to dances during the latter part of their two years' courtship.
A story is told of Ellen's brothers having some pet birds (magpies) which could be trained to talk by splitting their little tongues. One day she expected Alex; and as she was getting cleaned up, she stepped out onto the porch to empty the wash basin (without her blouse on). The magpie saw her. When Alex came, it said, "Alex, Ellen's got her dress off. Alex, Ellen's got her dress off."
Mother enjoyed telling us about Echo and the dances which were had there about this time. It was a railroad station and a boom town for the railroad men as there were many of them employed by the Union Pacific Railroad Co. In Echo there was a big hotel, store, school, church, and three or four saloons (liquor stores). A large dance hall was upstairs above the hotel. Music was hired to play for the dances.
Occasionally there would be a Masquerade Ball where everyone was expected to be dressed in disguise to be someone different than himself. It could be anyone from a "ho-bo" to a queen and each wore a mask over their face. Mother had a sister-in-law (Laura Bull who married Moroni Richins) who was very artistic and an expert in making costumes for such occasions. Costumes could be hired from a company in Salt Lake, which most people did, especially the men.
These four, Mother, Laura, Father, and Moroni, decided to dress up and go separately to the dance. Laura fixed beautiful frilly dresses, fixed their hair, and got good looking masks for Mother and herself so they wouldn't be known by any of their friends or by Alex or Moroni. They went to the dance, and everyone was guessing who the other person was. When the music started, the young man went to the girl he wanted to dance with and very politely held out his arm to her, and she bowed her head and took his arm, and they danced without speaking to each other. The dances were mostly square dances such as "plain quadrilles" or "Virginia reels". The dancing went on until time for intermission when the musicians and dancers rested. At this time all who were masked formed a big circle all around the hall with the young man choosing the girl he would like for his partner for the rest of the evening to be by his side. Of course, Alex (our father) thought he had chosen the "Belle of the Ball" and Moroni felt the same about his choice. At this time everyone was asked to take their masks off. Of course, Alex had chosen Ellen as his partner, and Moroni had chosen Laura but claimed they were not right sure. (But someone could have whispered?) Refreshments were served and more dancing went on, probably round dancing. A few years later Masquerade dances were enjoyed at Henefer also.
In the springtime of the year 1887 they were making plans to be married. At that time it was recommended that everyone be rebaptized before going to the temple. The baptisms were done in the river. It was March, and there was quite thick ice on the river. A hole was cut through the ice, and Mother was baptized by her Uncle Stephen Beard, and he (Alex) was baptized also.
They set their wedding day for March 16, 1887 to be in the Logan L.D.S. Temple. They traveled by wagon and horses the day before their marriage and came back the next day after marriage. A wedding reception was planned at the home of her parents. About 75 relatives and friends came to wish them happiness. Plenty of good food was served, and many lovely gifts were received. The bride looked very lovely in her blue velvet dress with "hoops" in the skirt and nicely trimmed. Mother kept many of her nice gifts for many years, then gave them to her children.
Soon after they were married, Mother went with her husband to Almy, Wyoming where he was employed in the coal mine. They were very happy and lived there until the spring of 1889 when there was an epidemic of measles in the area. Father contracted them and was very sick. As soon as he was well enough to be out, they went back to Echo and lived with Mother's parents a short time. They were expecting their first baby which was a girl born April 12, 1889. They gave her the name Sarah Margaret. About three weeks later they moved to a house about one and a half miles west of Henefer. It was an old log house with dirt roof and poor floors. It was probably one of the very earliest in Henefer.
Mother has often told us about the house leaking when they had a heavy rain storm, and she had to put pans or other utensils on the beds and floors to catch the muddy water as it came through the roof. This house and farm were owned by Edward Richins (Mother's father). We stayed in this old house about three years then we moved into a new and nicer log house which was built by Edward and our father. It was only a short distance from the old house.
We lived on this farm about twelve years, during which time Mother gave birth to six other children namely, Alex, Herschel, Earl, Eunice, Ray, and Melva. She had a lot of sickness and many hardships during these years.
We had some very good neighbors and friends who visited often; and when the children were well, she really enjoyed herself. Some of the neighbors often came to our home to spend the evening playing cards. One time her baby, Earl, got sick and was taking convulsions, and she was really worried about him. Someone came to the house and asked her how she could expect her baby to get well when she had the "Devil's plaything" in her house (meaning the cards). She immediately went to her cupboard drawer and got the cards and put them in the stove to burn. From that time on she never played another game of cards or allowed them in their home, except cards for children.
She always tried to keep her house clean. The log walls inside were kept white with a solution of white lime mixed with water known as "white wash" and put on with a brush. This task took a lot of time and energy as did many other home tasks.
She was thankful and happy to be living in a new house. The home was built at the mouth of a canyon and sometimes there would be wild animals come down the canyon especially coyotes which would come at night and howl for hours (or it seemed that long). She has told us about one coming right close to the house one night when Father was away, and it was making a terrible noise as it kept going around the house. She was afraid it would get into the house through a window which was very low and close to the ground, but it finally went away. The next day she was told by her brother (who stayed with them overnight) that he had been hunting the day before and had shot the nose off a coyote, and it had come down the canyon in such suffering that it would have fought anything. Even the dogs were frightened of it.
Sometimes there would be rattlesnakes around the home. One night as it was getting dark our brother, Earl, went outside to the coal house to get a bucket of coal. He almost got a rattlesnake on the scoop shovel with the coal. He called to Mother and she immediately went out and killed it. She always told us children to watch for snakes, especially during the summertime.
Mother had a hard time with so much work and sickness with herself or some of the children, but she always had great faith that all would be well if we lived as we should. As children, we all had the children's diseases.
About a month after her sixth child, Ray, was born, she began to get swelling in her stomach. The doctor could not find the trouble and her condition was getting worse until she was very sick. Her loved ones were very much concerned about her condition. Neighbors and friends were offering help in every way possible. She always had great faith that she would get well and also in administration. Two elderly faithful men walked from town to administer to her and give her their blessings. These men were Brother Robert Jones and James Paskett. The doctor decided that she had a growth which was a tumor, and she must go to Salt Lake for an operation as this was serious. Arrangements were made to get her to Salt Lake as soon as possible to a hospital.
She was taken to the railroad station at Henefer in a white top buggy drawn by a team of horses and was lying on a cot (or stretcher). Arrangements had been made for the train to stop to take her on. Father and the doctor went on the train with her. Relatives and friends followed them to the train, as they were very much concerned about her condition and if she would come back alive. One of our neighbors (not of our faith) came to see her before she left. He put his hand on her head and said, "May God speed you back to your family."
She was taken to the Holy Cross Hospital where Dr. E. J. Hosmer who went with her, did the operation. He removed a large tumor. Some of the orderlies who cleaned up after the operation said, "that woman will never live. They have taken her liver out," but it was clotted blood. After the operation Mother recovered very well, continually thanking the Lord for his blessings to her. We were all very thankful to have her home again after two weeks stay in the hospital.
We children stayed at our two grandparents' homes. Many friends offered their services in many ways. Our Grandmother Richins came and stayed with us about two weeks after Mother returned home. Then a girl from town came and helped with the housework. Mother was soon able to go on with her daily responsibilities.
It was not too long after this that she discovered she had a hernia on her side; and in a short time, she had another hernia on the opposite side. She had these for the rest of her life; and in her last few years, they caused her a lot of pain and distress.
When this baby boy was about a year old, Mother had a terrible cold which turned to pneumonia; and she couldn't seem to throw it off. After about ten days she was very seriously ill, and the doctor was very much concerned. He stayed by her bedside watching her carefully until about midnight when he said, "She has passed the crisis." She began to improve--again she had faith through prayer and administration that she would be made well.
Sometimes there were accidents in the home with the children. One morning when Mother was preparing breakfast on the black flat top cook stove, she had a long handle kettle of oatmeal mush cooking. Two of the children were playing with a rubber ball and threw it over the stove. Their little brother, Raymond, (about two and a half years old) put up his little arms to catch the ball and hit the handle of the mush kettle and spilled the hot cereal all down one side of his face, ear, and neck. This was a very bad burn, and the skin all peeled off; and when it was healed, he had terrible red scars on his face for many years.
When Margaret was about nine years old, she had her hands burned by a rope being pulled swiftly through them while helping her father unload some hay.
It was about this time that we were blessed with another baby girl in the family who was Melva, a welcome little sister. When all was well at home, everyone was happy.
Everyone seemed to have time to go visiting with neighbors or relatives. Very often we went to visit at our Grandparents Richins' or to our Grandmother Calderwood's family and spent the full day, and they came to visit us quite often. They never failed to come on our father's birthday which was July 8. Mother always had a nice dinner prepared for them.
Our parents both loved to dance, and one of the main features of entertainment in our town was dancing, which they enjoyed quite often. Mother's brother Alma, and his wife, Hattie, lived in Henefer; and when there was a dance, we children were taken to their home and put to bed while both our parents went to the dance and had a good time. Occasionally they went to Echo after a dance where they could buy refreshments.
Mother and Father always taught us to honor the Sabbath day, and we walked to Sunday School except when the weather was bad. We all went to sacrament meeting as often as possible. For many years Father was a ward (home) teacher, and he worked in M.I.A. during the time we lived in Henefer.
It was about the year 1900 when my Grandmother Calderwood asked Father if he would go and operate her farm. Her husband had died several years previous, and the other brothers were employed elsewhere. So our parents felt it their duty to go and take care of the farm for her. The farm was about two and a half miles north of Coalville and was well equipped with farm machinery and livestock.
We moved from the farm in Henefer in the springtime, about March. Mother was cooperative with Father and his mother in making the move and hoped for the best. We all knew we would miss our many good friends in Henefer, but we went back occasionally to visit them, especially when there was something special to go to in town.
We were very happy with our new location. The family home was much larger with ten rooms. We didn't use them all, but we were glad to have a larger and nicer house. Our close neighbors were the William Carruth family who were very good people and good neighbors. The school house was about a mile away. It consisted of one large room with grades one to eight. There were only a few students in each grade. We walked to school carrying our lunch in tin buckets. The lunch was usually plum jam sandwiches which we always enjoyed.
After about a year, Mother gave birth to another baby boy. He was given the name of Ross Richins Calderwood. He wasn't permitted to remain with us very long to our sorrow. When only three months old, he became ill with bronchial pneumonia. Mother gave him loving care and did everything possible for him; but he passed away November 13, 1902 and was buried in Coalville.
During the time we lived on this farm, there was work for everyone. We had milk cows, and Mother made butter to sell to regular customers. Every Saturday morning she went to deliver her freshly-made butter and bought groceries with the money received.
In her leisure time she liked to go to the river to catch fish. She usually took one of the children and a can of worms and nearly always had good luck. She always enjoyed fishing.
As time went on, another baby girl came to bless our home who was given the name LaRue. We were very happy with this baby girl. Each child brought happiness to our home.
With the family increasing in number, there was more work to be done; and more food had to be prepared especially when the fruit season was on. Every year Mother and Father would go to Ogden or Riverdale area in the wagon and get a load of fruit to be bottled or canned. There were peaches, pears, plums, tomatoes, etc. which kept us all very busy for a few days. They also bought cabbage, onions, carrots, squash, etc. to be put in a big pit or cellar for winter use. Mother managed everything well so there was no waste or spoilage.
Another event that we looked forward to was "threshing time" for the grain in the fall of the year. There were about a dozen men working to do the job. We were all busy then, too, preparing meals for these men; but everyone seemed to be happy even though they were tired.
It was in the fall of the year 1908 when Mother was expecting her tenth baby, that we moved over to Coalville just for the winter--then back to the farm. On November 7 another baby girl was born. She was a beautiful baby and was given the name Dora Ellen. At birth the doctor and nurse could see a deformity in her little hands which grieved us all very much. A short time later the doctor discovered a bad leakage in her heart. With this condition, Mother was giving her very tender nursing care.
After a few weeks, she began to cough which developed to be whooping cough, and she was in a very serious condition for several weeks before she began to improve. Previous to this time, Mother had been enjoying a cup of tea as many others did, especially the English people; but she felt within herself that it was not right to be using it. She humbled herself and made a promise to her Heavenly Father that she would never drink tea again if he would make her baby well and healthy. From that time the baby began to improve and grew to be a healthy child. Besides her sickness in infancy, she had a real handicap to overcome with her hands. This required much perseverance and patience of both Mother and child. During the years from childhood on she overcame her problems, which was difficult to do; and she was able to do as well with her hands as anyone else and better than most of us, especially with her beautiful handwork which she loved to do so efficiently. She was always happy and had a wonderful personality.
It was about the year 1910 that the Calderwood farm had to be sold as the Echo Dam, or Reservoir, was to be built; and the water would cover the farmland. At that time Father bought some land farther up this canyon and had to live on it, or "homesteaded it", for a short length of time. Mother and the younger children lived with Father in a tent one summer while their log house was being built. It had three rooms, which were two bedrooms and a large kitchen, and the necessary furniture to live comfortably. About this same time they were buying a home in Coalville, which they moved into, and lived there the remainder of their lives, except during the summer months when they lived in the canyon on their dry farm. On weekends they went back to their home in Coalville to spend Sunday.
The road from Coalville to their farm was not good at that time. It was quite high on the mountainside in places and was rough, rocky, and hazardous for traveling. One day as Father, Mother, LaRue, and Dora were going to the farm, they were riding in a wagon with two spring seats on it. The road was rough around the mountainside and Father was driving carefully; but one of the wagon wheels hit a big rock; and the back seat tipped backwards, throwing Mother and LaRue out on the rocks. They were both badly bruised, which was painful; and Mother was sick for several days. However, there were no serious injuries.
Father and the older boys worked hard clearing the ground and planting the crops. After a few years they had large fields of grain and some alfalfa. They had a very good garden where they grew many kinds of vegetables; and there were potatoes grown, too. The soil was very good and produced a good crop of most everything that was planted. At harvesting time when the Summit County Fair was on, they usually got first prize on their wheat, barley, potatoes, and other produce such as peas, beets, corn, radishes (white and red), etc.
Threshing time was hard work, too, for two or three days while filling the big bins with grain. Many meals were prepared from the produce of their garden except meat; and for dessert there was usually wild currant pie or apple rolley-polley pudding, which was a special treat.
We womenfolk were always on hand to help at threshing time, and some would stay overnight. We couldn't sleep well because of the rats overhead in the attic with their rap, tap, tap most of the night with their long tails. Everyone was busy early in the morning preparing meals for about 12 or 14 men and several women and children. The big threshing machine was run by horsepower and large belts. There were about four teams of horses. Everyone was happy when the grain was in the bins. Father always said, "Never let your wheat bin get empty. Always keep plenty on hand."
After the threshing was done, it was late in the evening; and Father, Mother, and their little grandson, Buddy, (about three or four years old) were riding back to Coalville with Harvey (their son) in his old Ford car. Before they got to Coalville it was getting dark, and the car lights went out. As they were going into Coalville, there was a water ditch with a plank bridge to cross over. Father was trying to light the way with a flashlight; and Mother kept saying, "Harvey, do be careful." He said jokingly, "don't worry, Grandma, you are just as safe as if you were home in your rocking chair." A few minutes later they came to the ditch and went off the plank bridge with the two side wheels of the car; it tipped over sideways into the ditch. There wasn't much water in the ditch, but little Buddy was screaming as he seemed to have gone out first and was in the water. Mother said, "Oh, get Buddy out before he is drowned," and Harvey said, "He isn't going to drown, Grandma, as long as he can make that much noise." No one was hurt, and they walked home safely.
After a few years of dry farming, Father and the boys were contemplating having another project to work on besides the farm. They talked it over with Mother, and decided they could raise foxes there in the canyon. Preparations were made by buildings, pens, etc. The following year they were raising fox. This took constant care, and someone stayed there in the canyon to take care of them. After about two years they realized the foxes were not paying off so they sold them all.
Later on they decided to grow turkeys which could be done in the summer and early fall. This also required constant daily care to avoid loss of the young birds. When it was time to kill them in the fall, they were in real good condition. Much help was needed to kill and get the feathers off so many of them. There were more women than men. When they were ready for market, they lost out again; and this ended turkey growing.
About this time, the older boys of the family were getting married and supporting themselves. Father was taking care of the farm by himself except in the summer months when he needed help from his family. Mother was in good health and had been helping her own family in cases of sickness. She was always on hand when her grandchildren were born and gave both mother and baby good care for the ten days or more that the mother was in bed. She fixed extra special meals for the mother of the baby.
Besides taking care of her own, she did nursing for the local doctor for a few years. During this time she went to Salt Lake City to take care of her mother who was very sick with cancer. She left her family for six weeks and gave her mother tender loving care until she was called home to her own family who were sick with measles. After returning home to take care of her family, she was stricken with a bad cold; and in a few days she had pneumonia. Her mother passed away, and she was unable to ever see her again or attend the funeral services for her.
She continued her nursing which was mostly maternity cases. She assisted Drs. French and Oldham in delivering about 150 babies and took care of mother and baby for about ten days. She always had her nice white front apron, which was nicely trimmed, to wear when nursing. As she grew older, she discontinued nursing except for sickness in her own families where she was always ready to help.
When Father reached his seventieth birthday on July 8, his brothers, sisters, and all his own family came to spend the day with him on the dry farm in the canyon. This was a large group--some coming long distances. Everyone brought their own lunch except homemade ice cream, cake, and the drink, of which there was plenty. The lunch was eaten country style on the tables or on the grass in the shade of quaking aspen trees. After the meal there was visiting, talks, and tales told of past experiences while living on their farm at the mouth of the canyon; and a few jokes were told by some of the menfolks. The day wouldn't be complete without a scotch song or two led by Uncle Robert. Everyone was happy and enjoyed the day in the canyon.
When we think of days to remember, our wedding day comes to our minds as a very special day. It was March 16, 1887 that was that special day for our Mother and Father. After 25 years they were celebrating their Silver Wedding Anniversary by having friends and relatives come to their home to greet them and leave their best wishes with them.
Twenty five years later we were honoring them on their 50th anniversary or their Golden Wedding Day. A party was planned by the family, and invitations were sent out to many friends and relatives to come to the Coalville Opera House to help them celebrate this event. A dinner party was planned for six o'clock in the evening for the immediate family and a few neighbors. It was held in the Dormitory Building at Coalville.
After dinner everyone enjoyed a program which had been prepared and games were played. It began with a song by three small children who were Bobby Calderwood, Arthur Richins (two grandsons), and Bobby Richins (a cousin), who played the guitar also for them. There was also a song by Bonnie Jean Calderwood and Bobby Calderwood titled, "On Our Golden Wedding Day."
A game that we remember well was a "cracker eating contest." Soda crackers were hanging by strings which were all fastened to a line across the room. The object was for those who could eat the most crackers in a limited time to win a prize. There were several in the group trying to win, but they all started laughing which made it difficult to swallow the crackers. Our brother, Alex, was trying so hard to win and his mouth was so full of crackers that he couldn't swallow them or blow them out, and he started to choke and strangle. At this point one of the neighbor boys, Ray Carruth, quickly picked up a bucket and ran to Alex so he could empty his mouth, nose, and throat and be able to breathe again--which he did. Of course this caused a lot of laughter; and at this time, the tears were running down Alex's face; but he was always ready to try anything that anyone else could do.
The party continued on, and there were other games and interesting numbers given. This ended the dinner party.
At 8 p.m. we were all on our way to the Opera House where Mother and Father were happy to welcome so many friends and relatives and receive greetings from each. Mother was wearing a beautiful blue velvet dress, and Father wore a nice dark suit. Flowers made the occasion more cheerful. A program had been arranged as follows:
Prayer: A relative (?)
Song: Granddaughters Fae, June, and Etna
Talk: Member of family, 5 minutes, son, Ray
Dance: Small Granddaughters Wilma and Louise Rasmussen
Song: Daughters Margaret, Eunice, Melva, LaRue, and Dora
Talk: Father's brother, 5 minutes, Robert Calderwood
Song: Grandsons Bobby Calderwood, Arthur Richins, and Bobby Richins who played
 guitar
Talk: Mother's brother, 5 minutes, Moroni Richins
Song: Sons Alex, Herschel, Earl, and Ray
Talk: Neighbor, 5 minutes, Ray Carruth (comic)
Presentation: Ray's family
Punch and cookies were served to all. This was a day to be remembered.
After Mother reached her seventieth year, her health began to fail. She was suffering with a growth in her side which doctors said was a serious condition. She was taken to Salt Lake City to specialists who recommended radium treatments. These treatments had a bad effect on her to the extent that she was very seriously ill. She was at the home of her daughter, Eunice, and husband, Ephraim Taylor. She always had faith that she would be well again and requested they call the elders in frequently to administer to her and give her their blessings. Our father was there also to assist in the ordinances and help care for her. Eunice gave her mother tender loving care for about six weeks until she was taken back to her home and family to recuperate and get well. Through her faith and testimony of our Savior, her health was restored. She instilled this faith in the hearts of her children and tried to teach them to be good wives, good husbands, and good citizens.
Some time later, Mother had a great desire for a family gathering of her brothers and sisters, as they had not visited for quite some time. A family reunion was planned for the Edward Richins family including their companions. (Parents were deceased.) This was to be held at the old family home, which was about one and a half miles from Echo and close by the "Witch Rocks". Emery and Gladys Jones were living there at the time. (Gladys was a daughter of Nephi.)
Everyone was enthused about this event as they had not been together as a group for visiting and socializing for many years. It was summertime and everything was green and beautiful, and it was good to be together again at the old home. A very nice meal was enjoyed by all as they recalled fond memories of their earlier years. Interesting events and experiences of "By Gone Days" were related by each one. Those present were Alma, Ellen, Moroni, Nephi, Ebbenezer, Joseph, Jared, George, and Florence with all companions who could be there. Pictures were taken and it was a happy day for all.
Though Mother was in her declining years, her health was good; and she was able to care for herself. However, her daughter, Dora, and husband, Joe, were close by and gave her loving care through her remaining years. She also visited with other members of her family.
Throughout her life she loved flowers and had many beautiful house plants, some of which were very special to her; and she was proud of them. Many of her friends and relatives would stop in to visit her and enjoy her beautiful flowers.
She never did accumulate great wealth, but she was rich beyond measure with the love of family, friends, and associates--and faith in the Gospel of Jesus Christ. She attended her meetings as long as she was physically able to do so, but the time came when her health began to fail and her strength seemed to leave her. After about three days she passed away suddenly but peacefully. Her death came as a shock and sorrow to all, February 27, 1952, at her home. Funeral services and burial in Coalville were March 1, 1952.
Her family felt that her sudden passing came as a fulfillment to a promise given her in a Patriarchal Blessing on February 9, 1887, by Joseph L. Robinson, where in part he said, "...and because of thy faith and diligence, no good thing will be withheld from thee. Thou mayest live to be changed in the twinkling of an eye from mortal to immortal."
It is interesting to know, too, that Mother had attended Summit Stake Conference just three days prior to her death.
