# Mary Park Shepherd (1836-1924)

_A story of [[Richard Shepherd (KWJ5-L47)|Richard Shepherd]], [[Ann Henderson (KWJ5-L4M)|Ann Henderson]]._

## The story as it was written

Author unknown
 On the 13th of November, 1836, Mary Park Shepherd was born to Richard and Ann Eltringham Shepherd. She was christened in Christ Church, a beautiful old edifice in North Shields Parish, Tynemouth-on-Tyne, Cumberland County, England. Records show that the Shepherd family were educated people, prosperous, cultured, religious, and highly respected in the community.
 Here in this wind-swept country, which lies high above the waters of the North Sea on a rugged, rock-bound promontory, this loving and wise couple reared their family in comfortable and rather pretentious circumstances. Nine children were born of this union. Their father, Richard Shepherd, was a master mariner in the service of his King, for forty years. He owned his own ship, “The Comfort”, which was skippered by a professional seaman, while Richard, himself, sailed “The Emptess”, in the service of his country, as Captain.
 The children all grew up loving the sea, and adventures, with their adored skipper, Captain Shepherd. For any of them to suffer the pangs of seasickness was unheard of. A Shepherd was immune. It was altogether, a comfortable, colorful, satisfying life they lived.
 Captain Shepherd made long voyages to the Orient, or other distant countries, of several months duration. He loved to return to his home a little sooner than he was expected, to surprise his Ann and the children. Pushing the front door open he would toss his cap into the vestibule and call out, “Ahoy! Ahoy, there! All hands aboard.”
All hands would come to engulf him with open arms and there would be a joyous reunion. Out of his bulging pockets would come money bags full of gold coin, the reward for his voyage. Then, from his luggage would come presents for everyone, all having been selected with great care.
 Ann Eltringham Henderson Shepherd was a woman who maintained a well-ordered home for her family. She insisted upon good manners and orderly conduct from her children.
 Mary Park Shepherd was the fifth child born to the Shepherds. Margaret, born two years later, became her adored companion. One brother died in his youth, and two sons and one daughter died in their infancy, so the Shepherd family knew sorrow and grief, as well as the pleasant things of life.
 One day Ann Shepherd was at home alone. Answering a knock at the door she faced two well-mannered gentlemen who asked if they might go in and talk with her. Ann was content with her own religion but when the Latter-day Saint missionaries explained a few of the rudiments of the Gospel to her that first day, she was enthralled with their message and urged them to return so that she might hear all that they had to say regarding this new religion. It sounded feasible from the beginning, the doctrine of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. She called all the children in to hear the missionaries teach the Gospel even the two sons-in-law. Also Frederick Andrew Wallace, Robert’s nephew, who was courting Mary and was in their home at that time. They all listened, believed, and were baptized, and when Richard returned from his voyage, dear, precious Ann, his wife, prevailed upon him to join the Church.
 Immediately, then, she urged, nothing would do but that they join the others in the Church who were going to Zion, in the United States of America. Richard could not share her enthusiasm to sell out, make all the necessary sacrifices, leave family and friends and even their country above all, their beloved England, forever. He hesitated, knowing that this “Zion”, in America, seemed too far removed and too unpromising to be attractive. However, as a result of pleading and persuasion, he decided to sacrifice his life’s accomplishments, his business and his ambitions. He would take the family to Zion. When his decision was once made, he sold out his business interests, his ship, and finally, with great sadness in his soul, he wrote his letter of resignation to His Majesty, the King, and sacrificed his cherished commission.
 The preparations to leave England took considerable arranging and effort, but as time went on, Ann became increasingly anxious to leave. Word had gone out that the Shepherds, had joined “that Mormon Church”, and were going to America. The Vicar and members of the Church of England would have no communication with these families of long years staunch membership. Members of their families cut them off from the family records. Business associates of Richard Shepherd, friends and neighbors, turned their backs on them. They were quite alone except for the Saints and the missionaries.
 Finally plans were complete. Bounteous stores of clothing and accessories were packed. Household furnishings, bric-a-brac, barrels of dishes, glassware, silver, books, choice linens, shawls, and other precious things that the family loved, were crated and boxed for shipping. Passage was booked for all.
 They landed at New Orleans. There they transferred to a small boat which carried them up the Mississippi River to St. Louis. Here it was that tragedy overtook them. Mother
Ann contracted cholera on the river boat and was spirited off the ship under cover of darkness, by the Port authorities, and was never seen again by any of the members of her beloved family. Richard made attempts to find her, even her place of burial, if she had actually died, with no results. It was their opinion that the authorities were so fearful of the disease that they might have disposed of her body. The lack of information left them all fearful and doubtful. “Who were these heartless Americans who could dispose of a mother without a word of explanation?”
 Here the family and the others with them went their separate ways.
 Mary, especially, felt lonely. She had not only lost her mother, but the young man to whom she was engaged had separated from her at St. Louis and gone on to California.
 Richard, undaunted, went forward with plans to go on to Salt Lake Valley. After sacrificing everything in England which he held dear and now his beloved wife, he did not care to give up without a final effort to finish the journey.
 He purchased a wagon and fine outfit which adequately carried the precious belongings which they had brought. He learned to handle a team of oxen, and drove all the way across the plains in his fine broadcloth suits, which he was accustomed to wear.
 The girls, Mary and Margaret, accompanied him, heartbroken and kept his clothes and wagon neat and clean, prepared the meals and looked after him.
It was not a happy journey as none of them seemed to be able to recover their hope.
 The arrival of the family in the Valley, coincided with the devastating cricket invasion. Only a few had wheat, who wished to trade it for precious items in the Shepherd wagon.
 Shortly after they reached the Valley, Mary’s father became interested in some woman who won him away from his daughters. She would have nothing to do with the girls and prevailed upon Richard to “put them out to work”. The two girls were devastated.
 Mary began working in the home of John Taylor, friend to Joseph Horne and later President of the Church. Joseph became acquainted with her.
They were married on the 30th day of November, 1856 (with the full consent of Isabella), by President Brigham Young. They were sealed for time and all eternity in the celestial order of marriage.
 Joseph also married another wife at this same time. Her name was Elizabeth Ashford, born 10th May, 1812 in Little Partfield, Cambridge, England. Her father was John Ashford. No children came of this marriage.
 Joseph Horne provided a home for Mary, in a house he built on the corner of First South and Second West Streets, next to the home of Isabella, his first wife, and their then, eight children. Life was not easy for either Mary or for Isabella, under the new conditions, or for Joseph, either, if the truth were told. Both of these women were proud and independent, intelligent, refined, yet resourceful. They shared what was necessary of food and clothing, trying to be patient and kind as became Latter-day Saint women.
 Joseph was proud of his two fine families, and grateful beyond words for the good and gracious mothers of his children.
 Mary Shepherd Horne found her greatest interest in her home. She delighted in displaying the lovely things her father and mother had brought from England to enrich their lives in this western country. She loved to cook and it is well remembered by some of her grandchildren that she was a meticulous housekeeper and cook. The English dishes that she often prepared were especially appetizing, and the plum pudding that she served by candlelight with her own pretty ceremony, at the New Year celebration, with burning candles and flames lighting it up, made an unforgettable ritual. Mary’s home was her castle and in homemaking Mary became a deft administrator as well as a most competent and artistic decorator. Her home was a great contribution to the happiness and well-being of Joseph, her husband.
 Joseph and Mary had ten children, six sons and four daughters but they had the sorrow of losing every other baby at first.
 After her sons married, Mary moved to a cottage on South West Temple Street, that Joseph provided for her. It was situated between 17th and 21st South Streets on West Temple, as those streets are now numbered. This home she beautified to suit her good taste and it was outstanding in the neighborhood for its neat and attractive appearance. Mary loved to dress her children up nicely and take them to Church with her.
