# Autobiography of James McBride April 4th, 1876

_A story of [[James John McBride (KWJ5-DHB)|James John McBride]], [[Thomas White McBride (KWJR-J45)|Thomas White McBride]], [[Catherine Ruth John (LLQX-D8P)|Catherine Ruth John]]._

## The story as it was written

Preface
(With original spelling and punctuation)
As time speeds swiftly on—and age creeps over me, I am caused to reflect on the necessity of handing down to you a short history of my life.
I have therefore made an effort to furnish you with such truths as I yet retain in my memory.
And while this may be considered a very brief sketch of the past part of my life, it is nevertheless a truth.
Many of the accounts I write of my own life, are no more than common occurances with many of those who are cast upon this state of action, subject to its changes. While others picture plainly the unusual, unjust, inmerciful and almost indescribably evils heaped upon the Latter-day Saints; and with them I have been a sufferer. Not because I was ever convicted of crime, but on account of my religious belief.
I do not write with a desire to excite feelings—Strife I detest. I would gladly suffer evils as long as evils are sufferable—would willingly forbear, as long as there is virtue in forbearance—But since we, God’s People, have been driven from our homes repeatedly at the hands of a ruthless mob,--backed in their infamous crimes by acting officials; I have no hesitancy whatsoever in giving vent to my feelings, and exposing in my feeble way such facts as are stamped on the very tablets of my memory, which time cannot efface.
I greatly regret not having kept a journal of my life, from my boyhood—but I did not then realize the necessity of doing so. Consequently, a great deal is forgotten that might have been of interest to you.
I now ask you, my children, to continue my history, from this time should I not be able to. And I enjoin upon you, as a duty you owe to your posterity, to commence early in life, and keep your history, to hand down to them.
As I have said, this you may think a very short history from me, but if I were in possession of as much, written by my father, what a valuable book I would think it—What a precious gem! Often would I peruse its pages, studying the various changes through which my foreparents passed.
I hope, my dear children, that you will fully appreciate the value of the little history I leave for you.
May God bless you my child
CHAPTER 1
GENEALOGY: FATHER’S MARRIAGE: DEATH: A MOVE FROM VIRGINIA TO OHIO: FATHER’S TRIP BACK TO VIRGINIA: MARRIAGES: A MOVE 10 WAYNE COUNTY: THE LEASE: CLEARING THE FARM; A DEATH.
My grandfather’s name was James McBride: he was born in the state of Virginia, in which state he died at seventy or seventy-five years of age.
My Grandmother’s maiden name was Mary White. After her marriage to my grandfather, she gave birth to John, James, Philip, and Thomas. Of the girls there were Isabelle, Mary, and Sarah, and I believe another girl whose name I cannot tell, but was the wife of ___________ McDougal. Perhaps the names are not given properly, but as nearly so as I can remember having heard them.
I believe my grandfather was of Irish descent.
My grandfather’s name on my mother’s side, was Thomas John. His wife’s name was Ruth ____________. After her marriage to grandfather John, she gave birth to James and also a son who was scalded and died when quite young; his name I cannot give. Of the girls, Sarah, Elizabeth, Hannah, and Catherine (of course my mother) are all I have any information of.
Thomas McBride, my father, was born March, 1776, in Loudoun County, Virginia. At the age of about eighteen, he was married to Catherine John. At the time she was about sixteen years of age. I think they were married in Hancock County, Virginia--at least grandfather John lived in that county at that time.
To my mother were born; Rebecca, Ruth, Amos, Mary, Hannah, Elizabeth, Susan, Thomas, Sarah, Isabelle, James, Catherine, and Dorcas. There were two other girls born in the family, who died when infants, and I think they were not named.
The first seven above named were born in the State of Virginia, in which state Susan died.
My father left Virginia in the spring of 1810 and moved to New Lancaster, Fairfield Co., Ohio, a journey of about three hundred miles. Taking with him all his children living except Ruth: who was living with her grandfather and grandmother John, and remained in Virginia for some time after.
In the Autumn of 1810 Hannah died. She was then about three years old.
In the Autumn of 1813 my father returned to Virginia and brought home with him Ruth. I will now give you a short account of my father’s trip. He rode a pacing mare called snap- and not being able to furnish a better mode of conveyance, my sister, Ruth, who was then about fourteen, was obliged to ride and walk in turn with father. A trip, as you already understand, of about three hundred miles from Virginia to Lancaster, Ohio.
Rebecca and Mary were both married in Fairfield Co. Ohio.
Thomas, Sarah, Isabelle and myself were born in the same county.
In March 1820, father moved from Fairfield County to Wayne County, a distance of about one hundred and ten miles. Taking with him Ruth, Amos, Elizabeth, Thomas, Sarah, Isabelle, and myself. There he took a lease on a school section of land for fifteen years, which was situated on one of the tributaries of the Mohegan, called the Red Haw. The conditions on which the lease was taken, was that my father was obligated to clear not less than twenty acres of heavy timbered land. The clearing was to be divided into fields of not more than seven acres each, lawfully fenced. He was also to put out an orchard of not less than twenty-five apple trees, and twenty-five peach trees each. A log house, and a double log barn were to be built.
My father’s circumstances were very poor. He had but little stock when he took the lease, and unfortunately lost part of that. But bone and sinew were put to chopping and grubbing--and the younger hands to gathering brush--whether boys or girls, It mattered not--the cleaning must be done. The first year about five acres were cleared and put in corn. Heavy frosts destroyed the crop, so the first year there was no income from the lease.
In three years, however; the eighty acres were about cleared, and gradual income was realized.
Catherine and Dorcas were born in Wayne County.
Ruth, my oldest sister remaining at home, was here married to Perry Durffee; a few years after my father took the lease.
Amos McBride, married Keziah McBride-- a daughter of Robert McBride, but no relation to my father’s family. Elizabeth, afterward married a man by the name of James McMllian. My sister Sarah died about the year 1828.
CHAPTER 2
CHILDHOOD: THE GOSPEL: FIRST SERMON PREACHED ON THE RED HAW: MY PARENTS AND SISTER JOIN THE CHURCH: THE LEASE SOLD: A MOVE: A SOJOURN IN RICHLAND COUNTY: ANOTHER MOVE: A SOJOURN IN PIKE CO., MO: ANOTHER MOVE: A SHORT STAY IN RAY COUNTY: A MOVE TO CAIDWELL COUNTY: GETTING A HOME: I WAS BAPTIZED: THREATS AGAINST THE MORMONS: THE PREPARATION FOR GUARDING THE MILL.
To those whose eyes peruse these pages in search of the more particular history of my life, let me say, go back with me a few years, and I will endeavor to give you a plain account of my life from my childhood.
I was born on the 9th day of May A. D. 1818 in the county of Fairfield, State of Ohio. I was but two years old when my father moved to Wayne County. Of that early part of my life you have already read something in the first chapter of this work. And perhaps I could not say much more than I have already said that would be of interest to you about it.
While my father lived on the Red Haw, a branch of the Mohegan on the lease of which I have already given an account, came first to us the sound of the Everlasting Gospel, as revealed to man in those last days. It was the Gospel of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, proclaimed by two elders, Thomas Tripp and Harvy Green. I was then about thirteen years old. My father, who previously had not felt to join any Christian denomination, now opened his house and welcomed the elders to his home.
The first sermon preached on the Red Haw by the elders of this church, was preached in my father’s house in April 1831, by the above named elders. Soon after my father, mother, and Sister Isabelle were baptized and confirmed members of the church by the same elders.
My father sold the lease, and in August 1833, accompanied by brother Amos arid his family, and James McMillen and family, started to Jackson County, Missouri to join with the Church. The season being well advanced, he was not able to get further than to Richland, County, Ohio that season.
While there, Isabelle and Thomas both married.
My father stayed in Richland County till the spring of 1834; when, accompanied by Amos and family, James McMillen and family, Isabelle and husband, James Dayley and family, and Thomas and his family, he started to Missouri.
Having traveled about two months with ox teams, in the latter part of June, 1834, we arrived in Pike County, Mo.
The Church being very much scattered and unsettled we remained in Pike County about two years.
In the spring of 1836, the company above mentioned, moved to Ray County, and there joined with a branch of the Church. We stopped there about three months, during which time we suffered a great deal with ague and fever.
The howlings of the mob were heard on every side.
In September, my father, taking with him what of his children yet remained at home, and accompanied by James Dayley and wife, moved to Caldwell County and settled about three-fourths of a mile from Hahn’s Mill on Shoal Creek.
There, my father entered from Government, eighty acres of land and began to .make a home.
A branch of the Church was organized at Haun’s Mill, presided over by David Evans.
I was baptized into the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints by David Evans, in June 1838. At the same time James Haun and Isaac Laney were baptized.
Though many of the followers of the prophet Joseph Smith had been beaten, tarred and feathered, driven from their homes and their property confiscated by the use of mobocrats, their persecutions were not yet to cease. Threats were made against the Mormons, the rights of citizenship were denied them.
The little few now fully realizing the dangerous situation in which they were placed, decided to adopt measures to defend themselves against the raids of the mob. It was decided that a guard should be kept at the mill.
CHAPTER 3
ARRANGEMENTS MADE TO GUARD: THE MASSACRE: FATHER MURDERED: NUMBER MURDERED: WOUNDED: WOMEN SHOT: ISAAC LANEY’S ESCAPE.
One beautiful afternoon on the 30th day of October 1838. My father came home from meeting with the brethren at the mill. He talked with me, and told me the arrangements made. He was called to help to form the guard. I was sick at the time with the every-other-day ague, and father said on my well day I should take his place with the guard and that he would guard on the day that I was sick. That with himself and me, he wished to fill one man’s place. You will remember my father was then in his sixty-third year. During the summer he had been very sick--but having recovered, appeared to feel very well; in fact I think he looked better than I had ever seen him.
My sister, Catherine, was living at the mill with Haun’s family, leaving only me and my youngest sister Dorcas at home with father and mother.
Father was in good spirits and his countenance wore a cheerful expression. Having shaved himself in his usual style, leaving side beards, and taking with him his gun and blankets, started on his return to the mill to join the rest of the guard. Mother, with sister Dorcas started to visit a neighbor woman, living about a quarter of a mile distance from father’s place. This being the day on which I was sick the next day I should have taken father’s place with the guard. I was then in my twenty-first year.
The day was gradually passing, evening was coming on. The large red sun so characteristic of an Indian summer, shone through the smokey atmosphere. All was still.
My father had but little more than got to the mill--in fact not more than thirty minutes had elapsed from the time he left the house, when a gun was heard, and another! followed by the deadly crack of musketry, which told too well the fate of all who fell a prey to the blood-thirsty mob.
Perhaps not more than six minutes had passed from the firing of the first gun, till the massacre was accomplished, the bloody deed was done.
The firing ceased--the screams of mothers, daughters and the wounded, told the dreadful tale.
That bloody picture in the book of time: may it ever stamp with stigma the brow of the government that offered not a protecting hand to those who were ruthlessly cut down--wounded or were made widows and orphans at the Haun’s Mill Massacre.
With a single exception, the dead were left lying where they fell--in fact there were none left that were able to take care of them. Whether dead or alive, all fared alike--all were uncertain--all was pain and sorrow.
In vain did the affectionate wife with aching heart and streaming eyes watch through the long, long night for the return of her husband.
The 31st day dawned and again the rays of the morning sun kissed the landscape. As yet the extent of the massacre was not known.
Brother Amos, having been detailed on the previous day to get wood for families, was on his way to the mill when he was told there had been serious trouble there. His home was about three miles from the mill and as he was detailed on guard, was not at the mill at the time of the slaughter.
He went on, passing the mill a short distance, came to Haun’s house. The first object that met his eye in human form was the mangled body of my murdered father lying in the door yard.
Some of the women had dragged him from the creek into the door yard and left him there. One of his ears was almost cut from his head, deep gashes were cut in his shoulders; and some of his fingers cut till they would almost drop from his hand.
On further examination it was found that fifteen were murdered and fifteen wounded, one of whom was a woman, Mary Stedwell, who in trying to escape was shot through the hand and fell behind a log. Several bullet holes were found in the log directly opposite where she lay.
Alma Smith, a small boy, and I believe one _________Merrick, were the only wounded children that were yet alive.
Of the wounded men, three afterward died, making eighteen dead in all.
Isaac Laney, a young man who was baptized into the church at the same time that I was, was in the blacksmith shop when the mob began to fire on them. His gunstock was shot to pieces in his hands. He then escaped from the shop, ran to the mill, and climbed down one of the mill timbers into the creek, That being the quickest way for him to escape danger. From there he went into the house where my sister Catherine, Mrs. Haun, Mrs. Merril and some other women were. They administered to Issac and put him under the floor. He had received eleven bullet marks in his body. I was well acquainted with Isaac Laney and helped to take care of him until he recovered. He told me that when trying to escape from the mob, the blood gushing from his mouth would almost strangle him. While he was under the floor he said he suffered a great deal for want of water. The women, not daring to venture out to get water until they felt sure the mob was entirely gone.
Isaac recovered and lived thirty--five years from the day of the Haun’s Massacre.
CHAPTER 4
THE BURIAL: GOVERNOR BOGG’S EXTERMINATING ORDER: HOME SOLD THE MOVE: A SOJOURN IN ADAMS COUNTY, ILLINOIS: A MARRIAGE: ANOTHER MOVE: AT NAUVOO: MOTHER’S DEATH: ANOTHER MARRIAGE: A HOME MADE: MY MARRIAGE: THE PROPHET AND PATRIARCH MURDERED.
A few rods south of the blacksmith shop was an unfinished well about eight or twelve feet deep, but no water was in It. This made the sepulchre for the dead.
Fifteen murdered persons, including my father, were carried on a board one at a time and dropped into that well---by brother Amos, James Dayley, and Jacob Myers the only three able bodied men that were present.
It was plainly shown that there was no mercy for us.
What few men and boys that were of much age (yet alive) were under necessity of hiding away to escape danger.
About the first day of November being tired of lying out in the woods I concluded to venture a trip to the mill. I was anxious to see the grounds on which the slaughter took place, and learn of the possible general situation of affairs.
Accordingly, with feelings that I cannot here describe, I slowly wended my way to the spot. I walked over the grounds, noticing here and there the blood stained earth--and seriously reflecting on our then sorrowful situation.
On the outside the logs of the shop were defaced with bullet marks and on the inside of the shop the ground was scarcely visible for blood.
I traced the blood from the dead bodies of those who were carried and buried in the well. I went to the place and stood at the edge of the silent tomb of my beloved father. A silent prayer I offered to God and turned away.
Having spent a few minutes at the house, I went into the mill to look once again through it. While there a noise attracted my attention and I saw the woman of whom I have just spoken running and beckoning to me in an affrighted manner. I sprang to the doorway and saw about thirty rods distance a posse of men, coming in the direction of the mill. I did not feel right in trusting myself in their hands, but rather than let them see me run to escape, I would have died. I therefore walked from the mill to the barn, crossed it and quietly walked on until I was out of sight. Why they did not fire at me I cannot tell.
A few days after a company of men, commanded by Neamiah Comstock took possession of the mill. In that company was a man by name ______ Mopin, for whom my father, who was then a magistrate, had collected a judgment amounting to ten dollars and ten cents, just before his death. Mopin now threatened that my mother’s house would be burned down if the money was not forthcoming. I heard of the threats made and after reflecting for a time took the money and started to chance my fate with the mob.
In as bold a manner as I could assume, I went among them. They did not bother me and I soon thought myself quite safe. I found Mopin and presented him with the money. He took it and seemed somewhat effected on learning the situation of my father’s family. To remunerate me for my trouble he gave me ten cents.
I was quite small for my age—-smooth faced and very sickly--which perhaps in part accounted for my being allowed to depart in peace.
Having ventured thus far, I decided that I would again return and act as a spy.
One day, having worked my way back into their midst, I discovered that a man by name, Robert White, who was a member of the Church had turned traitor and gave the enemy all the information he could about the Mormon families and their situations.
The Captain who was aside instructing his man, I overheard him mention my brother Amos’s name as one having a gun which he said was hidden in a hollow tree. And if he refused to give it up when called for, they were instructed to shoot him down without further ceremony.
As soon as a chance presented itself, I left the camp, and as soon as I was out of their sight, made my way across the hills to where Amos lived and told him what I had heard. I advised him to go and get his gun and when demanded of him to give it to them, as we were betrayed and if he tried to keep his gun he would lose his life. I then hurried away before the mob came up. Amos did as I advised him.
A few days after brother Amos, James Dayley and David Lewis were taken prisoners. They were kept a few days, harrassed and tormented and then set at liberty.
While Comstock’s company remained at the mill, they used it to do their grinding. They would shoot down our cattle and hogs--not caring how much they were needed by the widows and children that had been left to care for themselves.
When they wished honey they would shoot down and take a hive to their camp, split it open with an ax and help themselves. This was indeed hard to endure, but to resist was death.
The governor of Missouri (Boggs) not being satisfied with the suffering already borne by the Latter-day Saints, issued orders requiring them to surrender their fire arms, give up their principal leads and leave the State at a given time.
The suffering caused by that exterminating order of Boggs could hardly be described. Families were turned out of their homes and the widows and orphans found themselves cast helplessly upon the mercy of the church. Some were without teams and almost destitute of food and clothing. Thus exposed to the storms of winter and travel a journey of more than two hundred miles.
It was now necessary to get rid of our home at the mill in the best way we could, If we could get something for it, well and good, and if not we were to leave it anyway. The place was worth about one thousand dollars.
In February 1839, the place was sold for an old vehicle worth forty dollars, an old horse worth twenty dollars, and a mare worth forty dollars; in all we did not get more than one hundred dollars for the place.
With the horse and mare to the carry-all and a yoke of oxen to a two wheeled cart, our traveling outfit was made up.
Dependent on this was my mother, my two youngest sisters and myself, my brother Amos and family, James Dayley and family, also Harrison Severe, a young man who afterward married my Sister Dorcas, numbering in all about seventeen persons.
We left Haun’s Mill on the 24th of February on our way to Illinois. The first day we traveled about nine miles and then camped in a house which had been vacated by one of the brethren.
The day had turned extremely cold and we decided to remain at that place till the weather became more favorable for traveling.
While there camped we were informed that our guns, which had been taken from the Saints at Haun’s Mill, and at the surrender of arms at Far West, had been taken to Richmond in Ray County, and that we could get them by first describing them, swearing to the description and paying a fee of sixty cents for each gun.
James Dayley and myself decided to ascertain the truth of the matter and if possible, get our guns. My main object was, however, to get possession of my father’s gun. With which, you may remember, father was shot.
Accordingly about the 26th, we started to Richmond. At the same time the main body of our company started on their journey to Illinois. We had no horses to ride--no teams were traveling that direction, consequently we were compelled to go on foot.
And now, let me say, this was the beginning of the three hardest days suffering from fatigue that I ever experienced.
The first day about one hour before sunset, we arrived in Richmond and after describing our guns, taking the oath, and paying the required fees, we were directed into a room in which was stacked several hundred guns, all of which justly belonged to our people, but of which they had been unwarrantedly deprived. My father’s gun was not to be found by us, but fortunately I got my brother Amos’s gun and James Dayley got his.
Having done all we could we turned our faces in the direction of our company. After traveling about ten miles on our way at a late hour we stopped for the night at the home of brother Pleasant Ewell; who had been a good friend to many of the Saints and who gave us lodging, supper and breakfast.
At an early hour of the morning we were again traveling. After a hard day’s trip, just before sunset, we came to a place where we were informed our company had passed about eleven o’clock that day. We were now on the road our company had traveled, which made us anxious to push forward. We had been without eating since early breakfast so we arranged with the man of the place for our dinners for which we paid twelve and a half cent each.
A few miles ahead commences a prairie through which we would have to travel for about eighteen miles. The country through which we were traveling was a new country and It was not thought strange there to travel ten or more miles without seeing a house. But with the hope that our company had camped at the edge of the prairie, and we might overtake them, we traveled on. Darkness came upon us. We reached the prairie but found none there.
The wolves were howling around us in almost every direction. We were indeed tired, but to lie down in the cold and trust ourselves to the hungry appetites of howling wolves seemed hopeless, so we still traveled on.
Repeatedly I proposed to my traveling companion to stop but he would not consent to the proposition at that critical time.
Slowly we trudged on until at a late hour when we saw to our right and about a mile distant a fire in the timbers. We left the road and went in the direction of the fire. When we got to the place we gathered wood to keep fire and there camped for the rest of the night.
Before day dawned the shrill clarion of the dung-hill cock informed us we were then but a short distance from a house. We went to the house and got our breakfast which was very acceptable to hungry and weary-footed travelers.
About eleven o’clock that day we overtook our company.
We had traveled an average of about forty miles each day--and you are left to judge the good feelings we enjoyed at again joining our friends.
After a tedious journey and a great deal of exposure from which many died, we arrived in Adams County, Illinois. Here we built a house about four miles east of Payson. I rented a piece of land which I farmed one season.
In the spring of 1840, about the last of April, I started to Nauvoo, Hancock Co. taking with me my mother and sister Catherine. Accompanied by Amos and family, James Dayley and family, and Harrison Severe and wife; also a young man by name, William Pope, who afterward married Catherine.
At Nauvoo I got in with a man named Creamer from whom I got a small place and raised a crop of corn the first year.
The gathering of the Church was at Nauvoo.
In the year 1841 I rented a portion of a farm from a man named John Eagle, which I cultivated.
My circumstances were very good and all went well until about July when my mother was taken with the King’s Evil, from which she died about the 26th of July, 1841.
At that place Catherine married William Pope.
In the Autumn of 1841 I moved north about two miles and built another home, where I remained about a year and a half and then disposed of it.
In the spring of 1843 I bought a piece of land about one mile north on which I built a home and made some improvements. While on that place I married Olive Mahetable Cheeney on the evening of the 7th day of March, 1844.
Olive Mahetable Cheeney, daughter of Aaron Cheeney and Mahetable Wells, was born on the 15th day of May, A.D. 1817 in Bloomfield, Cataraugus County, State of New York.
On the 27th day of June, 1844, the Prophet Joseph Smith, and Patriarch Hyrum Smith were murdered at Carthage Jail, Illinois.
CHAPTER 5
CONTINUED PERSECUTIONS; ANOTHER MOVE NECESSARY; ORGANIZATION OF COMPANIES; STARTED WEST; THE OUTFIT; A SOJOURN AT FARMINGTON, IOWA; MOVE DOWN DES MOINES RIVER; SICKNESS AND DEATHS; HOW EARNINGS WERE DIVIDED; THOMAS MCBRIDE HANGED AT FARMINGTON BY A MOB; RESCUED BY OLD IKE GRAY; THOMAS’S DEATH; THE JOURNEY RESUMED; STAY AT APPANOOSE COUNTY.
The hand of the assassin was not yet stayed. The persecutions of saints were very severe. And having long suffered the gross impositions of all who wishes us evil, it was discovered that we would again have to move.
Accordingly, on the 28th of January, 1846, the breaking up of the Church at Nauvoo began.
My first son, Brigham McBride, was born at Nauvoo, Hancock Co., Illinois on the 6th day of February, 1846.
In order to keep affairs in the best possible condition, it was necessary to organize the Church into companies. First, headed by a captain, would be a company of hundred families. These families divided into two companies of fifty families each. Over each of these was a captain. These fifties were subdivided into companies consisting of ten families each ten having a separate captain.
Of the ten of which I was a member, James Dayley was the Captain.
In April, 1846, we left Nauvoo, directing our course westward.
A span of horses (of very inferior quality) to a small wagon-- a yoke of oxen and wagon, made up the traveling outfit for our entire company--to which was also added a widow woman and family.
The first day we moved to the banks of the Mississippi--taking first a part of the company, their bedding, clothing, and provisions and unloading it at the river. Then returning with the teams for those remaining behind, and so on until at evening the company was all together. This was the way we traveled each day, and by this means we traveled about twenty-five miles in ten days--all camping together each night.
We stopped at Farmington, Iowa. There the company took a job of grubbing at which we worked during the summer, our wages amounting to about twenty-five cents per day each.
In September, 1846, we left Farmington and moved down the Des Moines River to Tom’s Mill where we stopped. There was a deal of sickness in our company during the fall and winter which resulted in the death of my son, Brigham, aged about eleven months; also Wyat Pope, son of William Pope and Catherine McBride, and an infant son of James McMillan and Elizabeth McBride.
At one time I was the only man in the Company able to work for about ten days. Though previous to that time my health had been very poor, worse than the health of the rest of the men of the company.
The company shared alike in all that was earned. If part were sick they shared equally in the earnings of the rest. And when I worked alone all I earned was equally divided.
During the winter brother Thomas was very sick but his health was now gradually improving. About the first of March, 1847, he went to Farmington and while there in a store was seized by a mob and hanged to the porch timbers of the building. While hanging there his toes only allowed to touch the ground--the mob seated around laughing and looking on--a man by the name of Issac Gray, came up and cut the rope. Thomas fell to the ground--his face black and his life almost gone.
Old Ike Gray, as he was usually called, was not a Mormon, but sympathized with the persecuted. Though a rich man, he wore an old buckskin coat and was always ready for the worst. He said a man could not be treated in that style while he was around and the mob not desirous of getting into difficulty with so rough a fellow as Old Ike, concluded to quit the job until a more suitable chance presented itself.
Thomas recovered so as to go home, but about three weeks afterwards he died, and though we must not censure too readily, yet it was generally believed that being hanged was, in part if not altogether the cause of his death.
Having made some additions to our teams, we started from Tom’s Mill with three yoke of oxen and three wagons. And though we could not travel with much less trouble than on our previous journey, we were not able to haul provisions sufficient to do the company any length of time.
We traveled about sixty miles and stopped in Appanoose County, Iowa. In fact it was now impossible to proceed any further. Our teams were not sufficient to draw our families and provisions to do them across the plains, and after leaving that settlement, we would have but little chance to obtain provisions.
The great plains extending then west to the Pacific Coast was before us. We did not know yet where we were to make our final home. The orders were to “Move West”. Many companies had already gone ahead; but we did not know where they would stop.
The frontier settlers, among whom we now found ourselves, were filled with hatred toward the Mormons. Bitter threats were made against us and we were watched on every hand.
A council was held by the little company, it was evident we could not at that time go any further--what should we do? We must stop.
It was now necessary to court the friendship of our worst enemies. Camp was made, some commenced to improve the lands while others sought work of the settlers in order to get means to buy provisions for our families. We had not been there long till we gained a very good name. The work we bargained to do was done well. We could soon get work anywhere and were consequently able to make ourselves quite comfortable.
No time was lost in idleness--cabins were built to winter in and the next spring we commenced to farm our own places which had improved.
Our object was to gain means with which to travel west, and the sooner this could be accomplished the better.
Aaron McBride, my second son, was born in Appanoose County, Iowa, on the 10th day of July, A. D., 1848. My son Amos McBride was born in Appanoose County, Iowa, on the 2nd day of Jan. 1850.
During our stay in Appanoose County, our company was considerably reduced in number.
CHAPTER 6
MOVE TO KANESVILLE; THE COMPANY DIVIDED TO CROSS THE PLAINS; JOINING CAPT. GARDNER SNOW’S COMPANY: CROSSED THE MISSOURI RIVER; DEATHS FROM CHOLERA; THE STAMPEDE; A CHILD RUN OVER: BUFFALO ON THE PLATTE RIVER; CHIMNEY ROCK; ARIVAL AT SALT LAKE.
On the 17th day of May, 1850, having disposed of our homes in Appanoose County we again started West. In company with myself were Captain James Dayley, Harrison Severe and William Pope families. Also my sister Mary and her children.
Having traveled about three hundred miles we came to Bluff City, then called Kanesville. We stayed there a few days, during which time it was decided that the entire company could not safely venture to cross the plains that season.
A council was held--two families could go, but the rest would have to stay there until they could obtain more means. Harrison Severe, having a better traveling outfit than either of the men of the company, was determined to cross the plains that season. One family could go with him. Who should it be? It was finally decided that I should go.
James Dayley, Wm. Pope with their families, and sister Mary Riddle, came and her children were left to follow on as soon as possible.
We bade our friends good-bye until we should again meet them in a future day, and Harrison Severe, myself and families again started westward.
Having traveled about fifteen miles, we got to the ferry on the Missouri River. There we joined a company of fifty families under Capt. Gardner Snow, Joseph Young, a brother of President Brigham Young, was president of the company. Thomas Rich was captain of the ten in which we were included.
The organization and all the necessary arrangements having been made, we crossed the river on the third day of June 1850--on our way to Salt Lake, a distance of more than one thousand miles over uncivilized country.
About the first days travel after we crossed the river there was a case of cholera in the company which proved fatal. In all there were eight cases of cholera in our company, during the first month from which seven of that number died.
While traveling up the Platte River much might. be said that would be of interest, but I will pass over the principal and only make mention of a few incidents which transpired.
One forenoon, as the company was quietly traveling along, suddenly the front teams started. In an instant the first eight teams were stampeded. Women and children were in the wagons, but most of the drivers were on the ground. For the drivers to keep up with the teams was impossible. Away went the teams, helter skelter. Those who have not seen the like can hardly realize the excitement of the moment.
For perhaps forty rods or more wheels were whirling rapidly and wagon sheets were cracking in the wind--when, almost as simultaneously as were started, the teams principally in abreast, stopped. Some of the oxen had been dragged to the ground. Chains were wrapped around yokes and some of the oxen were astriddle of chains.
The stampede was as equally unaccounted for as was the sudden stopping of so many frightened teams.
The drivers hurrying up and putting their teams in order were all anxious to know if anyone was hurt. It was found that Lyman Severe, son of Dorcas and Harrison Severe, at the time the team started, was standing on the tongue of my wagon, which was very common for the children to do crossing the plains, and in trying to jump from the wagon fell and was run over by the next wagon coming up; he was not so badly hurt but that he soon recovered.
There were many narrow escapes by drivers, who, in trying to follow their teams were crowded between oxen and wagons while being overtaken and passed by them. No one however was much hurt.
From the time we left the Missouri River near Bluff City there were no white inhabitants until we got to Fort Kearney, as Grand Island, a distance of one hundred and fifty miles or more.
While traveling up the Platte River, a distance of twenty-five miles, the buffalo were as numerous as a well collected herd of cattle. To estimate their number would be impossible by us. It was necessary that someone should be kept ahead to turn them out of the road in order that the teams could pass with safety. As fast as the wagons would pass the buffalo would close in thickly behind us.
Over a vast space of country where today fields of golden grain are dotting lands, the hum of busy laborers are heard--and the iron horse protected by the hand of civilization whirles thousands of people across unmolested--then only roamed the Indians and wild beasts. Indeed the scenes on the plains in those days were wild and romantic.
Among the many curiosities that attracted the gaze of the traveler was one called the chimney rock which was about two hundred miles from Fort Kearney. It was not a rock but a kind of Adobie clay. We traveled in sight of the Chimney Rock about two days before we got to it. In the great distance it looked very much like a flag pole, but gradually grew larger until we camped at the nearest point to it from the road. We were then two miles off. Harrison Severe and I went to examine the curiosity. It was a column more than one hundred feet high and about twenty feet square which was standing on a hill, perhaps three hundred feet above the level of the plain. The column was covered with names and dates as high as men could reach. On a fragment laying near the column I cut my name. This was on the 5th of August 1850. Having climbed the main hill, a distance to the south, we could see on the top of the column what we supposed to be raspberry briars growing. I have since heard that the Chimney Rock has crumpled down.
Perhaps a hundred and fifty miles or more brought us to Fort Laramie. We were yet on the Platte River. From Fort Laramie we traveled over a wild mountainous country for about four hundred miles.
We then came to a place called Fort Bridger. A white man, fleeing from Justice with changed name, had sought refuge among the Indians and built a home there.
From Bridger, a distance of about one hundred and thirteen miles, brought us to the city of Salt Lake--the destined place for the Latter-day Saints.
We arrived in Salt Lake City 4th October 1850.
CHAPTER 7
MOVED TO TOOELE; THEN TO GRANTSVILLE; CATTLE DRIVEN OFF BY INDIANS: THE PURSUIT; CATTLE KILLED; MOVE TO PINE CANYON; RETURN TO GRANTSVILLE; A BRANCH OF THE CHURCH ORGANIZED: FORT BUILT; BIRTH; DROUGHT AND GRASSHOPPERS; SCARCITY OF FOOD; MARRIAGE; BIRTH.
We must now find a place to settle, and decided to move to Tooele. Having got to the place where Tooele City now stands, and not being satisfied with the place, Harrison Severe and myself concluded to move to a more favorable appearing place, twelve miles north and west from Tooele now called Grantsville City. We arrived at this place on the 10th day of October 1850.
One white man--living in a wagon bed and herding stock, was the only inhabitant of the place at that time. We built the first house built in Grantsville, and done the first plowing in the place and were consequently the first settlers.
We had but little means with which to support our families and our nearest market was Salt Lake City--thirty seven miles east of Grantsville. During the winter, we made several trips to Salt Lake City, hauling coal by which we managed to make a living. We done what fencing we could, when not otherwise employed.
About the 20th of March 1851, on going out in the morning to get our cattle we could not find them. Indians tracks were found. It was evident they had driven off our cattle. Only two were to be found of Harrison Severe’s and mine were all gone but one cow.
We went to the South Cedars where several parties were engaged in burning coal--with them, and what few herders could be gathered together amounting to eight in all, we started in pursuit of the Indians.
We followed them west into Skull Valley and on to the cedar mountains where we found one of Severe’s oxen lying dead. Our provisions were now nearly exhausted in fact we had but four biscuits to each man, and were sixty miles a foot from home.
The Indians were now routed at their camp, we found several of our cattle killed. We took what meat we wished and as the Indians had got out of our reach and there was no prospect of recovering any live stock, we turned toward home. We were now left without team sufficient to do our work. There was no one from whom we could get work, and we concluded to move east across the valley to a small settlement formed at Pine Canyon.
From a coal burner at South Cedars we got an old ox, very much the worse from his hard usage. With the ox we yoked my cow and loaded our wagons, hitched the two yoke of animals to Severe’s wagon and he started. He went as far as the six mile springs, and leaving his wagon and family went back for me. The same day I got as far as the dam at Benson’s Saw Mill (now known as Richville) and there I camped. Severe was yet at the six mile springs.
The second day I went back and got his wagon. Leaving mine at the Mills. That night we got our wagons together, and camped about four miles from Pine Canyon.
Our team was now very much fatigued, the road was very muddy, and we were traveling up hill. I stayed with the families while Harrison Severe went to Pine Canyon and got a man and a yoke of oxen to help us there. While there, we worked during the summer, principally in the canyon getting out lumber and house logs.
We paid ten dollars per hundred for flour and a proportionate price for goods and groceries. We farmed some ground at that place, from which we got about twenty bushels of wheat, and what potatoes and beets we wished for our own use.
Notwithstanding the high prices paid for all we consumed, we saved and invested three hundred and eighty dollars in work oxen.
About the first of December we moved back to Grantsville in company with five other families. We then began to improve our places what we could and put in our crops.
In the season of 1852 Severe and me raised about five-hundred bushels of wheat.
A branch of the church was then organized, presided over by Benjamin Baker and consisted of seven families.
James Orson McBride was born in Grantsville, Tooele County, Utah on the 5th day of September 1852. In 1853, in all, we numbered about thirty families. Presided over by Thomas Henry Clark, who had been sent to preside at Grantsville, the Autumn of 1852.
The Indians were very bad, continually driving off, and killing stock. They were then doing a deal of mischief among the southern settlers of the territory. The Walker Indians then appeared to be the worst in their raids upon them.
We were compelled to guard our cattle by day and closely corral them at night.
It might be of interest to you to know that the spot on which my orchard now stands is the spot on which our cattle was then corralled.
In the spring of 1853 we were instructed to build a Fort in order to secure ourselves against danger. We decided to move our houses close together--and when we could, to build a wall around them of sufficient height and thickness to make our families safe. We accordingly began our work--our minds filled with care and anxiety. Houses were torn down and hurried to the appointed spot.
During the Autumn of 1853 our settlement was strengthened by quite a company who had crossed the plains that season. Having been busily engaged in moving houses and doing other work which we could not with propriety leave undone--as yet, we had not built our fort wall, but were all living closely together. In the winter of 1853-4 we commenced and built the fort.
The north-west and a part of the south sides of the wall were built with dirt dug from the outside of the wall, and closely packed by beating with hand malls. The wall was five feet thick at the bottom and was twelve feet high, and eighteen inches thick at the top. The east side and remaining part of the south side were built of adobies-—under which was a rock foundation. The wall made of adobies was three feet thick at the bottom, twelve feet high and eighteen inches thick at the top--covering an area of thirty rods square making one hundred and twenty rods of wall. Which was made at a cost of twenty five dollars per rod, averaged amounting to three thousand dollars.
Had the gates been put up and all of the top of the last wall been completed, the cost would have been much more.
Elam Wells McBride was born in Grantsville, Tooele County, Utah on the 18th day of September 1854.
The North Willow Creek which now furnishes water sufficient to yearly irrigate hundreds of acres of grain at that time was but a very small stream. The settlers had undertaken to farm a great amount of land that year. The season was a dry one and grasshoppers came in great numbers, cutting down and eating the greater amount of what vegetation was yet growing. However, we were not to starve, a kind hand was stretched forth in our behalf. That year I raised two hundred and three bushels of wheat--which was the largest amount of grain harvested by any other one that season. The next largest amount was about ninety bushels. Many of the settlers raised but very little grain some none at all.
A few of the brethren went north of Salt Lake City to Ogden, and other places, and obtained some wheat. Others who were not able to buy must chance themselves with other sufferers.
The winter of 1854-5 passed, and with spring, commenced the suffering among the families of our little settlement. Their scanty supply of provisions gathered the past season was nearly exhausted--Some were in fact already without bread-- except, as those who yet had divided with them. As the season advanced, we gathered and used grease-wood greens, in fact, some almost lived on those and other greens and roots which they gathered.
We had quite a sufficient quantity of milk, but even that did not satisfy the hungry appetites of families. Men staggered with weakness as they went to and from their labors.
I sold what wheat I had to spare, for two dollars per bushel, at home among my neighbors, but was at the same time offered four dollars per bushel, by men from other places.
I afterward divided my flour till I think I was three days without bread.
John W. Cooley, harvested the first grain in the place in 1855 which was barley. Of that, each family was allowed about half a bushel, which we would grind and make into thickened milk. In this way, we managed, till we began to gather our wheat crops, which were not very good. But as soon as it was fit to use, families would boil and eat it with milk. Some was ground on coffee mills to make bread till threshing was done.
On the 7th day of November 1856, I married Marion Louisa Redden. Marion Louisa Redden was the daughter of Return Jackson and Laura (Track) Redden, was born in McLane County, Illinois, on the 31st day of August 1841.

CHAPTER 8
JOHNSON’S ARMY; MOVE SOUTH; SCARCITY OF CLOTHING; PERMISSION TO RETURN HOME; GOOD CROPS: SOlDIERS CAMPED; SELLING OUR HAY AND STRAW; CHEAP OXEN AND WAGONS; BETTER TIMES; BIRTHS; DEATH: MARRIAGE: TESTIMONY OF JAMES MCBRIDE.
In the year 1854 and during the presidential reign of James Buchanan, Johnson’s Army as it was called, was started here to Utah to put down Mormonism, and made threats that the Mormon leaders and their men followers should be hanged. The Army was not, however permitted to reach Salt Lake that season, but wintered at Fort Bridger.
In the spring of 1858 President Brigham Young told the Saints to move South. In April I started and went to Utah County and there waited further orders.
A deal of suffering was at that time experienced among the people. Though we had raised plenty of grain and vegetables from our farms, it was a difficult matter to get comfortable clothing. We were a people in a similar situation, one with another. None had anything of consequence, with which to help another. We had no resources. What we had, we could keep, but could earn nothing. It may well be imagined the situation in which a few months would place us.
Thousands of people were on the move to give way for the intruding Army. Men, women and children, in many instances were almost naked, perhaps an old shirt and pants, a ragged dress, a few rags for the children, would make up the family clothing. Some were more destitute, a few had something better to wear. I stopped at Spring Creek, three miles south of Payson.
The Indians were very bad and it was necessary to guard our stock. A small company of us built a stone corral, in which to corral our cattle at night. William Pope and myself made a dug-out. With our two wagons and the dugout, we had plenty of room for our families, while we stopped there.
On the last day of June 1858, about sunrise, William G. Young, who was at the time Bishop of the Grantsville Branch of the Church, arrived at Spring Creek, and informed us that the brethren were permitted by President Brigham Young to return to their homes. By eleven o’clock, our wagons were loaded and we were on our way home. We arrived in Grantsville on the 5th evening of July.
The grain I had sown before I left my place was now standing at full height, and looked well--in fact, I never before or since raised a better crop. My potatoe crop was also very good. Our homes were about as we left them. The trip south had been a tedious one, and we were thankful for the favorable condition in which we found ourselves once more.
We were yet as destitute as ever of clothing. We had obeyed the instructions of the servants of God in moving from place to place, and His protecting arm had been stretched forth for our good through the past, and He surely would not forget us. The winter came on, we had plenty of hay and straw, more than we needed for our own use.
The soldiers sent here to annihilate the Latter-day Saints were then stationed at Camp Floyd--about forty miles south east of Grantsville--though they were sent here to do us harm, they were of great benefit to us. They had brought with them horses, mules, work oxen and wagons--also large quantities of merchandise and provisions. They needed our hay and straw for their animals. We would sell if we could get our price. We then decided that Bishop William C. Young should manage the selling of our hay and straw--and the brethren were agreed to comply with whatever arrangements he would make with the Government officers in the matter.
Our hay was sold for forty dollars per ton--our straw for one dollar per cubic yard, we could buy a very good yoke of oxen for thirty dollars-—their wagons for a very small price, they were here the soldiers had no use for them, we could buy them very cheap.
We could buy such of their merchandise as we wished and were consequently able to furnish our families with quite comfortable clothing and other necessaries in our houses.
The over-ruling hand of God, had once again been turned in our favor. The enemies of the latter-day Saints had been made, by circumstantial changes, to use their stores of greenbacks and merchandise to the advantage of the Saints.

On Saturday 19th of June 1875, Marion Louisa McBride, wife of James McBride departed this life at the age of thirty-three years, nine months and nineteen days. Many were the friends who followed her to her last resting place. She was an honest woman, and a faithful wife to me. Was filled with determination and integrity to do good. She was ever willing to administer comfort to the needy and the sick--and often did so, at her own sacrifice. And will long be held in rememberance by many to whose wants she so kindly administered.
The morning before she died, having been annointed with Holy Oil while having hands laid upon her, brother William Jefferies (our President) pronounced great blessings upon her. The promises made her, were many and great, and my dear children, if you wish to meet your mother again, you must so live, as to merit the favors of God upon you, for your mother will receive her reward with those who are permitted to enter into the Celestial Glories of our Heavenly Father.

TESTIMONY
I am now in my 58th year. Since the year 1848 to the present time (April 4th, 1876) I have lived in peace in Grantsville. All my family that are yet alive are living near me.
Every effort made by the enemies of this people to destroy them has been unsuccessful.
I feel to acknowledge the hand of God in all things and bear testimony of the truth of the Gospel of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.
