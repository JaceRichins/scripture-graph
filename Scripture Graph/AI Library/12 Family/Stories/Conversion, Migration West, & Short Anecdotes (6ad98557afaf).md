# Conversion, Migration West, & Short Anecdotes

_A story of [[Christian Larsen Hyer (KWJH-WBR)|Christian Larsen Hyer]]._

## The story as it was written

Excerpt from "What We Know about the Hayer Family" pages 16-17:
Christian Hyer says, "We located ourselves in Mesquite county, Wisconsin Territory, in the spring of 1843, when an elder of Israel by the name of Ole Olsen-Hayer, came into the neighborhood, preaching the gospel of the Son of God; and as I was convinced of the truth of the principles that he taught, I immediately embraced it, and was baptized the 23rd of June the same year. As there was no branch of the church there, I moved to LaSalle county, Illinois, where there was a branch.
"In the fall of '44, for the first time I beheld President Brigham Young, Heber C. Kimball, and Parley Pratt. In the month of February, 1846, I, in connection with others of the Church were called upon to come to Nauvoo, which we did, and there there received our endowments in the Temple; and in the Seventies Hall, I was ordained a Seventy.
"In the spring of '49, I left my father's family in LaSalle county, Illinois, and came to Salt Lake City. We were seven wagons in company from the settlement, crossed the plains in company with Ezra T. Benson, and arrived in Salt Lake City, 25 Oct. 1849. Lived in the 6th ward in the old fort until the spring of '50, when I moved to North Canyon Ward, now called Bountiful."
A member of this company, by the name of Peterson speaks of Christian Hayer, sometimes called him Chris Hayer. There has been some controversy as to who dropped the "a" in the name; we believe Christian Hyer did this after he arrived in Utah. We also learn from another diary that he played the violin while he was crossing the plains and even played for a wedding on the plains - something we did not know he could do.
Five years after his marriage, he was called on a mission for the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. He presided over the Scandinavian Saints and assisted them in preparing to cross the plains into Utah. He was given an honorable release in the spring of 1858. The following day he returned to his home and found his family all well. Upon hearing there was good land in Cache Valley, he and a few other families left for that place on 12 March 1860.
This account of the journey was taken from Marriner W. Merrill's diary: "M.W. Merrill, Christian Hyer, Edwin Pace, Goudy Hogan and others with comparatively little trouble except for cold, wet snow storms, dark nights and no chance for a fire some nights, finally reached Logan, where they found a few settlers and the Church herd of cattle between Wellsville and Logan. The next day they went on to Richmond. The land here seemed to be fairly taken up, so they moved on to High Creek expecting to explore that section. They finally concluded to settle where Cove now stands but when ready to move north, the head team of oxen, driven by Brother Merrill seemed by some instinct to be determined to go back south to Richmond, and there was no stopping them. Brother Merrill was asked by the other brethern why he went south instead of north as agreed upon. He said "My oxen were determined to go south and I could not change their course." The outcome of it was that land was provided and permanent homes were established in Richmond."
This is from heresay. At first, times were hard. And one time while grandfather was cutting his wild grass for hay, which had to be cut with sythes in those early days, he took off his jeans and hung them on the willows on the bank of Cherry Creek. But when he had his load of hay and ready to start home, he went for his breeches and they were gone - stolen by the Indians. He, in connection with M.W. Merrill and Wallace K. Burnham, built and owned the first flour mill that was erected in northern Utah, and is today one of the leading mills in the valley. He was, for a number of years, president of the Richmond Cooperative Mercantile Institution, and led an active life both in an ecclesiastical and civil capacity.
Taken from an account written by his son Joseph Hyer. "Father was a man of few words, but when he spoke his word was law. I do not mean by that he was overbearing, or harsh, for I believe there never was a kinder man to his family. He was never known to whip a child, and he had the love and confidence of his entire family, and indeed, all who had the pleasure of his acquaintance. He fulfilled to the very letter the injunction in the scriptures; "Never let thy right hand know what thy left hand doeth." It was a customary thing to see father on the eve of a holiday time, going the rounds of the city with a basket on this arm, visiting the poor and those who were in distress, and his name is held in reverence today by that class of people.
"Father, like all of the other early pioneers, had a hard struggle in conquering the elements, building bridges, and making the desert blossom as a rose, and though he never amassed great wealth he was what could be said of all of grandfather, Larse Hayer's sons, in comfortable circumstances. Spiritual things came first with father, temporal things came secondary. He was a great believer in the saying: "Seek first the kingdom of God, and its righteousness, and all other things shall be added unto you."
"Anyone, who was not acquainted with father, on reading the foregoing might think it sounds boastful, egotistical, and conceited, but it was said of him at his funeral service: "He was as good a man as it is possible to be and live in the flesh." He had his weaknesses as all men have them, but it can be truthfully said of him that the world is better for his having lived in it, and would surely receive the welcome plaudit, "Well done, thou good and faithful servant, enter into the joy of the Lord."
Christian Hyer's descendants are mostly blue eyed, and have light or brown hair. They have square jaw-bone and a square hand, which indicates resourcefulness, stubborness, and executive ability. A few of his descendants have prominent upper front teeth, which we have traced to the Hogan side.
Kemp, Annie Hyer. What We Know About the Hayer Family, page10. Logan, UT: Published 1954.
