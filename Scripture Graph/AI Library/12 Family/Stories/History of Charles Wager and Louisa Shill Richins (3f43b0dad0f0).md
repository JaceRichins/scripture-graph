# History of Charles Wager and Louisa Shill Richins

_A story of [[Charles Wager Richins (KVP1-SVD)|Charles Wager Richins]]._

## The story as it was written

Richard and Charlotte Priscilla Wager Richins lived in Sheepscomb, Gloucester, England, when their seventh child, Charles Wager Richins was born. The date was August 17, 1828. Robert Chapel and Prudence Golding Shill lived in Syde, Gloucester, England, when their daughter, Louisa Shill, was born on June 29, 1829.
 Charles grew up in England. His mother died when he was just six years old and his father died five years later. His Aunt Hannah raised the five boys and one girl of his family. Charles left school after six weeks and went to work on nearby farms as a chore boy. He had learned to read and write, then taught himself mathematics, at which he was quite good.
 Louisa was the youngest of her family and grew into a lovely, kind young woman. At the age of seventeen, she was converted by missionaries from The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints. Charles Golding (Louisa’s brother) and her parents also accepted the truths of the restored gospel. She was baptized into the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints on July 17, 1846, by George Humphries. She attended the Caudle Green Branch of the Church.
 When he was 21-years old, Charles Richins was taught the gospel of Jesus Christ by Louisa’s brother, Charles Golding Shill. He was baptized into the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints on December 31, 1849, by Charles. He served in several callings, including that of presiding over the Cheltenham Branch, while helping many to be baptized and confirmed into the Church.
 Charles and Louisa were married in Syde, England, on January 27, 1851. They had ten children—Hannah Louisa (born September 9, 1852, Fastons Ash, England), Charles Robert (March 25, 1856, Salt Lake City, Utah), Wellington (born January 18, 1858, Salt Lake City, Utah), Prudence Priscilla (born January 8, 1860, Salt Lake City, Utah), Golden Freeman (born March 31, 1861, Henefer, Utah), Orson Oriel (born April 2, 1862, Henefer, Utah), Louisa Rebecca (born March 26, 1864, Henefer, Utah), stillborn female (born September 18, 1866, Henefer, Utah), Judith Shill (September 11, 1867, Henefer, Utah), and Marland Golding (born March 14, 1871, Henefer, Utah).
Charles and Louisa were married just two years when he left his young wife and first child in England (February 28, 1853) while he and Joseph W. Young traveled to Salt Lake City, arriving on October 10, 1853. About a year later, Charles sent for his wife and daughter, using money borrowed from the Perpetual Emigration Fund. Unfortunately, daughter, Hannah Louisa, died while on the journey to Utah. There was great sorrow by this young couple.
 Louisa’s parents planned on going to Zion. Her mother, Prudence, died in England on September 13, 1851. Her father, Robert, then lived with Charles Golding, traveled to America with him and his family. He made it as far as St. Louis, joining with other members waiting to cross the prairies. He died October 6, 1855, in St. Louis.
 The reunited couple stayed another six years in Salt Lake City. Charles was a laborer and acquired real estate worth $500. Charles and Louisa were sealed in the Endowment House on March 28, 1860. Near the end of their stay in Salt Lake City, Louisa became ill and Charles brought in a young woman to help with the housework during Louisa’s pregnancy. Her name was Esther Stowe Ovard. She was from Loxely, England.
 In 1860, Brigham Young called the Richins to move to Henefer (first called Heneferville). Louisa and the children lived in tents and a dugout area on the side of the mountain during the first year that they came to the area. Charles built a home of brick over the dugout area. It became know at the Big House. It was always open to emigrants, family and friends; anyone who needed a bed. They rarely ate a meal that wasn’t shared by someone visiting the home.
 Charles was once walking to his home in Henefer. He was attacked by a pack of wolves. His only defense was a walking stick. He was protected and arrived home safely. Charles had health problems. He had a bout with Inflammatory Rheumatism, which turned his hair white.
 Louisa made a practice of writing letters to her loved ones. She would finish a letter and take it right down to the post office to be mailed the same day. Many of her letters were sent overseas. She collected stamps from many countries. She gave her stamp collection to her granddaughter, Perilla Ann Bond Millett.
 In 1861, Charles took a second wife, Esther Stowe Ovard. This marriage was performed by Brigham Young on March 9, 1861, in the Endowment House. They had ten children, all born in Henefer—Adeline Elizabeth (born May 5, 1863), Henry (born September 8, 1866), Hannah Edith (born October 27, 1867), George Madison (November 23, 1870), Abbie Letticia (born September 19, 1872), Emeline Hattie (born November 15, 1873), Olive (born July 28, 1876), Parley Thomas (born August 19, 1877), Hosea (born April 3, 1882), and Charles Noble (born March 18, 1884).
 In 1877, Charles was sustained as the presiding Elder and was later called as Bishop of the Henefer Ward when the area became a Stake. Present at his ordination to Bishop were Elders John Taylor, Lorenzo Snow, and Franklin D. Richards.
 Federal authorities caused many difficulties for those practicing plural marriage. It was decided that Louisa should move in with her son, Joseph, and his wife, Prudence. In 1878, they traveled to Thompson Valley, Arizona, but became discouraged with the conditions there and returned to the Big House in Henefer, Utah. In 1882, they made another trip to Arizona, this time settling permanently in Mesa. Louisa continued to live with Joseph and Prudence, but purchased land nearby with money given to her by Charles.
 On April 11, 1878, Charles took a third wife, Agnes Mary Willmott, in Salt Lake City. She was a young woman he had met in Coalville, but originally from Bassingbourn, England. This wedding was performed by Wilford Woodruff. They were parents of ten children—Jesse Willmott (born March 16, 1879, Henefer, Utah), Eunice Ann (born November 28, 1880, Minersville, Utah), Edward Byron (born August 22, 1882, Henefer, Utah), Lois Elizabeth (born September 15, 1884, Henefer, Utah), Agnes Priscilla (born September 4, 1886, Pleasant Grove, Utah), Bertha Louisa (born November 20, 1889, Colonia Diaz, Mexico), Beatrice Willmott (born March 9, 1891, Colonia Diaz, Mexico), Charles Richard (born April 14, 1893, Colonia Diaz), John Willmott (born September 13, 1895, Colonia Diaz, Mexico), and Elza Roxie (born December 25, 1897, Colonia Diaz, Mexico).
 Charles, his three wives, and his large family lived harmoniously and got along well together. For the short time all were together, each wife had a bedroom in the Big House. One of the sons said, “There were no half brothers and sisters, we were all members of the same family and this relationship has remained so throughout our lives.”
 Charles was a successful farmer and stockman. On July 24, 1874, he furnished the beef for a big celebration of the members of the Church arriving in the Utah Territory. He had considerable real estate and helped build dirt roads in the Henefer area. He was also a County Commissioner of Summit County for several years and a delegate of the “People’s Party” from Summit County.
 Charles was a friend to the Indians. He gave much food to the Native Americans to keep them comfortable and peaceful. Charles believed that it was better to feed the Indians than fight them. He was very generous when they came to his home. On one occasion, some Indians were drunk and violent. To avoid any trouble, he offered them any one of the beef in his herd. The Indian chose a new prized bull that Charles had just bought to sire his herd. Rather than cause problems, he let him have it.
 Charles was strict in living his religion. He would not permit profanity in his home and he did not allow distance to prevent his family from attending Church meetings and activities. He was a very spiritual man.
 With polygamy now outlawed, Charles bought some land in Pleasant Grove and was living there with Agnes when he was arrested for practicing plural marriage. His second wife, Esther, was to be called to testify against her husband, but moved around the “Mormon Underground” to avoid going to court. Without the main witness, the trial was dismissed for the present. Agnes decided to go south. There were narrow escapes on the way to Arizona. Crossing the Colorado River and nearly being captured by Indians were a few of the problems. In 1880, Charles and Agnes returned to Utah and set up a home in Pleasant Grove. The Deputy Marshall again came after Charles. Agnes went into hiding.
 In the Fall of 1888, Charles and his family traveled down to Mexico, where they would be safe from U.S. Marshalls trying to arrest polygamists. In Colonia Diaz, Charles went into the cattle business. He was soon organized and had a dairy working (milking 50-75 cows). The family helped produce butter and cheese. Charles spent most of his time out on the range. He also worked as a contractor with the railroad and in the coal industry.
 Esther and her family moved down to Mexico for a short time, but did not like it there. They returned to Henefer. Esther continued to live in Henefer and supported her family by taking in washing. Louisa traveled to Mexico on several occasions to visit Charles and Agnes. She would ride the train to Dublan, where she would be taken to Colonia Diaz by one of Charles’ sons. In 1902, she made her last trip to Mexico and returned to Mesa in March.
 Louisa was a strong woman who was willing to go through many trials because of her firm belief in the gospel of Jesus Christ. She used herbs to doctor others and healed many people and animals through her understanding of medicinal plants. In fact, in her home in Henefer, she had one room filled with herbs, and was the only person allowed in that room. She was a midwife, often called upon to help with delivering children.
 She was called “Grandma Richins” by everyone. She was an especially good cook. She was expert at making big, light-brown loaves of bread. She also excelled at making butter and cheese. Louisa never gave up the English tradition of drinking tea. During the meal, she liked to drop crusts of bread in the cup of tea and spoon them out when they were completely moistened.
 She loved being around her grandchildren. She always had jam sandwiches and cookies prepared for them. She would take time out of her busy schedule to be with them. Her grandson, Marlow, would often come in the house, grab her, and start dancing. She played along and all of the kids laughed with great delight.
 Charles served thirty years as bishop in Henefer and was a counselor to Bishop W. Darby Johnson in Colonia Diaz for many years. Then, he served as Stake Patriarch. He was ordained in May, 1901, by John Henry Smith. Charles also had a gift of healing. His great-grandchild was thrown from a wagon and a wheel passed over the baby’s neck. Charles blessed the baby that appeared dead. It was immediately healed.
 Louisa was a small woman. She always dressed neatly in a plain skirt or dress, dark laced shoes, and a sun bonnet. She was an avid reader. She loved newspapers, magazines from England, and Church books.
 In later years, she tried to spend every Sunday afternoon with her brother, Charles Golding Shill. She would ride in a buggy over to his house in Lehi (next to Mesa) and have dinner with him and his family before returning to her home.
 Charles was told in a Patriarchal Blessing that he would have much of the world’s goods. In his later years, he owned many acres of land in Henefer, Pleasant Grove, and Colonia Diaz. Louisa died of a stroke on April 23, 1903, in Mesa, Arizona. Charles died on August 27, 1903, in Colonia Diaz, Mexico. Esther passed away on July 30, 1924, in Henefer, and Agnes died in Provo, Utah, on February 16, 1925.
