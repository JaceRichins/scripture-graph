# THE TREK WEST EXPERIENCE OF CHRISTIAN HYER

_A story of [[Christian Larsen Hyer (KWJH-WBR)|Christian Larsen Hyer]]._

## The story as it was written

THE TREK WEST EXPERIENCE OF CHRISTIAN HYER, OUR FAMILIY'S IMMIGRANT, CONVERT, PIONEER ANCESTOR
 First, I note that Christian was born August 9, 1817 in the Telemark Province of Norway. In 1842 at the age of 25 he emigrated to America with his family. They first settled in Wisconsin where the family were taught the gospel and baptized by a relative, Christian being the first to convert. The family took up residence in LaSalle County northern Illinois. There was a largely Norwegian group in this place known as the Fox River settlement.
Christian received his temple blessings in Nauvoo on February 3, 1846 -- by one report this was the same day the first wagon crossed the Mississippi River in the abandonment of Nauvoo. Christian did not join the saints in that exodus but continued to live with his family. After a couple of years Brigham Young told many members still living back east to come to Utah. Christian then determined to go west in spite of strenuous efforts of his family to restrain him.
 On the 18th of April 1849 a company of 22 Saints left La Salle to travel to Utah. troup,largely Norwegian, included a Dahl family, a Sabe family, an Olson family and several young people including Canute Peterson, Christian Hyer, and Sarah Nelson. The company of six wagons journeyed about 200 miles to Burlington, Iowa, but found the town deserted except the ferrymen on the river crossing and a few guards who had been left to watch the town. Much of the town had been strewn with lime because of a Cholera epidemic so they quickly left the place and camped 8 miles away on a beautiful creek. When the group reached Chardon Point one of the group, Sarah Ann Nelson, was seized with violent attack of Cholera. The sisters of the company tried to relieve her but to no avail. Canute writes that he felt impressed to go into the woods to pray for her recovery and says that he earnestly sought the Lord to spare her life. He felt the Holy Spirit in strength and determined to give Sarah a blessing. He returned to the camp and reached his hand between the wagon cover and the wagon box, gave Sarah a priesthood blessing and rebuked the Destroyer. She was immediately healed and said she knew who it was that blessed her. They traveled on in peace and safety and reached Kanesville where they found three additional pioneer companies. They joined the company of Apostle Benson which was the last one to continue on to Utah that season.
They paused about five miles east of Kanesville to feed their teams and while camped there Apostle Orson Hyde came and married Canute and Sarah Ann Nelson on the afternoon of July 2, 1849. As they continued their journey they came to the Elkhorn River about thirty miles from the upper crossing of the Missouri River. There they found two large companies stranded on the bank of the river distressed that they were unable to cross. The problem was that the ferry had been left on the opposite side of the river without anyone tending it. The group had thought of various was to solve the problem but to no avail. moreover, the river had swollen to the height some twelve feet due to heavy rainfall up stream. The question arose regarding the possibility of someone swimming the river with a rope to retrieve the ferry. In the discussion Canute felt that he may be able to accomplish the task and volunteered. A brother Ira Sabe offered Here assist in the swim to help with the rope. As it happened both were greatly fatigued in the effort and knew that if they failed to reach the ferry they would both drown. Canute made a heroic effort, and reached the ferry, saving both of men. The crowd on the opposite bank, who watched the drama with intense interest, were ecstatic and shouted for joy. With the ferry back in operation the men soon had a number of the wagons across the river.
 During the following weeks the groups traveled peacefully along the Platte River for some distance. They had a prosperous journey in that game on the trail was abundant: buffalo, antelope, elk etc. It is not clear what Christian's role was in much of the activity -- we lack a narrative of his experience.
 When the companies reached Independence Rock they were met by a group of fellow pioneers, Brethren who had come from the Salt Lake settlement with both oxen and wagons to assist them. A Brother Thomas E. Ricks was assigned to assist the Norwegian group and they had great praise for his accommodating manner. The group came to have great love and confidence in him. He remained with them until they reached the Salt Lake valleys and was of great assistance.
 Christian's company followed the Sweetwater trail though the weather became stormy and windy -- they were uncomfortable at times but made good progress in their journey. They made two crossings of the Sweetwater and then some higher ridges, part of the Wind River Mountains to reach a place called Willow Creek. Here they were stopped by a severe snow storm that lasted almost two days. The group was constrained to stay in their wagons and Canute notes in his account that Christian Hyer was the first person out to make a fire, soon joined by Canute. Then the two of them started up the creek to find their animals. the found quite a number still alive but a larger number had perished. Back in camp the people listened anxiously and were discouraged by their report. It must have been a trying situation reminiscent of the Willy and Martian companies were many people died, in part because they did not have wagons and animals like Christian's company.
 Apostle Benson, the head of the company was anxious regarding the George A.Smith company that was several miles back on Strawberry Creek and asked for volunteers to go back and check the situation. Canute and Christian volunteered and started back on foot, a hard and tedious journey in snow almost waist deep. They found the Smith company in a situation much like their own. Some their strongest men were just going down the Sweetwater to hunt for their cattle. They returned to their own camp and reported on the situation. Meanwhile, some of the strong men of their own company had gone and found their cattle in good condition considering the severity of the storm. An abundance of large willows had served as both feed and shelter. In all they had camped in that place for three days. They had lost about 70 or 80 animals so of necessity they had to yoke every animal that could do any work. Traveling some ten miles they came out of the snow to clear ground and reached Pacific Springs where their cattle had good food again. They traveled on with good fortune until they reached the main Salt Lake settlement, October 25, 1849.
 (The information noted here is taken from the Autobiography of Canute Peterson, a fellow Scandinavian, about the same age as Christian -- Canute later was for years the Stake President of Sanpete Stake)
Written by Paul V. Hyer (October 2013)
