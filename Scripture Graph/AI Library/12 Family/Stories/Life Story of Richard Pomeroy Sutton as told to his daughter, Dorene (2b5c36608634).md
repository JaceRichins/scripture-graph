# Life Story of Richard Pomeroy Sutton as told to his daughter, Dorene.

_A story of [[John Allen Sutton (KWZ8-RFV)|John Allen Sutton]]._

## The story as it was written

LIFE STORY OF RICHARD POMEROY SUTTON
I was born on February 7, 1912, in Chicago, Illinois. My father was going to medical school there at the time.
My father, Richard Jacobs Sutton, the son of Richard Shepherd Sutton and Sarah Jane Jacobs, was born December 13, 1882, at Paris, Idaho. My mother, Mabel Rich, was born on Valentine's Day, February 14, 1886, in Paris, Idaho, the daughter of William L. Rich and Ella Amelia Pomeroy. She and my father were married in the Salt Lake Temple on October 5, 1904.
My great-grandfather, John Allen Sutton, was from Leicestershire, England. He was converted to the LDS Church by missionaries and was the only one of his family that was converted. His family were against him joining the church, but he did anyway. He left England and came to this country and eventually wound up in Salt Lake City. He traveled to Bear Lake at the time Charles C. Rich did or sometime thereafter. He lived the rest of his life there and worked as a blacksmith. I understand from some of the relatives that he was also the country doctor and the country dentist. If anybody had a toothache, he'd sit them up on the anvil and pull their tooth in the blacksmith shop. He had seven sons and one daughter. He died in 1913.
One of his sons was my grandfather, Richard Shepherd Sutton. He lived in Magna, Utah. His first wife was Sarah Jacobs. She died leaving three young children, my father and his sisters, Ethel and Clara. A few years later he married Sarah’s younger sister, Elizabeth Mary Jacobs, who died 10 or 12 years after that. He and both of his wives are buried in the Magna cemetery. Most of my relatives are buried in the Paris, Idaho cemetery.
On my mother's side of the family, her grandfather was Charles C. Rich, who was one of Brigham Young's apostles. His life history has already filled several books, so I won't go into much detail, except that he was a polygamist and had six wives and 50 living children. He and his wives are buried in the Paris, Idaho cemetery.
After my parents were married, they lived in Magna, Utah. My father worked as a team contractor and among other things, he contracted to haul brick from Salt Lake out to Garfield to build a smoke stack when they built the Garfield Smelter.
Dad worked hard and saved his money and was able to accumulate enough money to build a new home in Magna. It's the home that John R. Sutton lives in now. While they were living there, Del and Ella were born. Del was born in 1905 and Ella was born in 1907.
When dad was living in Paris and met mother, he had not finished high school. After they were married, she kept after him to get more education and talked him into going to medical school. He commuted from Magna to Salt Lake where he went to the LDS Academy to complete his high school education and get a diploma. He then applied to the Chicago College of Medicine and Surgery in Chicago and was accepted. They moved back to Chicago in about 1909.
Del and Ella could both remember living in Chicago and Del told me many times about roller skating on the sidewalks there.
Dad graduated from medical school in 1913 and we moved back to Paris, Idaho. Uncle Will Rich was in general practice there and was in poor health and unable to withstand the severe winters. He and Dad made arrangements for Dad to take over his general practice and he went back east to take a postgraduate course and specialize in dermatology.
I was just a year old when we moved to Paris. Mabel was born in 1914, Iva was born in 1915, and Bill was born in 1917.
My first remembrance of living in Paris is probably about 1917 or 1918 when I was about five or six years old. I recall that World War I was going on. I knew several of the men who were called to active duty, including Elzo Humphreys, who later married Aunt Mary, and Uncle Lyman Rich, my mother's younger brother.
I remember that when Uncle Lyman returned from the army, Mother had him over to dinner one day. Dad special-ordered a gallon of fresh oysters shipped in from the coast and we had a big oyster feed, which was a rare treat in Paris, Idaho in that day and age.
As we were eating the oysters, every once in a while we would bite down on a little pearl, some similar in size to small buckshot and others larger than a B-B. There must have been 10 or 15 of various sizes and after we'd found one or two, the rest of the family were very careful in eating the oysters to make sure they didn't swallow one. We saved them and dad took them down to a jeweler the next day. The jeweler said, "Gee, if you hadn't cooked them, they'd be worth $400 or $500." But cooking had taken all the glitter and glisten off and had spoiled the market value. It was a pleasant remembrance but not too profitable.
We lived in a big three-story house in Paris that was owned by Grandfather Rich. We lived on the main floor and there were two or three bedrooms on the third floor. Del and I had a bedroom on the third floor. Dad converted the middle floor to a sort of hospital where he kept a few patients who were unable to be cared for at home. I guess in that day and age, for a country hospital, it served the purpose.
While living in that house I remember having a little pussycat. I have a picture showing me dressed in pongee rompers carrying a cat around, and it still seems like I can remember that cat, playing with it in the back yard and chasing the chickens.
We used to get Montgomery Ward and Sears Roebuck catalogs and I remember looking forward to getting a new catalog so I could look through it. I seem to recall wanting an army uniform and I think mother ordered one for me for Christmas.
We had a porch around the front of the house with banisters and posts. I remember that Del tied a rope up to some fancy woodwork over the steps and put a swing board on it and made a swing for Ella. He was pushing her and she was swinging back and forth out over the front steps when all at once the ropes came undone and Ella fell to the sidewalk and broke her leg. I would guess she was 10 or 12 years old.
When Clarence Rich got married and needed a home to live in, we moved out of Grandfather Rich's three-story house and into a house dad bought that was up on the hill. There were cement sidewalks just in front of our yard but the roads were just dirt roads. We didn't even have gravel like we do nowadays. They graded the roads like they grade them today, so that they come down into kind of a slope on each side so the rainwater will run off the road.
I recall an incident while living in this house that involved Dad's Model T Ford which he kept parked out in front of the house. Dad had gone fishing one day with Dr. Hansen, a veterinarian, and left his car parked in front of the house. I was about five or six years old at the time, and a bunch of the neighbor kids and I were fiddling around the car. Mabel and her friends were in the front seat and we were giving them a ride. Model T cars didn't weigh much and three or four kids could push it three or four feet back and three or four feet forward. As I recall there was a key but Dad didn't ever use a key--you could just turn the switch off and on.
We turned the switch on and put the brake down to make it easier to push and as we were pushing it forward, it rolled off this gutter-like dirt depression and started down the street with the two girls in the front seat just screaming at the top of their voices. I guess they were only three or four years old and neither one of them had sense enough to turn it off or to steer it. It went down the street about half a block and then bumped into a tree. That's when Mabel went through the windshield and got that cut on her chin. I can remember they sent somebody up to get Dad and I also remember Mother coming down and carrying Mabel back. Her chin was really gashed open. Dad gave us all a scotch blessing for fiddling around with the car.
I started school in Paris and I can't remember my first grade teacher but my second grade teacher was Minnie Broomhead. She was a stinker. Del used to say that the kids in his grade would take licorice and candy and gum to school and eat it in school. She called it licor-ice and she'd give them a scotch blessing if she found any of them chewing gum or eating licor-ice in school. She was an old maid and not a very good-natured one, if I remember right.
In about the second or third grade, I remember seeing my first dead person, Uncle Amasy Rich. He had been back east on a trip and died, and they shipped him back in a closed casket. He was enclosed in the casket, but you could open the casket and see him through the glass.
Ordinarily I went home for lunch, as all kids did in those days, because Paris was such a small town and the school was within walking distance. This particular day I took my lunch because I had permission from Mother to go down and view Uncle Amasy, whose body was in the church. When I told the teacher I brought my lunch, she gave me a scotch blessing and said I was supposed to go home for lunch. She made me sit in the classroom with a couple of other kids to eat my lunch. About that time, Ella came and knocked on the door and told me that I was supposed to go down to the church with her. So we went down to see Uncle Amasy and ate lunch on the way. That was my first vision of a dead person.
Our house on the hill was a nice house in those days. Dad remodeled the front entrance way of the house and built a driveway so he could drive his car into the basement of the house, which served as an excellent garage in the cold winter months when the car was not in use.
Marion was born in 1920 and Irene was born in 1921. When Marion was a toddler, he fell into a ditch near the house but was saved from drowning by his rubber pants that kept him afloat until someone found him.
In the fall, probably about the first part of October, duck season opened. A few miles east of Paris there were thick marshes where the ducks were plentiful and Dad took me took me duck hunting at a very early age. As the ducks flew over, Dad would shoot one out of the air and if it would fall in the water, the dog would get it and if it fell to the ground, then I would retrieve it.
We would go out early in the morning and get our limit of 24 prior to 10 a.m. and return home so Dad could go to work.
The whole family gathered that evening in the basement to pick the feathers (which Mother saved to make pillows) and clean the ducks for cooking. Mother then stuffed each duck with dressing and cooked them in a double roaster, eight at a time. Each of the adults and the older children had a large duck and the kids had the smaller ducks. Everyone had a whole duck and sometimes two. We had duck dinner for about three days. We considered that a real banquet!
We had a big barn with a hay loft that was full of hay. We always had three or four milk cows and two teams of horses for Dad to use for transportation when he was unable to use the car. Since Dad had to have light, fast horses, he bought race horses. One team consisted of a mare and a gelding. The mare was named Hazel and the gelding was Dick.
When the county fair would come around in the fall, they'd have a race every week or so at one of the nearby towns, and Dad would take these two teams and run them in the races. Del was the jockey and would ride both Hazel and Dick in a running race.
The other team consisted of two black horses, one was a trotter and the other was a pacer. Dad would put a harness on one of them and fasten the harness onto a little cart called a sulky (I'm sure you've seen them on television). It had two wheels and a seat for the driver and fastened to the harness at the horse's side. The driver sat over the top of the horse's legs, just back of his tail. He'd braid the horse's tail to keep it out of his face. Dad used to drive the harness horses in the races and Del would ride the running horses.
After the races the horses had to be cooled down slowly and that was my job. They'd bring the first horse, which was all wet and sweaty, into the barn area where they kept the horses until they were getting ready to race. I had to wash it down and put a blanket on it, and lead it around in a circle to cool it down. They could only have about two swallows of water. So I got to see the first race but while the rest of the races were going on, I had to cool the horses down.
These horses were petted along and babied and trained in the summertime for the races, but in the wintertime they were strictly work horses for a purpose, except on some occasions. After Bear Lake was frozen over and the ice was three or four feet thick, they scraped the snow off the ice in areas and then raced these teams on cutters.
A cutter had sleigh runners on it and a seat for two people. They'd measure off a certain distance, a quarter of a mile or thereabouts, and they would get a standing start. A gun would go off and they would put the horses up to a dead run, and they'd bet on who was going to win. That was the racing pastime in the wintertime. Of course the horses were shod with shoes with sharp cleats in the bottom so they wouldn't slip on the ice or on the slick roads.
Dad had the Model T Ford and later on, a Buick touring car. The Buick had removable side curtains with plastic windows that could be folded and stored underneath the seat. When it rained, we had to jump out of the car and snap them into place to protect us from the rain.
He was able to drive during the summer months but in the spring and the fall, the roads became impassable and impossible to drive a car on because they were muddy. If you can picture a country road that's never had any gravel or cement or hard surfacing on it, that's the way all the roads were, even Main Street. In the spring and fall you could walk across the street in downtown Paris and be up to your ankles in mud. Dad had to put his car away in the garage because he'd get stuck every block if he tried to drive in the mud, so he had to utilize the team for transportation when it was muddy.
He had a light buggy that had a top over it and he'd hitch the team to the buggy to make his house calls if it was only a mile or two, and if it was only a couple of blocks in town, he'd walk. Sometimes he had to make trips to Bloomington, St. Charles, Fish Haven, or Laketown. Bloomington was about three miles, St. Charles was about eight miles, Fish Haven was about 12 miles, and it was about 18 or 20 miles around the side of the lake to Laketown. Sometimes Dad had to make calls clear around to Laketown, and that was over the border into Utah.
If he had to go to one of the closer towns, he'd take his team and at an ordinary walk or trot they were able to take him the complete distance. If he had to go further than St. Charles, to Fish Haven or Laketown, then he would stop at the house of a fellow in St. Charles who had a team, I guess you might call it a relay team. Dad would drive his own team as far as this fellow's place in St. Charles and then rent this man's team to go the rest of the way. This fellow would unhook Dad's team from the buggy, or the sleigh if it was wintertime, and take the horses into the barn and put blankets on them to warm them up, and feed them oats and hay. Then he would hook his fresh team up to the buggy or sleigh.
Meanwhile, the man's wife would have a pot of coffee on the stove, especially if it was in the middle of the night, and Dad would get warm by the fire and have a hot cup of coffee. By the time the man got the teams changed, Dad would leave St. Charles and drive on up to Fish Haven or Laketown and take care of the miscarriage or some other type of emergency. When things were under control, he'd turn around and come home as far as St. Charles, trade horses again, and drive his own team back home.
In the spring and fall, he used the buggy and in the wintertime, he used the sleigh. As I recall, every winter we had an average of at least four feet of snow on the level. We had a four-foot fence surrounding an orchard next to our house and Del used to ski over that fence every winter on some homemade skis that he made.
In those days we didn't have snowplows, we had horse power. So in the wintertime when we had snow, Dad utilized a sleigh. The sleigh had two sets of runners. One set was fixed on the back and the other set had two runners in front that turned like the wheels of a car turns, so it had maneuverability. It had a box on it that was approximately four or five feet wide and maybe eight to 10 feet long. It had wooden stays up over the top of the box like the pioneers had on the covered wagons and was covered with heavy canvas right up to the front. He could pull the drawstring on the canvas and close out the front except for a little opening about four inches wide for the reins from the harness on the team. The reins would come back through this opening so he could hold onto them.
Dad had a car battery on the sleigh and a spotlight fixed on the front wooden loop that held the canvas. When he was traveling at night, he'd snap the connections from the light onto the car battery so that he had light to shine out in front of the horses so they could see where they were going.
Also, we had a big rock that stayed in the oven all the time, except when Grammie would take it out to bake bread and roast meat. Whenever she wasn't utilizing the oven for cooking, that big rock, which was just about as big as the oven, stayed there all the time and stayed warm. Dad would use that big rock in the sleigh to keep his feet warm.
Del was about 13 years old and for four or five years he took care of the horses. Del and I slept upstairs and I can remember on many occasions when Dad would get an emergency call in the middle of the night and would call Del and tell him to get the team ready. Del would go out on a cold winter night and wade out to the barn through the deep snow to harness the horses to the sleigh. He had lights in the barn and he'd pull the sleigh inside and hook up the team and drive it around in front. Then he'd take some hot pads and carry the big rock out and put it in the front end of the sleigh so dad could put his feet on it to help keep his feet warm.
Dad would wear two pair of heavy underwear and heavy socks and lace-up shoes that came up over his ankles, plus overshoes. Over that he wore a suit vest, a coat and a heavy overcoat that came down to his ankles. As I recall, he had two different coats. He had a black beaver skin type and when that wore out, he got a new horsehide coat, which was heavy and warm. He also wore a fur cap that he pulled down over his ears, and heavy long gloves.
If he had to go further than St. Charles, he'd call the man at St. Charles and tell him he was just leaving and ask him to have the other team ready. He'd drive his team to St. Charles, usually at a fast trot. As soon as the man hooked up a fresh team to the sleigh, Dad would proceed to finish his trip to Fish Haven or Laketown where he had the emergency. He'd take care of the emergency and then drive his team back to St. Charles. They would hook his own team to the sleigh again and he would head for home. When he got home, he'd change clothes and go down to the office for the rest of the afternoon. If Del was in school, Dad would put the horses in the barn himself.
I remember one day he got home about noon and I asked him how he got along and he said, "Oh, I got along just fine but I about lost the horses." I said, "What do you mean?" And he said, "I just about lost them in a snowdrift." He said the wind had blown a big snowdrift in the road and they couldn't see where the road was. The horses got off to the side of the road and all he could see were their ears sticking up (I think he was exaggerating a little bit). He said he had to get out of the sleigh and wade through the snow and get the horses by the bridle and lead them out to more level ground.
Such were the hard winters that Grampy endured while he practiced medicine in Paris. By 1922 he decided that life was too short to spend it with those terrible winters and without some of the more modern conveniences, like paved roads. So we moved from Paris down to Magna, Utah.
In those days, the road over Logan Canyon was just a dirt road and was so narrow in places there was not enough room for one car to pass another. If you met a car coming in the other direction, one car would have to back down the grade to a point where the other car could pass through.
When we moved from Paris to Magna, there were eight of us in Dad's Buick touring car. I believe Del had gone on ahead with the horses. Besides the passengers, we had many of our belongings with us.
Back in those days, the cars had a running board on each side of the car and Dad purchased a metal guard that he was able to screw onto the outside of each running board. It ran the length of the running board and was about 12 to 15 inches high. That enabled us to put some of our suitcases on the running board.
We had some goats while living in Paris and Dad wanted to bring two of the little baby goats with us to Magna. They were only about a foot high and weren't weaned yet, so we had to feed them with a bottle. He put a baby goat each side of the car on the running board and tied the halter to the metal guard attached to the running board.
So here we were, coming down the narrow dirt road through Logan Canyon with the car loaded down and a baby goat on the running board on each side of the car. We must have been a sight to behold!
While living in Magna, Dad again became involved in horse racing. He bought a race horse that was all black except for a white star on his forehead. He was a pacer and won quite a few races around the Paris area. He ran him in the races at the Utah State Fair and took second place. Dad was offered $500 for the horse but turned it down. He thought he had a gold mine and said, "He'll take first place next year and then he'll be worth $1,000!" He kept him in Magna and a year later the horse had smelter smoke so bad that he didn't have any breath left. He was just like somebody with emphysema. Dad finally gave him to Uncle John, one of his relatives in Southern Utah, to drive on the mail route. That was the end of Dad's horse racing career.
I think Del was in his junior year when he moved to Salt Lake to live with Uncle Bill and Aunt Clara Tanner. He lived with them for a year and then in his senior year he came back to Magna and went to Cyprus High School. He played on the basketball team and was captain of the team.
We had a couple of milk cows and while Del was home, he'd milk the cows. When Del was living in Salt Lake, I took care of milking the cows and sold milk to some of the neighbors. I had a pony and rode my pony to deliver the milk. I also had a paper route and delivered papers every night. I think it was the Salt Lake Telegram.
I saved about $18 or $20 and Dad made up the difference to make $35 so I could buy a new bicycle. I went into Salt Lake with him one day and we picked out a new bicycle. I rode it home from Salt Lake out to Magna. I expect I was about 11 or 12 years old at the time.
While we were living in Magna, my brother Robert was born but died shortly after birth. He is buried at the Paris cemetery next to Dad and Mother.
Dad didn't have a very good practice in Magna because there were so many people that worked at the Copper Company and had their own company doctor or went to a doctor in Salt Lake. Dad's cousin, Tom Sutton, owned a grocery store in Idaho Falls and with Tom’s encouragement Dad decided to move his family there. I was just starting high school at the time.
When we moved to Idaho Falls, I went to work for Tom in the grocery store. I didn't know it at the time, but Dad paid my wages for the first winter that I worked there. I was paid $3 a week for working after school. I'd leave school at 3:30 or 4 o'clock and work until about 6 or 6:30 when we got out. So that was my introduction to the grocery business. Tom used to hand me $3 every week out of his pocket and Dad would pay him back, but I didn't know that until I'd worked for Tom for two or three years. He kept me on and I worked all summer and then, while I was going to school, I worked after school.
We moved to Idaho Falls in 1926 when I was a freshman in high school, and I graduated from Idaho Falls High School in the spring of 1930. It was a class of approximately 125. About half the class showed up for the 50-year reunion and much less for the 55-year reunion. We tried to have a 60-year reunion but there weren't enough living and in good health to make it worthwhile, so we canceled it.
My youngest sister, Dody, was born in 1927 while we were living in Idaho Falls.
In the fall of 1930 I enrolled in the University of Utah and went for one year. However, times were pretty hard and money was scarce and Dad couldn't afford for me to go back the next year. I worked for Tom in the grocery store until he went broke. He let out too much credit and people weren't able to pay their grocery bills, so he went broke and his creditors took the store over. I worked at odd jobs after that and didn't have a steady job.
I had a girlfriend that I was serious about and had gone with for about two years. Her name was Gwendolyn Brown.
OUR LIVES TOGETHER
After going together for two years, we were married on August 21, 1933 in Dillon, Montana.
Gwen: We lived in the Price Apartments at 137 7th Street in Idaho Falls. It was a two-room apartment on the second floor with a bathroom down the hall. The rent was $20 a month. There was no washer, so all clothes and bedding had to be washed by hand on the washboard in the basement. It wasn't large but we were very happy there.
Dick:I managed to get a job with C. C. Anderson Company in the grocery department. Tom Sutton was the manager. I was a clerk and my salary was $60 a month. I'd get a check every two weeks for $30. We were able to get by and save a little money. We weren't able to afford a car so we walked wherever we went or rode with somebody else. I worked at C. C. Anderson for probably a couple of years and then worked for Bybee Grocery. He owned a grocery store and offered me a little more money so I went to work for him but I only worked there for a couple of months.
Bud Davis, a farm boy from St. Anthony, bought out a corner grocery store with a little money he'd made raising pigs. He offered me $25 a week to come and work for him. He hadn't had any experience with grocery stores, so I went to work for Bud Davis and worked for him for about three years.
Gwen: The next year we had a baby girl, Dorene, who was born on March 22, 1934, at the Idaho Falls Hospital. She was a sweet, beautiful baby and started talking so cute soon after she was a year old. We found a baby sitter for her when she was about a year old. The baby-sitter was Mrs. Frederickson and she was very good to her. I went to work for Woolworth's earning $12 a week. I worked as a clerk and also did the ordering for some departments.
While Dick was working at the grocery store I had a frightening experience there. I had Dorene in the buggy and went to the store to walk home with Dick but he had gone on an errand. Just before the store closed, two men with masks on came in and held us up. They made me turn my face to the wall. They took the money from the registers and left. They never were caught.
Shortly thereafter I went to work for Rogers Bros. Seed Co. sorting peas. It paid a little better money than Woolworth's did and we were saving money so Dick could go back to medical school. While I was working there, my eyes started bothering me. Dick looked through the samples his dad had left him and found a tube of ointment for eyes that had a familiar name on it, so he put some in my eyes.
The next morning I went to work but I couldn't see well enough to do my work. I went to a doctor that Dick knew and after he looked at my eyes, he asked me if my husband had put something in them. I explained that he had, and he told me that the ointment had dilated my pupils. He laughed and told me not to let him put anything else in my eyes. I was off work for two or three days. My boss was Dick's good friend and he kidded Dick about that.
When Dorene was about 2 years old, she got scarlet fever. She wasn't too sick but we were quarantined for about 10 days to two weeks, and Dick had to sleep down the basement of the apartment. Dick's sister, Iva, was there with us and before the doctor came, she hid in the closet so she wouldn't be quarantined. As soon as the doctor left, she went home.
Dick:Dad moved the family to Oakley, Idaho, which was a small farming community, and opened an office in Burley, a town about 20 miles from Oakley. He also set up a small maternity hospital in Oakley so he could deliver babies there and save himself from the long drive to Burley each time he had a maternity case from Oakley. He also had an office in the Oakley Hospital where he also saw patients.
A little German nurse, Margarete, worked for Dad at the Oakley Hospital. She worked there for many, many years and we all came to feel a special affection for her.
I must recount a story about Margarete that occurred in the early part of winter. It happened that during this time of the year the hospital was empty - they didn't have any maternity cases. Dad was the county doctor and he was paid by the county to take care of the indigent who unable to pay for themselves.
A sheepherder came in to see him who was sick with pneumonia. Dad checked him over in the office and told Margarete that they were going to have to admit him and take care of him because he was a pretty sick man. Margarete said, "But doctor, I can't let that dirty man get into one of my clean beds." Dad told her to go ahead and give him a bath first then.
So Margarete put a breech cloth around him and some kind of a plastic protection around her uniform, and she got in the shower and scrubbed him down with White King soap and a big scrubbing brush. She then went down to Clark's Mercantile Store and bought him two pair of winter underwear. She put one pair on him and when he had recovered from his pneumonia and was released from the hospital, she gave him the extra pair of underwear to take with him.
Coincidently, it happened that approximately a year later this same man came into the hospital again with pneumonia. Dad checked him over and decided he was sick enough that he needed to be hospitalized. Again, Margarete washed him up before she put him to bed. As she was washing him she found that he had on both pair of the original underwear that she had given him the year before and they had the original price tags on them. He'd worn one pair until the weather got cold and then he put the other pair on over top. She threw those away and bought him two new pair to send him out with.
Gwen and I were cautious and careful with our money and saved it. It became apparent that the grocery business was a poor way to make a living the rest of my life, so I decided that as soon as possible I would go back to school. We had saved about $400.
Every Friday night the Rex Theater would have "bank night" and give away money. As I recall, they would offer so much and if the person wasn't there, then they'd double it the next week until the person whose name was chosen was present. It only cost 25 cents for the movie and we tried to go each week just in case they'd call our name, although we didn't have much hope of winning it.
Then one Friday night we had company early in the evening and when they left, we hurried down to the theater and as we entered they were calling my name. Someone shouted, "Here he is!" We had won $250!! With the money we had already saved, we now had a total of $650. That was sometime in the summer of 1937.
That fall we both quit our jobs and made arrangements to move to Salt Lake so I could go back to the university. We were going to take Dorene to Salt Lake and find a baby sitter to take care of her, but Gramp insisted that she stay out in Oakley with he and Grammie and my younger sister, Dody, who was only a few years older. He said that she'd get a lot better care and a lot better bringing up with Grammie taking care of her.
Although this was tough for Gwen to part with her little girl, who was three years old at the time, we finally acquiesced to Grampy's offer and left her with Gramp and Grammie and we moved to Salt Lake in September, 1937.
Gwen: I didn't realize how hard it would be to be parted from our little girl. My pillow was wet with my tears many nights. How I missed her! During the next four years, we had her with us on holidays and on vacations in the summer. Dick's folks were wonderful, loving people and I will be ever grateful for what they did for us, but I know now that it was a mistake for us to be separated and if I had it to do again, I would never have let Dorene go.
We made arrangements to ride to Salt Lake with a produce man that brought fresh lettuce and vegetables from the market in Salt Lake and took back a load of potatoes in the truck. On the way back to Salt Lake I rode between the driver and another fellow in the cab of the pickup and Dick rode in the back on the potatoes. What a way to go–but it got us there! With a few boxes and a suitcase, we had all our earthly belongings. We also had $650 in savings.
We arrived in Salt Lake in September, 1937 and Uncle Bill and Aunt Clara Tanner helped us find a little apartment at 504 East 8th South. It was one room with a pull-down bed, kitchenette and a private bath. We got some secondhand furniture and were very comfortable there. Our landlords were Sam and Cecilia Redman, who were very kind to us. We felt a great affection for the Redmans and remained friends for many years, maintaining contact until they passed on.
Dick: I registered at the University of Utah that fall. The first book assigned for my literature class, Tales of Canterbury, cost $50. After thumbing through the book and realizing the cost of everything, and what it was going to take to learn everything, I was about ready to give up. I hadn't been in school for five years and it was discouraging at first.
Gwen worked at Woolworth's and made $15 a week and I got a job working Saturdays at Success Market, a grocery store out at Sugarhouse. I started at the university and on Saturdays, I had to go to school until noon. I rode the streetcar to school and when I'd get out of school, I'd go out to Sugarhouse to work at about 1 o'clock and worked 12 hours until 1 a.m. on Sundays. Then on Sunday, while Gwen washed and ironed, I made stew and studied.
Union wages were 30 cents an hour but I made $3.60. Through the kindness of the butcher and produce man, they'd give us a good helping for our money and we were able to get our week's supply of groceries for half of my day's wages. We would make a big pot of stew on Sunday that would last us for most of the week. With Gwen's salary, we were able to pay the rent and get what clothing we needed and have a little spending money. At that time, movies were 25 cents, reruns were 10 cents, and milkshakes and streetcar fare were 10 cents. We both took a lunch and both rode the streetcar to work.
By carefully budgeting our money, we were quite self-sufficient in paying for our own living expenses. Tuition and school expenses, however, were more than we could earn and those expenses were paid for out of our savings until they were exhausted, and then it became necessary to seek financial help from dad to help pay for school expenses. Incidently, after
medical school and internship had been completed, we found that the money sent by dad amounted to slightly over $3,000.
During one summer I worked for Melling Landscape, a contractor, hauling fill (soil and rocks) for new homes. Loaded with a pick and shovel, I would drive out to the hills around the Bountiful area and dig soil from around roots of scrub oak and fill the truck and return to the new home and dump it and smooth it out.
The dump truck was a 3-ton older model Ford. It was in poor mechanical condition and didn't have good brakes. When driving down South Temple, great caution was required to judge the timing of the lights to avoid abrupt stops, which were literally impossible. Doing that type of strenuous work in July and August was the most strenuous work I have ever done.
In my third year of pre-med at the University of Utah, my cousin, Dick Tanner, who had returned from a mission, was also a third-year med student there. We rode together with his dad. Uncle Bill ran out of gas quite frequently as he would buy about two gallons at a time.
Dick and I were pleasantly surprised to find that in larger classes seating arrangements were in alphabetical order (Su, Ta). Lab experiments were done in groups of two and since we were seated next to each other, generally we were partners in lab work. This was the case from then on until the end of medical school.
My third year of pre-med passed uneventfully. It was a great day when the announcement was made about the middle of our third year of pre-med that out of 100 applicants, both Dick Tanner and I were among 30 accepted to the School of Medicine at the University of Utah, which was a two year medical school.
Medical school in the fall of 1939 was very interesting but very different. I had a class 8 to 11 daily--gross anatomy (dissection of human body). The first day we attended class, we were assigned by pairs to a dissecting table. It had a tin top and was approximately two and a half to three feet wide and approximately seven feet long, with elevated edges about two inches high sloping towards the foot.
We had been given printed instructions earlier to bring a large scrubbing brush and a bar of soap, along with a dissection kit which consisted of a little plastic folder containing various instruments, including scissors, probes, knives, forceps, etc. The first day in class, partners were assigned in alphabetical order so Dick and I were partners at the dissecting table.
One pair at a time, we were instructed to go to the next room and out of a large tank containing 16 human bodies floating in formaldehyde, each couple was required to reach their arms down into the tank, with one to lift the legs and one the head, and lift the body onto the cart and transport it to the dissecting table. The body was very well preserved and was very stiff and rigid. It was covered with greenish scum. The first day was spent in scrubbing the scum off the body.
At the end of class period we covered the body with large cotton clothes, wet with embalming solution, and a waterproof canvas. About half the students became sick and had to leave class. We had one girl in our class, Denise Quinn McAllister. The odor was very strong and caused
the nose to weep and eyes to burn because of the fluid used for preservation. The anatomy instructors, Dr. Bloomingfield and Dr. Edward Hashimoto, were excellent.
The first year of medical school was very interesting, fascinating and extremely difficult. I studied approximately four to six hours every day. Besides gross anatomy, other classes that
first year included histology, embryology and biochemistry. I completed the first year of medical school with about a B average.
In 1941 I had completed four years of college and was awarded a BA Degree in Anatomy. In the second year of medical school all students applied for transfer to a four-year school to complete the last two years of medicine. Dick and I were accepted at Northwestern in Chicago.
During that summer I acquired a job at the city cemetery, mowing lawns from 6 a.m. until 2:20 p.m., with a half-hour for lunch. When I got through work at 2:30, I would go home, clean up and drive to Lagoon and work there from about 4 p.m. until 1 a.m. My prime job there was sweeping and cleaning out family picnic areas, but I also worked for at every ride and concession in the entire amusement park.
Dick Tanner also worked at Lagoon and during slow afternoons, with a little buying of used material and a lot of scrounging, we constructed a 2-wheel trailer with walls about four foot high. The purpose of the trailer was to haul our personal belonging back to Chicago.
In the fall of 1941, Gwen and I, with Dick and Dale Tanner, left for Chicago about two weeks before school started in an older used car, used by Billy Tanner on his mission, pulling our homemade trailer behind. We took Highway 30 with a detour to visit Estes National Park. The trailer was loaded and covered with waterproof tarp. The trip to Chicago was enjoyable and uneventful.
Gwen: We had planned on staying in a motel on the outskits of Chicago on the evening of our arrival, but there were no motels available, so we found ourselves in the midst of downtown Chicago at midnight looking for a place to sleep. We ended up staying at the Whacker Hotel, which we were later told had been the headquarters of Al Capone.
The next day we went to the admissions office at the medical school. Through them we were given several references for living accommodations, and we went apartment hunting. What apartments! Most were undesirable but we found some unfurnished apartments at 1140 North LaSalle. Dick and I were on the fourth floor, and Dick and Dale Tanner were on the seventh floor.
The apartment was a one-room kitchenette with a bath and pull down bed. We bought a new mattress at Montgomery Ward's and searched the second hand stores for the rest of our furniture. Our little apartment looked nice when we fixed it up. We later sold our few pieces of furniture to the next renters when we left.
We had some good experiences while living in Chicago even though we were both busy with work and school. We enjoyed a close relationship with Dick and Dale. Several times the four of us were invited to the homes of some of our church friends from a nice area in North Chicago. We were attending church more often than we had been. We attended a movie occasionally.
We experienced the bitter cold there and also the very humid heat of the summer. It was a wonderful experience seeing the sights and learning more about things. It was there we watched Sonya Henie ice skate and went to a couple of concerts.
On November 5, 1941, we got word that my sister, Norma, had died with rheumatic heart disease. It was my first experience with the death of someone close. It was a very difficult time for me and I cried and cried. We couldn't afford for me to go home but we did send flowers and letters. I can accept death much easier now than I could then, since I have a much better understanding of the gospel.
Dick got a part time job on Saturdays at an A&P Market located a block from the apartment, and I worked for a large bakery and later, at a Montgomery Ward order department. The bakery did a lot of business and we had to work hard and move fast to keep up with the customers. I worked in an area about the size of a kitchen, with shelves and a display case. They would bring pies and other bakery goods in on a cart and we would have to quickly move them to the display case in between waiting on customers who would be lined up waiting for service.
One of the girls I worked with was Marge Sladky, who was somewhat of a character and had quite a sense of humor. One day when we were especially busy she asked me where she should put a pie she was holding, and being in a hurry, I said, "Just drop it anywhere," and she did just that! Right in the middle of the floor!
Dick: Medical school was a continuation of the same old grind, attending school in the daytime and studying in the evening. We did goof off and go to a movie once in a while. It was at a time when they gave a way a dish with each ticket.
The weather was high in humidity, hot and humid in the summer and bitter penetrating cold and windy in the winter. World War II was at its height, and medical school was on an accelerated schedule, so I attended school during the winter and summer months.
During the last year Dick Tanner and I obtained a job with the safety and security branch of the Army Ordinance Department. This branch was located on the 20th and 22nd floors of a downtown skyscraper building. This office trained inspectors to inspect ordinance plants where ammunition and other explosives were manufactured. The purpose of the inspection was to eliminate accidental explosions. The officer in charge, Assist. Col. Field, was an expert in explosives. If one explosion occurred at any one of the plants during our working hours, our office was first notified, and we would transmit the information to one of the colonels.
On a few occasions we sat up all night typing 135 teletype to all ordinance plants telling them to discontinue using a certain type of material. On other occasions we would have to arrange for chartered military aircraft to transport the colonel to the site of an explosion.
Our usual hours at this job started at 5:30 p.m. until 7:30 a.m. every other night. We tried to stay awake the first few nights but found it impossible so we took a little bedding and made a bed in the teletype room on the floor. One morning we slept in and were awakened by Col. Miles. After this he had a loud bell put on the teletype and furnished us with portable army cots and bedding so we were able to get a little sleep while still on the job.
Our job consisted of taking after-hours telephone or teletype calls and chauffeuring an army car, transporting the colonel around Chicago and occasionally meeting important people at the airport. This gave us adequate time to study most evenings and our salary made us self-sustaining.
During my last quarter as a senior medical student at Northwestern, I spent two weeks at the Chicago Maternity Center. By way of background, the Chicago Maternity Center was established around 1900 by Dr. Joseph DeLee, an obstetrician, and was built through donations. The poor families who lived on the south side of Chicago couldn't pay the expenses of a doctor or a hospital, so they'd try and have the baby themselves or with the help of a neighbor or a midwife. The mortality rate of infants and mothers was high.
Dr. DeLee's idea was to give the medical students experience in delivering babies and also to see if they could help lower the mortality rate of the infants and mothers. I'm quite sure the center involved students from each of the five Class A medical schools in Chicago, and each student had to spend two weeks at the center.
My turn to serve at the maternity center occurred during the middle of the winter. The first week I was the "junior" and the second week I was the "senior" student. Our names were placed on a list in pairs. When our turn came up and we were called, we traveled, usually by streetcar, to the patient's home. We carried with us two black bags containing the bare necessities, and the people had been instructed to set aside rolls of newspaper for our use. Dr. Lee found that newsprint didn't carry bacteria and was quite antiseptic.
When we arrived at the patient's home, if she had already had the baby, we would examine the baby and clean it up, and if the mother had no lacerations, we'd go back to the maternity center and we were still number one on the list again.
If the patient was determined to be in false labor, then we could call into the center and give them information and we were permitted to come back to the center and wait for the next case.
If it was a first baby, it was nothing for labor to last 12 to 36 hours, during which time we had to wait at the home or apartment, which was less than ideal.
It was common practice to see bedbugs and cockroaches running all over the floor, and we were instructed never to lay down on the couch or the floor without spreading newspapers on it first.
We would take turns examining the patient to check the progress of her labor and when it came time for delivery, the senior medical student was responsible for delivering the baby. We'd be real careful about the delivery so as not to get tears or lacerations, which would necessitate calling the center for an intern to come out and sew it up. This would delay us another two or three hours when we were badly in need of sleep.
After the senior student delivered the baby, the junior would suction out the mucus and spat the baby on the buttocks to get it crying. If it was crying loud and had good color, then we'd tie the umbilical cord and put gauze saturated with alcohol on the stump--the belly button. Then we'd dress the baby, wrap it in a blanket and give it to the mother.
The senior student was responsible for delivering the placenta and checking for lacerations. If there were no lacerations and the placenta was intact, then we were permitted to give the patient a drug to constrict down the uterus.
If things went well we'd wash up when we were finished and put our instruments back in the case and go back to the maternity center. We were then on the bottom of the list of about 12 and we'd get a good night's sleep before we'd be on the top of the list again.
If we were unfortunate enough to have a complication, the maternity center would send out an intern or a resident. If it was serious, they'd send an ambulance down to take the patient to the Cook County Hospital.
One night we had a lady that wouldn't deliver and the center sent out a lady obstetrician, who drove out to the slums in her big Cadillac. At that time, the average delivery in Burley was $35 to $50, and back then our attending lady charged $500 for a normal delivery and the head of the department at Northwestern charged $1,000 for delivery.
I was real happy when I had finished my two weeks because we were always behind on sleep. Sometimes we'd go for 48 hours and maybe get two hours sleep.
One weekend or holiday when Gwen wasn't working, I got a call to go to a certain address for a lady's second or third baby. I called Gwen to come out and meet me and we went together to the lady's house. After the delivery we handed the baby over to Gwen and she had the pleasure of washing and oiling and cleaning it up. She then dressed it and handed it over to the mother, which, besides watching it being born, was quite a thrill for her.
Gwen: Seeing a new little spirit come into the world was a special experience that I will never forget.
Dick: It was a bitter cold night, with about six inches of snow on the level and the wind blowing about 15 to 20 miles an hour. While waiting to change streetcars, we sought shelter in a doorway at the bottom of a stairway. There were half dozen others standing there too and one of them, a policeman, struck up a conversation with Gwen and I. He wanted to know where we were from and what we were doing in Chicago. Gwen explained and he said "You're from Idaho? Why you've even got shoes on!" Gwen didn't say anything to him but she told me later that it was a smart aleck thing to say. Of course we had shoes on because there was six inches to a foot of snow! And it was cold, cold, cold!
That concludes our experience at the Chicago Maternity Center.
I graduated from Northwestern University Medical School in March, 1943. Our country was involved in World II at this time, and all the graduates of the medical school had been deferred from active duty in the army until they had completed a nine-months internship. We moved back to Salt Lake City where I began my internship at LDS Hospital.
Gwen: When we returned home to Salt Lake, the Redmans again found room for us in part of their home on 7th East. For the next year and a half, I worked first at an arms plant and then at a radio plant. They were tedious jobs but they paid well. During this time I was attending church part of the time but was not as active as I should have been.
Dick: One-third of the interns were deferred from the army for a second nine months as junior residents, and one-third of those were deferred for a third nine months as senior residents.
There were 11 interns at the LDS Hospital and three of us were deferred for the second nine months.
The Utah Copper Company in Magna, Utah was an essential war industry and was in need of a company doctor. I was a junior resident at LDS Hospital until July, 1944, when I was assigned as plant physician at the Utah Copper Company plant. I remained there for approximately one year.
Gwen: When Dick finished his internship and residency (at $25 a month), we moved to Magna and we were furnished a house. We really enjoyed it after living in apartments for so long. Dick worked hard for long hours, including house calls, but made good money for the first time! I was then pregnant with our second child.
We had all of Dick's family there for Thanksgiving that year. The following Sunday, November 27, 1944, I went into labor and Jeannie was born at the LDS Hospital in Salt Lake. She weighed 5 pounds 2 ounces and was a small, beautiful little doll. (I was in the hospital 10 days with Jeannie and 14 days with Dorene). Dorene was now living with us again.
While I was in the hospital with the new baby, Dorene had a girlfriend sleep with her and only a short time after I got home, she broke out with the chickenpox. She was covered from the top of her head to the bottom of her feet. She was miserable but got along all right. In due time, the new baby had them too. We worried about her since she was so tiny, but she had only a few pox and was not very sick.
By the time Jeannie was two years old, she would pretend to read her nursery rhyme book. Our friend, Earl Wilcox, heard her and was so amazed. He said, "Our daughter is the same age but she can't do that." He was impressed!
It was in Magna that we got our first car–a used one–and I finally started to drive.
Dick: In July, 1945, Dick Tanner and I were called to active duty in the army. There were 1100 doctors just out of training who were assigned to Carlyle Barracks, Carlyle, Pennsylvania for basic training and tropical medicine. We were there for six weeks. I was then assigned to Lovell General Hospital at Ft. Bevens, Massachusetts for approximately a year, during which time Gwen stayed in Salt Lake with Dorene and Jeannie. While I was stationed there, Gwen had an opportunity to come for a visit. She rode with some of the other families of doctors who were stationed at Ft. Bevens and rode with me to Boston, returning to Salt Lake by train.
Gwen: Before Dick left for the army we moved into a small house on the west side of Salt Lake. We didn't like it there and soon found a nice apartment on 7th Street. I stayed there with the two girls while Dick went to Ft. Bevens in Massachusetts. Helen Bingham, Dale Tanner and I went back to Massachusetts to visit the men while they were stationed there. We also went to New York and saw, among other things, the Statute of Liberty, before returning home.
Dick: I was stationed in Ft. Bevens for approximately a year. Because the war in Europe was over and they were closing down Ft. Bevens, I was then transferred to Letterman General Hospital in San Francisco. Gwen flew back to Boston in a DC-3 and rode back to Burley with me prior to my leaving for San Francisco. After I had been stationed in San Francisco for approximately a month, I found a suitable apartment for Gwen and the girls in Richmond, California.
Gwen: The children and I stayed in Salt Lake until Dick found a place for us in the housing area in Richmond, California near San Francisco and then he sent for us. We rode there on a train and the girls loved it! We enjoyed our stay there. It was nice to be together again.
One day while we living in Richmond we had pumpkin pies for dinner and had left one pie on the cupboard while we went for a long drive. When we arrived back home, the pie was covered with little red ants. We could see a whole column of them coming in from the outside. We soon got rid of the pie and the ants too.
We had a phonograph there and that is when Dick and I perfected our jitterbug dance, which was popular then. We also did a lot of sight-seeing while we were in the area.
Dick: I was discharged from the army in approximately April, 1947, and we came back to Oakley. We stayed with the folks until we found an older but nicely furnished home in Burley which we purchased for $10,000. Dad had an office in Burley, as well as in Oakley, and I took over his Burley office and then helped him two days a week at his Oakley office. This gave me the opportunity to repay Dad and Mother for their loan and for taking care of Dorene. While they were living, all receipts from the Oakley practice were deposited to their account.
When I began my practice there were only three other doctors in the Burley area. I estimate that we all practiced from 70 to 100 hours a week many times. I was on call day and night, seven days a week.
Dad had had three heart attacks and was a severe diabetic, and he was only able to spend part-days working in the office. His disability became increasingly worse until he was unable to leave the house and spent the last two years of his life with mother nursing him at home. Percy Smith kept him supplied with oxygen. He died on June 3rd, 1951, of congestive heart failure, complicated by diabetes.
About the time that Dad became confined to the house, Mother was out in the yard feeding the chickens and fell and broke her hip. Apparently she yelled for help and someone stopped and took her in the house and they called me. I went out to Oakley and examined her and suspected she had a broken hip. We had an ambulance bring her to the Cottage Hospital in Burley for x-rays, which revealed a fracture of her right hip. We gave her a hypo for pain and made her as comfortable as we could in the back seat of the Buick, and took her to Salt Lake to Dr. Okelberry at the LDS Hospital.
Dr. Okelberry operated on her and put in a pin/plate combination for fixation so the bones couldn't move. She remained in the hospital for several days and then we brought her home to Oakley. She was able to get around the house on crutches and/or a wheelchair and take care of Dad, fixing their meals, etc.
Approximately every three or four months I'd bring her to the Cottage Hospital for x-rays to see if there were signs of healing of the fracture, but none of the x-rays revealed any evidence of healing. The x-rays were sent to Dr. Okelberry for his review.
In April, 1951, Dr. Okelberry reported, after examining the x-rays, that since it had been well over a year since the fracture, there should have been sufficient healing for her to try partial weight-bearing with one crutch. After about two weeks of partial weight-bearing, she was aware that the pin had broken. Nothing further was done for her leg at that time because Dad's condition became critical.
Dad died approximately two weeks later and was buried June 8th, 1951, in Paris, Idaho.
We took Mother directly from Paris to Salt Lake to see Dr. Okelberry, where he re-operated and put in a new pin/plate. He could see no reason why there hadn't been healing. We brought her back to Oakley, and she had been home for two or three weeks when she noticed her abdomen was causing her a great deal of discomfort. I felt concern because of her symptoms and suspected she might have cancer.
I took her to Salt Lake to Dr. Cowan, who was a cancer specialist, and he did an exploratory operation on her abdomen and discovered that she was full of cancer. The cancer was so widespread it was impossible to determine the primary site, possibly ovarian. Dr. Cowan recommended that we bring her home and treat her with TLC and not prolong her life with hospitalization or other life support measures.
In the meantime, we moved her belongings into a small apartment on 16th Street in Burley, where we could be close to take care of her. Someone in the family stayed with her at all times until her death. She died on October 22nd, 1951, and was buried in the Paris cemetery next to Dad.
Gwen: In the spring of 1948, we moved into our first real home in Burley at 1451 Miller Avenue. Some furniture came with the house and we added new things as we could afford them. Dick had an office on Overland Avenue in Burley and spent two days a week at the Oakley office.
Our beautiful baby boy, Bob, was born on January 1, 1949. Our first boy! How proud we were, especially his dad. He was the first baby of the year from Cassia County and we received several nice gifts. He was a happy, good-natured child and was so tender-hearted. A scolding for punishment was as effective as a spanking (none of them got much of that).
Dick: In 1949 we had one of the worst winters that I can ever remember, with heavy snow and extreme cold. It was so cold the pop bottles on our back porch froze and popped. One evening we had a real severe snow storm and blizzard and it closed all the roads in the area. They were able to open the roads downtown with the aid of road graders and some of the city equipment, but the roads two or three blocks from the center of town were drifted, some of them 10 and 15-feet deep with snow. All the country roads were blocked with high drifts. They stayed that way for about four or five days, until they finally got through them with a snow plow.
About the fourth day a man called and wanted me to make a house call on his elderly mother out in the View area. I explained that I'd be happy to come and see her but that the roads were closed and I wasn't able to get out there. He told me they'd just opened some of them and said if I'd go out towards the Oakley highway to the Albion turnoff, they'd opened three roads to the east. He said if I would go out to the third road that was plowed and turn and go to the end of the road where the snow plow turned to the right, it would bring me right up to his house. He said if I'd just follow the road I could get right to his place.
So I drove out there in my car, which had chains on, and was careful to follow directions. I examined the lady and found nothing critical and prescribed some medication for her. It was approximately 8 or 9 o'clock that evening and I hadn't been home for supper, so I got back in the car and started for home.
Coming back on the Albion highway I forgot to count how many crossroads had been plowed out and I went across the main Oakley road onto the west side, where they'd also plowed out some roads. After a couple or three miles, I came to the end of where the snow plow had been and there was nothing but a big drift in front of me. I backed up and turned around and then tried another route. After about 30 minutes, I was as lost as I could possibly be.
I finally got out of the car and climbed up to the top of a drift, which was three or four feet higher than the car was. I could see the red lights from the radio station, and then I became oriented as to where I was. I then followed this road back to the main Oakley highway and was able to drive home without getting lost again.
That winter we had the heaviest snow I have ever seen. The drifts on either side of the road were as high as the cross pieces on the telephone and light poles. Fortunately we didn't have any more severe storms that blocked the road, except temporarily, and we managed to get through the winter without any catastrophes.
Gwen: Our second son, Steven, was born on October 12, 1950. He was a beautiful sweet baby with curly blonde hair. He was impish and full of life and kept us all on our toes. We called him our little "Dennis the Menace." He was good for us.
Dick had remained in the army reserves and was called again to report back on October 13th for the Korean war, leaving me in the hospital with the new baby and in tears. How I missed him! He was released from the army in April, 1951, and we were so happy to have him back home and be a family together again.
My dad passed away on December 14, 1952. He had been ill for some time and it had been difficult for him to breathe. We will miss him.
The next few years were busy ones. Dick worked long hours in his practice and I was busy with the family. I was going to church more often but wasn't too involved. Then, in 1954, I was called to be a primary teacher for the guide patrol. I did this for 12 years. I soon learned to love the boys in my classes. The gospel lessons were good for me and strengthened my testimony, and I enjoyed the scouting lessons with the boys, including the five-mile hikes and cooking over a bonfire.
When Bob was in my class in 1961, the scouts in our ward were invited by the class in Downey, California to come and visit for a week. A caravan of four cars went and I drove one carload of boys. When we arrived there they treated us royally and took us to many places, including Disneyland, Knotts Berry Farm, Marineland, a big-time ball game, and a beach party. We also went deep-sea fishing one day. I passed out the seasick pills to those who wanted them and the only one that said, "No thanks" is the only one that got sick! Something special every day! It was a wonderful trip. Then, two years later, Steven's group went but they didn't need me as a chauffeur that time.
As the children grew I was involved with P.T.A., including being president of the Junior High P.T.A. I belonged to a music club, a home demonstration club for many years, and a literary club for several years. I also worked on the polls as senior judge for several years. Dorothy Harpster and I started the heart fund drive in Burley and worked together for a few years, and then I took over the heart memorial and did that for about 25 years. I was on a bowling league for a long time, with a high score of 231!
Dick: A hobby I greatly enjoyed and one in which Gwen and the boys participated was trapshooting. Many pleasurable hours were spent out at the gun club. I also enjoyed playing the piano and later, the organ. At family get-togethers several of us played harmonicas.
Gwen: We bought a boat and enjoyed family outings on the Snake River and at Minidoka Dam, and for several years took the family and went to Bear Lake for a few days at a time. We always had a good time there, boating, swimming, roller skating, fishing and hiking. Also, we attended some of the Rich and Sutton family reunions that were held there. They had little cabins on the shores of Bear Lake and each family would have their own cabin and then visit back and forth with one another. Dick: In 1979, I retired from my medical practice and sold my office building and all my equipment and supplies. I had many interesting cases during my 33 years of practice and delivered more than 4,000 babies. I enjoyed serving the people of the area and had many choice experiences.
One memorable experience occurred in the early stages of my practice when I was called upon to exert all my energy and intuition.
I had Robb Smith, who was my brother-in-law Percy's dad, in the Cottage Hospital with a bleeding ulcer, and I had an OB patient in labor in Dolph Maternity Home, a private home that the lady with very little experience had set up for delivering and caring for babies, and a second lady in Oakley that was in labor. I didn't know which way to go first.
I got Robb Smith on the examining table and had the lab technician there match his blood, and we found that he matched with Thurlow, his son. In those days we didn't have facilities for drawing blood and then giving it back to the patient, so we'd set one on the operating table and the other was on a gurney or a cart next to him.
We had a tube with a needle in each end and a three-way stopcock in between attached to a 50cc syringe and I'd turn a little spigot and fill the syringe full of blood and draw it out of Thurlow's arm, and then I'd turn the three-way stopcock, and as I pushed the blood, it would go into Robb. Then I'd repeat that--turn the stopcock again and draw 50cc of blood out of Thurlow and switch the stopcock and push it into Robb.
I sat there and gave Robb a little over a pint of blood and got his blood pressure up. His color was better and he was not actively bleeding, so I admitted him to the hospital and prescribed medication for him.
About that time, I had a third OB come into the Cottage Hospital. She was in early labor and I was sure wouldn't deliver as soon as the one at the maternity home or the one at Oakley. As soon as I left the hospital, I went down to Dolph Maternity Home and got there just in time to deliver the baby. Margarete was calling me from Oakley to hurry, that she was holding the baby back. She'd given the mother a few drops of ether and was having her breath deep to slow
down her pains. I delivered the baby at Dolph Maternity Home and then drove about 80 miles an hour to Oakley and got there in time to deliver that baby.
I called back down to the hospital and the OB there was in active labor but not urgent, so I took my time coming back to the hospital and finally delivered the third baby that night. I then checked on Robb Smith and his bleeding was under control and he hadn't lost any more blood, so I went home to bed about 3 a.m. I didn't have very many hair-raising experiences like that all coming at once.
One of my most worrisome cases was a little girl about three or four years old, who was struck with a car out in Oakley. She was running across the street and just kindof bumped into the fender of a car that was going real slow and it caused a cut in her head. Her mother picked her up and put a handkerchief or something over her scalp so that she wouldn't mess up the car and her clothes and she brought her down to my office.
I took her into the office and cleaned up the laceration, and in cleaning off the scalp, I could feel that the skull underneath the scalp was irregular. When I felt it with my gloved finger, I could feel a depression in the bone. There was rather profuse bleeding from the scalp and I put four or five silk stitches in, just enough to control the bleeding, and sent her out to the hospital for a skull x-ray.
She was about half-way to the hospital, which was about seven or eight blocks away, when she went into a coma and had right-sided continuous convulsive seizures. She was in that condition when she arrived at the hospital. When they called me, I dropped everything and ran out to the hospital and that's the condition I found her in. She was in a coma with constant shaking seizures on one side of her body.
I called Dr. Louis Schricker, who was a neurosurgeon in Salt Lake that I was well acquainted with and a doctor I'd referred many cases to before, and explained the situation. He told me this was a “real emergency and something that you've got to do yourself and you've got to do it right now. You take this little girl into surgery and open that cut up. What she's got is a depressed skull fracture and you've got to pry loose the broken piece of bone that's pressing on the brain and get the pressure off the brain or she's going to die."
I called Dr. Annest to help me and also Miss Bird, who was the nurse anesthetist, as well as the administrator of the hospital. We took the little girl into surgery and at this stage she didn't need any anesthetic. We shaved enough hair off the scalp that we could sterilize an area and then put sterile towels and drapes around the area. We took the stitches out and opened the wound a little further and we could see where a triangular piece of the skull bone had been compressed down below the surface of the rest of the skull.
In the Cottage Hospital there weren't any special type instruments, like a trephine, for removing a portion of the skull and neither were there any at the Rupert hospital, so we had to do with what we had. It so happened that with a sharp knife and other instruments, we were able to pry this depressed triangular piece of bone up, first one edge and then another, until we got it above the level and loose enough that it came completely off. It wasn't attached to any of the three sides.
As soon as we elevated this triangular piece of bone from pressing on the brain, we found a blood clot, which we removed with suction. As soon as the pressure was removed, the little girl's seizure immediately stopped and the little girl started to come out of the coma and cry for her mommy, so the nurse had to give her some anesthetic.
There was bleeding around the edges of the dura mater, which is the hard fibrous covering of the brain under the skull. Being very cautious and careful, we were able to clamp and tie off or cauterize and stop most of the bleeding. There was still a little bit of oozing, so we put in a small drain about as big around as a pencil made of very soft thin latex rubber. It comes in a tube of varying length and we cut off a length about six inches. We put part of it underneath the edge of the dura mater and then sutured the rest of it, leaving this little drain in so that any blood that continued to flow would drain out through this drain tube and not accumulate and cause pressure.
We didn't know what to do with the extra piece of bone that we'd taken out, so we discarded it and sewed up the scalp. We then put a dressing on it and as we were finishing, the anesthetist
stopped the anesthetic and let the little girl wake up. She was alert and vigorous and crying for her mommy.
I sent her down to Dr. Schricker the next day for follow-up care and I never did see her again, but I heard that she got along real well and didn't have any complications.
That is just an example of the things that family doctors have to take care of when they're in the country and don't have the facilities or the benefit of rapid transportation to Class 3 & 4 centers like medical schools have for complicated injuries and procedures.
