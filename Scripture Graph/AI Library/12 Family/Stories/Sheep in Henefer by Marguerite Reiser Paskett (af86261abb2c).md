# Sheep in Henefer by Marguerite Reiser Paskett

_A story of [[Alma Ether Richins (LLC5-YZB)|Alma Ether Richins]]._

## The story as it was written

Brigham Young, with his inspired forethought, advised the Saints on their Journey to the Great Salt Lake Valley, to take with them 4 few head of sheen, as well as the best machinery for spinning, weaving wool, cotton, flax or silk.
In one of his discourses, he said: “ I want them to save their wood and to keep it in this Territory. If we have not factories sufficient to work all the wood that grows in this Territory and in these mountains,we will send and get more machinery and build more factories, and work up the wool for the people.
Parley P. Pratt advised every family crossing the Plains to have one wagon, three Yoke Of oxen, two cows , two beef cattle and three sheep.
Each companv that followed brought with them a few sheep, realizing their value as a source of wool which was needed for the manufacture Of cloth.
In a letter written by Wilford Woodruff to Orson Pratt in England, in April 1857 he wrote, The attention of many in this city and terri¬tory has been called to the importance of sheep raising. We can never attain to commercial independence necessary to our prosperity and welfare, until we can grow our Own wool and cotton and supply the market with home manufactured cloth". (D. U. P. files).

For many years, the Great Salt Lake Desert was open to all sheep men. First come, had the best feed. Later when the Tay¬lor Grazing Law came into effect, desert allotments were made a designated area, certain number of sheep, dates of entry and exit and a fee for this privilege. This was also true on the forest reserve. When a man sold his sheep, the grazing right on the desert or forest permit went with them. As time passed, the government took much of the west desert grazing area for bomb practice and some forest areas for recreational purposes, thus radically reducing the number of sheep permitted. Graz¬ing on the watersheds of the high mountain areas which supplied culinary water to cities was forbidden, making it necessary for some sheep men to sell their privately owned lands to cities and the government. So owners of sheep have felt this squeeze. This has become a contributing factor to the reduc¬tion of sheep in the state of Utah.
There were few places of employment for the young men of Henefer. In early days, so many of them worked on their fathers' farms or herded sheep. An older man herded the sheep and the younger man was the camp tender. His work was to take care of the horses, set up the camp after ho had moved it to a place where the sheep could be bedded around it, cut wood and cook the meals and haul water.
The preparation for winter started in the fall after the lambs had been marketed, the old ewes cut out and sold or kept home and fed in the winter. These were the toothless old biddies" that could not survive the winter on desert forage. The sheep camp was now to be "home away from home" for at least 5 months. It was a wagon with wooden bows, over which was placed a layer of canvas, then one of oil¬cloth, then another covering of canvas, which in later years was waterproofed to keep, out the winter storms and cold. Later, these iron tired wooden wheeled wagons were outfitted with rubber tires and now many are metal covers "Home On The Range" house trailer type. On the outside of the early camps were necessary pieces of equipment in the box like compartments, such as, the wagon Jack, can of axle grease, kerosene can, axe, shovel, wash board, etc. The "Jockey box" in front of the wagon, held the hammer, horse shoes, rasp for taking care of the horses* feet and the shoe stand to repair the men's shoes.
The door of the camp had two parts the bottom half which was of wood could be secured tightly, the upper part was canvas covered and slid open.
Inside the camp, every thing was compactly arranged the stove, cupboards, water bucket and table were all within easy reach of the camp cook. The heavy iron stove with its bake oven was on one side of the doorway, the cupboard was next to the stove, the clock and kerosene lamp were on top of the cupboard. On the other side door on the projection shelf stood the water bucket with cup or dipper hanging over it. On the same side was the table which was fastened by a leather strap to the bows when not in use and sup¬ported by hinged wooden legs at meal time.
The supply of groceries consisting of canned goods, flour, sugar, beans, bacon, etc. was stored under the crossways projection and could be reached by lifting this board which also served as a seat. Eggs and vegetables had to be stored in the camp, to prevent freezing. The bed occupied about
one half of the camp crossways, with a sliding window under the shelf over the bed. This shelf held magazines, books, cloth¬ing and musical instruments. On the sides of the camp fastened to the bows, were pockets made of denim that held the comb, looking glass, penails, playing cards, harmonicas and the account book. The gun hung from leather straps fastened to the wagon bows, making it easily accessible if a coyote or other animal bothered the sheep. Drinking water was carried in ten gallon metal cans. When water had to be hauled from long dis¬tances, none was wasted.
In November, the sheep were trailed from ranges close to Henefer, over Little Mountain, down Emigration Canyon, through Salt Lake City, past Garfield and other smelter towns. Great care had to be taken so the sheep would not drink from the polluted ponds that had accumulated smelter dust, as this would make them sick. The trail then went on to Grantsville, Rush Valley, Dell or Cedar, depending on the winter grazing allot¬ment, or Lakeside.
The forage for the sheep wasbud brush, shad, scale, grease¬wood. This natural browse was salty. At other seasons, sheep were given course sheep salt but in recent years, mineralized salt blocks have been used. Even the little lambs enjoyed this salt lick.
About March first, when the winter feed was gone and before the Spring feed started to grow, the sheep would scatter and be hard to handle, so an extra man had to be hired. The generous sized sheep camp quilts made of heavy materials, mostly pieces of old woollen clothing, were appreciated then.
As long as there was snow, the sheep could quench their thirst that way. If it became too crusted, sore mouths would make it difficult for them to eat. When the snow was gone on the flats, the sheep had to be driven to the tops of the low hills for moisture. Every second day, the drive for water was necessary. The men would start out early with the herd, take their lunches with them and not get back to camp until dark. The camp tender's job was to melt snow for the horses. If the snowfall was light, it took a long time to gather enough snow for the horses. Some of the older men were cross and cranky and the young camp tenders couldn't even melt snow to please them. Before private wells were dug and gasoline pumps or engines installed, tank cars of water were Purchased from the rail¬road and water troughs filled. Cost $20 $30. In 1965, $27 per tank car. A 12,000 gallon car watered the sheep twice for $50 or two herds would arrange to use it. One herd one day and the second herd the next, to avoid paying demurrage to the railroad. (K.J.P.) As the herds trailed for water, great clouds of dust enveloped men and animals. When it rained, the desert mud was very sticky and would bog the wagon down.
Often it was necessary to feed both hay and grain, when the winters were severe. In the winter of 1949, when there was a very heavy snow storm, baled hay was dropped by airplanes in isolated areas. State Highway snow plows, breaking a way through the heavy drifts, headed feed trucks for that region and as each camp was reached, their particular truck of hay and grain stopped and the snow plow proceeded on to the next camp to make a roadway. This systematic method made it possible for one snow plow to help many outfits in that locality.
When grain and groceries were shipped out on the train to Lakeside, the camp tender would take the commissary wagon. This was also mail time. Imagine the disappointment after a long trip, not to get a letter from home! Now camps are supplied by trucks, direct.
How did the men pass their time when the day's work was done? If they were musically inclined, the harmonica, banjo or guitar were brought out, if they were fortunate, a hand cranked phonograph. Many of the men who had not been able to attend school beyond the sixth grade, took advantage of this time to study. The Bible, Western and Detective Stories were found in all camps. If some one was going for mail soon, a letter could be written.
The glare of the sun on the snow would often cause the her¬der to get "snow blind" for a few days. These men were rugged, used to the rigors of winter. Their supply of medicine consisted of, mentholatum, carbolated vaseline, "Ward's Liniment", mustard for plasters and onions which were sliced thin, sprinkled with sugar, for a cough medicine. No doctor within many miles, made them self reliant. The younger men would sometimes decide to grow beards and sur¬prise their families when they returned in the spring, after a long stay on the desert.
The barking of the many sheep dogs, announced the arrival of company at the camp, which was always a welcome change, if it was only another herder who might have news from the home town. A pocket knife would soon be brought out and whittling begun. They carved many intricate things beside shavings.
Have you ever eaten at a sheep camp? Sour dough bread, meat fried in deep fat, sheep camp beans with mutton, coffee from a pot half full of coffee grounds, rice pudding and the usual jam. The camp tender could make good pies, too! Any stranger was welcome to stop and eat, even if the camp tender wasn't there, but to leave dirty dishes after was a breach of etiquette. The camp door was never locked.
Every herd had a certain number of black sheep which were the markers. By counting the blacks, the herder could pretty well tell if he had lost any sheep, as the natural conclusion was that some white sheep would go with the black sheep that strayed. If a black sheep was killed for mutton and this was not written in the account book, it would cause unnecessary searching.
When a mutton was killed at camp, this was done in the evening and the carcass was left out all night to cool thoroughly. Then in the morning, it was split and then put in a clean seamless sack and put in the bedding in the day time, hung out again at night until the meat was gone. It had to be hung high enough so the dogs would n't get at it. The dogs were fed meat trimm¬ings and table scraps an extra pan cake was fried for each dog no fancy dog food in packages. "A good sheep dog was worth as much as a man". To watch some of these intelligent dogs obey their master's shout or whistle to round up a stray or bunch of sheep P was very interesting. The dogs were protected from the cold weather by a canvas curtain that was unrolled from its fastenings on the outside of the wagon box. The dogs would crawl under the camp wagon shelter. The horses had heavy horse blankets to protect them from the winter's cold.
Herders' wages were $40 $50 in early days. Some of the young men got $15 $25 a month and now hardest wages are $200 $225 a month or more.
Pay days for the sheep man was twice a year after the wool was sold in the early summer and the lambs and cull ewes sold in the fall. The merchants would carry their accounts until the sheen men received their money. The sheep were assessed in Box Eider County, if they went to the desert and the tax money was pro rated between the counties where they grazed Summit, Davis and Box Elder counties.
If the herds aot mixed, the sheen men concerned were noti¬fied when the sheep would be corraled, so they could be picked out. Every sheep man had his own ear marks and brand. The sheep men of Henefer and surrounding areas were honest in notifying others when the sheep would be corraled.
Days on the desert were somtimes very exciting! About 1909, rabies among coyotes or the desert was bad, as they would go crazY, having no fear of men or dogs attacking them. David De:4rden of Henefer was returning from a trin to Lakeside for supplies. It was cold, so he walked behind the jerky iron tired wagon to keep warm, giving the horses free rein. A coyote bit Dave on the calf of his leg and as Dave jumped into the wagon, the animal followed him. He got the coyote out and returned to camp. He was sent home to Heneferg where Dr. Dorland treated him for rabies. Dave was quite ill for ten days. James Taylor of Henefer also had his dog and a coyote get into a fight on the desert. The dog had to be shot as a rabid dog would also bite people it would have to be their best dog, of course.
Tom Porter of Henefer, came in from the desert for treat¬ment for rabies. When the herders heard the sheep leave the be ground and they would go after them, the coyotes would chase the men. One man who was chased by a coyote at night didn't have time to open the camp door and climbed on top of the camp. He had to sit there in his underwear for two or three hours, as they were afraid the coyote would get into the sheep camp, if the door was opened. (K. J. P.)
We were camped between Davis Mountain and Little Granite in Skull Valley, when a plane circled over our camp several times to attract our attention. Finally ' in a weighted cloth, it dropped a note which read, "Did you see or hear a plane like this one last Saturday morning early. Could you look around this country for one lost since Friday night. Contains pilot and load of mail. Please report to Salt Lake City. Thanks a million." The pilot's name was Vaughn. His body was found over a year later near Cedar City". (Kenneth J. Paskett still has this note)
In April, the sheep were trailed back to Henefer over the same route as in the fall. At first, the trail through Salt Lake City was on 13th South. Then it was moved to 21st South and later to 27th South. The green lawns and shrubbery looked good to the hungry sheep and angry housewives stood with their brooms trying to keep the sheep off their property. In recent years, Salt Lake furnished a patrol man to help the herds through town as quickly as possible. Now, for many years, the sheep have been trucked or sent by train, as trails are fenced and no 11eed is available. The increased number of trucks and cars made the use of the highway dangerous. Many people thought the sheep had no right to be on the highway, not realizing that the livestock owner paid his share of the taxes that built and main-tained the roads. Every driver of livestock on the highway was required to carry a permit and a flagman with a red flag had to precede and another flagman had to follow the sheep at distances that would give drivers of vehicles warning of approaching live¬stock.
Owing to late cold spring weather in this high mountain area, many sheep men did not shear until after lambing , while some favored shearing on the desert before loading the sheep on the train or in trucks. A sheep man's livelihood depended on the weather.

Hyrum Dearden had a shepring corral on the east side of the river, about one half mile toward Croyden, across from the home .of William Davis. Hyrum contracted shearing at six cents per head. His record book shows he sheared 160 head a day for twenty one days in succession, using blade shears. Alma Bond, a later shearer, had a record of 133 in one day, using elec¬tric clippers. Now shearing is done by travelling outfits of Mexicans who board themselves. This is a welcome change from the practice of the sheep man boarding the crews. Some own¬ers had permanent cook shacks where their wives or local people preDared the meals. When the shearers were boarded by the owners, a hot noon meal was taken up to the corrals and this was extra hard for the wives speaking from my own ex¬perionce. At least twenty people could be expected for the noon meal, excluding the visitors who liked to watch the shear¬ers and be invited for dinner.
How the children liked to play on the filled wool bags! Though they invariably picked up a few ticks in the process. The fleeces, as they were shorn, were tied with paper twine and tossed up to the wool tromper in the sack, who would push the fleeces down tightly. When filled, the sack was eased up by a long pole and the mouth of the sack was sewn tii7,htly with twine, lowered to the ground and the next sack was ready for filling. In the days when every rural family had cows, there was al¬ways a great demand for pet lambs, in the spring. The boys would ride up to the sheep camps on horse back to get these lambs. Those were some that weren't doing well, perhaps a weak twin or orphan lamb. These were taken home and fed on the bottle. When the novelty of feeding them wore off, it became another task for the housewife. Some farm herds were started with these pet lambs, for who could eat a pet lamb after it had been fed and loved by the children.
Around the first part of July, the sheep were moved to the summer ranges, where the lambs would grow fast and be ready for market the latter part of September or early October. If this range was in the high mountain areas where camp wagons could not be used, tents and pack horses were used. The camp tender on his horse had three or four pack horses in single file and followed up the herd. Their pack boxes (cupboards) which fitted in saddle bags, stove and stove pipe, tent, hobbles, water can, axe and shovel, rattled as the horses jogged along, accompanied by the sound of sheep bells tinkling ahead.
The tents would be set up in the trees, pine boughs cut and the bed be made on these. Later, air mattresses were used and sleeping was more comfortable. An ingenious camp tender would fashion chairs and a table out of trees at hand, usually qua*ken aspen. The pine trees would sigh as the wind blew and the summer thunder sounded very close. Here "crystal sets" would bring in the news, before battery radios were used. A climb to a higher elevation at sun¬down afforded a beautiful view as the sun set. Flowers and berries were in abundancehere! Deer flies, horse flies and yellow jackets were a nuisance and bears were often in this locality. Food had to be put away carefully, as the dogs could get into a tent. Horses were hobbled and wore a horse bell so they could be located in the morning.

AN INTERESTING INCIDENT
Because many of the sheep men had their young sons urith the sheep, every one was very much concerned when the following occurred:
"Ellis Richins, son of a prominent sheep man, Jared Richins, of Coalville, was kidnapped July 11, 1927 from his father's sheep camp on the ridge above "Willow Flat. The herder Lloyd Richins of Henefer, had gone around the sheep when "Bally Dean" came to the camp. This man had been hanging around in that area for a couple of days and had eaten at the camp.
Ellis, who was thirteen years old and another lad, Louis Richins, were at the camp when Bally Dean came. He asked which boy was Jared Richins' son and Ellis told him he was. Bally then pulled out a gun and took Ellis, warning Louis not to make any noise or he would kill Ellis.
In a previously written note, which he left at the damp, he de¬manded $10,000. In this note, he referred to "the man" apparently intending to kidnap Jared, but not finding him there, took the boy, Ellis, instead.
They walked all night over the head of Lewis' Peak toward Toll Gate Canyon in Silver Creek. He did not harm Ellis in any way but tied him to a tree with chains.
Men from all the neighboring towns joined in this search for Ellis and the kidnapper. Ellis could hear their voices but dare not cry out. A Mr. Healy from Salt Lake, who had joined in this hunt, walked up on them and engaged "Bally Dean" in conversation. While they were talking, Ellis worked him¬self free from the chains and ran down to the Toll Gate Construction camp in Silver Creek.
"Bally Dean" was arrested. He I was wanted on other kidnap charges and was sent to the Utah State Penitentiary where he died a few years later.
Ellis Richins stayed with the sheep and finally bought his father's outfit. He was engaged in this business until the time of his death in 1965 at the age of fifty two years".
This information was furnished by his wife , Metta Richins, Feb. 20, 1967.
The sheep men were very much concerned about the kidnapping. Daddy met us at the shearing corral as we were on our way to the summer range at the time. He told us if they came to our camp to give them any thing they wanted. When "Bally Dean" was caught, some people said he should be jailed for life, some said he should be hung or have his tobacco taken away from him while he was in jail. (K. J. P.)
ANOTHER INTERESTINGINCIDENT
We were on the summer range in 1928 or 1929. The horse feed was poor and the horses were thin, so we decided to herd the sheep on foot as much as possible. We were camped at Mud Springs and turned the sheep into the head of Center¬ville Canyon.
I started around the sheep in the afternoon, leaving Dell at the camp as he was young. I stopped at the creek to get a drink and rest a few minutes. I heard the puppy yelp like he had been hurt. He came when I called him and I saw one side was covered with mud. I decided to cut my initials on a quaken asp tree and had just started when the pup yelped again. The brush was thick and I could only see a few feet. I called to the pup again and when he came, a bear came with him.
The bear was black and very shaggy. He had not shed all of his winter fur. He was about the size of a big wether. I decided to run;after running about one hundred feet, the bear had come right up to my heels. I didn't have a gun, only my pocket knife.
I had heard Daddy tell about a trapper that had been cornered by a bear and when the bear got close to the trapper, the bear stood up. The trapper used his hunting knife and slashed at the bear, letting his insides out. Instead of the bear attacking him, it just sat down and started to lick himself.
I couldn't get away, so I decided if the bear came closer, maybe a similar thing would save me. I had just opened my pocket knife when our dog, "Nig" jumped on the bear. While they were fighting, I beat it for the camp to get a rifle and horse and went back down to where I had last seen the bear, but I couldn't find it". (K. J. P.)
