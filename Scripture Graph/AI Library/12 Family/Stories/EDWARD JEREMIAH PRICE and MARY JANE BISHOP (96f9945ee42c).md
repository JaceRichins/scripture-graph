# EDWARD JEREMIAH PRICE and MARY JANE BISHOP

_A story of [[Edward Jeremiah Price (KWV9-ZK2)|Edward Jeremiah Price]], [[Mary Jane Bishop (LRRX-9SM)|Mary Jane Bishop]]._

## The story as it was written

EDWARD JEREMIAH PRICE and MARY JANE BISHOP - by Madge Cummins Shipley (condensed version for FHE grandchildren)
Edward J. Price, of Welsh descent, was born in Ellesmere, Shropshire, England, in August 1818, the third of seven children. Mary Jane Bishop, also Welsh, was born in Shrewsbury, Shropshire, in December, 1818, the fourth of nine, four of whom died young. As a youth, Edward left home and enlisted in the English navy, a life he loved. Mary Jane’s parents died, and she was left at age 20 to care for her four younger brothers, Richard, John, William and James. She worked in a laundry to help keep the family together. Richard and John eventually became self-supporting. Mary Jane, with William and James, accepted the message of the LDS missionaries, and were baptized, eventually embarking for America on the ship Kennebec in January 1852, with a company of Saints. Here they became acquainted with the sailor, Edward Price.
Edward learned of the gospel from Mary Jane and her brothers. Through association with the Saints, he was convinced of its truth. When they arrived in New Orleans, Edward left his sailor’s life and went with Mary Jane and the boys to St. Louis. Here Edward was baptized, and he and Mary Jane were married, in March of 1852.
During the summer of 1852, the new family crossed the plains with a covered-wagon train of Saints. For the first year they lived in Salt Lake City, where in 1853 their first child was born, a daughter named Mary Jane. The two brothers continued to live with them until they were married. A great love existed between Mary Jane and the boys, and they maintained that loving relationship all their lives.
In 1854, pioneers were being called to make settlements in the valley near Utah Lake. Edward and Mary Jane moved to this area, where they lived for six years in the Provo area. During this time four more children were born to them, three of whom died soon after birth. It was a great heartache to this family as they lost these babies, Sarah Ann, Edward, and James. A son, John Lloyd, born in 1859, survived.
In the spring of 1860, they moved to Franklin, Idaho, and helped pioneer that settlement. Their names are on the monument there, erected to the memory of the Franklin pioneers. This northern outpost of Cache Valley was in Indian country. They used their wagon boxes for homes, arranged to form a fort, and men were always on guard. In 1861 they had another son, named Thomas, who lived only a month.
In 1862, Edward learned that English Saints had settled in the south end of Cache Valley. They moved their family to Avon, near the Little Bear River. This new settlement was at the forks of two Indian trails, one leading to Wyoming and the other to Ogden. Indians were often nearby, camping in the river bottoms, so the settlers built their cabins and dugouts in fort formation, with someone on guard at all times.
By November 1865, they had lost three more infant sons shortly after their birth, Thomas, Joseph and Peter. Peter had died in October of 1865, eleven months old. In November, Edward and Mary Jane, with Mary Jane and John Lloyd, made the long trip, by horse and buggy, to Salt Lake City, where they were sealed as a family in the Endowment house.
A few years later, the Saints were advised to move their settlement three miles north, where the country was more open, and they could better protect themselves. More land was available. In the spring of 1868, Edward and Mary moved their family to the new location, named Paradise, and help establish that area. About fifty families were settled there at that time.
The Price home was a humble two-room log cabin, and a seven-acre farm. Their material possessions were few. They supplied their own living, growing their own food and keeping a cow, pigs and chickens. The need for money was not great—the house was made of canyon logs, so there was no house payment; travel was by horse and buggy, so no gas bill; there was no electricity or phone, so no utility bill. And they were all good neighbors, sharing whatever they had, and helping wherever help was needed. Most families raised a little sugar cane for molasses, and had a few sheep, selling wool not needed at home.
In the years 1869, 1870, and 1872 the grasshoppers destroyed the crops. Men, women and children were called out with brooms, sacks sticks, aprons or anything with which they could beat or drive them into piles of brush where they could be burned. Food was scarce, not a morsel was wasted, they shared selflessly. With these conditions, these good people learned habits of thrift and good management more than they had already experienced.
Edward and Mary Jane served faithfully in the church, wherever they were called, Edward in Priesthood responsibilities and Mary Jane in the Relief Society presidency for many years. In addition they shared their temporal skills. Edward’s experience at sea had taught him to splice a broken rope so skillfully that the repair could not be detected. He taught many young men how to splice a broken rope. And farmers of the surrounding area brought their broken derrick ropes to him for repairs. Mary Jane was a friend to all, always trying to alleviate pain and sorrow and suffering. She was a fine seamstress and made burial clothing for the dead for many years.
The loss of so many infant babies through the years gave both Edward and Mary Jane exceptionally tender feelings toward children, all children. They were lenient with their faults, and never spoke unkindly to a child. But their children and grandchildren were given a strict religious upbringing and moral training. The grandchildren loved them devotedly. Since their home was near the school, the grandchildren were invited, in regular turns, to have lunch on schooldays with Grandpa and Grandma Price. They always had a special treat, a piece of Grandma’s “currant bread.”
Mary Jane died in 1904 at the age of 86. At this time Edward went to live with his daughter, Mary Jane, and her husband, Samuel Oldham. Edward was almost completely blind. He died on New Years Day, 1906, at the age of 87. His funeral was held in the little rock church where his family worshipped, and he was laid to rest at the side of his wife in the Paradise cemetery.
