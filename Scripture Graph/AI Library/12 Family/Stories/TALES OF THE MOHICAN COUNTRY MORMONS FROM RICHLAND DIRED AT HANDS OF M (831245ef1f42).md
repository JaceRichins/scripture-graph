# TALES OF THE MOHICAN COUNTRY MORMONS FROM RICHLAND DIRED AT HANDS OF MISSOURI MOB By D. W. Garber

_A story of [[Thomas White McBride (KWJR-J45)|Thomas White McBride]]._

## The story as it was written

Mansfield News-Journal Sunday, 4 Mar 1962 - [Amanda Hansen sent to Ruth in 1975]
Thomas McBride, who lived on the Forks of the Mohican on the Red Haw., was murdered 30 Oct 1838 in Missouri, becaise he was a Mormon, McBride, who left Richland County in 1833, was hacked to death with a corn cutter by a wild mob at Haun’s Mill, Mo.
Frontier ministers. Angered by the loss of members to the “new religion”, pounded their pulpits and preached long, loud and with little restraint against Joseph Smith and his Saints.
Terrible accusations were made against the Mormons, families were divided, and the scars of hatred, which resulted, are evident today. Descendants of some families who “lost” members in the westward migration of the church are still reluctant to talk about it. And bitterness is still expressed by older people for who the” Mormon troubles” had reality.
Although there is no one living who can personally recall the time of conflict, the discussions so often listened to in the family circle by these older people remain as fixations.
Ostracism and abuse were punishments inflicted upon those who joined the church of Joseph Smith and the converts were forced to abandon homes. They departed like a lost people seeking a new Zion, and in many cases because of the intense feeling, family ties were forever broken.
Thomas McBride was a Virginian, born in 1776. In 1820 he moved to the Forks of the Mohican and settled on the Red Haw where he leased eighty acres of Virginia Military School land and set to work building a cabin. The family was very poor.
Mrs. Agnes J. M. Dummer, a granddaughter of Thomas kindly loaned a journal kept by her father James McBride. It tells of the hardships endured while the family was..[illed.] out a home in the wilderness, and it describes the massacre when Thomas McBride fell a victim of anti-Mormon hatred. The son wrote: “…bone and sinew were put to work chopping and grubbing, and the younger hands to gathering brush, whether boys or girls it mattered not, clearing must be done. The first year about five acres were cleared and put into corn. Heavy frosts destroyed the crop, there was no income from the lease.”
McBride is described in a contemporary account as a short but sturdy man, with a happy round face fringed with a beard, but without a mustache. Little tufts of hair grew from his ears. His eyes were soft and friendly. Thomas was a humble man.
Joseph Smith founded the Mormon church in 1830 at Kirtland, O…, and soon after Sidney Rigdon, a Campbellite preacher, became one of the first converts. Rigdon visited the McBride cabin and baptized the family. McBride’s trouble began after it became known that they had accepted the new religion. The family was threatened and abused and in desperation they moved from their home. Bigotry reached a high crescendo.
The McBride journal is graphic in describing the family’s experiences: “The first [Mormon] sermon preached on the Red Haw was preached in father’s house…My father sold the lease, and in August, 1833, accompanied by my brother Amos and his family, started to Missouri…”
The season was too far advanced and the party remained for the winter with James McMillen family, west of Newville in Worthington Township.” The journal continues, “In the spring of 1834, accompanied by Amos and family, James McMillen and family, James Dayley and family and Thomas and his family they started for Missouri.”
Having traveled about two months with ox teams, we arrived in Pike County. In September, 1836, my father taking with him what children yet remained at home, and accompanied by James Dayley and wife, moved to Caldwell County and settled about ¾ of a mile from Haunt’s mill on Shoal Creek. Their father entered from the government, eighty acres of land and began once more to make a home..”
The Mormon settlement at Haun’s Mill was soon threatened by a Missouri mob and the families, fearing trouble, organized a loose guard formation for their own protection. The nightmare which followed is told by the son: “On 30 day of October,1838, my father came home from meeting with the Brethren at the mill. He talked with me and told me the arrangements made. He was called to help form a guard, I was sick at the time with the every-other-day ague, and father said that on my well day, I should take his place with the guard and that he would guard on the day that I was sick. That with himself and me, he wish to fill one man’s place. My father was then in his sixty-third year.”
“My sister Catherine was living at the mill with Haun’s family, leaving only me and my youngest sister, Dorcas, at home with father and mother. Father was in good spirts, and his countenance wore a cheerful expression…taking with him his gun and blankets he started on his return to the mill to join the rest of the guard.”
“My father had but little more than got to the mill... in fact not more than thirty minutes elapsed from the time he left the house, when a gun was heard. And another! Followed by the deadly crack of musketry, which told too well the fate of all who fell prey to the blood-thirsty mob. Perhaps not more than six minutes passed from the firing of the first gun, till the massacre was accomplished, and the bloody deed done…”
“Brother Amos having been detailed on the previous day to get wood for families, was on his way to the mill when he was told there had been serious trouble there. He went on and passing the mill a short distance came to Haun’s house. The first object that met his eye in human form was the mangled body of my murdered father in the dooryard. He had been shot with his own gun after having given it into the mob’s possession, was cut down and badly disfigured with a corn cutter and left lying in the creek. Some of the women dragged him from the creek into the dooryard and left him there. . .”
“Of the wounded men, 10 afterwards died, making eighteen in all. A few rods south of the blacksmith shop, was an unfinished well, about eight or twelve feet deep, but no water in it. This was the sepulchre for the dead. Fiften murdered persons, including my father, were carried on a board, one at a time, dropped into that well by my brother Amos, James Dayley and Jacob Myers the only able-bodied men that were present.”
It is difficult to understand the bigotry and blood lust, which made possible the massacre of innocent men, women and children. One member of the mob named William Reynold, with fiendish delight, justified his murder of a small child with the old Indian saying, “Nits make lice.”
When news of the terrible slaughter at Haun’s Mill was received in Richland County, and it was realized that many of the victims were members of local families or their friends, sympathy was expressed—but forgiveness—never. The seeds of intolerance had been sowed deep and not until a generation had passed_________[illeg] the roots start to decay.
Jacob Myers, who had helped bury the dead, led a train of more than 50 wagons from southern Richland County to Missouri in 1836. A wealthy miller and the mill-wright, Myers built the mill on Shoal Creek where the massacre occurred. He was made a high priest in the Kirkland Temple by Joseph Smith, and as presiding elder headed the three branches of the church in the county. The three congregations in Perry, Worthington and Green townships were forced to leave because of the abuse heaped upon them.
 Thomas McBride, a mild and harmless old man, was murdered because of his religious belief. More victims of the Haun’s Mill Massacre came from Richland Co. than from any other place, three of those killed, and eleven of the wounded—including two of Jacob Myers’ sons.
