# James John McBride 1818-1881 By Clain Barrus

_A story of [[James John McBride (KWJ5-DHB)|James John McBride]]._

## The story as it was written

We, the descendants of James McBride, are indebted to him for an excellent autobiography. He provides us with much precious family history. t is well written and I am impressed with some of his colorful phrases, such as: "As time speeds swiftly on and age creeps over me..."
He wrote those words when he was fifty-two years old (and I am seventy-two). Here is some more of his literary language: "The sun slowly sank beneath the western horizon and darkness spreads its broad mantle over the universe...all were uncertain--all was pain and sorrow...In vain did the affectionate wife with aching heart and streaming eyes watch through the long, long night for the return of her husband... the rays of the morning sun kissed the landscape." In reading his manuscript, I get the impression that he was a man of letters.
President Spencer W. Kimball wrote, "We renew our appeal for the keeping of individual journals and records and compiling family histories." One hundred and five years before President Kimball made that appeal, James McBride penned this message to his descendants: "I enjoin upon you, as a duty you owe your posterity to commence early in life, and keep your history to hand down to them"
What a great loss it would be to us if James had not taken time to preserve for us much priceless knowledge about our dear relatives. In my chapter on the life of Thomas McBride I have had to rely upon James' history for much of my source material.
Thomas, with fourteen others, was buried in an unfinished well about eight or twelve feet deep. They were put into the well by his son Amos, assisted by James Dayley and Jacob Myers. James wrote: "I went to the place and stood at the edge of the silent tomb of my beloved father. A silent prayer I offered to God and turned away."
He gives this account of those trying times: "The suffering caused by the exterminating order of Boggs' could hardly be described. Families were out of their homes, and the widows and orphans found themselves cast helplessly upon the mercy of the Church. Thus exposed to the storm of winter and travel a journey of more than two hundred miles."
Carter E. Grant wrote: "With their last recourse denied, The Saints had no choice but to leave the state. It was here that Brigham Young, then thirty-seven years of age, stepped to the front and became the chief directive force in the exodus from Missouri.
"...Throughout the winter and spring of 1838-39 some fifteen hundred Mormons loaded their wagons with whatever they could carry and made their way eastward about 200 miles and crossed the Mississippi River into the friendly state of Illinois." ("The Kingdom of God Restored", p. ^23).
It was an overruling Hand that James was not murdered instead of his father who was serving guard duty in his stead. He was sick at the time with every-other-day ague, and was in their home about three-fourths of a mile from Haun's Mill. James’ mission in mortality was not to be completed when he was twenty years old. I believe he was assigned in the pre-mortal state to live at least another forty-three years and become the ancestor of a numerous posterity who honor and revere him as a great soul.
James refers to: "the unusual, unjust, unmerciful and almost indescribable evils heaped upon the Latter-day Saints; and with them I have been a sufferer. Not because I was ever convicted of crime, but on account of my religious beliefs." I greatly admire his strength of character and his faith which enabled him to stand steadfast in the gospel even at
the peril of his life. He set us an excellent example for us to follow when we are called upon to travel through tunnels of darkness for the gospel's sake.
We can see from the following experiences which James relates in his autobiography, that he was a brave man: "I sprang to the doorway and saw about thirty rod's distance a posse of men coming in the direction of the mill, but rather than let them see me run to escape, I would have died. I therefore walked from the mill to the dam, crossed it and quietly walked on till I was out of sight. Why they did not fire at me I cannot tell.
"A few days after, a company of men commanded by Nemiah Comstock took possession of the mill. In the company was a man by name of Mopin, for whom my father, who was then a magistrate, had collected a judgment amounting to ten dollars and ten cents, just before his death. Mopin now threatened that my mother's house would be burned down over and up if the money was not forthcoming. I heard of the threats and after reflecting for a time, took the money and started to chance my fate with the mob.
"In as bold a manner as I could assume, I went among them. They did not bother me and I soon thought myself quite safe. I found Mopin and presented him with the money. He took it and seemed somewhat effected on learning the situation of my father's family. To remunerate me for my trouble he gave me ten cents."
Their farm at Haun's Mill, valued at $1,000, was sold for about $100 in value. Many of the Saints were beaten, tarred and feathered, driven from their homes and their property confiscated for the use of the mobocrates. Threats were made against the Mormons; the rights of citizenship were denied them. James, and his relatives traveling with him, fully realized the dangerous situation in which they were placed. They were poorly equipped to make the 200-mile trip from Haun's Mill to Missouri. James wrote: "With the horse and mare to the carry-all and a yoke of oxen to a two wheeled cart, our traveling outfit was made up. Dependent on this was my mother, my two youngest sisters and myself, my brother Amos and family, James Dayley and family, also Harrison Severe, a young man who afterward married my sister Dorcas, numbering in all about seventeen persons."
While they were camped along the way, James said, "we were informed that our guns, which had been taken from the Saints at Haun's Mill, and at the surrender of arms at Far West, had been taken to Richmond in Ray County, and that we could get them by first describing them, swearing to the description and paying a fee of sixty cents for each gun. “James Dayley and myself decided to ascertain the truth of the matter and, if possible, get our guns. My main object was, however, to get possession of my father's gun; with which, you may remember, father was shot."
On this occasion James McBride and James Dayley walked 140 miles in three days. (One day I walked 15 miles and it seemed a long distance). He said, "The wolves were howling around us in almost every direction. We were indeed tired, but to lie down in the cold and trust ourselves to the hungry appetites of howling wolves seemed hopeless, so we still traveled on. "After walking 140 miles in three days, James said they continued their journey to Illinois and, "After a hideous journey and a great deal of exposure from which many died, we arrived in Adams's County, Illinois." (How difficult that grueling journey must have been for James' aged mother who was traveling with them).
They later continued their journey on to Nauvoo, where Mother McBride died of King's Evil (tuberculosis of lymph glands esp. in the neck) at the age of about sixty-three.
James continues his narrative: "The hand of the assassin was not yet stayed. The persecution of the Saints was very severe. And having long suffered the gross impositions of all who wishes us evil, it was discovered that we would again have to move."
They left Nauvoo in 1846, directing their course westward. Again they were poorly equipped to travel, having only, "A span of horses (of very inferior quality) to a small wagon--a yoke of oxen and wagon, made up the traveling outfit for our entire company--to which was also added a widow woman and family.
"...The great plains extending then west to the Pacific Coast, was before us. We did not know yet where we were to make our final home. The orders were to 'Move West."...The frontier settler among whom we now found ourselves, were filled with hatred towards the Mormons. Bitter threats were made against us and we were watched on every hand." His brother Thomas was seized by a mob and hanged to the porch timbers of a building.
He further states, "On the 17th day of May, 1850, having disposed of our homes in Appanoose County, we again started West. In company with myself were Captain James Dayley, Harrison Severe, and William Pope and families, also my sister Mary and her children."
James and part of his company arrived in Salt Lake in October, 1850. They continued on to Grantsville and arrived there the 10th day of October, 1850. They hadn't been there more than six months when their cattle were stolen by the Indians.
James McBride and Harrison Severe were the first settlers in Grantsville. They built the first houses and did the first plowing and planting at that place. However, there was one white man there when they came. He", was living in a wagon bed and herding stock.
My grandfather James Orson McBride was born in Grantsville, September 5, 1852, less than two years after his parents arrived there. I had an intimate association with him, having lived with him when I was going to high school. How I wish I had asked him to tell me stories about his distinguished father. I get the impression that James was a gentle, peaceful man. His son Orson gave me this excellent advice: "It is better to keep the good will of a bad dog." Perhaps my grandfather learned that truth from his noble father who penned: "I do not write with a desire to excite feelings--strife I detest. I would gladly suffer evils, as long as evils are sufferable--I would willingly forbear, as long as there is virtue in forbearance."
James had faith that no matter how severe their problem's may be, the Lord would see them safely through. In referring to the time in Church history when Johnson's Army was sent to Utah to civilize the Mormons, he wrote: "A deal of suffering was at that time experienced among the people. Thought we had raised plenty of grain and vegetables from our farms, it was a difficult matter to get comfortable clothing..."We had obeyed the instructions of the servants of God in moving from place to place, and His protecting arm had been stretched forth for our good through the past, and He surely would not now forget us. The winter came one, we had plenty of hay and straw--more than we needed for our own use.
"The soldiers sent here to annihilate the Latter-day Saints were then stationed at Camp Floyd--about forty miles south-east of Grantsville--though they were sent here to do us harm, they were of great benefit to us. They had brought with them horses, mules, work oxen and wagons--also large quantities of merchandise and provisions. They needed our hay and straw for their animals. We would sell, if we could get our price..."Our hay was sold for forty dollars per ton--our straw for one dollar per cubic yard. We could buy a very good yoke of oxen for thirty dollars--their wagons for a very small price --they were here, the soldiers had no use for them--we could buy them very cheap. We could buy such of their merchandise as we wished and were consequently able to furnish our families with quite comfortable clothing and other necessaries in our houses.
"The overruling hand of God had once again been turned in our favor. The enemies of the Latter-day Saints had been made, by circumstantial changes, to use their store of greenbacks and merchandise to the advantage of the Saints." James made a request of his descendants to, "lend a helping hand in behalf of our fore parents and relatives in the Latter-day work."
It is my hope that in compiling this history, that I will be able to direct our attention to "our fore parents and relatives" so that we will be inspired by their shining example to devote our lives "in the latter-day work."
James concluded his autobiography by saying, "Every effort made by the enemies of this people to destroy them has been unsuccessful." Then he bears his testimony of the gospel as follows: "I feel to acknowledge the hand of God in all things and bear testimony of the truth of the Gospel of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints."
He performed many acts clothed in beauty. He rose from despair to great strength by seeking, knowing, finding and obeying God. His darkness of the night was dispelled and he had purpose in his life and courage in his heart. How grateful we are to him for his excellent autobiography and for the example of his noble life.
