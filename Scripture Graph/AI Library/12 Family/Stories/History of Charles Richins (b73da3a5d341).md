# History of Charles Richins

_A story of [[Charles Wager Richins (KVP1-SVD)|Charles Wager Richins]]._

## The story as it was written

History of Charles Richins
In the country of Gloustershire, England, noted for its agricultural and dairy farming, Charles Richins, son of Richard Richins and Charlotte Priscilla Wager was born August 17, 1828. His mother died when he was but six years old and his father died five years later, leaving him an orphan when just a mere lad. His father's sister known as Aunt Hannah was left to mother the family of five boys and one girl.
Father immediately began to shift for himself hiring out as chore boy on a farm. From then on he never knew parental love. His educational opportunities were very limited. He did not attend school after he was eleven years of age. He learned to read and write and was exceptionally good in mathematics through his own efforts. He told his son, Jesse, that he only attended school six weeks.
He heard and accepted the gospel when he was twenty-one years old and was baptized December 31, 1849 by Charles G. Shill. After he joined the church he worked in it holding different positions. At one time he presided over the Cheltenham branch. Records show that he baptized and confirmed many into the church.
He was married to Louisa Shell before coming to America. They had one child born in England. He left his wife and baby girl and started for America arriving in Salt Lake City October 10, 1853. He traveled in the company of Joseph W. Young who was son of Lorenzo Don Young and nephew of President Brigham Young. Before they started Captain Joseph W. Young gave them this advice: "Contend with no one. Pray for those who are set over you and they will prove a blessing to you."
(They were allowed one wagon and one tent to ten persons. Their rations or allowance of provisions was one pound of flour and a portion of bacon each day, but were at liberty to provide any extras they could afford. They had four oxen and two cows to each ten. The wagons were for their baggage and they walked along side of the wagons--perhaps riding once or twice a day for just a short distance. They were supposed to cover twenty miles a day. There was a captain over every ten also captains of fifty. John R. Winder was captain over Charles' fifty. A great deal of patience was required by both captains and people to perform a trip of over a thousand miles across the plains.)
In about one year after his arrival in Salt Lake, Father sent for his wife and baby, but he had the great sorrow of losing his two-year old daughter while they were crossing the ocean. It was terrible to think of her being left in a winding sheet and water grave and this grieved them greatly.
He lived in Salt Lake City for about seven years and enjoyed working in the church there. In 1860 they moved to Henefer, Summit County where he was sustained as presiding elder. He held this position until Summit Stake was organized. At this time he was chosen as Bishop of the Henefer Ward which position he held for about twenty-five years. There were present at the time he was ordained bishop: Elders John Taylor, Lorenzo Snow, and Franklin D. Richards of the Quorum of the Twelve Apostles.
He married Ester Stow Ovard March 9, 1861 in the Endowment House. They were married by President Brigham Young. To this union ten children were born.
Father was financially quite successful. His babyhood environment in an agricultural community assisted him in his stock raising and dairying. He also did well at railroad contracting and the coal industry.
During the early days of Henefer Father had many very narrow escapes of his life. At one time while walking home from his work which was a distance of about twenty miles, he was attacked by a pack of wolves and his only weapon of defense was a stick he had picked up to aid him in his long walk. With the aid of this stick and an unseen protective power he came out unmolested by the fierce animals.
About this time he had a very severe attack of inflammatory rheumatism which lasted about six weeks. The pain and fever was so severe that it turned his hair completely white. But this did not keep father from winning the heart of another beautiful girl, for on April 11, 1878 he was married to Agnes Mary Willmott, our mother. She was his third wife. They all lived in Henefer in a fourteen-room house, it being the best house at that time there and is still standing but not in use.
About this time the raid against polygamy began and mother had to leave Henefer in 1879 and go to Kaysville and from Kaysville to Mesa, Arizona traveling all the distance by team. They had some very narrow escapes while on the road.
While they were crossing the big Colorado River, the water being very high, father was shown a sage brush across the river and was told to come out even with that for if he turned a little to the right or left he would strike quicksand and all would be lost. Mother and baby, Jesse, lay in the bottom of the wagon frightened nearly to death for fear that all would go down, but providence was in their favor and father came out with the team stride the sage brush. After they had crossed the river they camped at the foot of the Moocown Moutain. Here they came in contact with Indians who were on the warpath. They learned that a battle had been fought in their previous night's camp ground and that they had narrowly escaped being captured by these savages.
In the fall of 1880 they returned to Utah and stopped at Minersville where a baby girl was born and they named her Eunice. When the baby was six weeks old they came to Provo stopping for a while at the home of Mr. and Mrs. Boordman. Here their little boy Jesse was very seriously burned but recovered after careful care.
From Provo they went to Henefer. During their stay there two children, Byron and Lois, were born. Here they were molested again by the deputy marshals and mother went into hiding. Again they went to Kaysville, it being the home of her mother. They remained there just a short time. They went from there to Pleasant Grove. Here mother had her first real home and it was in this house that Agnes, another baby girl, was born.
Peace reigned but a short time as father was arrested for polygamy one morning just at sunrise as he took his team of horses to drink at a ditch across the street from his home in Pleasant Grove, Utah. Mother had been subpoenaed as a witness against her husband, but rather than go on the witness stand, she with her four children went again to Mesa, Arizona. She left one child, the little boy-Byron, with his father at this time. Grandmother came and kept house for father. Father went to stand trial but as mother, the main witness, was not there father paid the bonds and the trial was dismissed for the present, but father had to appear again later. Jesse was 8 years old and Eunice 6 years old. They were also subpoenaed as witnesses but were not put under bond. This was contrary to law.
In the fall of 1888 father and the boy, Byron, met mother and her four children in Deming, New Mexico, and from here they went to Old Mexico. Leaving mother in a little one-room adobe house, father returned to Provo to stand trial, but as mother did not appear, the case was dismissed.
Father went to Henefer to his family there. His first family lived in Arizona where he went and stayed but a short time before returning to Mexico again. His second family did not want to go with him, but later they came and stayed only a short time when they returned to Henefer. Here in Mexico five more children were born: Bertha, Richard, Beatrice, John, and Roxie.
In Mexico they went into the cattle and ranching business. Although they went through the hardships of frontier life, they soon got things around them to make them comfortable. Father bought up a large herd of cattle and went into the dairy business. Often we would milk from fifty to seventy-five cows and make butter and cheese. Father was very particular that his animals be given the best of care. Never could the cows and pigs be neglected. Besides the milk cows he had a big herd of range cattle. He bought a large tract of land for his range cattle to feed on. He spent most of his time riding the range taking care of the cattle. Father always furnished plenty of work for his children besides having some hired help.
Jesse, mother's older son, was called on a mission when he was but nineteen years of age. This left father with just the hired help, but he soon made use of his girls and they spent many a day riding the range with him. He never allowed them to ride astride, but furnished them with a side saddle.
Ranch life has its troubles and sorrows and father and mother had their share. One of the sad things was the loss of their thirteen year old son, Byron. He was dragged to death by a horse. This happened on a Sunday morning as the boy had gone out to get the horses for us to go to Sunday School. He did not have the saddle on his horse. He found the horses and unhobbled them. Then when he went to get back on his horse, he threw his arm over the horse's neck and in doing so threw a loop around his own neck and the horse jumped from under him and ran away dragging the boy through the big mesquite bushes. It broke his neck and killed him instantly. It was hours before we found him and when we did what a mangled mess he was. Poor father and mother never really had the love for ranch life after this.
Father had many narrow escapes of his own life. He was thrown from his horse several times and nearly killed. At one time when he was crossing the river, the water was so high the horse could not swim with father on its back so he slid off behind and held to the horse's tail and swam across the river that way.
When father went to Mexico, he still continued his public work and shortly after arriving there he was put in as counselor to Bishop W. Derby Johnson, a position he held for many years or until he was ordained a patriarch May 1901 by John Henry Smith which position he held for three years or until his death August 27, 1903.
Father to a great extent had the gift of healing. On one occasion a grandchild, a baby about eight or ten weeks old, was thrown from a wagon, the wheel passing over the child's neck. When the baby was reached she was from all appearances dead for she was black in the face. Father was called and he came to meet us as we ran back to the house with the baby. He was asked to administer to her which he did, and the child was immediately healed. He also had a very keen discernment when anything was wrong with those away from home. On several occasions he has told parties to go in search for the missing ones because he knew something was wrong.
Father was seventy-five years of age when he died, his death being caused through getting over-heated while running after cattle trying to drive them to water. Well do we remember his last birthday as we had over one hundred invited to his birthday dinner which was to be held Wednesday, but he took sick Monday after the exertion of running and the party had to be called off. He lived about three weeks.
He was the father of thirty children, 14 boys and 16 girls. Each wife had ten children. He also had 112 grandchildren. At present there are thirteen children living. All three of his wives have followed him in death.
