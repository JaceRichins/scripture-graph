# Life Story of Susannah Jones Stephens and James Henry Fowler

_A story of [[James Henry Fowler (KWCR-4KP)|James Henry Fowler]], [[Susannah Jones Stephens (KWCR-4KR)|Susannah Jones Stephens]]._

## The story as it was written

To the farming community of Henefer came the Fowler and the Stephens families in the 1860s. Both families moved to Utah from England after accepting the Gospel of Jesus Christ.
 For the Fowlers it all started in 1840 when Apostle Orson Hyde (Of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day Saints) went to Pendlebury, Lancashire, England as a missionary. There he was received by Esther Wilcock and Thomas Lythgoe, some of the first Pendlebury people to embrace the Gospel. The Lythgoes opened their doors to the missionaries of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints and in time became acquainted with Brigham Young and John Taylor both of whom later served as presidents of the church.
 On the 29th of May 1846, Rachel Lythgoe was born to Esther and Thomas Lythgoe. She later made her way to Utah where she married James Macefield Fowler in Henefer on the 20th of January 1866. They were endowed and sealed in the temple on the 9th of November 1868.
 James Macefield Fowler came to Utah for the gospel’s sake in 1863. He settled in Goshen, Utah then later moved to Henefer where he lived the remainder of his life. His main occupation was farming but he did many types of work. He hauled ties for the railroad and helped build the first bridge in Henefer over the Weber River. In addition he helped build the log meeting house in Henefer.
 James and Rachel were the parents of eleven children, four boys and seven girls. Three died in infancy and Ira Alma was killed while working at the cement plant at Devil’s Slide near Henefer. James Henry was the eighth child in the family. Those older were Esther Ellen, James Thomas, Mary Ann, George, Lucy, Ruth, and Daucaus. Children younger than James Henry were Agnes Ann, Electa Victoria, and Ira Alma.
 The Stephens family ties into the gospel as a result of missionary work done in England by Robert Jones. When Thomas Henry Stephens fell in love with Mary Jones, she introduced him to her brother Robert and he taught Thomas the gospel. Thomas was baptized March 7, 1861 in Brimpsfield, England. He was the only one in his family to accept the gospel and it caused bitter feelings towards him. He left his home in England and sailed for America in 1864.
 After arriving in New York he started the long trek to Utah. Months of hardship and suffering were experienced as he walked nearly all of the 2,000 miles to Utah. They married on the 25th of December in 1865 and settled down to farming in Henefer. They were endowed and sealed to each other on June 16, 1873.
 Thomas Henry and Mary Jones Stephens became the parents of nine children: Susannah was next to the youngest with only Lucy Ann younger than her. The older children were William Thomas, George Henry, Heber Charles, Fredrick, Mary (who died as a child of diphtheria), John and Robert.
 Now we have given a little background on the Fowler and Stephens families we will talk more specifically about James Henry and Susannah J. Stephens Fowler.
 James Henry was born at Henefer, Summit, Utah on August 2, 1876. The event took place in a two room log house with a lean-to built on the back that was petitioned and used as bedrooms. This home was located in almost the same spot that the red brick home (owned at this time in 1966 by Joseph and Reta Ovard) was later built. He was blessed on October 1, 1876 by his father. On June 30, 1889 when he was 13 years of age he was baptized and confirmed a member of the LDS Church by John Paskett.
 James was always a good natured, loving boy and he brought much joy and happiness to his father. (His sister, Lucy, said he was the apple of his Dad’s eye). In fact, his father affectionately called him “Little Jim”. His mother died when he was eleven years old so he was mothered by his older sisters, Esther Ellen, Mary Ann and Lucy. This made him very close to them and he always showed love and respect for them as he visited with them in their homes and helped their husbands with their work whenever he could. He in turn helped care for his younger brother and sisters when the older girls were attending church or busy in other activities.
 His childhood playmates were Charles Jones and Ted Lythgoe. He and Charles were always together and like other boys had a few mischievous tricks of their own. (One day Jim was found in the chicken coop beating the chickens with a whip to make them lay). It wasn’t all play for James or anyone else in those days. He had much work around the home to do such as feeding pigs, gardening, picking currants, etc.
 James’ father owned a farm up Main Canyon and one down at the mouth of Main Canyon so he spent most of his younger days helping on the farm. Before he was married he tended sheep camp for Deardens a winter or two out on the desert. They were long hard winters and the wages were meager but they made the work as interesting as possible by playing pranks on each other.
 Their home life was very humble but sufficient. The home was well managed even though their mother was not there to give them love and advice. It was always open to friends of the family and nearly all the time it was filled with people. The new red brick home was not built until almost of the family was raised. James said he was almost too old to enjoy it. He had slept out in the hay barn during the summers and upstairs on the landing of their little log home during the winters.
 As was common in those days, James only went to the eighth grade in school. He learned reading, writing, and arithmetic as that was the rule then. He didn’t participate in sports and ball playing as his brother George did. Instead he found his pleasure in working around the home. He did enjoy dancing and was considered a very good dancer. He attended many dances in Echo and other surrounding communities with other young people of Henefer.
 While James was growing up in Henefer there was a young girl, Susannah Jones Stephens, who was doing likewise. She was born on the 23rd of July in 1880 in the old Stephens home which is now (1966) owned by Parley Stephens. She was blessed by Robert Jones on September 5, 1880. Shortly after she turned eight she was baptized and confirmed a member of the LDS Church on August 5, 1888 by John Paskett. (Inasmuch as Susannah was still living at the time of this writing man of her exact words will be quoted in telling her life’s story.)
 “When I was young I went to school in the old meeting house. I only went through fifth grade. I wrote left-handed and Ma had the teacher teach me to write with my right hand. Every night after school she tied my left hand in back of me. The teacher’s name was Miss Allston and she boarded at our house.”
 The school at that time was held in the old brick meeting house. (The first chapel built in Henefer where the present chapel, at this time 1966, is now located. Now, 2018, the Henefer Wards have been in a new chapel for a few years.) Later school was held in an old wooden school house, part of which is still on the Alma E. Richins property and was used by him as a grainery (granary). Susannah said she attended school in both places.
 Her childhood friends were Laura Batchelor and Liz Wilson. They had many good times together. Some of it was spent on the lawn in front of their home. They always went to Sunday School and after meeting would walk down to the track and back. (They didn’t have any cars at that time). One Sunday she says: “Bert took a load of girls for a ride in the wagon. We had a team names “Shot Gun” and they sure could go. I was sitting on the back seat with Liz Wilson and Liz Lythgoe and as we went over the ditch (near Seffa Davis place) all three of us were thrown out over the back but thankful to say we were not hurt.”
 She and her younger sister Lucy grew up together and they had a number of interesting experiences. “One time Lucy and I were riding down in the pasture below Ordell Stephens’ place and Uncle Will came after us but he never caught up. He said Ma wanted us so we hid. I got in a big barrel and cut my knee. I still have that scar today.”
 There wasn’t much time for play in those days when a girl got big enough to work. Susannah says: “I used to help in the house as well as milk fourteen cows night and morning [of course, it was by hand]. Fred helped milk but Bert didn’t like to milk so he put alum on the cows teats to dry them up. I also worked for Allen Jones down in the meadow. I rode horseback to work and then when I got through I had to take my pay in store pay and then Pa would give me the money. They paid me seventy-five cents a week. I also worked for Uncle Tom Richins.”
 Susannah remembers when she and Liz Wilson went to Salt Lake to keep house for Fred and George who had a meat business. Fred ran the slaughter house and George handled the retail part of the business. She says: “They were very good to us. Fred often took us into the city to see the shows. One day the ‘dummy’ train stopped by our home and gave us two watermelons. We saw Fred coming and we thought he would get after us so we threw them to the pigs.”
 Instead he said, “You should have kept them and I would have helped you eat them.”
 At one time Fred and George’s mother and father went to Salt Lake to live and to help with the butcher shop. When they were there Heber and Lulu lived in the Stephens home and looked after the younger children. Susannah tells of some incidents that happened while Lulu was living with them. “I would go down to Uncle Wills and Aunt Hannah’s place (they lived in a home owned by LaVell Stephens in 1966) through the path in the fields in back of Inez Eakins place. Tom Ovard helped me wash clothes at Aunt Hannah’s. He turned the washer for me and he would say: ‘Sis put the colored ones in with the white ones.’ We sure had a good time together. He worked for Pa and every night before going to bed we would have a bowl of bread and milk and sit with our feet in the oven whenever it was cold.”
 “ When Aunt Lulu and I had the red measles Uncle Heb was our nurse and he kept us in bed for ten days. He only gave us tomatoes to eat and they hurt our sore mouths. Then he gave us some sefran tea to bring out the measles on us.”
 She remembers the good times they had on the square playing ball and running races on the Fourth of July. They had contests to catch roosters and drive nails and shw would always win so they finally disqualified her from the competition.
 During her late teens Susannah taught a class in Sunday School. She used to make the fire and put the lights on every Tuesday night for the Young Ladies Meeting. On 30 June 1901 (before she was married) she was set apart as secretary and treasurer of the Relief Society by Bishop Micha F. Harris. She sang in the choir and under the direction of Judith Beard sang at the Salt Lake General Conference.
 The official start of the romance between Susannah and Jim was when they went together to a school play held in the old brick meeting house in Henefer. They dated, “not steady” she said, “for four years.” One thing they liked to do was dance. There was always time for a dance in those days.
Before the Henefer dance hall was built they went to Echo to the dances. They hitched up their carts or sleds and made a real night out of it. Thackerys had a store in Echo and a dance hall above the store. They would dance until midnight and then have intermission so they could go to Joe Stories for something to eat and then back to the dance where they could dance until morning if they desired. “We went to Morgan one night to a dance where we had a good time. While coming home it started to thunder and lightening and then rained so hard we couldn’t see the road ahead of us. While coming over the slue bridge (by Derrel Shills) the neck yoke broke so we had to walk the rest of the way home. Parley Richins, Vennie Paskett, Jim and me were together and we all got wet.”
 James Henry Fowler and Susannah Jones Stephens were married the 24th of October 1901, by Bishop John Paskett in the Stephens home. To help them get a start in life her father gave them a cow and her brother Heber gave them six chickens. Their first home was a log house owned by Petersens on the lot where Bernard Jones lived in 1966. They only lived their for about eight months. James had purchased lumber at Bountiful and had it shipped to Henefer. Their only $5.00 they spent for the freight. The rest of the money they borrowed from Lucy Lake and Eliza Tristrum. They first built the two rooms that are now the living room and the front bedroom of the house. They were ready by May of 1902 to begin. Hyrum Shill and Frank Warner were the carpenters and Jim helped all he could.
 The two rooms served the family for nine years. They bought a cabin from George Beard for $20.00 and put it up against the back door to cook in. Before their youngest child, Veda, was born they built on three additional rooms and a pantry so they moved the “shanty” as it is now called away from the house. Mother (Elva Mary) in speaking of their home wrote: “We were always proud of our home, especially the bathroom. We had a large tin tub and each year it was to be enameled. The first one that got out of the tub always had enamel stuck to their heels and bottom.”
 Susannah remembers well their trip to Salt Lake to be endowed and sealed in the temple. It was on October 11, 1904, the same day Harvey Richins got his endowment to go on a mission. They were ready to start the session and they had to hold it up for Jim. They kept calling out his name and he didn’t show up for some time because he had lost his drawers. (At that time the men folk wore drawers over their garments.)
 On another occasion before they were married she and Jim went to Salt Lake with George Tristom. They went by horse and buggy and as they rode through Devil’s Gate the water splashed up in their faces. After her brother, Fred Stephens, was married Susannah said they went to visit them one time and Fred suggested they go into Salt Lake to a show. They got lost and it took them a long time to find their way back to Kaysville. (She said Alma Richins was to the show and he could have told them the way out of Salt Lake but they thought they knew the way.)
 Susannah has lived to see many advancements in the mode of transportation. The first car she rode in was a Model “T” owned by Will Stephens. He gave some of them a ride up Main Canyon and at ‘Triston’s Hill” (Need Ted Fosters) it stopped and they had to get out and push.
 Jim had one bad sick spell, an ulcer the size of a dollar in his stomach. It took nearly all summer to recover and during that time William Cromar, the Storekeeper and neighbor, called every night to see how he was feeling and to offer his help. Jim had barely recovered from his sickness when he came down with whooping cough which meant another long siege for him and the family.
 In the spring of 1919 the family was blessed with a nine month old boy. His name, Claude Arnold Richins. He was the son of Hosea and Meriam Richins and was born the 18th of September 1918. His mother passed away with the flu in the spring of 1919 and his father came with tears in his eyes requesting them to care for his son. They agreed to keep him as long as Hosea wanted them to. A year or so later Hosea passed away (19 April 1920) so Claude became a regular member of the family. Elva wrote: “Father loved and cared for Claude just as he did his own children. To this day we all consider him a member of our family. He has always been a loveable, kind, helpful person. He has shown great love and respect for the family and is very grateful and devoted to our mother.” In speaking of their home life Elva wrote: “Father was a hard worker and a good provider. Our home life was very pleasant. (Just the usual scraps). We were neither rich nor poor but always had plenty to eat and wear. Father used to resole our shoes with the leather cut from worn out belts from the cement plant. They were rather heavy but made good strong soles to hold on our ice skates.”
 Veda said: “One time Mama hit me and broke by little finger.”
He mother’s response was, “yes, but you wouldn’t do as you were told.”
 The family had a small farm on the west side of town, formerly owned by James Macefield Fowler, and a small farm on the east side of town given to them by Thomas Henry Stephens. They had a team ofhorses and a few milk cows, chickens, pigs, and sheep. The children were given the lambs as pets to raise and obtain the money from when they were sold. The farm was so small that Jim had to find other work to provide for his family.
 He drove a horse and a two wheel cart to Echo for many years to work at the coal chutes. He also drove to Devil’s Slide to work at the cement plant. He spent a few winters on the desert tending sheep camp. During the summers he often worked with Harry Anderton and Tom Richins and they often lived in a sheep camp and only made it home on weekends.
 When peas were raised in Henefer for the Morgan Canning Company he worked at the pea viner as a repair-man. He left for work so early that they took his breakfast and lunch to him at work each day. He also did some sheep-shearing and although he wasn’t too fast (65 to 80 a day) he did a good job.
 The Hardest job he ever had was hauling clay. He and John Stephens were the first ones to haul clay from Owen’s Canyon. They hauled it by team and wagon, shoveling it on and off by hand. They put it on railroad cars and shipped it to the Salt Lake Pressed Brick Company and the Interstate Brick Company in Salt Lake. They were long, hot days and left him dead tired.
 On March 4, 1921, Orlondo their oldest son, was called on a mission to Japan. He was only 18 years old at the time and they said to him, “It is a long way from home. Don’t you think we should have it changed?” He answered, “No, if that is where the Lord wants me to go, that is where I will go.” They were happy he could go and it consoled them to know that Orlondo’s first cousin, Aldo Stephens, was also going to Japan at the same time on a mission.
 On 17 May 1923, after two years of missionary service, Susannah said she had a feeling that something was wrong with Orlondo as he kept following her around in her dreams. She and Lucy decided to go talk to Bishop Oriel Stephens and on the way to his place he met them and said, “Susannah, I was coming down to your place, I’ve some news for you.” She immediately said, “Orlando’s died, hasn’t he?” He had died of blood poisoning from carbuncles in Saint Luke’s Hospital in Tokyo, Japan.
 Elva tells of the experience in these words: “It was a sad heart breaking experience waiting for over three weeks for the body to arrive home. Grief stricken and heavy hearted father drove to the cemetery day after day, dug the grave and cut the planks to cover it with, all by himself. People were wonderful and did all they could to help and to bring comfort. George Albert Smith, Council of the Twelve, was sent by President Heber J. Grant to speak at the funeral. Pres. Grant sent letters of comfort and a favorite book of poems. The belief and understanding they had that he is still a missionary brings much comfort and satisfaction.”
 Jim didn’t attend church regularly but he did live his religion in his everyday life. He abstained from liquor and tobacco and he was honest in his dealings with his fellowmen. In fact, his word was as good as his bond. He didn’t believe in going unnecessarily in debt and managed to stay pretty well out of debt.
 Elva said: “He was always of service to his friends and neighbors. He was good natured and sociable. I don’t think he had a hard feeling toward anyone. If there was someone sick or in need he always visited them and helped them in some way. When the epidemic of small pox was in town our family was quarantined with them and Grandma Esther Richins and Uncle Noble and family were vaccinated for small pox and they were sicker than us so father went over after dark and got their wood, coal, etc. in and helped them.”
 James was ordained an Elder in the church by Free Malen on 17 Spt. 1904. A little over ten years later on the 1st of February 1915, he was ordained a Seventy by J. Golden Kimball.
 James was a man who lived by the clock. He liked to go to bed early and get up early. He liked his meals on time. Before he went to bed each night he always cut the breakfast meat. He was neat and clean and always kept his tools and working equipment in their proper places. He took pride in the yard and his farm. When heavy winter snows came he used his horse and wooden snow plow to clean off the walks and to make trails for the people in town just to be of service to them.
 He never traveled very far from home. The greatest distance was to Idaho one spring to shear sheep. He only went to Ogden and Salt Lake a dozen times or less in his life. What shopping they had to do was done in Coalville or Morgan. He seemed to find his greatest satisfaction in working hard and being at home with his family. He had a wonderful family and they had many enjoyable times together.
 Their family consisted of three boys and three girls. Two of the boys, Orlondo, who was born on April 27, 902, and Claude whom they raised as one of their own, have already been mentions. Thomas Merle was the second child being born September 3, 1908. The oldest daughter was Elva Mary, born September 10 1908. After he was Verna S., born May 14, 1911. Then Veda Lucy was born May 9, 1916.
 Susannah said all her children were born without a doctor’s presence. Her mother helped her when Orlando was born and then Betty Dearden, Dina Shill, or Emma Iverson helped her with the others.
 On the very day Susannah was to be honored at an open house for her 85th birthday her oldest daughter, Elva Mary Richens, died after a sudden illness. Elva was the second of her children to precede her in death and once more it was a trying experience for her.
 All of their children were married in the temple and raised fine families of their own. When James died only his two oldest granddaughters had been born.
 Elva said: “If I could have but one wish granted me it would be that he could have lived to see his grand and great-grandchildren. His love was so great for children, I know he would have been one of the happiest men on earth if he could have known all of them instead of just Mary and Yvonne, the two oldest ones, whom he found so much pleasure in for such a short time. I am happy that our religion teaches us that if they live worthy they will someday know him and enjoy a family relationship that will last for eternity.”
 Death came quickly to James Henry Fowler. After only a few hours of sickness he passed away from a heart attack at 2 P.M., on May 11, 1930. It was a shock to everyone and left his family and friends grief stricken. He had a large funeral service. His early childhood companion, Charles R. Jones, was the speaker and told of his wonderful character. (I personally fee indebted to him even though he died seven months before I was born. I have carried his name, James as my first name all my life and I didn’t appreciate it until mother told me just two years before she died that when they decided to name me James, her father appeared to her in a dream and showed his satisfaction their giving me his name.”
 A mother with three children still at home, widowed at the age of fifty, had areal struggle ahead of her but Susannah was equal to the task. It was a family with great love for each other and they stayed close together and helped each other. Even while her own family was growing up she had taken in borders and served as a mid-wife to help meet expenses of their home. She served as a mid-wife when her own grandchildren were born as well as for many others in Henefer, Hoytsville and Devil’s Slide.
 She says: “Many came to my home to have me take care of them. I fed them and cared for them for ten days at a total cost of twenty dollars. I served as mid-wife around eighty times. In addition I did nursing work for Doctors Dorland, French, High, and Oldham. If I had my day over I would be a nurse as I’ve always enjoyed being around the sick.”
 It is easy to see why most of the town’s people call her Aunt Susannah. When people died or were dying she was called for. She remembers putting tubs of ice around the dead before the practice of embalming them was started. She has certainly had her share of welcoming spirits into martality as well as seeing them leave. She has always been one to pay respect to the departed. There have been very few funerals in Henefer during her lifetime that she hasn’t attended.
 In addition to her nursing and taking in borders she got a job as janitor of the Henefer school house. It paid $35.00 a month. Verna helped her do the work most of the time. She also had two or three cows which she milked to send cream to the creamery. Claude liked to milk and helped out until he went into the military service. Merl didn’t like to milk and when he did it, it didn’t take him long to dry them up but he helped out in other ways. The family also had a chicken coop and some chickens.
 With the help of her children she was able to meet the challenge of rearing her own family. It is a credit to Merl, Elva, Verna, Veda and Claude that they have never allowed their mother to live off welfare but have supported her in her later life just as she did them in their earlier life.
 Verna was the last in the family to marry so she lived with her mother and helped out all she could. Even after she was married she and Glen and later their daughters drove fro Clearfield for a number of years to spend weekends with her. A granddaughter, Mary Richins, stayed nights with her for five or six years while she was in high school. Verna, Veda and Elva have made sure their mother’s home was always clean and comfortable. Elva and Veda have lived close to her and have helped her and she has visited in their homes nearly every day.
 Merl and Claude are always ready and willing to do what they can to make improvements on her home and to make her happy. They never forget her on special occasions and whenever possible they get her to visit with them in their homes. She has always enjoyed eating with her children and grandchildren and they have enjoyed having her. She likes to help with the dishes after she eats. When canning time rolls around she spends a number of days at her daughter’s or granddaughter’s peeling peaches, pears, and tomatoes.
 An example of how she has made good use of her time is her crocheting. Not only her family but many friends and neighbors have some of her work in their homes. She spends many hours watching television and crocheting. She seldom makes anything for herself. It is usually a birthday, Christmas, or wedding present for a grandchild or great-grandchild. About a year prior to this writing she got arthritis in her hands so badly that she couldn’t crochet. Prescribed medicine and treatment helped but she still couldn’t do the work she wanted to do. She asked for a blessing from the Priesthood and one day my father (Norman) and I gave her a blessing. Since that time she has been able to crochet and it is the hope and prayers of all of us that she will be able to always use her hands.
 She has been blessed with good health throughout her life. She has always been active physically. In fact, she likes to go out in public and mingle with her friends. She has always taken every opportunity to go places with her family when they have invited her. She went with Veda and Jimmy to California and she has been with Merl and Grace to the Washington and Oregon area. She is always ready to go even at a minutes notice.
 In addition to taking care of her family she was always active in the church. During her lifetime she has served as secretary to the YWMIA, counselor in the Primary Presidency, teacher in Sunday School, teacher of religion class held after school, first counselor in Relief Society for eleven years, secretary and treasurer of relief Society, and a Relief Society visiting teacher most of her life serving under twelve different presidents.
 We remember her as an active church attender. We look forward to fast and testimony meeting when she always bears her testimony by requesting that the congregation sing one of her favorite hymns with her. [As I type this I, daughter of her niece-Margaret E. Stephens, recall being in Henefer on a Fast Sunday when she requested, “I Know That My Redeemer Lives” I had never experienced such a request. I now think of Aunt Susannah every time I sing this hymn.]
 When the Henefer D.U.P. Camp was organized she was appointed second vice-president and is now Chaplin having been an active member ever since it was organized. She has helped organize the Thomas Henry Stephens Family organization and as the only living child of Thomas has served as its honorary president the past number of years.
 On July 23, 1960 the family held an open house to honor Susannah on her 80th birthday. Many friends as well as those of the family paid respect to her on that day. One of the things we remember most about Grandma and her family is the many times we have gotten together on special occasions. Every Christmas when we were young and up until the grandchildren and great grandchildren got so numerous we couldn’t all get in her front room, we used to have a family Christmas Eve Party.
 At the time of this writing (Feb. 1966). Grandma is 85 years old and has thirty-three grandchildren and 19 great-grandchildren. May she continue to enjoy good health and strength and have joy and happiness in the future. It is the desire of all of us that we many carry on the Fowler and Stephens names and pay honor and respect to them.
 This was written by James Alden Richins, grandson of James Henry and Susannah Jones Stephens Fowler. Information obtained from history of James Henry Fowler, written by his daughter, Elva Mary Fowler Richins, and from interviews with Susannah and other members of her family.
 Aunt Susannah passed on the 24th of Dec. 1978 and after being parted from her sweetheart, Jim, for about 48 years and has joyfully joined him for eternity. My Grandfather, Robert Jones Stephens, died before I was born. Although a few of his siblings were still alive while I was very young and I am sure I met them sometime (Uncle Fred and Uncle John, and Aunt Lucy) my memories elude me as I was under the age of 10 but Aunt Susannah remains a bright memory in my life. Living in California we didn’t get to visit often although after moving to Utah we would visit almost yearly, she remains a happy memory of my childhood and the love her niece, my mother, had for her. (Peggy Ahlborn)
