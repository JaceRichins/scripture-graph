# The Life of John Allen Sutton

_A story of [[John Allen Sutton (KWZ8-RFV)|John Allen Sutton]]._

## The story as it was written

The following story came from a journal written and kept by our Grandfather, John Allen Sutton, Sr. The story itself was recorded from this journal by his daughter, Margaret Sutton Budge, and his granddaughter, Margaret Ann Budge Wilson.
 This history is very authentic. The wording and expressions are truly his own. It begins when he was born and it tells about events that happened throughout his life, until after he returned from his mission to Great Britain in 1883. This was the first forty-nine years of his life. Much happened after that and another story could be written about those years from 1883 until his death in 1913 at Paris, Idaho.
 He believed in his God and in his Church, and no task was too great for him to undertake if it was to further the work of his religion. He had a strong will, a good mind, and the strength to complete that he began. He weathered many heart­aches and frustrations which seemed to come his way.
 The Icelanders spoken of in the story made the first Icelander settlement in the United States in Spanish Fork, Utah. A story of these people was published by the Daughters of Utah Pioneers, “Our Pioneer Heritage”, Vol. 7, in which John A. Sutton was mentioned as the man who brought them across the ocean on the steamship, “Wisconsin”.
 We are grateful to him for keeping a record of these many interesting events in his life. And to Aunt Margaret (Mag), and her daughter, Margaret, for recording this in a story form for us to read. The original journal was given to B.Y.U, and can be found in the L. Tom Perry Special Collections Library. Oral Sutton Mower
A BRIEF SKETCH OF THE LIFE OF JOHN ALLEN SUTTON, OF PARIS, BEAR LAKE COUNTY, IDAHO,
 I was born in the town of Leire, Leicestershire, England, November 16, either in 1832 or 1834, I believe the latter. My father's name was John Sutton, son of Thomas and Susanna Sutton. My mother's maiden name was Ann Allen, daughter of Richard and Ann Allen, all residents of Leicestershire.
 My childhood days were spent at school until I was eight years of age. At that age I had an affliction which came near terminating my life. It was an infection of the glands of the neck to which caused a great swelling. My father and mother resorted to many devices for a cure but could find none until I was taken to a physician of Irish persuasion who had come into the country. By his skill he effected what he thought a permanent cure. From this time until I was 13 years of age I spent my time tending sheep, milking cows, and as a general farm boy.
 At this period there was a railway passed through the village where I was born, called the Midland County Railway, which brought many people into that section of the country, of all characters, causing a great deal of means to be brought to the place, and consequently, many vices. These events caused much trouble and sorrow in many families through dissipation, etc.
 At fourteen years of age my father wished to put me out as an apprentice to learn the trade of wheelwright. The parties concerned wanted twenty-one pounds as bounty money for me to serve seven years to learn the trade. My father not being willing to pay that amount, asked me if I would learn the blacksmith trade, that being my father’s trade, blacksmith and farrier. I told him I would like to learn the trade and so it was arranged.
 Nothing particular occurred until the summer of 1849. That would make me fifteen years old. There came to our village two men professing to be Mormons, or Latter-Day-Saints, by name, Edward Stevenson and Henry Bowns. They held their meetings in the open air and many people flocked to hear them. I, with others was very much taken up with their teachings as they were feasible and seemed according to scripture.
 For several weeks these men came Sunday after Sunday. I still investigated their principles, but being young I was too timid to talk to them concerning their doctrine. Two families embraced the gospel, by name, Welsh and Douglas. Not until November eleventh of the same year, 1849, did I make up my mind to ask them to baptize me, as I was thoroughly convinced that the doctrine was true and according to scriptures. I then spoke to one of the elders, Henry Sanders, who told me if I believed I could be baptized. I replied that I did.
 This was on Sunday. There was a great commotion in the place, for there were many enemies to this people already in the village who declared that if the Elders went to baptize me they would drown him. Not being daunted, we started to the stream of water and I believe there were nearly one hundred people followed us. The Elder told the people that he was going to perform one of the ordinances of the gospel of Jesus Christ and if they would be quiet he would show them how it was done. He then took me into the waters and baptized me, and not a word was said by any person. Thus I entered into a new life to me.
 After receiving confirmation on the next Sabbath Day, I became very fervent in the performance of all the duties they required of me. My father and mother, brothers and sisters thought I was very foolish and that it would not last long, but finding that not to be the case and that many people were joining the Church in the little village persecution began to rage and my greatest enemies were those of my own household. Not that I believe my father and mother would have ill-treated me, had it not been for the prejudice of the different sects that we were surrounded with and from whom my father gained his support by his trade, they threatened to take away his work unless I would cease going with this people. On account of this my father was often cruel to me, to the extent that I left home and tried to gain a livelihood by any trade.
 Prior to this I was ordained to the office of an Elder and each Sabbath we used to go to the different villages to preach. My father felt the great need of my help and tried to induce me to return home again. My mother came to me at the town of Leicester, and through her tears and persuasions I went home again and remained there until the spring of 1854.
 About this time I became acquainted with one Eliza Billingham, whom I afterwards married. I had now made up my mind to leave the land of my nativity and to go to America, to Salt Lake City, it being a part of our religion to gather from the world. So strong was this feeling on me to gather, that nothing seemed to stay the progress. Still I was poor.
 Three months prior to my starting to America, I had no means, but President John Angus told me if I wished to go that the way would be opened for me. I placed such confidence in his word that I commenced to make preparations to leave. The fare from Liverpool to Salt Lake City was ten pounds in what was known as “The Ten Pound Company”. It appeared impossible for me to raise the amount, but by saving all the money I could get a hold of I managed to get enough to pay my fare to New Orleans. An incident occurred at this time that seemed fraught with danger to my taking departure. In consequence of my not being of age, my father said he would stop me from going.
 After all preparations were made, my fare across the ocean paid, and my boxes and things were sent to Liverpool, I went to bid my folks goodbye and my father was away from home. I feared he had gone to get an officer to stop me but I said farewells and started out without seeing my father and went to the house of one John Douglas in the same village and stayed for a few hours. In the meantime my father came home and made inquiries for me. They told him I was gone. He said, "Go and bring him back, I want to see him before he goes." So my brother, Thomas, came down to the house where they supposed I would be and asked for me. I questioned my brother as to whether there was an officer waiting for me and he said there was not, but that my father wished to see me before I made my departure. I went home and my father said to me, "Then you are determined to go, are you?" I said, "Yes, father, that is my intention." He said, "I think you are very foolish, you do not know where you are going, you are going into a new country and you have no money, nor friends. Now if you will stay with us for one year I will give you this (throwing his purse on the table) besides paying you good wages and will send you off comfortable." I told him I would not stay if he gave me all he possessed. Then he said he would not give me a farthing. I shook hands with him and bid him farewell. He then called me back and gave me a sovereign, which proved a great help to me at Liverpool.
 I took the train and arrived at Liverpool with many others going to cross the ocean on the same ship. In consequence of the ship not being fitted up properly, we were detained in Liverpool two weeks, from the first to the twelfth of March, and the expense incurred used up all my money with the exception of four shillings. On the twelfth day of March we went on board the ship, John M. Wood, with six or seven hundred passengers, all members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-Day-Saints. The company was presided over by Robert Campbell.
 After setting sail on the Irish Channel we encountered a terrific storm. The captain ordered all the hatchways down and kept all the passengers in the lower deck. No tongue could describe the scene between those decks for 48 hours. Boxes pitching, pots and everything loose flying about, and nearly all the ship's company seasick. Such an experience I shall never forget while on earth.
 On the second day at noon we were driven back within sight of Liverpool. After this we set sail and got into the Atlantic Ocean. Nothing of interest occurred, having fair weather until we passed the Island of Cuba, when a terrific storm came along, which tore the sails to shreds and moved the main mast nearly six inches out of its socket. But it was all over in fifteen minutes.
 We soon arrived at the Gulf of Mexico, where we were becalmed for about ten days, the sea being smooth as glass. We finally came to within twenty miles of the city of New Orleans. After some trouble in negotiating with a steam tug to takes us over the Bar, we were towed into the mouth of the Mississippi River. Next day, after seven weeks of ocean voyage, we landed in New Orleans. There were two deaths and two births on the voyage.
 Preparations were made as quickly as possible to take the company from New Orleans to St. Louis. How to go I did not know as all the money I had was four shillings. Finally President McDonald loaned me $2.50 which took me to St Louis. The voyage up the river was fraught with many events. The cholera was very high and many of the passengers died. Some would be taken down with the disease and would die within three hours; but fortunately my health was good and I was able to assist in taking care of the sick and in burying the dead.
 When we arrived within four miles of St. Louis, the captain feared he would be fined for carrying so many passengers, so he put four hundred of us off and we walked to St. Louis. I arrived in St. Louis with only five cents in my pocket, not knowing any person nor where I should go. Fortune favored me. Passing through the streets I met one of our ship’s company who was very pleased to see me. He took me down to a restaurant and I ate a very pleasant meal and I thought I had never had better food before and never expected to again, so hungry was I.
 My next trouble was to know how to get up to the frontier, a town by the name of Kamas, the outfitting post for the emigration that year. After resting over night with my friend, I traveled around St. Louis to try to find work at my trade. All the shops seemed full and I could not get work till I came across the 'Espenshied Wagon Company' where they told me to come to work in the morning. I started to go to work and got near the shop, still something told me not to go in, for what reason I could not tell. I stood for a few minutes and then started down to the wharf. I went on board the Thursday Packet, F X Aubry, and inquired if they wanted any hands to work up the river. They said they did, so I hired to them to go to Council Bluffs for a dollar a day and my board. I being somewhat green as to the work on boats, I suffered many insults and much abuse, for the mate was a very cruel man, especially to new hands.
 While on the boat, the engineer found out I was a blacksmith, so he wanted me to work in the shop, which pleased me very much; but the mate swore I should not work there and that I should stand my regular watch, so fearing more cruelty from the hands of the mate, the engineer locked me in the iron grated shop. When my watch came around the mate went to hunt me up at my berth, and with oaths, swore he would kill me if I did not come out and go on my watch. I made no answer. He then took a piece of timber and broke my berth down, but not finding me he went to the engineer, who told him that I could not go on my watch as I was working for him. An altercation then took place which nearly resulted in the death of one of them.
 Finally the boat arrived at Kansas City, which city at that time comprised one store and two homes. I informed the mate that I wished now to leave the boat and asked him for my pay. Finally after a volley of oaths they paid me my six dollars and let me go. I had now enough money to pay what I had borrowed and a nice margin to spare, the sum of three dollars.
 We waited at Kansas City some time, the cholera being very bad, and spent our time in preparing wagons, making ox shoes, ox yokes and bows, and fitting up. One day upon going into the store at Kansas City I saw a coin on the floor. I looked at it and placed my foot upon it. Finally I picked it up and found it to be a sovereign. Knowing it did not belong to me I went to camp and told John Angus what I had found and asked him what I should do with it. “Well, my boy,” he said, “If anyone inquires for the lost money, give it to him; if not, I don't know of anyone who needs it worse than you do and I shall consider it a God-send to you.”
 My next trouble was to know how to go across the plains, one thousand miles. I hadn't money to pay my fare and my case looked rather dark, as all the trains that hired men to drive teams had gone. I finally succeeded making a contract with a man by the name of Jarvis to take me across the plains. I was to fix up his wagons and milk his cows on the journey. Therefore, in the beginning of July the company moved out on the plains and was organized. There were about one hundred wagons and Job Smith was appointed captain.On the first part of the journey nothing of interest occurred except the running away of cattle, breaking up of wagons, getting stuck in the mud, a great deal of which was in consequence of unskilled drivers. While crossing a creek at what is called the Catholic Mission, not far from Big Blue, one man got drowned. He was sick and while crossing a log, he fell in and was drowned before anyone knew it.
 We arrived close to Fort Carney where we encountered a terrific storm, the water rising so high that our cattle were stampeded and we had to remain there two days. Finally we took up our line of march and passed along the Platte River on the south side until we got to Ash Hollow. We camped there and drove our cattle across the Platte to feed. I was one who was detailed to cross the river and herd the cattle. Being very tired, I laid down and went to sleep. I was roused suddenly by something rattling. I looked and there was a great rattle snake ready to make a jump. Having seen many of them on our travels and hearing many incidents related to them, I dared not move for fear he would bite me. Finally I made one spring and got out of his way. Fortunately for his snakeship there was nothing I could kill him with than a buffalo chip.
 At this place we encountered Captain Richardson's Company which had started one week ahead of us. He made his brags that he could beat us into Salt Lake City by two weeks. We told him all right, but we thought not. We then traveled on and crossed the South Fork or the Platte. The river being dangerous to ford, we got as many as ten and twenty yoke of cattle on to one wagon. I crossed the Platte that day about six times, it being about one-half mile wide. Rest assured that when night came I was ready for sleep.
 When we came within ten miles of Fort Laramie we encountered the Sioux Nation of Indians, 6,000 strong. They were a noble race of people, of fine physique. They were making chiefs. Their method was to form a circle, stick secures through the breast, twist a rope around them and the one that could endure to walk around this circle the longest was considered the greatest chief.
 The next day the Danish Company came up. The Indians took two of the cows and killed them. The captain reported at the garrison of Fort Laramie what the Indians had done. A company with one cannon and an Indian interpreter were then ordered to go and demand the pay for the cattle. While parleying with the Indians there was a misunderstanding, between the chief and the interpreter. The captain ordered one of the men to shoot the chief, which he did, and in less than twenty minutes the Indians killed all the company, broke their cannon and threw it into the Platte. There was then great fear that the Indians would take Fort Laramie. Men were sent in every direction to all posts around to make forced marches to get out of the way. Our company traveled all that day and that night in the Black Hills. We had been without water for one day, when we caught up with the company, James Brown of Ogden, being the captain. He told us we could not stop, for there was not enough water for his company and ours and that he would shoot the first oxen unyoked. A man by the name of John Hill, who drove the team ahead of me, said, “All right, we will see.” He stepped up to his wagon and got out his blunderbuss, then went up to Old Buck and let him out of the yoke. “Now,” he said, “Shoot.” Captain Brown saw that it was no use to resist any longer and concluded we could stay.
 Arriving at Platte Bridge, it was thought best to divide the company into three parts. Here an incident occurred which seemed as though I was to be left on the plains, for Mr. Jarvis' provisions had given out and he told me he could not take me any further. Still I had found a friend in Mr. Ford, now living in Centerville, Utah. He had lost two of his sons who had died of cholera. Mr. Ford told me he would be glad if I would come and drive his team and the change was a blessing to me for I had to suffer many indignities from Mr. Jarvis. I drove Mr. Ford’s team till he arrived at Salt Lake City, which was on the twenty third day of September, and we had made our camp at Jordan Bridge.
 Now for the first time I realized the saying of my father, "You know not where you are going; you have no money and you will have no friends and you will be in a foreign land." It was true, I had $2.50 in my pocket and I was nearly destitute of clothing, I did not have friends. One Charles Welch, a native of Leire, took me in and gave me my supper, a bed, and breakfast. That was all he was able to do for me as he was only a laboring man. I then returned to camp and sat down on my bundle. I had no box, for I had thrown that away on the camp grounds at Kansas. I wondered what I should do.
 While I was sitting meditating, one Elder Louis Robbins came down to camp. He was once president of the conference where I came from. Noticing me he came up and shook me by the hand and gave me a cordial greeting. “So you have got here at last, my young man,” he said. He asked me if I had any place to go. Telling him I had not, he requested me to pick up my bundle and go with him as I was welcome. My heart leaped within me to think that someone had that much respect for me. When I got to his home, I found that his very kind and amiable wife was a friend of mine from the same conference. They told me to stay and rest myself for two or three weeks and then I would be able to go to work. No one could tell how thankful I was. I did not spend but two or three days before I started out to hunt work.
 My first experience at my trade was at the Tithing Blacksmith Shop, the president of which was named Tanner, a very uncouth man to strangers. Being unaccustomed to their mode of work, after five days of trying my best to please I left the shop in disgust.
 After traveling around for a day or two in Salt Lake City, I came across one by the name of James Hennifer, and inquired if he did not want to hire a hand. He asked me what I could do and I told him I could do most anything in the blacksmith line. He asked me if I would like to come in as a partner with him. I told him I would if we could make suitable arrangements. We struck an agreement and I became a full-fledged partner in the blacksmith business in Salt Lake City. The first span of horses I shod came to $6.00 and I spent that to buy me a pair of breeches. I began to think that I should soon be respectable again.
 In the spring of 1855, I became very anxious to get my wife and her relatives. I laid the matter before Squire Wells, who was emigration agent at the time. He told me he would be able to send for them and I could pay for it in blacksmithing. It was too late for them to come that year, so it was arranged that they should come in 1856.
 In the spring of 1856, I left my friends, Brother Robbins and went to board with Charles Smith’s wife, whose husband was on a mission in England. I thought I could assist them as they had a small family of children. My business was pretty good until the fall of 1855 when Brother Charles Smith came home from his mission. He brought with him a young lady whom he afterwards made his second wife. Needing the room in his house, he said he would be glad if I could get another place to board, although I had paid him my board for the winter. Not wishing to intrude, I settled up with him, he paying me a silver watch and several other articles as a balance due me. I then went to live with Phillip Margetts, the noted tragedian.
 In the spring of 1856 there was a great outbreak among the Indians on the Humbolt. The governor ordered out a posse of armed men to go with the Marshall to arrest one Carl Murray who was supposed to be on the Humbolt, he having committed the crime of murder, which had excited the Indians to commit the depredations. This threw a great deal of blacksmithing in the hands of Mr. Margetts, in the shoeing horses and fitting out the company. While I was shoeing I got hurt with one of the horses, which caused a great deal of pain and suffering, affecting my lungs very bad and again I felt the want of a mother. While brother and sister Margetts were very kind to me it was not like home.
 I lived in anticipation of my wife's coming from England and that buoyed up my spirits. As I had just received word of their starting the thirteenth of August, myself and Washington Perkins started back upon the plains to meet the handcart company, taking with us a load of flour which was all we had to live on with the exception of two dried suckers. We did not expect to return under six weeks. The reason of such scarcity of food was that the grasshoppers had eaten off the crops and there was a famine in the land. Had it not been for the kindness that existed among the people there would have been great suffering; but with few exceptions, they would divide the last loaf with each other.
 My trip upon the pains was a very eventful one. We arrived at Fort Bridger after five days travel. There we found the Old Snake Nation with the U.S. Agent giving them presents. Being suspicious that the Indians would steal our horses, I called upon one Louis Robison, who lived at Bridger at that time. He told me that my best plan would be to put my horses in the hands of the Indians as they would take care of them and deliver them to me the next day for a few pounds of flour. I did so, but when the morning came the Indians could not find the horses. I went out myself to find them but could not. My. Robison told me they must be stolen. He told me to ask Mr. Ferguson if he would not go and find them for me. I asked him if he would find them for me and offered him $2.50 and twenty pounds of flour. I always believed that Ferguson had secured them for the sake of getting the reward. I will state here that this man, a few years after, committed the crime of murder in Salt Lake City, for which he suffered the penalty of the law and was hung by the neck until dead.
 My partner got very much intoxicated at this place and used very abusive language. I chided him and told him I would not hear such language in my presence. For about three days while we were traveling east he was like a man with no tongue, for he scarcely spoke a word.
 We traveled till we got to the three crossings of the Sweetwater, which is some 370 miles east of Salt Lake City and there we made our camp. No tidings yet of any emigration, but in a few days we got word from Deer Creek, still 200 miles farther east, for us not to come any further as they had plenty of provisions. We had been there about two weeks when several trains passed us going to Salt Lake City, which supplied us with some food to eat other than flour. Several men, one Thomas Margetts, brother of Phillip Margetts and a family by the name of Cowdy came along. They were particular friends of mine. They afterwards got murdered by the Indians about half a day’s travel from Fort Carney, with the exception of Thomas Margetts’s wife who was taken captive by the Indians as was supposed, because nothing of her remains could be found at the camp.
 One beautiful day about noon, along came the company of emigrants whose captain was one John Banks. This company was destitute of flour, having only about three days’ rations on hand. We concluded to let them have our flour and took pledges for the same, silk dresses, guns, pistols, tools, and anything they had. They were to redeem them when they got to Salt Lake City. Whether they did or not I am unable to tell.
 This man, Banks, was afterwards one of the leading men of the Morrisites, whose death occurred by being shot with one Morris and several others who refused to surrender themselves to the action of the law.
 While we were encamped at this place, which was the 23rd of September, we encountered a fearful storm and the snow fell to the depth of about eighteen inches, which made things look very gloomy for those poor emigrants that were still hundreds of miles back on the trail, dragging their handcarts along.
 Two days after this or nearly so, the two first companies came along, one of whose captain was Daniel McCarthy, and the other was Ellsworth. It was a curious sight to see six or seven hundred people with about four to a handcart. These two companies arrived in Salt Lake City without any accident and with very few deaths. All seemed healthy and robust, but my people were not in these companies. I then had to return to Salt Lake City, three hundred miles. After resting for a few days, counsel was given by President Young for men and teams to go back upon the plains again to meet these two remaining companies, one of whose captain was Wilsey and the other Edward Martin of the last handcart company. There was still one wagon company back whose captain was John A. Hunt. I don’t wish to recall the suffering of those people for it is horrible to contemplate. I and others were eye witnesses to their sufferings. Those who did not perish by the way, arrived in Salt Lake City on the thirtieth day of November 1856, on Sunday.
 I will here mention that while traveling up main street of Salt Lake City, word was brought to us that Jedediah M. Grant was dead. He having been in a trance for some time had revived again, told the things that he saw in the spirit world and after this conversation he gave up the ghost.
 A more pitiful sight I never saw nor do I wish to see than those poor creatures that had just arrived in Salt Lake City, but thanks to providence they were in the midst of friends who took them in, fed and clothed them, and gave them shelter.
 I will state that I met the company that I thought my people were with near Canyon Creek. I went into their camp one cold November night and went around their fires to see if I could find my folks or someone that I knew. Finally I came to one campfire and there saw the mother of her whom I loved. I also saw my father-in-law. I sat around the fire some time without making myself known to Mrs. Douglas, the mother of my wife. She burst into tears when I finally made myself known.
 I asked her where Lizzie was and she could not speak for some time. Finally she told me she was dead. I asked her where Willie was, my brother. He was dead also. You can imagine my feelings better than I can describe them. I felt as though my hopes were all blasted.
 I hired a house in Salt Lake City for her father and mother to live in during the winter and one William Severn and wife who now reside in Montpelier. The winter passed off without anything of interest. There was a great depth of snow that winter. My friend, Douglas, and myself started on a trip to Ogden to see a friend by the name of Charles Welch, a native of our place. We undertook to travel it on foot, forty miles. The snow being so deep we could not make it in one day. We stopped at the Bishop’s in Kaysville Ward, whose name was Smith. We asked him if he would not give us shelter for one night. Although he had a large house and appeared to have plenty of room, still he would not entertain us and what to do we did not know. We tried to start again but could not get along. Finally a friend took us in for the night, but didn’t ask us to have anything to eat, so we retired hungry, sore footed and worn out. We waited for our breakfast in the morning till about 9:00 and then had a splendid meal. We continued our journey and arrived in Ogden safe and very tired indeed. We had a very pleasant visit with our friend in Ogden.
 On our return trip to Salt Lake City there came up a great storm while crossing the sand ridge and we got lost; still we thought we were on the road all the time and kept traveling along but when the storm cleared off we were miles toward the foot of the mountains, out of the way. Still we arrived that evening at Farmington, but the people were not very hospitable. We tried several houses to get a place to sleep but failed till after dark in the evening. Finally we called upon one Mr. Ware, a tanner. We asked him if he would give us a night’s lodging as we were hungry; had no money, had no place to sleep. “Yes,” he said, “my name is Ware and you can stop here.”
 We arrived in Salt Lake City again all safe and sound. I still continued to work with Mr. Margetts. At the April Conference, Mr. Margetts was called on a mission to Great Britain with many others. They were to cross the plains with hand carts like the emigrants had done the fall before. So Mr. Margetts, Seymore R. Yound, and David Wilkins made one team and a jolly crowd they were. They made the trip from Salt Lake City to the Missouri River in less than five weeks, without the loss of a man.
 On the twenty fourth day of July, 1857, President Young invited the people, as many as felt desirous to take a trip to the head waters or Big Cottonwood to celebrate the entrance or the pioneers to Salt Lake Valley. Prior to this I had made the acquaintance of Margaret Shepherd, so I invited her to accompany me. On the twenty third day of July we started and made our camp in the canyon, and on the twenty fourth, err nightfall, we arrived at the head waters. When we were in our height of glee and amusement, along came one Judson Stoddard, the U.S. mail carrier, and brought to us the sad news that the U.S. had stopped our mail to Utah Territory.
 President Young then called the people together in the Bowery, that was built for the purpose of amusement, to inform them of the feeling that existed between the U.S. Officers and our people. The real cause of this trouble was through the misrepresentation of one Judge Cradelbaugh and Judge Brochers, declaring to the President of the United States that the people of Utah had destroyed the public records and defied the power and authority of the U.S. After some patriotic speeches by the prominent men of Utah, the people with one voice declared that they would maintain their rights as the Pilgrim Fathers had done against the tyranny of King George, after which we proposed to enjoy ourselves on the occasion for which we had come to celebrate.
 We returned after three days’ enjoyment to Salt Lake City. In the meantime all efforts were made to gain information as to the correctness of the statements made by the mail carrier. Finding that no information could be gained, President Young consulted with the officers of the territory as to the advisability of calling a portion of the militia of the Territory and sending them out on the plains to see if the information we had received was correct and whether the U.S. Government had actually, a portion of their army to come to Utah to take away the rights of the citizens of the territory. They arrived at the conclusion that Colonel Burton should take a company of minute men, of which I was a member, to start out east to see what information we could gain.
 Accordingly, on the thirteenth day of August, to the best of my recollection, we met in Canyon Creek Camp, east of the Big Mountain, and organized. We started east and arrived at Fort Bridger, one hundred and twelve miles east of Salt Lake City, kept by Lewis Robison, formerly occupied by Mr. Bridger, a pioneer of ’46. We killed some beef there, dried some meat, and took our journey eastward and traveled to what is known as Devil’s Gate, three hundred and fifteen miles from Salt Lake City. Gaining no tidings nor information for the object of which we were in pursuit, Colonel Burton detailed three men, Jesse Earl, Frank Knowlton, and Twigley, to go eastward and reconnoiter and if they found troops to return immediately. These individuals went in disguise as Californians. One could speak the Spanish language and had been to California.
 After two days’ travel east on the Platte River, they came in contact with the U.S. Troops, some two thousand in number. They went into their camp, seeking protection from them and a fresh supply of food to carry with them. They found out all that was to be known and in the morning, saddled up their animals, placed their provisions on their pack animals and made a detour through the mountains, passing the troops and arriving twelve hours before the troops at the Devil’s Gate. This was the first news we had received of the troops.
 We then went to work and cached all the provisions that we had no immediate use for, also goods that belonged to the emigration of ’56, such as pianos, organs, clothes boxes and all manner of implements. We then dispatched news to Salt Lake City of all the information we had received. We then started west and made our encampment at the headwaters of the Sweetwater, at the foot of the Windriver Mountains. We conducted a reconnoiter to watch the movements of the troops on their arrival at Pacific Springs. Colonel Burton, with a battalion of one hundred men, thought best to try and cripple the troops as much as possible.
 One night we made ready to stampede their animals and organized ourselves in such a manner as to see that no man would fall into their hands if possible. Accordingly, we moved to their camp and ten men were detailed with dry bearskins, dumballs, etc., for the purpose of stampeding their animals. We succeeded in stampeding over three hundred herd of their animals and we all escaped safely from their camp. It caused great tumult in the camp of the soldiers. It took them from fifteen to thirty minutes before they began to rally.
 We then made a forced march of seventy five miles without food and made our encampment on the Big Sandy. Colonel Burton seeing his little band tired, weary, and hungry, ordered them to dismount, hobble their horses and go to sleep and he would stand guard himself. After three hours of sleep the bugle was blown and the men were in their saddles again. We then made a forced march north to Fort Bridger. Arriving there many of the company were sick through exposure and were scarcely able to perform duty. By this time many troops had arrived from Salt Lake City to assist in protecting the lives of the citizens of the territory from what we considered our enemies. But to cut this narrative short, the people gained their purpose and kept the troops at bay and they encamped at Fort Bridger and stayed there all winter.
 In the meantime our old friend, Colonel Cane with General McCullough had offered their services to the President of the United States to come to Utah and inquire into the facts of the case, to see whether the people were in open rebellion or whether they had been misrepresented by those judges. Governor Young made a general call upon all the able-bodied men in the territory to come to the standard and if necessary fight for their rights, their homes, their wives and children. The call was not in vain, for all who were able to bear arms came to the standard. Many of them without shoes upon their feet and many with a rag tied upon their head for a hat and many without a coat to their backs, traveled to Echo Canyon, at the time the only in-road to Salt Lake City.
 It would be hard to detail the trying scenes we passed through in the short period of one night, but suffice it to say that not one shot was fired upon those men that had come with the intention of destroying our people. No accident befell our people with but one exception-one man was accidentally shot.
 Colonel Cane and General McCullough came by way of California and were kindly received by the people as peace commissioners. A council was held with Governor Young and other Utah officials in the Council House of the city. The results of this council were that the people had been misrepresented and the U.S. troops were allowed to pass through Salt Lake City to their place of designation, Camp Floyd. They agreed not to destroy any property belonging to the people until such time as a conference could be had with those in command.
 In the meantime, President Young counseled with the people who said they had been driven from their homes in the east, their property destroyed, they had to pass through wilderness and seek shelter on then foreign soil, but still being loyal to their country and had raised the flag of the nation in the barren wastes of Deseret, 1,000 miles from civilization. President Young did not propose to allow mobs of any party to receive the benefits of their hard earnings and toil. Rather than to do this he proposed to the people that we shall all travel south, barricade our homes and possessions and place them in such a position that the torch could be applied at a moment’s notice. To this proposition the people agreed. The exodus was started in May, 1858, and all the people north of what is known as the point of the mountain of Salt Lake Valley left their homes and started south, camping in different settlements south of the point of the mountain. The scene of these travels would make the heart sick, for many of the people were in abject poverty. I know families where there were five daughters who had only one dress between them.
 I will here state that on the 26th of April of this same year, 1858, I married my present wife, Margaret Hannah Shepherd, and from this union was born ten sons and one daughter.
We moved south to Provo and built what is known as a wicky-up in one of the streets. I left my wife there to spend her honeymoon while I returned to Salt Lake City to join my company on guard. There were many incidents to relate if I had time which would be amusing to those who read this sketch, but I will for forbear and say that the troops arrived in Salt Lake City, passed quietly, according to previous arrangements, through the city and camped on the other side of the Jordan River. I will maintain the first news I heard of their being in the city. My friend, Harry Margetts, at whose house we were camped, came in and said, “Come and see those French beggars with **** pot hats on.”
Everything passed off very peaceable and we returned to Provo, the headquarters of the Territorial Officers. In a few days arrangements were made for the people to return again to their homes.
 I went to Provo where my wife was, packed up our things and returned to Salt Lake City. I had no home of my own at that time, but was renting a house. Our furniture consisted of three chairs, a clothes box for a table, and a small step-stove. We also had one teacup and saucer, one plate, and a tin cup and plate. That was our stock in trade at the time.
 I went to work at my trade, blacksmithing, with R. B. Margetts. We took a contract to make a lot of stone hammers for the army, a fortunate thing for us at that time. By this means I was soon able to buy myself a home which I did in the fall of 1858.
 In January, 1859, my first son was born, John, by name. This gave us a great deal of consolation and paid us for the trials we had in moving. There was nothing of interest from this time till the year, 1861, when on January 6th we had another son born, by name, Richard. In 1862 I thought best to move into the country, so I went to Weber County, and purchased what I supposed to be a farm. This was in the winter, snow fell to a great depth, a great many stock died, and when the snow disappeared from my farm in the spring, I found a great big hollow and that was full of water and a small willow patch. Mr. John I. Hart, the man of whom I bought the place was supposed at that time to be a good man, but I failed to believe it, as he surely swindled me and since that time he has been proved to be anything but an honest man.
 I started to work at my trade, but it was very difficult for me to get material to work with and I had to go on the sand ridge and dig up stumps to make charcoal with. I did not seem to prosper in this place and I met with a very severe loss. While attending meeting at Ogden, my shop caught fire and burned down with all my tools in it. Also, my wife’s sister, Mary, paid us a visit from Salt Lake City with two of her children; her eldest child, Anna, was taken sick and within a week died.
 Not seeming to prosper in this place I went to Salt Lake City to see if I could buy my place back again. I then went into partnership with Phillip Margetts and worked with him till Xmas of that year, when the building was pulled down to give place for another building. Then I rented a shop and went into business for myself. For two years and a half I had a very good business, then I took as a partner a man by the name of Peter Reinseimer.
 Nothing of interest occurred till August of 1869 when myself and one hundred other men were called on a mission to Bear Lake Valley. This was a considerable trial to break up home again, still I felt willing to respond to the call, so myself and Mr. Margetts came up in advance of our families and procured a lot in Paris, and commenced to build a house. In October of the same year we moved our families up and finished our homes so that we were able to live in them that winter. Mr. Margetts and myself formed a partnership in the blacksmith trade. That winter was a very severe one.
 Bear Lake Valley at the time was a desolate place, infested with grasshoppers and crickets. We could not raise any crops and we had to go to Cache Calley, distance of about seventy miles, to buy our bread-stuff. I was returning from Logan with our flour and valuables for my family, and in climbing up the Big Mountain my team baulked, backed down the hill, tipped over the wagon, spilled the flour and everything else. It took us from four o’clock in the morning till nine o’clock at night to get our things up to the top of the mountain. I made the remark then, that if I had to go to Cache Valley again for my bread I would go there and live, for I would never bring it up that mountain again. While going up the mountain a man over-took us and took three of our sacks of flour on his wagon. He came to Paris ahead of us and reported to Charles Rich concerning the incident, telling of the accident. He foolishly went down to my family and told them my wagon had tipped over and I had been killed. This news caused a great deal of trouble and distress to my family. Teams were getting ready to start out after us, but fortunately it was a false report and none of us were hurt.
 Finding our partnership business unprofitable, I sold out to Mr. Margetts and went into business for myself. I bought some land and commenced to raise my own grain. I found this to be the most profitable, as my family was increasing and I then had something for my boys to do.
 In the year 1870, my family had increased so that I had to build. I build a brick house with four rooms and a hall. On June 27, 1878 a very sorrowful accident occurred in our family. My eldest son, John, went to the canyon with his little 10 year old brother, Thomas. While the boy was drawing a drag of wood from the mountain side, he struck a small pole which fell, striking him on the head, and he died one hour afterwards. George Pugmire was at my shop at the time getting his horses shod. When the news of the accident reached us, he hitched up his team and we went to the canyon and brought the boy down. It was a very sorrowful scene, never to be forgotten.
 The next thing of interest occurred in the spring of 1882, when I was called on a mission to Great Britain. I was telegraphed for by President Joseph F. Smith. I had one day and a half notice to close up my business and start for Salt Lake City. My friends, prior to my departure, gave me a surprise party and we had a very good time together. Many were the congratulations and good wished for my success and safe return.
 On the morning of the third of April, I parted with my family and traveled by stage to Evanston. An accident occurred to the mail coach on the divide between Woodruff and Bear River, which necessitated leaving part of the wagon. There were four of us and we rigged up the front wheels. I sat straddle of the tongue and two of the others on the bolster and a pitiable sight we were when we got to Evanston, covered with mud and frozen stiff as a poker. An accident had occurred on the U.P. Railroad at the critical time and the hotels at Evanston were crowded. A cattle dealer kindly gave me part of his bed. Next day we proceeded to Salt Lake City, and arrived safe and sound. Many were the congratulations of my friends in Salt Lake City. After being set apart with the rest of the brethren going on missions I bid goodbye to my friends, many of whom left a coin in my hand to help me on my journey.
 I traveled on the U.P.R.R.to Omaha. T.W.Horsley, being my companion. We were delayed three days in New York City and took passage on the steamship, Wisconsin. Elder Edwin Spencer of Randolph being my berth companion. We were not many hours out from New York before we were sick, and remained so for two or three days. We then began to feel better and regain our appetites. We arrived in Liverpool, England, after ten days of voyage. Upon landing we were met by some of the brethren from Liverpool and were taken to the office of the Latter-Day-Saints and were assigned to Nottingham Conference.
 Before entering upon my duties, I went to my native place to visit my father, brothers, and sisters, and arrived that same evening at the place of my birth. My folks were very pleased to see me. They had not known of my coming so I took them by surprise. I spent three or four days with them and visited my friends and the old haunts of my childhood. But while they appeared very natural to me, still I had no desire to stay longer with them so I reported to my field of labor and was assigned to the Leicester District of the Nottingham Conference. I had great joy and satisfaction in my labors and succeeded in baptizing some of my relatives.•
 In the spring of 1883 I was moved to the Hucknal District and labored with Brother Edwin Spencer. I baptized while in England forty-nine people. My health while there was very poor; in fact, I scarcely saw a well day. I was permitted to return home at any period of time on account of my ill health by President John Henry Smith who succeeded Albert Carrington. In July a company of Icelanders arrived in Liverpool. The office in Liverpool had not been notified of their coming, so I was called to bring them home to Salt Lake City. I had one and a half days’ notice to get ready and return to Salt Lake City. I visited my relatives and friends and reported to Liverpool; bought a few things for my family and went on board the steamship, Wisconsin, and took charge of this company of Icelanders who could not speak a word of English.
 I being the only returning missionary, it was a very lonesome trip for me, still I was kept very busy attending to the wants of the company. After ten days passage, we arrived in New York on the twenty fourth day of July, 1883. After one day delay in New York we started for Salt Lake City and arrived there on the twenty ninth day of July, all in good health.
