# James McBride Biography

_A story of [[James John McBride (KWJ5-DHB)|James John McBride]]._

## The story as it was written

Tooele Transcript-Bulletin (Tooele, Utah), 15 February 1924, Fri, Page 5
James McBride Biography
I, James McBride, son of Thomas McBride and Catherine John, was born May 9, 1818 in New Lancaster, Fairfield County, Ohio. In April 1820 my father moved to Wayne County, Ohio and leased eighty acres of land on the Red Haw, a branch of the Mohegan river.
In March 1831 two Mormon Elders, Thomas Tripp and Harvey Green, preached the first sermon on the Red Haw in my father’s home soon after my father, mother, and sister Isabella were baptized. In August 1833, we started to emigrate to Missouri, but stopped in Richland County, Ohio. We resumed our journey in the spring of 1834, and arrived in Pike County, Missouri, in June of the same year. In the spring of 1836 we made another move to Ray County Missouri. Here the howling of the mobs was heard on every hand. In September 1836 the family moved to Caldwell County, Missouri, and father filed on an eighty-acre homestead on Shoal Creek, three-fourths of a mile from Haun’s Mill. In Shoal Creek I was baptized in June 1838.
At Haun’s Mill on October 30, 1838 there was a dastardly massacre committed by a disguised mob. It happened just before sunset. The mob consisted of about two hundred forty mounted men, bedecked in feathers and smeared with paint. They were headed by Captains William Jennings and Nehemiah Comstock as they came with a rushing gallop, into the little settlement of Mormons at the Mill. The presiding Elder of the Haun’s Mill branch of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, perceiving their evil intentions raised his hands as a sign of peace. But the mob heeding him not, continued their rushing advance and commenced firing. The stricken and horrified settlers fled, endeavoring to escape and find shelter. There ensued a volley of rapid musketry of many hundred shots that lasted about six minutes. Then there followed a ransacking search. A militiaman named Rogers came up to where my father was lying wounded and helpless, with his gun by his side. Rogers picked up the gun, which was a Kentucky cap and ball mussel loading rifle. Seeing that the gun was loaded, Rogers deliberately discharged it into my dear aged father’s breast. (Father was then sixty-three years of age.) Rogers then picked up an old rusty piece of a hand-mowing scythe, commonly used as a corn cutter, and hacked and cut my father until his arms, hands and body were frightfully mangled. All the while father was holding up his hands, with some of the fingers already cut off, imploring for mercy, but it was in vain.
October 31, 1838 my brother Amos, my brother-in-law James Dayley and others carried the dead bodies on a board, one at a time and dumped them into an adjacent well, about twelve feel deep, and covered them with straw and dirt. My brother Amos turned his back on the well when James Dayley slid father’s corpse off the carrying board into the heart rendering resting place to await the morning of the first resurrection. There were fifteen bodies buried in the one tomb. Three more of the wounded finally died. Some of the dead and some of the wounded were mere boys, as young as eight years old. One of the mob later boasted of killing one on the youngest boy’s saying, “Nits make lice. If he would have lived he would have been a Mormon.”
My brother Amos lived about three miles from the mill. Some time later a mob went and relieved him of his gun and took him from his home in the night. Without allowing him to dress they drove him in his nightshirt and bare feet about a mile and a half into the woods. They tied his arms around a tree, gave him eighteen lashes with a long hickory, cutting blood each lick clear across his back, and then they turned him loose with profane vile threats and with blood trickling down his back.
At a still later date my brother Thomas, while going to the store which was near his home, was seized by a mob. They put a rope around his neck and hung him to the porch. A Mr. Gray happened by just in the nick of time. He jumped thru the crowd and cut the rope saying, “I will not see a human being hung that way.” Thomas, limp and helpless, fell to the ground black in the face. In a few minutes he recovered enough to get home, but died about three weeks later, leaving a wife and child to mourn. There is one probable reason that I was not also mobbed and that is because at the time of the massacre I was sick at home, three-fourths of a mile from Haun’s Mill. I was at that time twenty years of age, but small of stature, puny and sickly. With such experiences as these how could the Mormons be censored for leaving civilization and emigrating to the wilderness of Rocky Mountains, hoping for peace and religious freedom.
February 24, 1839, we left Haun’s Mill bound for Illinois, and settled in Adams County about four miles from Payson. In the spring of 1840 another move was made to Nauvoo, Hancock County, Illinois. Here at Nauvoo my mother died July 27, 1841.
March 7, 1844, I married Olive Mahettable Cheney, daughter of Aaron Cheney and Mahettable Wells. My wife was born May 15, 1817, in Bloomfield, Cattaraugus County, New York. While at Nauvoo, my first son Brigham was born, February 6, 1846. In April 1846 we left Nauvoo going westward. We laid over at Farmington, Iowa and worked until September. Then we moved down the Des Moines River and stopped at Tom’s Mill. While there my eleven months old son Brigham died, leaving Tom’s Mill May, 1847 we moved to Appanoose County, Iowa, and while there a second son, Thomas Aaron, was born July 10, 1848. Here also a third son, Amos Orin, was born January 2, 1850.
On May 17, 1850 we left Appanoose County traveling west. The next stop was at Kanesville (now Council Bluffs.) June 3, 1850 we crossed the Missouri River bound for the Great Basin in the Rocky Mountains, we arrived with our little company in Salt Lake City, October 4, 1850. Six days later we settled at Willow Creek (now Grantsville). One white man living in a wagon-box and herding stock was the only inhabitant of the place at that time. I, my wife and two children, Harrison Severe, his wife and three children composed our company at that time. We built the first house and plowed the first land on Willow Creek and consequently we were the first settlers of Grantsville. We had but little means with which to support our families, and our nearest market was Salt Lake City. During the winter of 1850-1, we made several trips to Salt Lake City, hauling charcoal. By doing this and killing game such as deer, antelope, rabbits, wild fowl, and fishing, we managed to make a bare living for ourselves and families. We did what fencing we could when we were not otherwise employed.
March 20, 1851 the Indians stole and drove off all our cattle but three oxen and one cow. We followed the cattle and Indian tracks out into the Skull Valley and onto the Cedar Mountains sixty miles from home. Strung all along the way we found several our cattle dead. The Indians had killed them, used a little of the meat and left the rest to waste. Being on foot with only two biscuits each and having given up all hope of finding any of our cattle alive, we gave up the chase and returned home.
We moved to Lake View and worked in the canyon getting out house logs and saw logs. We hauled the logs down to the old saw mill (now the Grist Mill) ten miles east of Grantsville, where we got the logs sawed up into lumber, working in this manner were able to make our living as well as buy two hundred eighty dollars- worth of work oxen.
In December 1851 we moved back to Grantsville, five other families came back with us. They were the families of Clark, Baker, Durfee, Seva and Watson, making a total of twenty-six souls in all. A branch of the Church was organized with Benjamin Baker as Presiding Elder. A little later Thomas H. Clark was placed as Presiding Elder.
In the autumn of 1852 Harrison Severe and I thrashed about five-hundred bushels of wheat.
In 1853 twenty-three more families settled in Grantsville, they were: Dayley, Pope, Mecham, Walker, Fairchild, Steel, Bell, two Orr families, Martindale, Barrus, Clark, Blair, Abbot, Martin, Burton, Biemore, Burbank, Sabin, Phippen, Wrathall, Palmer, and Clark, In the autumn of 1853 quite a number more families settled in Grantsville, some of which were: Parkinson, Pea, Lee, McMurray, Matthews, Wilson, Whittle, Hudson and Hale. During the winter of 1853-54 we built a fort thirty rods square to protect our families from the Indians. The walls on the north, west and half of the south sides was built of dried mud. It was five feet thick at the bottom, twelve feet high and eighteen inches thick at the top. The east wall and half of the south was built of 6”x4”x2” adobes with a rock foundation. This part of the wall was three feet thick at the bottom, twelve feet high and eighteen inches thick at the top. The total length of wall built was 120 rods and the average cost per rod was twenty-five dollars, making the total cost of the wall equal to three thousand dollars.
In the summer of 1854 we had an uncommonly dry season and the grasshoppers destroyed a big part of our crops, but the kind hand of providence was manifested and none seriously suffered during the winter. I thrashed two hundred bushels of wheat that season, and refused four dollars a bushel in gold for my wheat to brother Isaac Lee of Tooele. Says I, “Brother Lee, I would be glad to sell you what wheat my own family doesn’t need but I know some of my neighbors haven’t raised their bread. They must have what I can spare. My neighbors got my wheat, and those that were able to pay, paid me two dollars a bushel, others promised to pay and the needy got it free gratis. I divided and kept on dividing until before the next harvest my own family went without bread for three days. Brother John W. Cooley had a small patch of barley ripen first in our neighborhood, and all were welcomed to come and help themselves. The barley was ground in coffee mills and mush was made, one happy day.
The spring and summer of 1855 was a season of much suffering. Greasewood and various kinds of weeds were cooked as greens for food. Roots and segos were eaten to sustain life. Some had milk to season the various preparations with.
Squatters right on homes was the custom until the government surveys were made. After that I filed homestead entry on West 1-2 N.E. 1-4 and E. 1-2 N.W. 1-4 of section 36 in T. S. 2 S. or range 6 W. Salt Lake City, containing 160 acres. All you who are living on or owning property around the old James McBride homestead (now Joshua R. Clark’s home) may know from whence to trace abstract title. Those having squatter’s rights within my homestead received title to their land by only paying for the making out of their deeds. The meeting house lot, the tithing house lot, and old school house lot, the tithing barn lot, and the cemetery enclosure, each and all received title on the same terms.
When Thomas H. Clark was Presiding Elder, Arieh C. Browers and I were branch teachers and A. B. Sabin was branch clerk. In 1856 a ward was organized with William G. Young as Bishop, and A. C. Brower first counselor, and I, James McBride second counselor.
In 1857 the Johnson army scare caused a lot of worry and distress. Their plans were thwarted as a poet wrote of their threats:
We’ll hang the men that’s got two wives,
We’ve got the ropes so handy,
That is we mean to we would have done,
But Smith burned them on Sandy.
Their plans being thwarted, the army instead of arriving in the valley, wintered at Fort Bridger, suffering much from the cold winter of 1857-58. In the spring of 1858, thru the fear of the arrival of Johnson and his army the saints started to move south, leaving enough men to fire the homes in case the army did arrive, and then the army would find only destruction and no Mormons to hang.
The kind hand of providence was again made manifest when a compromise was affected between the leaders of the Church and the army officers. The Saints returned to their homes in July 1858, finding flourishing crops. The army arrived and passed thru Salt Lake City, while they were in the city a concourse of children sang: “Many days we have lingered around our cabin door. O, hard times come again no more, etc.” It melted the heart of Colonel Johnson to hear the song and note, the general appearance of the innocent urchins. The army crossed the Jordan and established winter quarters in Cedar Valley which was called Camp Floyd.
During the winter of 1858-59 the soldiers brought mules and oxen to Grantsville to feed. They paid us forty dollars a ton for hay, and one dollar per cubic yard for straw. In turn they sold us provisions, blankets, clothing and in the spring they sold us mules and wagons cheap. Shall it again be said that the overruling hand of the true and living God was near? The stony hearts within an army softened. The soldiers were kind, sociable, friendly, and liberal in measurements and weights. In the auction of mules, oxen, wagons and equipment, they did not persistently urge for higher bids.
September 5, 1852, my son James O. McBride was born in Grantsville. September 18, 1854, my son Elam W. McBride was born. November 7, 1856, I married Marian Louisa Redden. She was born in McLaws County, Illinois, August 31, 1841. May 16, 1857, my son Heber K. McBride was born. February 10, 1859 Olive D. McBride (my first daughter by my second marriage) was born. November 20, 1862, my daughter Catherine M. McBride was born. She died December 27, 1862. September 22, 1864, my daughter Arminta Malinda was born. July 30, 1866, my son Joseph J. McBride. March 4, 1869, my son Hyrum W. McBride was born. October 25, 1875, I married Christina C. Johnson. She was born January 8, 1830.
My family consisted of three wives, Olive M. Cheney, Marian L. Redden and Christina C. Johnson, all dead, eight sons and four daughters, Brigham died at Tom’s Mill, Iowa, T. Aaron is living in Oakley, Idaho. Amos O. is living in Grantsville, Utah. James Orson is living in Burley Idaho. Elam W. is living at Oakley, Idaho. Heber died in Burley, Idaho. Olive D. wife of Owen H. Barrus, died in Grantsville, Utah. Dorcas L. wife of Charles L. Jenkins is living in Oakley, Idaho. A. Malinda is the wife of John Benson, Burley, Idaho. Joseph is living in Twin Falls, Idaho. And Hyrum W. is living in Carlin, Nevada.
The family and posterity of James McBride, January 1, 1924 numbered two hundred seventy-five souls, dead and alive.
I am now (April 4, 1876) in my 58th year. I have lived in peace in Grantsville. Every effort made by the enemies of this people to destroy them has been unsuccessful. I feel to acknowledge the hand of God in all things, and bear testimony of the truth of the Gospel of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.
James McBride died June 1, 1881, and is buried in the Grantsville Cemetery.
