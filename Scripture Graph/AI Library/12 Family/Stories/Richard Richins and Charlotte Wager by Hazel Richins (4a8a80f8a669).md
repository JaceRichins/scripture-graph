# Richard Richins and Charlotte Wager by Hazel Richins

_A story of [[Charlotte Priscilla Wager (LLQX-X94)|Charlotte Priscilla Wager]], [[Richard Richins (LLQX-X98)|Richard Richins]]._

## The story as it was written

RICHARD RICHINS AND CHARLOTTE PRISCILLA WAGER
RICHARD (MARCH 26, 1800 - NOVEMBER 8, 1848)
CHARLOTTE ( JANUARY 27, 1799 - SEPTEMBER 21, 1842)
Richard was the oldest son of Henry Richins and Sarah Haynes. His older sisters, were Sarah and Elizabeth. Born after him was a sister, Meriam, and three younger brothers, Henry, William and Joseph.
Like most parents, Henry and Sarah wanted a son to carry on the family name and were delighted when their third child was a boy. They took him to the Painswick Church where he was baptized and given the name of Richard. They were members of the Church of England and belonged to the Parish of Painswick, which included the village of Sheepcombe in Goucestershire England. This church believed and practiced infant baptism. The Vicar performed the baptism by sprinkling the baby's head with the water from the font, and givin.g him the name the parents had chosen. The date recorded in the Parish Register and Christening, was 26 March 1800.
Richards father Henry, the youngest son in his family, did not have the opportunity to attend school. He could not read or write. This limited him to manual labor, while his older brother, Thomas, who attend school was able to become an Overseer. (One who supervises or manages an estate or large farm). Henry knowing the value of schooling, apparently sent Richard to school; as I could find no place where he signed his name by marking an x.
Richard lived with his parents on a large estate called Ebworth, where his father was employed as a farm laborer. Ebworth was located on the northern part of Sheepcombe. Although Henry and Sarah had a struggle to raise seven children on his meager wage, the children had happy memories of their home and childhood. The country was beautiful with green hills and meadows covered with wild flowers. There were woods and streams where Henry and his sons spent happy hours hunting and fishing. They lived in a cottage that was built to lease to the farm workers. There was a garden spot and the commons where they could pasture a few cattle and sheep. Their home was next to the game park.
Henry VIII often came to hunt at the Sheepcombe forest in his day, but over the years, many of the trees had been cut and farmland replaced part of the forest. However a large game park had been preserved for hunting, and the guests there were often the Royal Family and numerous other gentlemen. The game park was jealously
guarded by a game keeper to keep out the public and protect the game from the poachers. The poaching had increased during the depression as the unemployed sought food for the family. This caused strict laws to be passed. The game keeper was allowed to shoot a poacher if caught in the act. Most preferred to turn them over for the law to prosecute. Poachers faced a fine as well as a prison term, which only brought hardship to their family. Some of the more compassionate Gentry would allow the poor to take rabbits from their game park.
It was an exciting time at Ebworth when the Gentry's carriages began to arrive for the fox hunt. Although the people could not hunt, they enjoy watching along the stone wall and hedge, which separated the Park from the fields. They could hear the dogs barking at the hunting lodge, anxious to get started. When they heard the bugle sound, they knew the hunt had begun. First to come into view, were the braying dogs on the scent of the fleeing fox, followed by the hunters, in their red caps and coats, sailing their horses over logs, stone walls, and hedges in order to keep up with the dogs and fox. When they had passed out of sight and the barking of the dogs ,grew faint the people returned to their work.
The Richins boys were not doubt excited by these fox hunts. It could have been the topic of their conversation for weeks. They may have speculated on how to hunt without being caught. Their parents would have warned them to stay out of the game park, but the warning probably sounded on deaf ears as they began to formulate a plan. Snaring a few rabbits was tame in comparison. They would have to plan a hunt when the dogs were locked in their pens, as a pack of barking dogs would surely give them away.
Sometimes their plans probably worked and other times it was a case of outrunning a winded game keeper or barely escaping through a small opening in the hedge, marked previously for such an occasion. Once safe from the game keeper's grasp and out of sound of his threats and warnings, they could laugh and enjoyed the challenge. Richard being the oldest was probably the ring leader. Henry was two years younger and William six years younger. Then there was Joseph, ten years younger and probably too young to join them. In their day there were to few opportunities to hunt and be mischievous boys because children were part of the labor force. Even small children were hired for a mere nothing to scare birds out of the gardens and orchards.
Richard was still in his teens when he married Charlotte Priscilla Wager, a daughter of John Wager and Charlotte Gill.
She was about 18, having been christened, 27 January 1799. Richard was about 17. Both were under age to be married without their parents consent. They apparently gained it, for after the vows were read, they were married. 28 October 1817, by Rev. Charles Neville at the Painswick Church. Samuel Holiday and Elizabeth Holberry witnessed the marriage.
Richard and Priscilla chose to live in Sheepcombe at Ebworth Park. Their home was next to Priscilla's mother, Charlotte Wager, according to the 1814 census.
Trying to discover Richard's trade, is like putting an old puzzled together with many pieces missing and other pieces that don't seem to fit into the picture. I searched the parish registers which often listed the father's trade when the child was christened. For the first four children he was listed as a laborer. This is such a broad term, he could be in any trade. For instance, his brother Joseph was listed as a laborer, while the census stated he was a foreman of the thrashing crew. Checking further, I found Richard was recorded to be a shearman in 1826. A shearman could have been someone who sheared sheep, or sheared metal to fit the curves and angles of machinery. So did Richard shear sheep or metal? To add to the confusion, when his son Edwin, was christened in 1830, he was listed as a hallier. An old English dictionary says a hallier was one who stayed at the hall where agriculture was taught.
I had notice that Richard's cousin, William Richins, had been listed as a hallier in 1825 and later that year the Vicar had listed him as an overseer, (one who supervises or manages an estate or large farm). William's father, Thomas, had been an overseer. Did Richard go to Oxford to become an overseer like his cousin and Uncle Thomas Richins? Maybe he did.
Then I found in another book that a hallier was one who hauled goods. I went back to the dictionary to see if hallier was a synonym under haul, or listed as an adjective, but I found hauler was one who hauled goods, but no hallier. So even though they sound alike, they are two different words: Hallier; One who stayed at a hall at Oxford and Hauler; one who hauls goods. The only question is, which one did the clerk mean to record? Did Richard haul goods or go to Oxford? Since there is a tradition that one of Henry's sons went to Oxford it could have been Richard. I then was anxious to see what the next christening listed. He was back to laborer. The 1841 census stated he was an Agriculture Laborer. His son's marriages listed him a laborer. Priscilla's death certificate and his own stated he was a laborer.
What happened to Richard's schooling, if he did go? Perhaps the answer lies in the cause of his death. He died of intemperance one given to excessive use of intoxicants. England, at this time, was having serious problems with intemperance, as they called it, but it was inevitable. There was no restrictions on drinking and no knowledge of it's danger. Even children and babies were given beer when the well water became distasteful from pollution. The mills were having problems with workers becoming so drunk they had to shut the mills down to end the fighting and riots. The farm workers were served beer during the rest period and the cash taken from their pay. A law was finally passed to prohibit serving beer during rest period because the laborers became too drunk to work and were drinking up all their pay leaving their families destitute. Also, Priscilla and Richard were young when they became parents. They were faced with heartache and sorrow. Did these lead him to drink?
Their first child was their daughter, Hannah. She was christened, 18 July 1819. At age 18 she became the victim of the dread disease, consumption, known now as tuberculosis. They did not know the cause or cure at that time. Some who contacted the disease, did not become ill, but were carriers and gave the disease to others. The illness could be transmit through the milk of an infected cow. One fifth of all the deaths in England was from consumption. The tuberculous could form in the lungs, bones or brain. It was very stressful when it reached the brain. Sufferers became delirious, crying out in pain and their body jerked and thrust around until death brought relief and peace. Richard was present when Hannah died. They buried her 2 March 1838.
Their second child was a boy. Delighted with a son, they took him to be christened, 20 ~arch 1821. They chose the name of John Edward in honor of Richards ancestors his great grandfather John Richins and his grandfather Edward Haynes. He was not given time to honor their names, as he lived only two months on earth before he was called back to his Heavenly abode. They buried their infant son, July 1821.
Their third child was a daughter, christened, Mary Ann. Her allotted time on earth was 16 months, time enough to endear her to her parents. They tearfully placed her body in mother earth, 13 April 1824.
With anxiety and fear they awaited the birth of their fourth child and hoped it could stay to fill their empty arms. He arrived in the Winter of 1824 or 1825. They took him to be christened on 23 January 1825. He was given the name of George Thomas. He survived his infant years and was employed before he was fifteen. He went to lodge at his relatives, Jeremiah and Esther Wager, who had a son, Jeremiah, near George's age and also a daughter Mary. He probably stayed with them until his marriage. We have not been able, (as of 1991), to find his marriage date, but we know he married Ann Pulum as her name appeared on the birth certificates of their daughters, Hannah and Mary Ann. They were perhaps named in honor of George's older sisters that died.
George was listed as a laborer and they were living at New .Town, Barton St. Mary, Gloucestershire, England. George and Ann had three children born in England, then in 1856 they emigrated to Canada. In those days, it meant they wouldn't see George Thomas again. It was
almost like death to a family. The depression during 1850 1860 caused many people to leave England because many were out of work and could not find any in England, so they migrated to other countries, especially the United States and Canada. It was also during those years that many of the converts to the Mormon Church left England to gather with the saints in Utah. George and Ann had another child in Canada in 1858. Ann died in 1859 and George then married Mary Bulgar. He died 25 February 1899. There was no record of their being baptized, so their names were submitted to the temple to have their work done and sealed as a family. On the 4 October 1934, George was sealed to Richard and Priscilla in the Salt Lake Temple.
There is a tradition that child number five was a twin. The male twin was born 6 June 1826, and was christened five months later on 26 November 1826. He was given the name of Richard's grandfather, Thomas Richins. The reported other twin was a girl the family called Charlotte Prisiclla. There is no record in the Parish Register of her birth or death. She is said to have died at birth.
It was at Thomas's christening that Richard was listed as a shearman. In the 1841 census, Thomas was 15 years and living at home. He was still living at Sheepcombe when he was taught the gospel and was baptized at age 24. He was baptized, 1 January 1850, by Elder William Davis. In the 1851 census he was listed as unmarried, age 24, and an agricultural laborer, and visiting at his Uncle William's home. Three years later, we find him in the city of Geoucester awaiting the reading of the banns, so that he could marry Harriet Deveraux, a daughter of John Deveraux and Esther Cockshed. They were married at the Saint James Church by the Rev. James Hollins, 15 December 1853, Harriet's mother Esther Deveraux and Jeremiah Wager were witnesses of the marriage.
Harriet's parents had belonged to the United Brethren congregation in Hereford. They had become dissatisfied with the teachings of the Weslyan Methodist Church and some six hundred had organized the United Brethren Church so they could teach and live the gospel as the Savior had taught it. They prayed for guidance.
WilfordWoodruff was teaching the gospel in Staffordshire and was successful in baptizing many people. The coming week was full of appointments for baptism. When he arose to speak at the city hall, the Spirit directed him to go south. He bid farewell to his astonished audience and left the baptism in the hands of his fellow missionaries and traveled south to Hereford. There he found the United Brethren congregation and baptized all but one member. Harriet's parents were baptized, 30 July 1846, by William Webb and confirmed the same day by Elder Thomas Price.
When Thomas was married he was listed as a waterman, which referred to one who traveled the water ways on a barge. When they returned to Harriets home town of Eastnor in Herefordshire, he was an agricultrual laborer again. Their first child was born there in 1855. In 1856 they joined the Deveraux family and a group of Saints to come to Zion. They stayed in Salt Lake City until 1861 where they were sent to Goshen to settle. (See Life Story of Thomas Richins and Harriet Deveraux, Published by Richins Surname Association).
Richard and Priscilla's next child was their son Charles. He was born, 17 August 1828. There was no record of his christening. There seemed to be some confusion to their records, at this time. Rev. Neville had become ill and could no longer carry on the work of Vicar. He was replaced by the Rev. Ostreham. There were a few scattered entries in Painswick and some in Sheepcombe and some missing or not kept.
Charles, like most of Richard's sons, received some schooling and was able to read and write. He was living at home in 1841 at the age 12. The following year, 1842, his mother died. It is not known if Richard kept the older boys, with the help of his mother, Sarah and mother in law, or if they were dispersed to different relatives at that time. Charles was living in Sheepcombe when he heard the gospel and was converted. The Branch Membership Records listed him from Sheepcombe, when he was baptized, 26 December 1849. By Charles Shill
and confirmed the same day by Elder Charles Blackwell. He was the first Richins to be converted and like many converts, was anxious to share the truth with his family and loved ones. He was ordained a Priest in the Aaronic Priesthood, 16 June 1850, and had the privilege of baptizing his cousins Leonard Richins and Peter Richins on 7 July 1850. They were confirmed by Elder Charles Blackwell. He was later ordained to the Melchizedick Priesthood and could then confirm and preside in the Branch. He married Louisa Shill, 27 January 1851, at Syde Parish Church, with Rev. W. Moore performing the ceremony and Leonard Richins and Louisa Williams as witnesses. He was listed as a laborer; on the birth of their first child Hannah Louisa, 9 September 1852, he was listed as a husbandman.
Charles was not only the first Richins to be baptized but also the first to immigrate to Utah. He arrived in Salt Lake City, 10 October 1853. He was a help to those who came later. In 1860 he was asked by President Brigham Young to help colonize Henneferville on the Weber River and later sent to colonize in Mexico. He married in polygamy two more wives, Esther Stowe Ovard and Agnes Mary Willmont. He was the father of 30 children. (See Life Stories Of Charles, Louisa, Esther and Agnes Richins, Published by Richins Surname Association)
Richard and Priscilla's next child was also a boy. He was christened, 16 May 1830, and given the name of Edwin. It was at his christening, that Richard's occupation was given as a hallier. Edwin only lived 10 months and was buried, 10 March 1831. Was it Edwin's birth and death, that ended Richard's days a hallier? Or was it from his drinking problem? At the next christening Richard was once again named as a labourer.
The next child was their son, John. We have not found his christening and there is some confusion on the day of his birth. The membership records give the date of, 25 March 1833, but the family Bible has, 20 March 1833. In the census of 1841, John was listed as age 9, living at home with his parents. His mother died the following year and he and his younger brother, Edward went to live with his relative, Esther Wager, who at that time a widow. Her son, Jeremiah, was living at home and like John was classed as an agriculture laborer. The 1851 census also lists John and Edward as agriculture laborers. John was 19 and Edward was 16. Their older brother George had also stayed there years before.
Three years later John was at Brimsfield to marry Charlotte Taylor, daughter of Edward Taylor and Ann Nichols. He had not reached his 21st. birthday, and Charlotte was only 19, so they needed the consent of Charlotte's parents. They were married at Brimsfie1d Church, 18 May 1853, by the Rector W. Moore. The marriage was witnessed by Charlotte's brother, George Taylor, and Louisa Gyther, whom George later married. Charlotte was a member of the LDS
Church and John was taught the gospel and received a witness that it was the true church. He was baptized by his brother in law, George Taylor, 5 February 1854. They had a daughter, Hannah Louisa, born in 1854 at Foston Ash in Cranham. John was then a husbandman. In 1856 they joined the Taylor Family and a group of Saints and set sail on the S.S. Thorton for New York and Zion. They stayed in Salt Lake until 1861 when they were sent to Goshers to settle. ( See parts of EIRoy Nelson Family History, Copy in possession of Richins Surname Association).
Edward was Richard's and Priscilla's next child. He was born in April 1834. There is a discrepancy in the day. The obituary and endowment records have 25 April, his history has the 16 April. He was christened, 24 August 1834. He was about seven when his mother died and he and his older brother John went to the Wager relatives. In 1851 census they were with Esther and her son Jeremiah. He had received some schooling as he could read and write. He was about 19
when he was taught the gospel. He was baptized, 28 February 1853, by George Humphries who then confirmed him on 3 March 1853. After he joined the church, he had a desire to gather with the saints in Utah and his brothers in Zion. He worked and saved his money until he was able to pay his passage on the ship, "Charles Banks", It docked at Boston and he joined the William Cooper emigrant train and arrived in Salt Lake 7 November 1855. He settled in the Echo Henefer Area. (See Life Stories of Edward, Caroline Ellen and Sarah Richins. Published by Richins Surname Association)
Six years after Edwards birth, the last of Richard and Priscilla's children arrived. He was born, 18 May 1840. He was christened, 28 June 1840, and given the name William. He had ,just
passed his second birthday when his mother died. Who cared for him after her death is not known.
William died of consumption on 4 May 1849, when nine years old. Died at the home of J. Richins, who was present at his death. There was at that time three Richins whose name started with J. John, William's brother, but he was with his brother Edward at the Wager home and to young so that eliminated him. There was Joseph, the eldest son of William, but he also was to young and unmarried when Priscilla died. This left Joseph Richins, a brother of Richard. He had married Ann Davis in 1829. Their first son had died. There were three daughters, Maria 10, Juliann 7, and Ellen 6, and two sons, Mark 5 and Peter who was the same age as William. Joseph apparently took William to raise with Peter.
Joseph's wife, Ann, died of complications of child birth just four months prior to Williams death. Maria, their eldest daughter, took over the care of the motherless family. She was present when the baby died. Joseph was present when his nephew, William, died of Pulmonary Consumption.
Only five of Richard's and Priscila's eleven children lived to be married. They had lost three or possibly four infant children, plus William at age 9 and Hannah at age 18. Though they had married young they only had 25 years together. Both died young. Priscilla was only 43, when she died, 21 September 1842, of complication of child birth. She was buried four days later on 25 September. Richard died of intemperance at his mothers home, 8 December 1848. It may be that it was only after Priscilla's death that Richard began to have a serious drinking problem. It was a year after Priscilla died that he lost his father also with gangrene of his leg. Was it after his father's death that he went to live with his mother seeking comfort from each other?
When I began to write the history of Richard and Priscilla there seemed only bits of information and no clear picture of their lives As I collected information from different sources and meditated and prayed that I would give correct information and tried to make them a human family and not just statistics. Personalities began to emerge and they became people with strength and weakness. To me they were real people with problems and adversities, but with a strong love that bound them together as a family.
I visualized Richard as a young boy full of mischief, who enjoyed a challenge and who somewhat like David of old, went to meet the challenge with a few pebbles and sling. Perhaps he was a little immature in that he did not weigh the cost of his daring actions. He married before he reached maturity. He was perhaps moved by his emotions and love, as most teenagers are, than the responsibility of marriage.
I feel that there emerged a loving, tender hearted man. Even if he drank there was not a complaint or whisper of abuse or cruelty. He was there to give his daughter comfort in her dying hour. He was at Priscilla's side when she was suffering severe spasms. I picture him holding her in his arms to try to quiet and comfort her. I can see his sorrow as the dividing of his family after her death. Depressed and lonely he sought forgetfulness in drinking.
As I visualize Priscilla. I see her as a quiet girl who also gave stability to the family. I feel to weep for her as she gave birth to precious infant but was left with empty arms. And her heartache as she watched her only surviving daughter develop the dread and fatal disease of consumption.
I visualized her fears and anxiety when her own health began to fail. Who would care for her boys and especially little William? I feel that she was able to look down from her Heavenly abode, with pride and joy as her sons grew to maturity and kept the family united with love and concern.
As I have read the histories of these sons: Thomas, Charles, Edward and John, the spirit brought to my mind the words of the Savior, "By their fruits ye shall know them." Richard and Priscilla brought forth choice and faithful servants of our Heavenly Father. They gave great strength to the church in the early precarious, times when many of the elect fell away. They must have had good teachings in the home, including the commandment, to love their fellowman. How right it is to have them sealed as an eternal family and their names recorded in the holy temples.
