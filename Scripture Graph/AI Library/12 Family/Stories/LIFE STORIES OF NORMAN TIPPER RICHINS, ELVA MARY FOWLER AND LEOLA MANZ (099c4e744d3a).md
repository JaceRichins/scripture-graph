# LIFE STORIES OF NORMAN TIPPER RICHINS, ELVA MARY FOWLER AND LEOLA MANZEL FARR

_A story of [[Norman Tipper Richins (KWC8-FFB)|Norman Tipper Richins]]._

## The story as it was written

NORMAN'S LOVE FOR HIS PARENTS
When I think of my Father I think of the honesty and the devotion he stood for and the love he had for his wife and his children. When I think of my mother I think of her kindness and the love which she contained within her soul and her desire to raise her family uprightly before the Lord. I think of both of them keeping the commandments of their Father in Heaven and wanting their family to do the same. They were a wonderful light and guide to me and I loved them very much." Theses words, taken from a tape recording made in 1980, express the deep feelings Norman had for his parents. He has always been proud of his heritage. He remembers well when he was a young boy and was racing his horse a few blocks away by Fawcett's and the horse ran into a post and threw him to the ground knocking him out. His mother saw the accident from her home and ran to his aidp carrying him home on her back. When he was 31 his mother passed away. He was working on the state road down below Morgan when they gave him the news and he said, "I will never forget it."
While a young boy he stayed with his mother's mother, Esther Ovard Richins and helped her. He said that at night he would often wake up and go from the cot on which he slept to her bedroom to make sure she was all right. "Oh, how I loved her." he said, she was one of Charles Richins’ plural wives and was left to raise her family alone much of the time. He said she never complained or said anything negative about the other two wives. "My father was good to her," Norman said, "and they thought a lot of each other. She died in our home.", This early training in his home was no doubt a contributing factor to his own concern and love for others both in his family and out of his family. He is truly an example of a Christ like person who has shown love and concern for his fellowmen throughout his life.
NORMAN'S CHILDHOOD AND EARLY LIFE
Norman was born April 3, 1905, at his parents home in Henefer, the eigth son born to them. Three brothers died young: Alma, Archie, and Grant. Living older brothers were Hillman, Jewell, Carl, and Clifford. Born after him in order were Reed, Afton, and then two sisters, Esther, and Pearl. Norman grew up in a family of eleven children including Glen and Dale, nephews, who were raised in their family. The home of Alma and Hattie was a happy home. It was filled with excitement of family, relatives and friends. Seldom did the lights go out before midnight.
With such a large family of boys Hattie had a lot of work to do. There was cooking, washing, ironing and house cleaning. The boys were expected to help out. Norman remembers getting up early before he went to elementary school to help his mother with the washing. He pushed the handle back and forth on the washing machine to clean the clothes. He turned the cream separator in the kitchen and then worked the handle of the churn to make butter from cream. There was no electricity at that time and no inside bathroom facilities. The outhouse was almost twenty yards from the house with a sears catalog, the forerunner of toilet tissue. Baths were only on Saturday nights and took place in a large round tub filled part way with warm water heated on a coal stove. Two or three of the children took turns using the same water and then it was dumped out and filled for the next round.
The big home was heated by wood or coal burning stoves. Leaving the rooms near the stoves hot and the ones far away cold. In winter time the water often froze up. Alma spent a lot of his time during the winter with a blowtorch under his house trying to thaw out the water pipes.
They raised their own pigs and then butchered them. They cured the hams with Morton Salt and then put them deep in the wheat bin to preserve them for summer use. Their refrigeration was an icebox filled with blocks of ice cut from the river in wintertime. It was placed in the ice shed and covered with coal slack so it would not melt and could be used in the summer. Norman remembers accompanying his uncle Noble with teams and wagons to section seven on the divide for wood. After the first good snow they took teams and wagons to Coalville for the winters coal. They took wheat to the Echo mill and return with flour, which was stored in the grainery on a hanging loft so mice couldn't get to it.
The boy's bedrooms were upstairs. There were three small rooms and the beds were always filled with the boys or their relatives and friends. Dallas Richins from Echo, a cousin to the boys, spent many nights there. He was a wrestler and was big and rough. At times it sounded like the top floor of the house was going to fall in. They did break the beds. Norman said, "I don't know haw my mother ever stood it. I never remember her getting out of patience or whipping me, although maybe I needed it several times." Norman said, "I don't think my father ever whipped me. One time I remember being in front of the house and saying something I shouldn't have and I don't even remember what it was but it upset my Father. He said something to me and I said, 'catch me if you can' and I started running toward the fence and as I started over the fence his foot booted me in the seat of the pants and lifted me right over the fence onto the other side."
Alma and Hattie raised their children with faith in God and devotion to the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter day Saints. Norman was blessed on May 7, 1905 by John Francis. He was baptized July 6, 1915 by Ephraim Taylor and confirmed that same day by his grandfather, Edward Richins. The only ordinance his father Alma performed for him was ordaining him a deacon on April 22, 1917. He was ordained a teacher by W. Earl Calderwood, when he was 17, on January 28, 1923. Five months later on May 27, 1923 his uncle Parley T. Richins ordained him a priest.
Alma and Hattie had a close knit family. They had family prayer in the morning and often met as a family to talk over and solve problems. Alma was second counselor in the bishopric and later he served on the high council. To fulfill his high council assignment he hitched up his team on a buggy and drove to Kamas for his meetings on Sundays. This left Hattie with the responsibility of getting the children ready and to their meetings each Sabbath. Norman said, "My father made it so his boys wouldn't have to work on Sunday if at all possible. He wanted them to go to church." Our Sunday afternoons one of their favorite activities was hitching up the team and taking the children to Echo to socialize with their cousins, the Heber Richins family.
On special occasions they often got the five gallon ice cream freezer out and made ice cream. The house was always filled with people at Christmas time. Norman remembers getting a pair of skies for Christmas and breaking one of them that very day. Another time he got a new sleigh and when he tried it out the runners went through the crust of snow and he slide off. The front of it and took the skin off his nose and face.
Norman didn't engage in sports activities much outside of school athletics. When a young boy he recalls going to the corner store and watchingtheolder boys play marbles and then playing marbles himself when he got older. He enjoyed the fourth of July celebrations at the town square next to their home. They had a parade, ballgames and fire works. The boys enjoyed frightening the girls with fire crackers.
In his younger days Alma was quite a muscian. He played in a band and entertained those at home. He could play the cornet, concertina, accordian, harmonica, the juis harp, and almost any instrument he picked up. Hattie played the piano so they encouraged their children to develop their musical talents~, and many of them did. Norman played the trombone. one night when the family was living in Salt Lake he went into a big long closet to practice his trombone so he wouldn't bother his grandmother Esther. who was living with them. His sister Esther said they heard a few notes and then it was quiet. They opened the door and he was sound asleep.
Other than dances in the old Henefer Amusement Hall, Alma and Hattie seldom went out for recreation. Norman said, "Father was always too busy. I don't remember him coming to any of my basketball games; although he loved sports and played baseball when he was younger." One place they did take their children was to the circus. Whenever it came to Ogden or Salt Lake they gathered the children, and later grandchildren, together and took them to the circus. Alma also enjoyed wrestling and Norman remembers his Father taking Carl, Clifford, and him to Salt Lake to watch "Strangler Lewis, and Ira Durn."
NORMAN'S HIGH SCHOOL YEARS AND COLLEGE YEARS
In 1921 Alma and Hattie spent the winter living in the Edward Richins home in Salt Lake so they would be close to the sheep on the Desert. Norman and most of the other children lived there with them. Norman said he walked to school at East High but, "I never did like it there because all the teachers were old maids. I made it through alright, but was glad I did not have to continue my schooling there." After that year he went to North Summit in Coalville.
'We had a good time while going to high school. Maybe we could have done better in school had we studied more and fooled less." Norman said in commenting about his high school experiences. He loved to go to Boyden's Drug store for banana splits. He took the lead in the school opera, "Prince of Hawaii" and sang, "Asleep in the Deep" and "Bells of the Sea," songs he has sung many times since. He was in the glee club at North Summit. While attending LDS Business College he went on a tour into Idaho with a singing group. He enjoyed the high school dances but didn't like to dance with the same partner all night. He said, "I used to dance with the girls who weren't asked by others to dance." He has never forgotten one junior prom when he went for his date she had an a dress which left her shoulders bare. He was shocked when he went to get her. He said, "I didn't feel good being with a girl dressed as she was but I took her to the dance and danced the first dance with her and then didn't stay around her until after the last dance when I took her home."
Evidence of the integrity and character of Norman is shown in the fact that when he was a senior he was hired to drive the school but and was paid $25.00 a month. He was dating Beatrice Roberts who lived in Coalville, and one time he took her home part way home on the bus and then stopped and walked her two blocks home while those on the bus waited for him to return and drive them to Henefer.
One of his greatest interest at school was sports. He threw the shot put further than anyone else when he was a senior. He also tried to run the two mile race but got a pain in his side and only made it part way. His best sport was basketball. He played center on the first team. He said there was a lot of competition between North Summit, Morgan, and Park City. When North Summit and Park City played there was always contention and four or five sheriffs in attendance. One time Park City brought a casket to Coalville and put it in the gym with the words, We'll bury you" on it. Another time they brought a goat and led it around with a sign saying, "We've got your goat.' it worked as they beat North Summit by one point. When they went to Woodruff to play basketball the snow was so deep they could only go by bus to Evanston and then by train and sleigh from the to Woodruff. He said it was so cold they ran behind the sleigh on the way home to keep warm.
After graduating from high school Norman's folks wanted him to go to the LDS Business College in Salt Lake. They had offered to send him East to continue with his singing but he said, "I didn't go because I thought there was nothing in this world like those sheep." He did continue his voice lessons while attending the LDS Business College and even sang in the Tabernacle Choir one winter but it was the sheep that ended his college career. The spring he was living in Salt Lake attending college his father called him and said, "Norman I want you to go get you some overalls and a coat and catch the bus and go right out on the Desert to Dell. There has been a blizzard and the sheep are on the highway." He immediately fulfilled his father's wish and went out to look for the sheep. He found some of them on Cedar Mountain and he and the herder were able to round them up and get them back on the allotment. He never returned to college after that spring.
NORMAN'S WORK WITH THE SHEEP
Norman's father became a successful livestock man. He worked hard to obtain success. He spent a life time increasing his flo, and his lands so there would be something for his children. He was never out of debt until shortly before his death at age 86. His wife and family always supported him and helped him every way they could. He spent hours and hours away from home in his effort to provide for his family. Norman said, "he never complained. He was always happy, and I really enjoyed being with him. I was happy when I was working with my father."
Near the family home was a four bent barn, which also housed two or three teams of horses. They were used to mow the hay and bind the grain and pull the wagon's to haul the hay and grain during the summer. They were also needed for the push rakes and other work on the farm. In addition there were always two or three teams with the sheep so the camps could be moved and so someone could go for supplies when needed. During the winter some of the teams were used to pull the sleigh's to haul hay and straw from the stacks and the seven bent barn over the meadow to the cattle in the meadow. They also had good saddle horses around the yard at home for other use as well as for use with the sheep. Alma was forever trading and buying horses.
In addition to sheep they ran cattle. There was always the job of taking the bulls up to the cattle range in Echo canyon. They didn't sell calves but kept them until they were two or three years old. A long days work was a ride on horse back from Henefer to Saw mill Bench to cut out the steers and bring them back the same day so they could be taken to market. Norman says their father liked to get the boys in the corral with the cattle at branding time and watch as some of the mean cows put them scampering over the fence. "That tickled him," he said.
They did some dry farming west of Henefer. Hillman usually cut the wheat with three teams on the header and they stacked it until time for threshing. When threshing day came around It was a big day and Hattie cooked dinner for all the men. There were also some dairy cows that had to be milked and taken to the pasture on the peaks or the meadow so there wasn't a lack of work to do around the home.
While growing up Norman went with the sheep a lot. He use to lamb with Bert Stephens and Walt Grey. "I think I spent more time on the divide lambing than any of my brothers," he said. "I can never remember my father paying me a wage and I worked for him a lot. He always had money for me when I needed it to go to a show or a dance. He would dig down into his little round purse and give me enough money for the night." One year he said, "Norman.1 want you to select 75 ewe lambs from the herd and mark them as your own," and I did. That's how I got started in the sheep business.
He remembers first pulling camp for Hyrum Harris on the divide. They stayed in a tent that spring and he remembers that there was a lot of snow. He spent the next spring with Met Dawson. He used to help trail to the forest reserve. They went up Echo Canyon as far as Wahsatch and then up Yellow Creek. It took two weeks. It took two weeks. Norman said he spent several summers pulling camp on the Reserve before he started herding there after he was married. When he didn't go on the Reserve he went to Cottonwood. "One summer," he said, "I pulled two camps. I had a team of horses and a little two wheel cart. I would leave Henefer with sheep salt and supplies and it took me an entire day to get to the sheep camp. I went up Pine Canyon and around the head of Cottonwood and down the other side. I would stay a week or two and then return home for more supplies and head back again. One time he and Clifford left Henefer at 12 O’clock at night for Cotton wood. The herder's wife had died in Grantsville and they left to tell him as soon as they received news from his family. They rode their horses up Folley Ridge and when they got to Bybee they had to light a match to see which way the forks in the road went. They made it to camp okay and got the man down and on his way to Grantsville the next morning. One time when Norman was lambing sheep in Cottonwood he was trying to catch a sheep from off his horse. The horse was running and the hook in his hands hit the ground and the other end hit his chest. He said the force flipped him right off the horse and if he had not had bib overalls on the hook would have gone into him and injuried him. After that experience he always warned his boys about using a sheep hook from a horse.
Norman probably had more memorable experiences with the sheep on the Desert than any other place. He said, "I made many trips with my Father to the Desert. We used to have the southern desert, and there was a woman by the name of May Laird who lived there in a little shack by herself. The only animals she had were some pigs to kill the rattlesnakes that came around. She was very good to the sheepherders. Father and I stayed there one night and I remember it was very cold." He made trips to the desert with his uncle Jared and Uncle Heber. One fall he went with Jared to get the bucks. They gathered them up from Jared's, Heb's and Alma's herds and drove them to Faust where they loaded them on the train. Jared had an old rio truck that he drove. He made a bed in the back on planks for them to sleep on. Norman said, "Uncle Jared was never in a hurry. I drove the bucks and he went ahead and stopped and prepared something for us to eat." one time when he went with his Uncle Heber they went to Provo and then Delta to the southern desert. They were in an old dodge truck and they got stuck. Heb never got excited. He put his hands In the bibs of his overalls and said, "I'll bet they are getting hell on Wahsatch." That was his way of saying things are worse else where so why worry. He was really a fine man and I enjoyed being with him very much.
Norman told of the time after they divided up the Bureau of Land Management land and gave the sheepmen a specific allotment instead of having an open range and allowing the sheepmen to go where they wanted to go. Alma's allotment was at Lakeside and he went with his father one time in a large Oldsmobile truck. They got stuck at the sand knolls and had to walk nearly three miles to camp. That night the sand froze and it took them over two days to get out. "But father didn't get discouraged," Norman said. "He took things as they came. He was a jolly person. I never did see my father get very mad."
One spring Norman's father sent him to the Desert to resolve a conflict between Walt Grey, the sheepherder, and Clark Griggs, the camp tender. "I was at LDS Business College at the time and he asked me to go out as they had a fight and wouldn't talk. Walt had gotten Clark down and was choking him and Clark got hold of a rock and hit Walt in the forehead and knocked him cold. When I got there they wouldn't even speak to each other. I became the go between and I finally got them talking and working together. They had a herd of 3300 sheep.
Norman told about one year when he was with Walt Grey near Dugway and the sheep had not had a drink all winter. They had been on snow so long that when they put them on water at Simpson Springs they wouldn't drink. They caught some of them and poured water down their mouths to get them to start drinking again. Before lambs could by vaccinated for sore mouths they lost a lot of lambs. The only treatment was to catch them and put turpentine and pine tar on their lips. Another experience he remembered was the time his father sent them out to Lakeside in the truck. When they tried to cross over Wrathal Pass they got stuck and it was dark and the snow and wind was bad. "I forget who was with me but we sat there meditating on what to do. We didn't know where the camp was and there were four different directions we could have walked. We decided to go on the east side toward Skunk and found the camp there. I have always thanked my Father in Heaven for that guidance because if we had not chosen the right direction we would have lost our lives. The next morning we had to ride a workhorse five miles down to a well to get a cat to pull the truck out because the snow had drifted around it so deep.
He had other close calls on the Desert. Alden has heard him tell about a time when it was storming so bad in Puddle Valley that they drove in circles and were lost for some time until the storm let up and they were able to find the road. There was also the winter of 1948 when the sheep got snowed in and they had to get a cat to push a road into their sheep. It snowed so much they had to stay in Grantsville because the roads back to Henefer were closed.
Norman never lost his interest in the sheep. Even after turning the sheep operation over to his sons he still kept a hand in the operation and insisted that he go to the Desert, on the Reserve and up Pine to see the sheep. During the summer of 1976, at age 71 he and Leola herded the sheep in Pine for five or six weeks after Myron, Alden and Grant had taken a turn of three weeks each. If he wasn't with the sheep he was worrying about them. They were never off his mind for any length of time.
NORMAN AND ELVA'S COURTSHIP AND MARRIAGE
After graduating from high school Norman went to Salt Lake to school. He said he went out a time or two with young women in Salt Lake. When he returned home the next year he started to go with Elva. She was still in high school and had worked for Hattie Richins during the summers from the time she started high school until they got married a year after she graduated. Elva later wrote, "I learned many things from her about homemaking." Her mother, Susannah, was asked how long her daughter had worked for Hattie she replied, "quite a long time. That's how she got Norm, for pay."
They went to high school games together, on sleigh riding parties and to Como Springs in the summer on dates. Norman said, "she was a good worker and a fine young lady, and I fell in love with her." Esther said that whenever her Mother went to Ogden or Salt Lake on the train shopping she would always bring something back to Elva for her hope chest, so she and Alma were no doubt in favor of the marriage, In like manner Elva's mother thought highly of Norman. Hattie stopped one time to talk to Susanah after Norman and Elva were married and said, "Susanah I hope Norman never causes you any trouble," and when Susanah told us this story she added, "and he never has.
They went to their first cousin, Oriel Stephens, who was Stake President, for their interview prior to their marriage. Norman said, "I will never forget the advice he gave us." One thing he told us was never to get mad at each other at the same time." They took their mothers with them by train to the Salt Lake to witness their marriage in the Salt Lake Temple. Alma was with the sheep and unable to attend. They obtained their marriage license in Salt Lake County on the 14th of November 1927. They stayed with relatives that night and then were married November 15, 1927, by Elder George F. Richards, an apostle.
They had a reception in the Henefer Recreation Hall. Pearl said that Norman and Elva planned to stay in the north bedroom of his folks home after their reception but Susanah wouldn't let them. She wanted them down to her place. Esther laughed when she told how punctual Norman was for meals when he first got married. She said he would take right off in a hurry when it was time to eat so Elva wouldn't have to wait meals for him. That practice must not have lasted long because he was often late for meals later in his life.
ELVA'S CHILDHOOD AND EARLY LIFE
Elva was born on September 10, 1908, the third child and first daughter of James Henry Fowler and Susannah Jones Stephens. She was born at the home of her parents in Henefer. Her older brothers were Orlando and Thomas Merl. Her younger sisters were Verna S. and Veda Lucy. All of her Grandparents except her grand¬father James Fowler passed away before she was born. He lived alone in a large brick house later owned by Joseph and Reta Ovard. "Every time we visited him she said, "he would get us a soda cracker from a large black wooden chest he kept under his bed." He was a very gentle and kind man. He had lost the sight of one eye when he was a young boy and then in later life his speech was impaired by a slight stroke."
Elva wrote, "as the years go by my love grows stronger for my grand¬parents and I appreciate the heritage they gave me and I am grateful to them for their accepting the gospel of Jesus Christ of Latter day Saints in England and coming to the land of America and Utah so that I and my family may enjoy the privileges and opportunities that we do." Much of the genealogy work which we have available on our Fowler and Stephens lines, came about because of the efforts of Elva. She loved to learn about her ancestors. Alden attributes his interest in genealogy to the encouragement and example that came from his mother.
Elva lived in a home built by her parents. It was a five room home. She said, there was never anything elaborate or fancy about it but it was always comfortable and a place where we all grew up with love in our hearts for each other and for our parents." When she married she never moved more than a block away from where she was born. This enabled her to always maintain a close relationship with her mother and help her after her father died.
Elva's cousin, William Oriel Stephens, blessed her on November 11, 1908. When she was three months old she got small pox and was very sick. Her body was so covered with pox that she had to be packed around on a feather pillow. The doctor told her mother that she would not survive unless she took nourishment from her mother. They worked with her to keep her nursing and everyone told her in later years that it was a miracle and an answer to faith and prayers that she survived. In those days contagious diseases came around often. Elva said they got quarantined nearly every winter. She recalls being the first in the family to get the red measles and then Merl got them. He wouldn't stay in bed and caught cold causing them measles to go into his larynx, almost choking him.
Although they grew up with love for each other Elva said they had their family squabbles but they were soon forgotten. There were family chores to do and one night when she and Verna were assigned the dishes Elva said, "I thought Verna was playing in the yard too long so I went out and picked up a rock and threw it from the house to the fence where she was and it hit her in the middle of the forehead. Soon there was a goose egg so you can guess who did the dishes by herself that night." Verna told about one Christmas when she got a glass doll and a buggy from Santa and Merl got a pocketknife. He got mad for some reason and threw his knife and hit Elva in the thigh. In the commotion Verna's doll was broken so her father went to the store and bought her another doll.
One evening when Elva was hurrying to get the dishes done she tripped and dropped a stack of plates she was carrying. All of them broke and her mother was so upset she told her she couldn't go out and play that evening, but she later relented and let her go. Verna remembers Elva as the peacemaker of the family. Veda said she was always easy to get along with and was very even tempered.
Elva told in her history about the time Merl fell from a horse while taking cows to the field. The horse stepped on his little toe so Doctor Borland had to finish cutting it off. For a week or two the girls pushed their big brother every¬where he went in a baby buggy. The girls played jacks a lot and Elva was a real good Jack player. They played flinch nearly every night during the winter with their neighbors and cousins, Orville, Theron, Ray, and Lois. In the summer¬time they played hide and seek and kick the can. Merl got the only bike the family had but he wouldn't let the girls ride it so they would skip their meals and ride it while he was eating. Verna said they pulled off a lot of pickets from the fence while learning to ride the bike. The girls liked to sit on their father's lap and comb and curl what little hair he had. One time Elva saw George Jones in a public meeting and came up from the back of him and thinking it was her 'papa' (which they called him) she kissed his head. She was teased about that for a while.
A lot of sorrow came to the family in 1922 when Orlando died while serving a mission in Japan. Verna says, "we girls were walking up the street with Mama to go see Sylvia's new baby when we met Bishop Oriel coming towards us. He told us he had just received a message on the phone. Mama immediately said, "Londo's dead isn't he?" He had been inoculated for cholera which caused a carbuncle to form and he got blood poisoning. It took tbree weeks to get his body home for burial. Elva attended the Henefer Elementry school then graduated from North Summit high school in 1926. Two of the teachers she had ,W. Earl Calderwood, and C. B. Copley also taught her children and her two older grandsons, Lane and Joel. Elva's classmates were Inez Shill, Wanda Richins, Grace Roberts, Louise Toone from Henefer and Joye Wilde from Coalville. Merl and Grace and Norman and Elva did some of their courting together. Merl and Grace were married five months before Norman and Elva. Both couples were given china cupboards for their wedding by Merl and Elva's parents.
NORMAN AND ELVA'S CHILDREN AND FAMILY LIFE
After Norman and Elva got married they moved into two rooms of the Joseph Ovard home, and then two years later they built a new home on property purchased from Ben Hopkins. Joe Taylor and Joseph Wright of Coalville built their home. Norman said he hauled an old rock house off the property and there were enough rocks to fill in all the holds in the street for half a block in both directions.
When they built their home they had very little furniture. For the front rooms all they had was an oblong table Norman had built in shop and a little rocking chair and a china closet. Verna remembers that Elva was very proud of her new home and always enjoyed working to keep it and the yard looking nice. She was a good housekeeper. She could paint, wallpaper, and do anything else that needed to be done. Mary said, "I learned how to hang wallpaper on ceilings and walls and also how to paint from helping her do it. She used to house clean every spring and clean thoroughly every spring and fall." She was a hard worker and she enjoyed working in the yard. She loved flowers and had beautiful flowerbeds. She had a "green thumb." For years she had a vegetable garden until she got tired of trying to get someone to help her weed it and then she gave up and turned the garden into a pasture.
Just after starting to build their house they had their first baby whom they named Mary. She was born September 5, 1928, in grandma Fowler's big bedroom. Mary wrote, "Daddy was carrying me home one evening when he stepped on a nail and dropped me to the ground. He rushed back into grandma's but everything was okay. They said I cried a lot but later they decided I wasn't getting enough to eat. Norman was still working with his father but times were hard as it was during the depression. Mary remembers grandma Fowler telling her how bad Elva felt when all they had for lunch was some bottled tomatoes and fruit. So in addition he worked on the state road the first two summers after they were married. He drove a tenton roller to compact the asphalt they put on the roads.
In June of 1930 Elva's father passed away and that brought a lot of sadness to the family. Veda said, "When papa died Elva spent all night holding her hand on his head." She was expecting a baby at that time and six months later on December 21, 1930 their first son was born in the big bedroom of grandma Fowler's home. Norman was on the desert at the time pulling camp for Walt Grey. It wasn't until three or four days later that he got home to see his first son. He remembers that they were on Craig Mountain in Cooks Canyon when the man came to replace him. They couldn't decide on a name for him and then one night Elva's father came to her in a dream and after that they decided on James Alden for his name. (James being her father's name).
In 1933 when Mary was nearly five years old and Alden was two and one half, they spent the summer on the Reserve with their folks herding sheep. Hattie bought red material and gave it to Elva so she could make shirts for Mary and Alden to help keep them from getting lost. Elva had a little sorrel mare and Mary rode on the back of her. Alden rode in the pack bag on the side of his father on a horse named King. Norman has told many times of when King got away from him with Alden in the pact bag. He said, "I never had such a feeling in my life as I did when King was running around in the flat above the big meadow with Alden in the pack bag." Jack Clark and wife were herding for Deardens and the two families often made ice cream by putting ice cream mix in a bucket and turning it in the snow they had gotten from Mount Baldy. Norman enjoyed telling how Elva would get up early each morning and go with him to count the sheep. That same summer uncle Noble and aunt Electa were herding Fawcett's sheep so they visited with them. At times they rode to Holiday Park to pick up their supplies brought to them from home.
Lorraine writes this about her birth. "On April 30, 1934, when I was born Daddy was on the desert with the sheep. Alden was at home with pneumonia so Grandmother Fowler was staying with Mama helping with Alden. Mama had gathered ears and was very ill. Aunt Verna was in the hospital in Ogden with appendicitis and grandma Fowler couldn't go visit Verna during the entire 10 days she was there. Aunt Lucy was ill with pneumonia at this time also, and came to Henefer to finish her recouperation so needless to say, it was a very busy and trying time for all." Alden had to be operated on at home and have his lungs drained. Elva lost her hearing for a time. Norman hired La Von Richins and Ivy Wilde to help care for them and for the new baby, Lorraine. Norene Jones also helped out. Dr. High was Elva's doctor and Dr. French was Alden's, so both doctors were coming to the home.
Four years later on July 31, 1938, Grant was born. Mary remembers being sent to her Grandma Fowlers to play. Later she was told she had a little brother that weighed 10 pounds. Sever years later on June 27, 1945, Myron was born. He was the only one not born at home. Mary says, "Mama never did tell us she was expecting Myron but I remember Daddy taking her up to the Coalville hospital for his birth. I was working at the Park Lunch that summer." Alden remembers when going to the hospital to see his new baby brother. Myron vomited severly and had to be given drops to regulate the valve in his stomach. Later he got an infection in his eye and had to go to the Dee hospital in Ogden to have it taken care of. For a time they were afraid he might lose his sight.
There were only two bedrooms in our house so Mary and Alden slept together when small children. When Lorraine got older she and Mary slept together and Alden slept in a small cot in the same bedroom. Grant slept in the crib until he was six years old. When Mary was In the seventh grade she went to stay with Grandma Fowler at night and did so until she got married and then she and Joe stayed their for a few months afterwards until they moved into their little log house behind Leo Fawcetts. In about 1949 the big front porch was made into a bedroom for Lorraine.
The family home has changed over the years. Until the Henefer sewer system was built in 1939 there was no inside toilet. The outside toilet was in the corner by the coalhouse. The hot water tank used to be in the bathroom until that space was needed for the toilet and then the tank was moved to the kitchen next to the coal-burning cook stove. There was a coal burning heating stove in the dining room between the kitchen and the hall doors, which along with the cook stove was the source of heat. On Christmas eve the heating stove was "banked" so it would keep the house warm when we got up early to see what Santa Claus brought. When natural gas came to tw4n in 1947 the coal burning stoves were replaced with a gas range and a gas furnace. The hot water tank was taken out of the kitchen and a new gas water heater put in the basement.
There used to be about a 10 foot archway between the dining room and the front room with hard wood floor under the archway. That was a favorite spot where we put a rug and played marbles. Before the front bedroom was built on the home there was a long porch with the font door entrance where the door to the bedroom is now located. Elva had a rocking chair on the front porch, which she enjoyed sitting in but seldom found the time to use.
The windows on the east side of the dinning room and front room were different than they are now. There were three sectional ones in the dining room and only one small window in the front room. On the south side there was a three sectional window looking out onto the front porch. We used to hang Christmas wreaths in the front room windows that had a light in the shape of a candle in them. Quarantine signs were put in the front window when there was chicken pox or measles or whooping cough in the home. In the corner of the dining room was the bishop's desk for eleven years as there was no space for the bishop's office at the church. Alden liked tithing settlement at the end of the year because there was always candy and fruitcake put out for those who came to settle their tithing and he would sneak in and get some for himself.
There were few clothes closets in the house. In the children$ bedroom an old movable closet was used until the buitt in was made in the 50's. There was a small medicine cabinet just above the wash basin in the bathroom and that was all. It is a good thing we didn't have many clothes in those days, as there was no place to hang them except in the closet in the main bedroom and on hooks back of the hall door.
The back porch was not enclosed as it is now. There were screens on the windows and in the summer when the kitchen was so hot we moved the table to the porch where it was cooler to eat dinner. In the winters the screens were covered to keep it warmer. In the northwest corner there was a big wooden barrel where the pigs Norman slaughtered every year were cured. We rubbed curing salt on them and then left them there to cure until we were ready to use them. The Maytag washer and dirty clothes bag were next to the barrel in the back porch. Lorraine wrote, "I remember Monday mornings while I was attending grade school in Henefer as it was always wash day. We'd put the boiler on the stove on Sunday night then on Monday morning, the stove in the kitchen was stoked up to heat the water, the washer and tubs put up In the porch then in the winter wet clothes were hung all over the lines in the hall, over the doors, etc. to dry. I used to have to hang the clothes outside in the good weather. We usually had homemade tomato soup for lunch on Mondays." Alden remembers helping bring in clothes off the line and sometimes they were frozen stiff and had to be thawed out inside the house.
We had the usually childhood illnesses. Every year Alden would get a bad cough and have to stay home a few days. He didn't mind too much because whenever he stayed home he talked his mother into making donuts and they were a treat for the entire family. In addition, the children knew that when they were ill they could expect their father would bring soda pop to them from the store and Grandma Fowler would always come to see us. Lorraine said, "when we were sick we would sit on the bed and pick out patterns on the quilts and have someone guess, which pattern piece of patch work we had in mind. We spent hours doing this."
The only entertainment other than listening to some favorite radio programs like "'Jack Armstrong the all American boy," 'Let's Pretend, Blondy" and "I'm a Lemac now" were games of our own making. We played the usually games of flinch, touring, pollyanna,sorry, chinese checkers, and marbles. one of our favorite games (which we usually played when left alone) was red light." We closed the doors to the kitchen and moved the table out from the wall. Whoever was "it" was blind folded and then the lights were turned out. Others would hide by standing on the table, chairs, cabinets, stove or get on the water tank or just walk around the table to keep away from "it". When *it" touched someone if he could guess correctly who it was then they became "I t".
Mother put up with a lot of games played in the house and there were always lots of friends there with us. Lorraine said, "I used to complain that we always had all the kids at our house and it was hard to keep it clean 'but Mama would say, "I'd rather have them here where I know what they're doing," and she really meant it. She even let us draw rings on her front room carpet so we could play marbles. There were a few times when our squabbles got to her and when they did she would say "I'm leaving and not coming back until you kids start behaving better." She would leave and hide in the basement or go to her mothers for a while. As soon as she was gone we bawled our eyes out and could hardly wait until she got back. It always seemed to work as it put us on our best behavior for sometimes after¬wards.
Our home was always clean and well cared for. In 1947 President George Albert Smith, in Henefer on the Sons of Utah Pioneer Centennial, came to our home to use the bathroom. Norman wasn't embarrassed to bring him there because he knew the home would be respectable.
WORK ON THE FARM
In the basement of our home was kept the fruit that mother bottled each year. In addition the floor was usually filled with baskets of eggs to be cleaned and made ready to be taken by the Draper Feed man when he came each week with the chicken feed. It seems like mother was always cleaning eggs. Mary said, "I spent hours in the basement cleaning eggs." Lorraine and Alden also cleaned eggs but they were more reluctant participants than Mary. The children helped gather the eggs and feed the chickens but their Mother did most of the work with the chickens. Every morning, noon and night she was in the chicken coop. She took pride in her chickens and loved to have her neighbors buy eggs from her at very reasonable prices. She insisted that the chicken coops be kept clean. Alden hated chicken coop cleaning days. When he got old enough to drive the truck it wasn't bad but until then it was a chore. He usually got his friends to help him by promising to play rubber guns afterwards in the yard. The dirty straw was deep under the roosts and hard to get out with a rake so he tied ropes on a board and then made Grant get under the roots and hold the board down while he and his friends pulled the board out dragging the dirty straw with it. Cleaning the chicken coop usually included spraying for mites. The greatest satisfaction in cleaning the coop was seeing how excited the chickens were after they were given new straw to scratch in.
Each year we got some little chicks shipped in on the railroad. We had a brooder coop that was located between the present milk barn and granery. Mother took good care of the little chicks keeping the temperature just right and making sure they didn't get sick. In the spring of 1945 when she was expecting Myron, Alden saw the brooder door unlocked as he walked by and locked it so the chicks wouldn't get out. I didn't know his mother was in there at the time and she spent hours locked in there before she was discovered. She couldn't yell to attract attention for fear of scaring the little chicks. It was hot in there and she was only weeks from delivering Myron. Alden didn't remember her saying much about it but he does remember the reprimand he received from his father and how concerned his father was about the effect it might have on her and the baby.
We were all supportive of brooding the little chicks each year as some of them were roosters and we loved spring fryers. Our favorite Sunday dinner was fried chicken, potatoes, and gravy, peas, and Jell-O with whipped cream on it for dessert. They were the best eating chickens and she cooked them to perfection. Joe said, "I haven't had any as good since." Lorraine said that her friend Janice loved to come to our home or Sunday dinners. She was an excellent cook. She made the best breakfast and it didn't matter what time Norman or anyone else had to leave she always got up and prepared breakfast.
In addition to the chickens there were the milk cows. At first there was only one and it was milked by hand in the open yard or in a shed. Because Norman was often away with the sheep the milking fell to Elva until Alden was old enough to milk. Mary said, "I remember her going out in all kinds of weather to milk the cow. That's why I've always said I'd never learn to milk." Before the big hay barn was built there was a stack yard were the hay was kept. It was located where the biggest shed is now next to the hay barn.
We gradually increased our herd and got a permit to sell milk to the Cream of Weber Dairy in Ogden, so Norman had the milk barn built in the early 40's. We usually had eight to twelve cows to milk with the use of two surge milking machine. At first the milk was haul in ten-gallon cans kept cool in a milk vat and then we got a tank and it was hauled in bulk.
The sheep took most of Norman's time so Alden, Grant, and then Myron did the milking, except when they were gone and thei their mother did it. Even where the boys did the milking man, times their mother ended up washing up and cleaning up the milk barn so they could get to school on time or get somewhere else they wanted to go. Lorraine remembers that "Mama spent a lot of time scrubing the milk barn and getting it ready for the dreaded 'inspector' who was always coming or had just been there and marked on the Inspection report things that needed to be taken care of immediately." J. Edward Wright remembers that when Elva did chores she wore four buckle boots, a levi apron, and a coat.
Lorraine wrote, "Daddy was with the sheep when I was born and then every year on my birthday as that was the time they usually brought the sheep in from the desert. The night I got engaged to Merl, daddy was with the sheep so Merl had to go to the milk barn to ask mama if he could marry me and on that night April 30, 1952 my Grandpa Richins died.
It is a wonder that all the cows didn't dry up or get mastitis they way they were taken care of at times. Alden sometimes had friends, both boys and girls, around while he was milking. They would throw water on each other and run in and out of the milkbarn. One time they tied ropes on the tails of two cows and turned them out at the same time. One went out and the other one didn't. Myrna Fawcett, Lorraine's girl friend, was standing by the door and the rope caught her leg and made a deep cut in her leg leaving a nasty scar.
Myron wrote, "I think the milk cows were good to mom and dad even though they were tying. I know that the milk cows taught me some valuable lessons. I remember one morning Dad woke me up to go milk, and I didn't get up but fell back to sleep. When I finally woke up it was time for school. Dad was coming in the back porch. He told me that he would not wake me up twice any morning and if I didn't have any more interest than that he would do it himself. To this day when the alarm goes off I get up. I don't like to lay there a few more minutes and then get up."
One of the disadvantages of the milk cows and the chickens was that someone needed to be home every night to take care of them, therefore, we did not go on trips as an entire family. Our parents let us go with relatives or on school trips, and scouting trips. Mary went with her parents to a Richins' Reunion in Mesa, Arizona,when she was about ten years old. Osmond and Rachel Wright went with them. They visited Bryce Canyon, and Zions. Lorraine went with them on a trip to Yellowstone when she was in the seventh grade. Merl and Qrace Fowler accompanied them. When Alden was in Northern California in the mission field the folks and Lorraine went to see him and then picked him up from his mission when he was released. Lorraine and Myron accompained them while Grant stayed home to do the chores. When Alden was released from the military service they went to Seattle to get him. They took Myron, Lane and Grandma Fowler and were accomipained by Merl, Grace, and Roger. In 1972, when Grant's family was in Cincinatti while he was going to school, Father and Leola flew back to visit them and then they took a trip with them to Disney World in Florida. While there they visited Cape Kennedy and several other historical places, In the south. In 1983 Norman took a trip with Grant back to Little Rock, Arkansas, to pull two horse trailers back with furniture In them. It was the first time he had seen that part of the country and he thought it was beautiful but too hot. Myron will never forget a fathers and sons outing they had with the Ward on the Beaver River. They went fishing and Myron said, "there was an Island that had a fallen tree connecting it to the main shore. He asked me if I thought I could walk across the tree. I told him sure. He went first and I guess I looked at the water I can't remember a thing until I saw dad running out in the water to get me out about 200 feet down stream. He later told me I fell up stream and the fallen tree had a lot of branches down in the water. He thought, I had been caught by the branches. He can't swim but he was there to get me out."
One place Alden, Grant, and Myron did go with their father was on the Reserve to take supplies to the sheepherder. When they were younger it was a two day trip so they would sleep overnight in the back of the truck and take time to do some fishing. Many times they watched their father "spinner fish" on West Fork. He usually caught his limit as he was a good fisherman. Myron tells of one time when his father left him out on the hillside while he fished the willows. "I looked behind me and saw a moose calf. All of a sudden I heard Dad yell, 'Look out here comes a cow moose” and Dad came immediately. The cow moose went right for me to its calf. It really scared Dad because he knew the danger. I thought being that close to a moose was pretty neat. Alden remembers going with his father and Alma Toone to the head of the Weber. They rode over on horses and caught so many fish (grayling) they had a difficult time packing them out. Father loved the Reserve. He has been out every inch of it with the sheep. "I love to hear him tell the herders how he herded it," Myron wrote. "I remember riding with him up to Bug Lake and from there to the top of Bald Mountain. From up there he would say, 'where can you find a prettier site."' He often told of how they use to go over Moffitt Pass with the horses to get their supplies at Holliday Park. One fall the snow came early and he had to take the camp with the team in front of the herd to break trail through the snow to get them out.
We had some favorite animals on the farm. When Mary and Alden were young we had a big black dog named Tuffy that we let in the house to help protect us when we were left home alone. He was good sheep dog in the corral. When Lorraine was young we had collie dog that was beautiful. Grant wrote about our little black dog Lucky, "while Dad was on the Desert one time he found a little black dog that someone had left. He was a bird dog and mama named him Lucky. For several years wherever you saw Father you would see Lucky as they were always together. Lucky rode on the top of the cab of the truck from wich he fell off several times, but the falls never discouraged him from getting right back up for a ride. One day the wind blew him off, down by the cement plant and broke his front teeth out. We always had cats in the yard and around and in the house. Mary wrote, "Mama loved cats. I did inherit that from her. She had a cat Mitzer at the time of her death and it was really lonely afterwards. It would sleep at the foot of the bed on a mat she put there for her." Alden was given Pinky, a purebred Holstein heifer, for his FFA project. She was a beautiful cow and a good milk producer. Alden had a little gray mare, Queen, when he was young, which he rode to take cows. She was gentle so all the kids rode her and she wasn't very tall so it didn't hurt much when they fell off her. Lorraine rode her in the Centennial parade. We always had horses to ride. Father rode Mae in the Henefer Pony Express. He liked to ride Susy and Tico. Myron wrote, "Dad always looked good on a horse and he really knows how to get the most out of them. He really respects a horse and a horse always respects him. I remember getting a call at the seminary one day and Dad was crying. I asked him what the trouble was and he said Old Nig just died while he was riding him. Dad said Nig was a worker right to the end and that's what I want to be, a worker right to the end."
It use to be a fun time for us kids in the winter to go to the meadow to help feed the cattle. Father used a team of horses and a sleigh when there was snow on the ground. We rode behind on our sleighs or skis. In the summer time we used horses to mow the hay, rake it and then put it in stacks. We use to ride the horse to pull the hay up into the barn with the big Jackson fork. It was always a thrill to ride on a haystack on top of a big load of hay and come into town and unload it in the big barns. Trucks gradually replaced the horses. It then became special to ride on back of a big load of bailed hay. We hauled many loads into the barns and into open stacks. At first we lifted the hay into the barn by hand or by use of the Jackson fork and then we got bay elevators. In 1967 the elevator caught on fire while Myron, Grant and their boys were putting hay in the barn down by the river and the barn, hay and truck were all burned up. Luckily no one was burned or injured.
When Norman ran the dry farm to the west of Henefer we use to love to help conbine the wheat. The combine dumped the sacks in groups of five or six around the patch and we had to pick them up and load them on the truck and bring them to the granery in Henefer. We kids liked to run behind the truck as it brought the wheat off the dry farm. We use to have lots of squirrels around the wheat patch and so we poisoned them or shot them with twenty two's. The county gave a bounty of 1/2 cent each for their tails. They also gave a bounty on magpies and we used to have a lot of them in the hawthorn trees over the meadow. Because Norman ran the sheep for years with his brothers, Hillman and Jewell there were many meetings held at Grandpa Richins home or on the street corner by Uncle Hillman's. We got use to waiting for the meetings to get over so we would know the work plan for the day. There was always plenty of work but it wasn't the same every day. The thing about it was getting to work with our Father. Working with him more than made up for the times we never went together to parties or activities. It was special because he was always such a good example of honesty and a hard worker. He taught us some important values through our working with him. Lorraine recalls going with him on occasions and helping sharpen hay knives in Grandpa's old granary. She said, "it was fun when we were younger to go to grandpa Richins home and explore the old home or play in the sheep camp in the yard. Sometimes when we would go to Grandpa Richins home Daddy would get Grandpa to get his violin from under his bed and play us a tune. Shirlene, Margene and I would go to the parlor and play the old graphone, look at the picture books, then usually end up outside in the sheep camp mixing up all kinds of spices to pretend we were good cooks. Daddy nor Grandpa Richins ever lost their patience with us." Lorraine went on to say, "whenever I would ask him for something and he would tell me no, I asked why and he always said, 'because y's a crooked letter and you ought to know better."
One time while teenagers, Alden and Lorraine asked their parents if they could go to a show in Coalville on a Sunday night. They said, "you know what is right you decide." We didn't go but we would have gone had they not given us the responsibility to decide. Lorraine said , Daddy did give me a nickle each Sunday so I could go to the Park to get an ice cream cone."
Christmas eve was always special for us. We had the elementary school program at the old recreation hall and then we went to Grandma Fowlers until her family became too large and then we met in our home and have ever since. We were meeting December 24, for Christmas Eve at our home In Henefer when word came in 1978 to us that Grandma Fowler had just passed away.
NORMAN AND ELVA'S CHURCH AND COMMUNITY SERVICE
It can truly be said of both of them that they loved people and served their fellowmen. They accepted the church positions to which they called and taught their children to do the same. They also supported each other in their i church callings setting a good example of where priorities should be for their children.
If there was ever a conflict between church assignments and work that needed to be done around the house or the farm, church work came first. If anyone needed to miss a church meeting or a priesthood assignment or a church activity Father or mother insisted it be one of them and not any of their children.
Norman has been a servant to God since his early youth. He was baptized in the Weber River on July 6, 1913 by John Francis. He served as superintendent of the Sunday School, President of the YMMIA, and during the early years of their marriage as a Stake Missionary. He was ordained a Seventy on October 21, 1934 by Levi Edgar Young. He was set apart as a Stake missionary in 1936. Four years later on February 18, 1940, he was ordained Bishop of the Henefer ward by W. Earl Calderwood. His counselors were Roy Richins and Merl Fowler. When Merl Fowler moved from Henefer about a year later J. Edward Wright became his counselor. He served in that position for eleven years until he was released on January 21, 1951. Alden remembers his friend's coming up to him when his father was made bishop and saying, "now you have to be good."
As Lorraine said, "he has always been an excellent example, has attended his church and civic duties, and has been well respected by everyone in the community." He was a good bishop. He was bishop all during Mary, Alden, and Lorraine's teen years. It was an added help to them to keep the commandments for they didn't want to do anything that would disgrace their father whom they loved so much. Alden remembers one time when the bishopric called all the Aaronic priesthood together and talked to them about chastity. Each member of the bishopric stood up and said, “that one of the joys of their life now they were in their forty's was to look back and be able to say they had kept themselves morally clean.” That was a powerful message and Alden remembers commenting to himself at that time that he wanted to be able to have that same joy. J. Edward Wright said Norman was a very caring bishop. Those who asked for help or needed help got the assistance they needed and most of the time It came out of Norman's pocket and not from fast offering or welfare funds.
While bishop, Norman took a great interest in the welfare of the youth in the community. There was a man named Warman who brought movies to Henefer each Monday night. He brought his own projector and showed them in the old Henefer Ammusement Hall. In the wintertime it was cold so we had to sit close but we didn't mind that when we were teenage boys and girls. In 1947 while he was bishop the Henefer basketball tournament was started. It has been a yearly event ever since, bringing back former residents from miles away for three nights of entertainment. That event has no doubt kept the people of Henefer in touch with each other more than anything else that has happened in the community.
After being released as bishop, Norman was set apart to the Summit Stake High Council on February 18, 1951. He served in that position for eighteen years. During that time he made many close friends on the High Council. Norman and Elva were set apart as Temple workers in the Salt Lake Temple on October 26, 1960. They went to the temple every Wednesday afternoon and then on Monday's until Elva's passing in 1965. Norman continued in that assignment and then after he and Leola were married they were called as Temple workers on February 13, 1969. They served together in that position until being released on January 3, 1974, just prior to their leaving on a mission. Norman and Leola were later called to work in the Ogden Temple where they served faithfully from August 27, 1981 to May 2, 1984, when they were released because of Norman's illness. He always placed top priority on his temple assignments. He seldom missed for any reason.
On March 1, 1925, when nineteen years old Norman received a patriarchael blessing under the hands of Charles R. Jones. Among other significant blessings he was told he would be known for good among the people and that his words would bring a testimony unto others. He was promised that he would fulfill an honorable mission and that he would be called to preside and fill positions of trust and responsibility in the church. He was told that many would listen to his advice and counsel. It was said that he would have influence with the young and they would do good because of the example he set before them. A specific blessing was that he would be called to labor in the temples and there assist in the great work of the redemption of the dead. He was promised that this work would give him great joy and strengthen his testimony. He was told that a companion would be raised up unto him and that he would become the father of sons and daughters who would be among the choice ones to dwell upon the earth. The past sixty years has brought fulfillment of these blessings which has been his because of his righteousness. Elva did not have a patriarahael blessing but hers would no doubt have been much like her husbands.
At a stake conference June 21, 1970, Norman was sustained as Patriach of the Summit Stake and ordained to that position by Apostle Thomas S. Monson. When sustained a everyone's hand shot up simultaneously and Elder Monson said, you people must know Brother Richins as the Lord knows him." All Norman's children were in attendance exept Alden who was living in Centerville. He had been moved by the spirit to go to Coalville to conference but resisted and decided to attend meetings at home. He has wished many times he had 'listened' and been where he could have enjoyed that experience with his family.
In 1938 Norman was elected to the North Summit Gchool Board where he served for 14 years until 1952. He gained many close friends in that position and enjoyed it very much except for the time he was president of the board and had to release C. Ray Evans as the superintendent. Alden felt honored that his father as president of the school board, presented him with his high school graduation diploma. Lorraine remembers him sitting on the stand during her graduation. The school board spent some long hours together often not getting home until the early hours of the morning. Grant remembers a time when Father didn't get home until time to go docking lambs. He was so tired after docking that on the way home he fell asleep and ran off the road coming down the main canyon. No harm was done because luckily he drove off on the up hill side.
Norman also served on the County Farmers Home Administration Committee, the County Soil Conservation Committee, and the Agricultural Stabilization and Conservation Service for ten years. Norman was a member of the Henefer Pony Express riding group, which was in existence in the 1940's. The group practiced on the Henefer town square during the summer under the lights and not only did they get dirty but everyone who watched them got covered with dust. They performed in parades including the Salt Lake 24th of July parade. They also did drills at rodeo's in Henefer, Coalville, and Morgan. They participated in the Centennial Celebration of 1947 by taking the mail from Henefer over Bib Mountain to This Is the Place Monument at the mouth of Emigration Canyon. Norman made the first mile ride from the Henefer church to Sulpher Spings up main canyon. His brother Carl, who was the Mayor, handed him the mail to carry.
Elva was baptized September 10, 1916, by Thomas A. Arnold in the Weber River by the river bridge. She was confirmed that same day by William Orial Stephens. She was always involved in the church programs and activities. She served as A Sunday School teacher, both before and after she was married. She was also a teacher in Primary, and MIA. Her last church position to which she was called in January of 1963 was that of a first year beehive teacher. Most of her years of church service were in the Relief Society where she served 34 years as a visiting teacher. She was assistant Relief Society Secretary and Treasurer and then became the Secretary and Treasurer from January 1946 to August 1949. She was first counselor in the Ward Relief Society from August 1949 to June 1954. On Febuary 21, 1954 she was sustained as Summit Stake Relief Society President with Maxine Wright and ThelmaBrown as her counselors. She served in that position until August 1960. She loved Relief Society and enjoyed it very much. She did a lot of quilting and preparing food to take to those in need. Mother did a lot of painting and cleaning when the church house was remodeled. Both she and Father were proud of the new church when it was dedicated in 1956.
Elva received her Daughter of Utah Pioneers membership in March of 1937. She attended the meetings and conventions and was proud to belong to the DUP. She did a lot of genealogical work. She went on a tour of the archives inside the granite mountain at Little Cottonwood Canyon, which she felt was special one of the Church callings that she really enjoyed was that of a Temple worker. It pleased her to be able to do both genealogical work and temple work. This testimony given not too long before her death is a tribute of her faith and devotion to the gospel of Jesus Christ.

FOND MEMORIES OF NORMAN AND ELVA
I have always wanted to be like my Father. If someone said to me, "you look like your father" I beamed with joy for I have always thought of him as a hansome man and a man of sterling character. I learned while still young that Norman Richins was a man of integrity and honesty. I have never known him to do anything that would cause hurt to anyone. He has been so concerned about how others would feel that many times he has been taken advantage of by them. It can truly be said of him that it has cost him at times to be a good samiaritan.
One time when I was a Deacon I took a nickel out of the Bishops desk, which was in our home. Not long after mother said, "your father is short a nickel Have any of you taken a nickel. We all said 'no.' I got thinking that he had to have that nickel to make his books balance so I got a nickel out of the pocket of his bib overalls which hung behind the hall door. When no one was looking I threw it on the floor under the desk. Later that evening I pretended to be playing marbles on the floor and I 'discovered' the nickel and announced that I had found it. Mother took it and put it in desk. I felt that I had covered up my dishonesty. The next morning when I woke up I said to mother, "where's father?" She said, "Alden, your father is already gone. He is disappointed in you and does not want to associate with you." I all of a sudden realized I had not fooled anyone but myself. My parents knew what I had done. There came over me a feeling of despair. I loved my father and wanted to be with him. I had anguish of soul. I experienced feelings, which I am sure those who are cut off from our Heavenly Father will experience. It was so real and so terrible that I decided right then and there that I would be honest and never do anything again that might keep me from associating with my earthly father or my Heavenly Father.
Since that time there has always come into my soul a feeling of joy whenever my father has introduced me to someone as his son. He has been proud of his family and at times has gone out of his way to introduce us to a sheep herders or one of his former friends as his son or daughter.
Myron wrote about a time when he was eight years old. "I went with Dad up on the Divide to move a sheep camp. The camp was by Foster Spring and we were moving it to the herd of Bells through a real wet spot. We almost go stuck and I started getting scared. Dad looked over at me and said, 'oh, don't worry we have still got grandpa,' (that's what he called the lowest gear) to go. He always seemed to have a way to make you feel at ease."
Myron told about a valuable lesson he learned concerning praying. 'One night I had a friend stay over night when I was about fourteen. The next morning after the chores were done we went in for breakfast. As, we knelt for family prayer Dad asked me if I would offer the prayer. I said I will some other day, but not this morning. He said the prayer himself. The next morning he called on Mom and all the next week he and Mom alternated saying the prayer. Mom finally told me that I should make it right with Dad for not praying. 1 did! Dad said it was an honor to pray when anyone asks you no matter who was present. I told him I would never again refuse the opportunity." Another lesson Myron remembers learning from his Dad was on doing things when you are told. "We were killing a lamb and Dad asked me to go in the house and get a bucket of water. I said, “I l will in a minute." He asked me two more times. On the third time he reached up with the butcher knife to swat me on the back. I ducked and the knifepoint hit me in the shoulder and drew blood. I made him think it really hurt me, but he didn't have much to say. I can honestly say that’s the only time I can remember him raising a hand to me."
Father never lost his temper at anyone except an occassional animal. He got mad at a milk cow one time and kicked it when he had the milking boots on and broke his toe. He was always understanding with us even when things went wrong. Lorraine said, "when I wrecked our new Dodge the night I took it to the girl's dance, the first thing Daddy asked as we went into the bedroom crying was, 'was anyone hurt?' He didn't worry about material things because he was always concerned about people." Alden had a similar experience when he got side swipped earlier in the chevy. Father was more concerned about us than he was the car.
He has always been concerned about the grandchildren and has been thrilled to take part in their blessings and priesthood ordinances. The grandchildren have in turn been so thankful that he was a patriarch and would give them a partiarchael blessings. They love to be with him at family gatherings.
In writing about her mother Lorraine said, "Mama was an excellent cook. She seemed to be able to make a meal out of nothing. She always made the stuffing for the turkey (or chicken) for thanksgiving dinners. She was the official stuffing maker for Grandma Fowlers family, only she would never taste the dressing after she had put the beaten eggs in it. She cooked great dinners for the hay haulers and I've never had as good a breakfast as I use to have at home. She always had fruit, cooked cereal, milk, toast, eggs, meat and sometimes potatoes. Maybe the reason we were so smart in school is that we had a good breakfast to begin our day."
Alden recalls a most valuable lesson his mother taught him when he was still a young boy of eight or nine. He said, "Each fall our family went to East Layton to buy fruit from Dean and Ora Coonradt. They had two sons Jack and Lee. Jack was my age and Lee a year or two younger. One summer Jack drowned in a pond near their home. On the way home from the funeral I said, 'It sure is sad, Jack was their favorite son wasn't he?' Mother responded, 'Alden parents do not have favorites. They love all their children the same.' I have realized since then that what she said was true and that she and Father loved all of us children the same. They did everything they could to keep love in our family."
Mother had a tender heart. Mary wrote, "I remember her telling me that she cried during my graduation as I had just got engaged to Joe. Now I wish I had waited so she could have enjoyed the program. You don't realize how a mother feels when the children leave home until you experience it." At Myron's graduation she was crying on the way into the program. Alden asked her why she was crying at such a happy event. She said, "I know that after graduation your children leave and are away from home a lot and the thoughts of that make me sad." The boys did leave for a time for their missions and Alden and Grant to the military service but Mary and Lorraine have always been close to home. Mary only a block away. Mary wrote, "After we moved to the little log house behind Leo's I would go to Mama's every day and help her do her work. I did my washing with her until I got a washer. She helped me a lot to fix up the old house. We papered and painted." Then she added, "I stayed at Mama's for a month after Lane was born. They made us use their bed and they slept in the other room. After Joel was born I had rheumatic arthuritis and was in bed for a month up at her home. Joe was on the forest herding sheep. She helped me a great deal with all my babies except Rozanne. She especially helped when the twins were born. I remember Maina saying how Mama cried when she went over to tell her we had twin boys. She insisted on sleeping in their room the first few nights. Grandma Fowler came every day to help me for months. I am grateful to them both and owe them a great deal."
Elva enjoyed being with her grandchildren. She liked to take them with her to the store or on other errands. She was a willing baby tender for Bruce, Judy, Kathy, Diane, Mark, Paul, Linda, Tim and Brett. When she returned from the hospital one time after she was ill she took Linda in her arms and said, "you don't know your grandmother very well yet but we are going to get better acquainted." Linda was three at the time but unfortunately mother died before Linda was four. It is like Lorraine said, "I just wish Merl and Marie could have known and loved her as she was a great grandmother as well as a mother."
Her compassion for her children exceeded the concern for herself. Lorraine tells this experience. "I remember one time when Merl and I and our family had to participate on the Saturday evening session of stake conference in Coalville. We had practiced for a long time and I had made new white dresses and pink coats for the girls and bought Bruce new clothes. Mama called during the latter part of the day and said that she would not be able to come and see us as they had been working with the cattle all day and she had not been able to get her hair done. (Mama always had Mary or me do her hair) I can't remember what I said but I know I cried after I hung up the telephone. Well, when I looked down in the audience as the program began, there sat mama with uncurled hair and all when it was not fashionable to go out in public with wet hair and no dryers or curling irons were available then, just pin curls. Anyway, my lovefor her grew so much that day to realize that she would come and give me her support when I so badly wanted it."
ELVA'S PASSING
Mary wrote the story of mother's passing as follows: During the winter of 1962, Mama kept getting sore throats, colds, the flu, etc. and couldn't seem to shake them. She went to give blood a couple of times and couldn't because her blood showed it was low in iron. She went to Dr. High and found her blood real low. She was sick all spring and summer and went to the doctor all the time and was taking medication. One time it would be up, the next time it was down. She lost a lot of weight so we were getting rather concerned. I asked Dr. High if there was any other tests she should have and he was real snotty saying, "What kind of tests do you want. You can take her to anyone you want." He said he could find no signs of luekemia if that was what we were worried about. Then in September 1963, he decided to call in another doctor, Dr. Daines who was a blood specialist. He put her in the hospital for extensive tests. They found a lymphonia blood condition. (Her lymph glands under her arms and in the neck were swollen). It was a type of chronic luekemia where too many white bloodcells are present. He told us there was a drug out that they thought would control the condition. We took her to Ogden to the clinic once a week for a while. Dr. Daines finally gave her another medicine with the first medicine he had given her and her blood started to come back up. The doctor was pleased with her progress although it was slow. As it got better, she had to go less often to be checked. The doctor discontinued the medication when her blood stayed normal for a period of time. It was still normal when she went for a check up in May 1965. She had gained some weight but lost some again during the summer. I guess I didn't realize or didn't want to believe she was as sick as she was. I wanted to believe she would be okay and didn't think she could be taken from us.
In the middle of July Mama had a bad pain hit her in the stomach as she sat in Rhea Roberts funeral. I told her she should go see the doctor but she wouldn't. Dr. Howe, the surgeon, told us later that there were adhesions so probably the growth had ruptured some that day and had healed over. She didn't complain enough on July 31, 1965, Mama took sick and had a pain again. Her stomach began to bloat. We were all getting ready for Grandma Fowler's 85th birthday open house the next day. Dr. High was away on vacation so as she seemed to get worse, it was decided to take her to the hospital. Larry Dearden drove her to Ogden and made it in 30 minutes. She was examined and it was decided she needed surgery. Dr. Howe was called to operate on her. She was in deep shock. She was so sick. She was taken to surgery about 3 a.m. Her blood pressure was not good. They brought her out of surgery about 4:30 and put her in intensive care. Dr. Howe told us she was gravely ill and would be critical for four or five days. Her chances were one in four. He said she had a lymphonia (maligant) growth in her bowel that had ruptured, another side effect of the blood disorder she had. He had removed a section of the intestine that was dead and had sewed it back together. They removed between two and three quarts of pus from her. (partinitts). We were all so shocked and dazed we hardly knew what to say. The sign on the door said visits only five minutes every hour but they let us go in and out as we wished. She looked so helpless lying there; her eyes looked like they had a salve in them. Her color was so dark. She was in a lot of pain but they couldn't give much for it until they could get her blood pressure stabilized. She told Daddy to please get her out of there, as she was smothering in death. She kept asking for her mother. They worked with her but she steadily got worse. We had a family prayer with Merl Chappell as the spokesman. He asked the Lord that his will be done and thanked him for all our blessings.
At 3:50 p.m. on August 1, 1965, Mama passed away about 24 hours after she had first started with the pain. Daddy's watch stopped at that time. We all loved her so much and would liked her to have been with us longer but her job here on earth was finished and the Lord had a new and better job for her to do. Many close family members came to the hospital. Merl and Grace were there Saturday night. Sunday morning Grandma Fowler, Veda, Verna, Claude, Florence, Kenneth, Erma, Os and Rachel came. Later Carl, Jeanne, Esther, Leonard, Pearl and Glenn were there. Good friends Ted, Maxine and Talbot Richins and wife Naomi visited with us. All of Mama's children and their spouses were there except for Myron who was on a mission in Escanaba, Michigan. We had called and told him that she was sick and having surgery. Then we had to call him to say she had passed away. He decided to come home for the funeral but would go back and finish his mission.
The open house for Grandma Fowler was cancelled. Her death was a shock to everyone. Thea Boyer told me that our little town had a most mournful feeling that Sunday church (fastday) was the worst church she had been to as so many were missing and there was such a sad feeling. All she could think of to sing was, "The Lord Is My Shephard" and how appropriate it was for that time. All our neighbors were so kind and there ready to help. Food was brought in to all our homes in abundance and they did our chores and helped with the housework. We had the viewing at the house. There were over 500 people to the viewing that night and 200 more the next day. There were lots of pretty flowers.
There was a large crowd at the funeral. There was a peaceful feeling there. Roy Richins and Edison Stephens were the speakers. Thea Boyer, Dorena Rowser and Kay Dearden sang "Beautiful Sunset" and "In the Garden of Tomorrow". Darlene Richins gave a musical reading and LaVelle Stephens sang "God's Other Room." The prayers were by J. Edward Wright and Roy Dearden. It was a beautiful day. The valley looked so pretty and green from the gravesite on the cemetery. Mama looked lovely in her temple robes. The total cost to Walker Mortuary for taking care of her was $1,003. Daddy received money and many cards and letters. Her grave marker was put up in November by Mr. Walker at the cost of $138.69.
After Mama passed away it was hard to believe she wasn't up home. I'd have the feeling to go up to see her and felt she'd maybe be there but when I'd get up there she wasn't there. It took a long time to realize she had gone from us to live again with Heavenly Father. I have really missed her. I have wished many times that she was here to enjoy the accomplishments of her children and grandchildren. She'd be so proud of them all and would have enjoyed them all so much. Hopefully we will all be worthy to be with her in the eternities."
"It seemed impossible that she could be gone so fast. Only the week before the 24th of July, Mama, Daddy, 0s, Rachel, Ted and Maxine were on a trip to South Dakota Black Hills and the passion play, Wind Cave, etc. Daddy said you should have seen her buying t shirts for all the little grand children at wind cave. They said she seemed to feel good, ate good and enjoyed it so much. On Wednesday July 28th she and Daddy went with a group to the Logan Temple.
NORMAN AND LEOLA
It was a lonesome time for Norman after Elva died. He missed her companionship and the many things she did for him. Mary and Joe and Grant and Emma and Lorraine and Merl who were living close by helped fill some of the lonliness but that wasn't enough. The next summer he asked us children if we would fell all right if he dated and remarried at some time. He told us that if we didn't want him to he would remain single. We told him we understood his feelings and that we wanted him to remarry if that would make him happy. Not long after he started going with Leola and they were married November 3, 1966 for time in the Salt Lake Temple. We were pleased at the time of the marriage and have been ever since. The following is the story of Leolo's life and then her and Norman's life together as written by Leola herself.
Leola was born June 2. 1906, In her grandmother Storer's home on Dan Street in Ogden, Utah. She was the second child to come to the family of Charles Manzel and Marzette Storer, the first child being a daughter also, whom they had named Josephine.
As a child Leola was not blessed with the best of health and spent many days just watching the other children play. But she did like to study, and was able to keep up with the others in her schoolwork. When Josephine went to school Leola cried, and she was accepted into kindergarten at the age of four. But the next year she was not old enough to be accepted into first grade, so she spent two years in Kindergarten. This was at the Five Points School in Ogden, later known as the Lincoln School. This extra school preparedness probably paid off, for, as she progressed in her education, she skipped part of the second grade, and, later, part of the sixth grade. She has always been proud of a friendship bracelet she won in a citywide contest for an essay she wrote. She was in the fourth grade at this time. She says she will never forget the fright of a little girl standing at the front of the Ogden Tabernacle while the mayor of Ogden, Abbott R. Heywood, presented her with the silver bracelet. This was the first time Leola had ever been in the tabernacle, and it was so big, and there were so many people.
Dan Street was much like the story of Mrs. Wiggs of The Cabbage Patch. It was a short street with a dead end, and it had only about eight families living there. Everyone's happiness was shared by the others, as well as everyone's problems. A big vacant lot was as a park for recreation, and the neighborhood children played games such as Duck on Rock, Run Sheep run, Kick the Can and others. An old lamplighter came in the evening, in a horse drawn buggy, and changed the arc that gave light to the neighborhood. This was before electricity came to the homes. The first electric light in our home was from a cord hanging from the ceiling with a globe at the end. This was a big improvement over the kerosene lamps we had before. Those lamps were always a job to keep the chimney clean from the smoke, and to fill the lamp with kerosene. They were carried from room to room as light was needed.
When Leola was about four years old the family went to Valley Falls, Kansas, and she met her father's parents and family for the first time. This was a great experience for her and Josephine to get to know her far away grandparents, and her father's brothers, and his sister Lulu. Lulu was only a year and a half older than Leola. While in Kansas the Utahns had a frightening experience when a cyclone struck close to Grandpa Manzel's home. Everyone was rushed to the cyclone cellar til the hard damaging wind subsided. It was a dark and scarey night, but no one was hurt.
About a year later, in October of 1911, the family was hurried from their home, and out into a rainstorm to stand and watch their home, and all the things in it, burn to the ground. Leola got out of the house with one of her new shoes, just bought that day, and one of Josephine's old ones. Now that Leola is in her 70's the thought of a fire burning a home is still a terrible thing in her mind. She had nightmares for weeks after the fire.
Carl was born into the family when Leola was seven years of age, and the two girls almost idolized their little brother. And about two years later they were blessed with another baby brother. But he was not permitted to stay, and died soon after birth. Josephine and Leola held his tiny casket on their laps in the undertaker's car to take him to the Ogden City cemetery for burial. This was on the 9th of December and the weather was cold and stormy.
Not being members of any church, the girls sometimes went to the Congregational Church at Five Points, and sometimes to the Mormon Church, which was held in a frame building much like a small dance hall. Later a nice building was erected at the corner of Five Points between Washington and Harrisville Avenues. And the Manzels were as proud of this new edifice as anyone around, even though they were not members. Dad had paid funds to the building committee, and probably felt it partway belonged to the Manzels.
As a child, Leola spent several summers with her grandma Storer on the dryfarm in Dubois, Idaho. She felt that maybe the family was having a rest with her away, because she admits she that she was a spoiled brat. She remembers a neighbor, Mr. Sherwood, telling her one day that she would be a nice little girl if she weren't so ornery. That remark has stayed with her all through her life, and she has really tried to be a friend to people. She played a record on her grandmother's graphophone that has meant a lot to her. It was to be a theme song for Leola since trying what it said "Have a Smile For Everyone You Meet and Everyone Will Have A Smile For You." Leola says it works too! Grandma would have her walk through the sagebrush to Uncle Fred's all alone, to help her overcome fear. It was a real challenge at times too. When a badger would pop up out of a hole and just stare. She would wonder if he were friend or foe. Then the horned toads would dash by. It was frightening for a little “protected” girl all alone. But those things helped Leola grow in many ways.
Her Father's vacations were most always a few days spent at Grandma Dawson's in Henefer, so Leola enjoyed those days throughout her life. Her mother had grown up in Echo, and was a close friend of Frances Dawson who grew up a few miles away in Henefer. The two young women married two young men from Fremont Nebraska, who also were close friends. Through this close friendship, Frances' parents were as close to the Manzel family as though they were the children's real grandparents, and the children looked forward to the vacation in Henefer.
Her dad was not a religious man in the true sense of the word, but he was a good man with a big heart. Many little stray dog had been brought home by a big policeman because it was all alone and cold. Then mama had to find it a home. On Christmas Santa Claus had left Josephine and Leola each a nice brown sweater. They were so happy and proud of those sweaters. Dad went to work that night following Christmas. Next morning when he came home he asked the girls if they would let him have their sweaters for a little boy he had seen on the street who had no coat. The boy also had a little brother. So the beautiful brown sweaters made four children happy rather than just two little girls. And that was dad many times. He was loved by many.
The family moved from Dan Street about a year after the flu epidemic. And their home now, in 1919 was on Riverside Avenue near 18th Street in Ogden. Soon after the move smallpox struck the family, and Leola had that too. Mama was really bad, but we all survived. Edna Dearden was staying with them at this time and going to school with the girls. She and Josephine managed to either have real light cases if they had the disease at all. Grandma took over while dad was quarantined out of the home, and she put a quilt on frames and taught the girls to quilt while they were quarantined in.
During the World War 1, the United States Government took over many harness shops to make harnesses for their horses. J.G. Read Company in Ogden was one of those chose. Dad had learned the leather and harness trade earlier in his life, so he took leave from his police duties, and worked for the government at this time. After the Armistice was signed the business was returned to private ownership, and dad also gave his job back to return to his police work. He didn't complete one full shift when he was shot in the back of his head while investigating a robbery at a clothing store on 25th street. It was a terrible shock to Mama and her three children when her father and several officers brought the news early on the morning of May 9, 1921. But life must go on. Josephine and Leola were both going to Ogden High School, and Carl was still in elementary. It was hard but the family managed somehow.
Leola has always loved music, and was in the school operas and choruses, as well as in Church activities. She sang in the Ogden Tabernacle choir for some time. Though not yet,a member of the Church, she taught Sunday School in the younger classes. I don't think anyone knew that she was not a member. The home missionaries visited the family often, and Mama, Carl, and Josephine were baptized. But stubborn Leola would not go into the water because an uncle had told her she had to. Again that mean, stubborn German came forth after all those years of fighting it. However, she did go later and became a real member of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter Day Saints. She has always been active since her baptism December 14, 1922.
She has been a teacher in several age groups in Sunday School and in Primary. She has led the music in both of these organist on the ward level as well as on the stake. She has served on Sunday School and Primary Stake Boards in music as well as with teachers. At one time she led a stake chorus in Stake Conference also taking her stake group to sing with other choruses in the Salt Lake Tabernacle. Those were big thrills to Leola. She has directed several road shows, which were rewarding, also has taken part in them herself. And she has been in ward and stake plays. She was Ward Music Director for several Bishops through the years, and has directed choirs in the wards as well as for the stake. This was all fun and enjoyment for Leola. She has always said that if she couldn't have fun doing something she didn't want to do it. And she enjoyed the callings as Jr. Sunday School Coordinator both in the ward and on the stake. Also was happy as Mutual President and Primary President. She says she has worked in everything but Relief Society and Priesthood, but later she was called to teach the Relief Society Cultural Refinement Lesson in Henefer. All those activities have been wonderful experiences making for growth in Leolas life.
Mama remarried after Dad's death, and this was a very unhappy union for the Manzel family. Mama stayed with this man for 12 years before finally a divorce. While Mama was still married to him, Josephine and Leola graduated from Ogden high school together in 1923. Leola was not quite 17 and was too young to get a job. Mama didn't have the money for both girls to go to college, so it was decided that Josephine, being the oldest would go to Weber College. Weber was a two year college at this time. Leola went into a Millinery Store and learned to make hats. Her wages were nothing at first, and as she grew in knowledge of the business, she received $1.00 a week, later making the whole sum of $1.00 a day. She felt very rich at that. After Josephine graduated and began teaching school, she helped Leola enroll at Weber. The two girls have always been close and willing to help each other.
Leola felt very grown up after being out of school for two years, and she started her college and then teaching career with all good intentions. January 1926 changed her plans though as welil as her interest in school. Kenneth Lee Farr had just returned from a three year mission in France. He and Leola met a Weber's Founder's Day Dance in the Ballroom in Weber's new gymnasium. Leola had studied some French, both in High School and in College, and It really came in handy when conversing the French language with a missionary just fresh from using it daily. They would talk, and they would write letters in French to help Leola become more fluent with her words. They were married October 20, 1926 in the Salt Lake LDS Temple, the marriage being performed by Elder George F. Richards, a member of the Quorum of Twelve Apostles.
It was a happy marriage for the young couple, and they were blessed with four children, one daughter and three sons; Leola, named by her father, Kenneth Lee Jr., named by his mother, Owen Talmage, who was taken back to his Heavenly Father May 8. 1935 when but 21 months old. This was a hard thing for Leola to see her baby's life leave him while in his mother's arms. Her husband had had problems with his health for a while before Owen left. In the fall Leola had surgery. She seemed to have her choice then of leaving this life, but felt she was needed to care for the family and fought to stay. The following spring her husband became very ill and spent months in the hospital. Doctors said it was nothing but a miracle that his life was extended. Leola and Kenneth both knew it was the power of the Priesthood.
Finally the doctor gave both of them a clean bill of health and told Leola she could have another child. She had been so lost without her little Owen. David came to them after about a year, and has been a great blessing to his mother. Yes, Kenneth and Leola have both been great blessings too Leola says she doesn't know what she would have ever done without them.
David was only four months old when his daddy was on the way down again in his health, and was back in the hospital. They gave up the home they were buying on 29th Street. They just didn't have the money for the payments anymore. And they lived with Leola's mother for a while. Mama was a great strength and comfort to Leola. She took care of David so much when Leola was needed with her sick husband. Leola has said many times that she feels that Kenneth was spared for this many years for her to have David. She was so thankful for her children as she faced the many trials.
Kenneth was never able to work away from home after David was four months old. Leola went to work at the Defense Depot in Ogden, leaving her sick husband and a tiny two year old boy to care for each other. Many times Leola would come home from work and find Kenneth unconscious on the ground in the garden he tried to have. David would stand by him, and he would say "Mommy, Daddy's had troubles again." David was four years old when his daddy left them in the LDS Hospital in Salt Lake, October 4, 1944, after years of fighting to get well and care for his family. As one speaker said at his funeral services "The race is not to the swift, but to he who endureth to the end." Kenneth was true and faithful throughout all his years of sickness, and he loved his Heavenly Father dearly. And he loved his wife and children.
It was like a big black wall around Leola after Kenneth's' death. She felt she had no way to turn to free herself from this terrible feeling, and she was not able to return to work for about two months. Her daughter Leolat and Kenneth Jr. were both in school, and Leola enrolled David in a nursery. This arrangement lasted about two weeks and David became ill with a bad strep infection in his ear. The doctor said that David, though very young, was suffering from grief as much as from the infection. He had been so close to his daddy. So Leola quit work to care for her little boy.
The following year she went to work in the Quincy Nursery where she enrolled David, She needed to be close to him. After a few months with this arrangement, David became ill again and needed surgery. Strep filled the incision after stitches were out, and Leola fought again for David's life. This lasted for sometime, and soon after his recovery the nursery closed.
Meanwhile Leola Jr. had graduated from high school, and she got employment at the Navy Depot in Clearfield. She was so good to her mother, and she helped her care for the family until her marriage to Edward Roy Jensen in 1947. In the fall of the same year, Kenneth Jr, joined the Navy soon after his 17th birthday. Leola said that again was one of the hardest things she had to do when she signed for him to enlist. He wasn't in the service long before he wished hadn't made this decision. But it isn’t easy to break contract with Uncle Sam. Part of his paycheck he had sent home to help to his family all the time he was in the navy. Leola's children have been wonderful in all ways to her, and she has always appreciated the way they have been. Her children were all three found worthy and were married in the temple. They have all been active in the Church.
Leola had a few trips to the hospital through the years to correct problems, and help her to keep going. One time the doctor told her she may not come out of surgery, but she was just too sick to care. Fate, if one would call it that, had it that she recover after a long seige, for she had many things yet to accomplish in life, She returned to work at one time at the Defense Depot for a short while in what the ladies called "The Sewing Circle." repairing government clothing sheets etc. David was in school by this time and his Aunt Hazel cared for him until his mother got home from work. While working this time Leola was overcome with carbon monoxide gas fumes from the forklifts and as rushed to a doctor in a government ambulance. She still cannot tolerate the gas from a car.
When the Government closed the circle work, Leola had her choice to transfer to Hill Field. But she couldn't make herself leave a little boy so early in the morning to get himself ready for school. She called President Aldous Dixon at Weber College, and inquired about part time work so she could return to school, complete her education to be able to teach. Instead of part time employment, Leola worked fulltime for five years in the Cafeteria. Her nerves and her strength could not take any extra load after all the problems of the years.
The work at the College was for the school year only, and summers she worked with Ogden City Recreation, supervising the Fourth Street Park. This was good, for Leola was with her little boy, was working with an income, and she knew and loved children of all ages for miles around. She says it is really rewarding when those she has worked with, both in recreation and with Church, remember her after years have passed. Kenneth married Marlene Moosman soon after his discharge from the Navy, so Leola and David were together for several years, just the two of them, until he grew up and married Linda Urry. David was in Pre Med at the time he met Linda who was in nursing. His marriage changed his plans of school. When he was small he would catch the street bus after school, and come to his mother and they would go home together after her days work.
The work at the,College became a year round employment for Leola. When the main part of the college work moved to the new campus, the cafeteria was closed and Leola worked in the bookstore for a year. Then she was asked to take charge of the Receiving, Shipping, Stores and Inventory Department. The man who was in charge had an accident and was in the hospital. This was a humbling and frightening experience for Leola who knew nothing about this department. She said that she would freeze everytime the phone rang, or someone would walk in the door. She learned, and she made it through. The inventory clerk returned and Leola stayed with him as Assistant Inventory Clerk for 13 years until Norman Richins took her from it in 1966.
Leola's schooling at Weber College was mostly her recreation. She enjoyed a class in ceramics for about ten years, giving away about everything she made in pottery and in porcelain. She studied vocal with Roland Parry and did a lot of singing over the year ' s. both in duets and trios, and solos. She sang at many funeral services, as well as other functions. She has always loved music as was mentioned before.
Her marriage to Norman Tipper Richins came to her as a complete surprise. She laughs when she tells of her first date with him after yeari of never seeing him. She had been active every year in the production of All Faces West. This was about the thirteenth year for Leola. The afternoon following the last performance, the night before, she answered the telephone and it was Norman Richins calling. She knew he wanted a date and she didn't want a date. After 22 years of declining dates and finding excuses this call was different. And she fought the feeling hard and fast. What could she say? I don't want a date, no I don't, No I don't, She said something just smacked her and told her she couldn't hurt Norman's feelings. That did it, and Leola's years of loneliness and working her own way, completely changed. And when Norman said "I would like you to have dinner with me," she said "I would love to." And she meant it. This was on July 25 1966. They were married in the Salt Lake Temple on November 3, 1966.
When Norman came walking across the lawn to the house he looked so handsome with white hair on each side of his face. But he also looked like the same young man who had taken Leola to dances, shows, etc. years before. After 40 years of seeing each other but three times, the friendship of youth seemed to pick up just where it left off. Leola jokes now and says that Norman didn't like her when they were young, and waited until she was old and wrinkled, also that he thought maybe she had mellowed with age. But he found out differently. She has tried to make his life happy, and he has meant a lot to her. They have had a happy marriage. People have asked Leola what about it? You are sealed to your first husband Kenneth, yet you think so much of Norman. Her answer is always that it makes no difference at all in her love for Kenneth. She holds both of them on a high pedistal side by side, and she feels way below both of them. She knows when this life is finished so shall be her life with Norman, and she is enjoying him every minute while she is with him, and trying to make him happy. For years people would try to find friends for Leola before Norman came again into her life, and she was not interested. She never said, "I won't marry again," for one never knows what is ahead. But she did say that if she ever did remarry she would carry her husband on a pillow and take special care of him. And that has been the way Leola has tried to be with Norman.
Leola's children had an open house for the newlyweds after their marriage. Then the two went on a honeymoon trip to the Hawaiian Islands. This was a beautiful experience for both of them. But Norman was taken by ambulance to a hospital on the Island of Kawaii with food poisoning. Another frightening thing for Leola, but things came out right again.

Her new life in Henefer has been different in many ways from the life Leola had lived in Ogden all the years before. But Norman had been good to her, and her main interest and thoughts have been for his happiness and welfare. They have had some really nice experiences together. One was while Grant was in the University in Cincinnati. Norman and Leola flew there, and then visited the Southern States with Grant and Family, going to Disney World and Cape Canaveral as well as many other places down that way. They have been to Yellowstone Park, and they have made trips to Colorado, also to Texas with a group for a Soil Conservation Convention. This was a lot of fun going on a bus all the way. They had a beautiful trip on the Caribbean Sea aboard a big cruise ship, the Amerikanis, and visiting several islands in that area, also going to Trinadad and back to Caracus, Venenzuela.
The most rewarding thing the couple have done was hen they accepted a call from the Prophet to fulfill an LDS Mission in Oregon. Leola has said she wouldn't trade this experience for anything she has ever done. They labored right on the Pacific Coast for 18 months, living in Seaside the full time there.
They have also been Ordinace Workers for nearly three years in he Ogden Temple, having to be released due to Norman"s health problems. He gave Leola a terrible fright in October 1984 when the two went to Seaside to be with their loved ones in the mission field. The Little Branch of the Church had just completed a beautiful new Chapel and wanted Norman and Leola to come to the dedication. They never made it to the Chapel for Norman's heart stopped working while he was driving the car. Our dear Heavenly Father spared his life and he was able to return home.
Leola has been active in the Henefer Ward, and also the Summit Stake (now the Coalville Stake) since she came to make her home in Henefer. Her life has been full in many ways. She now has 14 grandchildren and 30 great grandchildren of her own. Norman has 29 grandchildren and 30 great grandchildren. Leola tells Norman's that she may not be their blood grandma but she wants them to know that she is their love grandma. And she does love them as her own. Her husband and her children are her main interests, and she says she hopes that she will be found worthy to be with them after her life is completed on earth.
