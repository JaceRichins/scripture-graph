# Caroline Hogan Hyer

_A story of [[Caroline Hogan (KWJX-KS2)|Caroline Hogan]]._

 When Caroline was six years old, her father decided to move his family to America to better their economic prospects. They sailed on the vessel, “Niord,” from Gothenburg in Sweden and arrived in New York harbor on August 15, 1837.
 They traveled first to Chicago, Illinois, for a short time, then on to a farming section near Ottowa, LaSalle County, Illinois. Sometime after 1840, they moved to Lee County, Iowa, where an Elder of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints brought them the gospel in the year 1843.
 After joining the Church, they moved to Keokuk, Illinois. In 1846, the family sold their farm in Keokuk in order to leave the Nauvoo area for Garden Grove, Iowa, where a resting camp had been built. They proceeded to Council Bluffs in 1847. They went to a place called Plumb Hollow where they lived for a year and a half. The Hogan family was part of Brigham Young’s Second division as they traveled to the mountains of the west under Zera Pulsipher. Brigham Young had returned to Winter Quarters and, leaving the city abandoned, took 2,400 exited Saints back to the Salt Lake Valley. The huge train had proven so cumbersome that it was split into three groups of teams and travelers to take the best advantage of the available water and forage for the animals. They arrived in Salt Lake Valley on September 21, 1848, settling in South Bountiful which became Woods Cross, Utah.
 Two years after arriving into the Salt Lake Valley, seventeen year old Caroline married Christian Larsen Hyer and they settled in a little log house near her parents. While Christian farmed and hauled wood from the canyon for fuel or building, Caroline cared for the house, spun their yarn, made clothing, gardened and raised the children. Caroline accepted the hard role of sharing her husband with her sister, Lovina.
In 1859, they were sent to Cache Valley (Richmond) to see if it was suitable for settlers. Their first home was inside the fort for protection from the Indians. The second was a dugout on the twenty acres they were working for a farm. It had walls lined with rock and a dirt floor, later a permanent home was built, she was determined to have it neat, orderly and clean. Not an easy goal with raising eleven children.
Caroline was a sweet refined lady, a good cook and had to be organized and industrious to keep such good care of her family. She had a talent for fine handwork. She did beautiful embroidery work. She braided straw, made hats, and trimmed them. She also made caps of jean material for her family. The cloth for the children’s dresses and suits was spun and woven by her. She raised trees with delicious tasting apples. The children were taught by good example. Caroline polished her stove until is shone like new. Hand woven rugs covered clean scrubbed floors and the front porch, given as much attention as the inside of the home, was scrubbed so often it was almost bleached white.
Christian died in 1901, when Caroline was seventy years old. She was a widow for sixteen years. She was generous in having others come to live with her. When she could no longer care for herself, she went to live with her daughter, Ellen Titensor in Cove. Caroline passed away on a cold December day in 1917.
Taken from “Pioneer Women of Faith and Fortitude”, Vol. II, pgs. 1339 and 1478-79
