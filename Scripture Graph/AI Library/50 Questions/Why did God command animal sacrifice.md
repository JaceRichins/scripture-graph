# Why did God command animal sacrifice?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Scripture supplies several complementary answers, not one single reason, and readers should keep observation, interpretation, and faith-claims distinct. The clearest explicit stated rationale in the canon is Moses 5:6-7: Adam is commanded to sacrifice, admits he does not know why beyond the command, and an angel explains the offering is "a similitude of the sacrifice of the Only Begotten." Genesis itself narrates Cain, Abel, and Noah's offerings (Genesis 4:3-5; 8:20-21) without recording either a command or a stated meaning — that explicit rationale belongs to Restoration scripture, not the Bible's own wording. Within the law of Moses, sacrifice serves several distinct, textually attested functions rather than one mechanism: atonement and forgiveness (Leviticus 17:11; 4:27-35), priestly consecration (Exodus 29), thanksgiving and fellowship (Leviticus 7:11-15; Exodus 18), and deliverance-memorial (Exodus 12). The Book of Mormon and New Testament converge on a Christ-centered fulfillment: the law was a "shadow of things to come" (Mosiah 13/16), pointed to a "great and last sacrifice" (Alma 34:9-14), and blood sacrifice was formally ended by the risen Christ, replaced by "a broken heart and a contrite spirit" (3 Nephi 9:19-20; cf. Hebrews 10:1-14; 1 Corinthians 5:7). What is established: sacrifice was divinely commanded, treated as meaningful, served multiple distinct functions, and ended at the Atonement. What remains open: how clearly ancient worshipers themselves understood a messianic referent, how the sacrificial legislation historically developed, and how strongly the prophetic critique texts (Isaiah 1:11; Hosea 6:6; 1 Samuel 15:22) qualify any claim that God delighted in the practice itself rather than tolerating it for the sake of obedience and instruction.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Explicit stated rationale**: Moses 5:6-7 has an angel tell Adam his sacrifice "is a similitude of the sacrifice of the Only Begotten of the Father" — the clearest textual statement of purpose in the canon, though unparalleled in Genesis's own wording (Genesis 4:3-5).
- **Atonement grounded in the legal text itself**: Leviticus 17:11 states blood "maketh an atonement for the soul" because "the life of the flesh is in the blood," giving the law's own internal rationale rather than leaving it to inference.
- **The system served several distinct functions, not one**: Exodus 12 (Passover, deliverance/memorial), Exodus 29 (differentiated sin/burnt/consecration offerings), and Leviticus 7:11-15 (thanksgiving peace offering) together rule out any single-mechanism theory.
- **Typology extended to a pre-Sinai episode**: Jacob 4:5 calls Abraham's offering "a similitude of God and his Only Begotten Son"; Genesis 22:13 independently narrates a ram given "in the stead of" Isaac, though Jacob's own wording does not mention the ram itself.
- **Explicit cessation at fulfillment**: Amulek's "great and last sacrifice" (Alma 34:9-14, not v.10 alone) and Christ's command that blood sacrifice "be done away" (3 Nephi 9:19-20) tie the practice's end directly to the Atonement.
- **New Testament typological confirmation**: Paul calls Christ "our passover... sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7); John notes Jesus's unbroken bones (John 19:36), though the scriptural source John has in mind is ambiguous between Exodus 12:46, Numbers 9:12, and Psalm 34:20.
- **Structural link between priesthood and law**: Hebrews 7:11-19 (not v.12 alone) argues a changed priesthood requires a changed law, of which sacrifice is one consequence.
- **Prophetic critique as a real counterweight**: 1 Samuel 15:22, Isaiah 1:11-17, Hosea 6:6, and Micah 6:6-8 insist ritual cannot substitute for obedience, mercy, or justice — evidence the canon itself resists a mechanical, ritual-for-its-own-sake reading.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The strongest explicit rationale is not in the Bible's own words.** Genesis narrates Cain, Abel, and Noah's offerings with no stated command and no stated meaning; the "similitude of Christ" explanation (Moses 5:7) is a Restoration text with no biblical verbal counterpart, so a reader working from the Bible alone has no explicit stated reason for the earliest sacrifices.
- **Prophetic critique in real tension with the typological account.** Isaiah 1:11, Amos 5:21-22, Hosea 6:6, and 1 Samuel 15:22 subordinate or seemingly reject sacrifice relative to obedience, mercy, and justice — read in isolation, these sound less like "sacrifice is a meaningful type of Christ" and more like a concession God tolerated rather than desired.
- **The moral objection.** Deliberately killing animals in worship can appear cruel or unnecessary if an all-powerful God could teach repentance and reconciliation without blood; no cited text directly defends animal suffering as such.
- **Retrospective typology.** Israel's sacrificial laws ordinarily discuss atonement, purity, consecration, and festivals without naming Jesus; Paul and Hebrews unmistakably read them through Christ, but this may reflect later Christian rereading rather than the earliest rites' conscious meaning. Hebrews 10:4 itself denies that animal blood can finally remove sins.
- **Unexplained mechanics.** Exodus 29 commands hand-laying repeatedly but never states its meaning; only Leviticus 16:20-22 (the scapegoat) explicitly narrates confession and transfer, and that reading should not be assumed for every hand-laying rite.
- **Comparative ancient religion and composite legislation.** Animal sacrifice was a near-universal ancient Near Eastern practice, and Exodus/Leviticus's specific forms plausibly draw on shared regional ritual vocabulary; mainstream scholarship also treats Pentateuchal sacrificial law as composite, assembled across periods rather than delivered whole at one moment. The canon does not address either point directly, and neither was sourced to named scholarship in this reading.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- The prophetic critique texts are best read in context: Isaiah 1 indicts a people whose "hands are full of blood" (injustice) even as they sacrifice; Amos 5 demands "judgment run down as waters" alongside its rejection of empty feasts; 1 Samuel 15:22 rebukes one specific disobedience, not sacrifice generally. None abolishes the system itself, and the same books elsewhere assume its ongoing legitimacy.
- Moses 5:7 lacking a Genesis parallel does not make it isolated: its typological content coheres with, and is echoed by, Jacob 4:5, Abinadi's "shadow of things to come" (Mosiah 13/16), and Amulek's "great and last sacrifice" (Alma 34). This is best understood as one coherent Restoration-scripture theological program restating itself across books — real internal coherence, though not several independent ancient witnesses converging by surprise.
- Differentiated offerings (sin, burnt, thanksgiving, Passover) do not by themselves disprove one overarching purpose; distinct proximate rites can still serve a single ultimate covenantal or christological end, even though the text's own diversity blocks any flattened single-mechanism account.
- Shared ancient Near Eastern ritual vocabulary does not exclude divine command. Mosiah 3:14-15 itself frames the Mosaic law as an accommodation to "a stiffnecked people" — God can be understood as instructing Israel through culturally available ritual forms while still genuinely commanding the practice and investing it with covenant and typological meaning.
- The text's silence on ritual mechanics limits certainty about *how* sacrifice functioned but does not defeat *that* it was commanded and purposeful; Hebrews' denial of animal blood's final efficacy (10:4) is presented within Hebrews as the reason the rites function as anticipatory shadows, not as evidence the system was pointless.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text itself requires, independent of assumption.** Sacrifice predates Sinai in the narratives (Genesis 4; 8:20-21; Moses 5), so a Mosaic-only explanation is incomplete. Different offerings have different immediate, textually stated functions, so no single mechanism should be forced onto all of them. Genesis records early offerings without a stated command or meaning; only Moses 5 supplies both. Prophetic critique attaches to corrupt reliance on ritual, not necessarily to sacrifice as an institution.

1. **Multi-function covenant ritual — independently supported by uncontested legal text**: Exodus 12, 18, 29 and Leviticus 1, 4, 7, 16 present sacrifice as atonement, purification, consecration, thanksgiving, fellowship, and memorial. Best-attested model for the system's diversity; does not answer why animal death specifically was chosen.
2. **Anticipatory Christ-symbol — supported within the Restoration canon, on different evidentiary footing than model 1**: Moses 5:5-8, Jacob 4:5, Alma 34:9-14, and 3 Nephi 9:19-20 form an explicit command-to-fulfillment line, echoed in the New Testament (1 Corinthians 5:7; Hebrews 9-10). Unlike model 1, its earliest explicit statement (Moses 5) is a modern revelatory text of contested antiquity, and its New Testament leg is openly retrospective — real canonical support, resting on accepting Moses 5's authority rather than uncontested ancient text alone.
3. **Pedagogical accommodation to a formed people — plausible, textually stated for the Mosaic law**: Mosiah 3:14-15's "stiffnecked people" language; Galatians 3:19-25's temporary custodial role. Does not by itself explain pre-Mosaic sacrifice.
4. **Covenant gift, communion, and gratitude — plausible**: Jethro's sacrifice and shared meal (Exodus 18:8-12) and the thanksgiving peace offering (Leviticus 7:11-15) show non-expiatory functions; cannot alone explain sin offerings or the Day of Atonement.
5. **Substitution or representative bearing — possible but limited**: Leviticus 16:20-22 explicitly transfers confessed iniquity to the scapegoat; extending that logic to every hand-laying rite (e.g., Exodus 29, which never explains the gesture) is not textually warranted and risks becoming ad hoc as a universal model.
6. **Obedience-test — possible, weakly supported alone**: Genesis 22:1; Moses 5:6. Real but insufficient, since the text immediately supplies a content-full answer (model 2) beyond bare obedience.
7. **Prophetic relativization — supported, in real tension with model 2**: Isaiah 1:11-17; Amos 5:21-24; Hosea 6:6; 1 Samuel 15:22. Pushed to an extreme this shades into a claim that sacrifice was tolerated rather than intrinsically desired.
8. **Comparative ancient Near Eastern religious practice — plausible, not addressed by the canon**: animal sacrifice was a near-universal ANE institution; Israel's system plausibly adapted shared regional ritual forms (unblemished victims, altar structures, priestly mediation) later invested with covenant-specific and, in Christian/Restoration reading, christological meaning. The text neither confirms nor rules this out and was not evaluated against named comparative scholarship here.
9. **Composite legislative history — plausible per mainstream scholarship, unsourced here**: Pentateuchal sacrificial law may be assembled across periods rather than delivered as one unified command; a claim about transmission history, separable from whether God commanded sacrifice at some point in it.

**Set aside as ad hoc**: a literal divine need for food or death itself (contradicted by Psalm 50:9-13 and by model 7's prophetic texts); a strict penal-substitution mechanism treating every offering's guilt-transfer as identical to the scapegoat (model 5 overextended); pure health/sanitation motives (no textual support).
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established** across independent canonical witnesses: animal sacrifice was divinely commanded, not a human invention merely tolerated; it served multiple distinct functions (atonement, consecration, thanksgiving, memorial) rather than one mechanism; and it was formally ended at Christ's death, replaced in Restoration teaching by the sacrament. **Open**: how clearly ordinary ancient Israelites understood a messianic referent in the ritual is not established by the text — the clearest explicit Christ-typology statement (Moses 5:7) is a modern revelatory text, and the New Testament's typological reading is openly retrospective; the sacrificial legislation's historical origin and compositional development (single revealed command vs. composite legal growth vs. adapted ANE practice) is likewise unresolved; and the prophetic critique texts (Isaiah, Amos, Hosea, Samuel) genuinely complicate any claim that God straightforwardly desired sacrifice rather than accommodating it. **A matter of faith**: that the typological explanation given in Moses 5:7 and echoed by Jacob, Abinadi, and Amulek is the true, God-given reason for the practice, rather than a later theological interpretation, cannot be adjudicated by textual or historical method alone and rests on accepting the Restoration canon's revelatory authority.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Read [[Moses 5]] first, especially Moses 5:4-8, while comparing Genesis 4:3-5 so the distinct textual claims remain visible.

- Study the rites separately rather than treating “sacrifice” as one ceremony: [[Exodus 12]] for [[Passover]]; [[Exodus 29]] for priestly consecration; [[Leviticus 1]] and [[Leviticus 4]] for burnt and sin offerings; [[Leviticus 7]] for thanksgiving; [[Leviticus 16]] for the Day of Atonement; and Leviticus 17:10-12 for the blood-life rationale.

- Trace the canon's self-critique through 1 Samuel 15:22, Psalm 50:7-15; 51:16-19, Isaiah 1:11-17, Hosea 6:6, and Micah 6:6-8. [[Obedience Brings Blessings (President Thomas S. Monson, April 2013)]] uses Samuel's contrast between obedience and sacrifice.

- Follow the Latter-day Saint interpretive line through 2 Nephi 25:24-27, Jacob 4:5, Mosiah 3:14-15; 13:27-32; 16:14, Alma 34:9-14, and [[3 Nephi 9]]. Then compare 1 Corinthians 5:7, Hebrews 9-10, and Galatians 3:19-25.

- For official Latter-day Saint synthesis, read [[Sacrifice (Gospel Topics)]], [[Sacrifice (True to the Faith)]], [[Thoughts to Keep in Mind The Tabernacle and Sacrifice]], [[Sacrament (Gospel Topics)]], and [[Chapter 15 The Sacrament of the Lord’s Supper]]. Treat their claims as doctrinal interpretation and identify which depend specifically on Moses 5.

- For scholarship on ritual meaning and competing historical models, consult Jacob Milgrom, *Leviticus 1–16*; Jonathan Klawans, *Purity, Sacrifice, and the Temple*; and William K. Gilders, *Blood Ritual in the Hebrew Bible*. Compare their arguments with the primary texts rather than using “ancient sacrifice” as a single undifferentiated category.
