# Did Joseph Smith copy the Book of Mormon from other books?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
It depends on what "copy" means. No specific nonbiblical source book has been demonstrated: Solomon Spalding's surviving manuscript (rediscovered in 1884, now at Oberlin College) is a different-style Roman-era romance with different names and no Nephite theology, so the classic Spalding theory survives only by positing a second, lost manuscript no one has seen; Ethan Smith's *View of the Hebrews* (1823) shares only the general Israelite-origins idea common in the region, not the Book of Mormon's narrative, names, or theology. Separately, and much more solidly established, the received English text is pervasively mediated by King James Bible language — a different and better-attested phenomenon than borrowing from a rival contemporary manuscript, addressed on its own terms at [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Isaiah and the King James Bible]]. A broader thesis that Joseph Smith synthesized ideas circulating in his 1820s environment, without transcribing any single text, remains a serious alternative to antiquity and is not refuted merely by disproving Spalding and View of the Hebrews. See [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] and [[Translation of the Book of Mormon]].
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Biblical reuse is established.** Extended Isaiah and Matthew material, the explicit engagement with John 10 in [[3 Nephi 15]], and the close Mark 16:16-17/Ether 4:18 sequence collectively establish pervasive biblical and often KJV-shaped mediation. This is the strongest evidence that earlier textual language entered the received English; it does not determine whether the mechanism was direct composition, loose translation, revelation, or editorial expansion. [[Book of Mormon Assessment]]

- **The final text is deliberately designed.** [[1 Nephi 6]], [[1 Nephi 9]], and [[Words of Mormon 1]] distinguish records and explain editorial choices; prophecy-fulfillment sequences and internal callbacks are likewise coordinated. These observations rule out treating the book as merely random verbal debris, but one final narrative controller can produce them in both ancient and modern models.

- **The named nonbiblical source theories are much weaker.** The extant Spalding manuscript supplies no demonstrated chain of distinctive wording, plot, and transmission into the 1829 text. *View of the Hebrews* is a genuine cultural comparison, especially for Israelite-origin speculation, but its broad parallels do not document Joseph's readership or dependence. [[B. H. Roberts]] treated those parallels as a serious test, not as documentary proof that copying occurred.

- **Production testimony has limited relevance.** Emma Smith's retrospective statement that Joseph used no manuscript or book during sessions she observed weighs against continuous visible copying, while leaving prior preparation, memory, oral composition, concealed influence, collaboration, and revelatory translation open. [[Book of Mormon Translation (Essay)]] should be read alongside critical production histories.

- **The ancient-source evidence is limited but not zero.** Nahom/NHM, if-and syntax, and [[Alma 36]] structure are substantially independent weak lines. Their convergence should not be dismissed one item at a time, but it remains too weak to establish ancient authorship or to neutralize the KJV-mediation problem. See [[Is Nahom evidence for Lehi's journey]] and [[Do chiasmus and Hebraisms show the Book of Mormon is ancient]].
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- A nineteenth-century composition could synthesize the KJV, sermons, popular histories, Indigenous-origin speculation, revival culture, and republican themes without copying any one book verbatim; failure to find a master source would not defeat this naturalistic model.
- Long, near-verbatim stretches of KJV Isaiah and Matthew appear in the text. Whatever the mechanism, this is textual dependence on an available printed book the text does not fully explain on its own terms. See [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Malachi before Malachi wrote]] and [[Why does the Book of Mormon reproduce King James italics]].
- View of the Hebrews was published in Poultney, Vermont, where Ethan Smith served as minister and where Oliver Cowdery's family is historically associated; this geographic proximity is an uncomfortable coincidence for treating the parallels as pure chance, even though proximity alone does not establish that Cowdery transmitted the book's contents to Joseph Smith.
- General cultural influence rarely leaves a receipt: Joseph could have absorbed View of the Hebrews's ideas orally or through intermediaries even without direct proof he read it.
- Dictation without a visible manuscript is not decisive: skilled oral composition, prior outlining, memorized biblical language, or collaboration before witnessed sessions could all produce fluent dictation, and retrospective witnesses had religious and family interests.
- Internal editorial complexity, chiasms, and prediction-fulfillment pairs can result from deliberate modern authorship as easily as ancient compilation; treating literary achievement as proof of antiquity is circular.
- The positive ancient-provenance cluster (Nahom, if-and syntax, Alma 36 chiasmus) remains weak: Nahom lacks a securely identified burial locality and denominator, proposed Hebraisms have English alternatives, and ring composition is not uniquely Hebrew.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- A thoughtful believer can acknowledge the KJV evidence without claiming source-transparent English. A loose or revelatory translation could render ancient material in Joseph's scriptural register, just as translation can adopt familiar target-language phraseology. This is compatible with the evidence, although compatibility alone does not give it equal predictive strength.

- The Book of Mormon itself represents scripture reuse as intentional: Nephi reads Moses and Isaiah and “likens” them to his people in 1 Nephi 19:22-23. Biblical intertextuality can therefore serve literary and theological purposes rather than functioning only as concealed borrowing. That internal explanation does not settle how nineteenth-century English wording arose.

- The sustained recordkeeping framework in [[1 Nephi 6]], [[1 Nephi 9]], [[Jacob 1]], and [[Words of Mormon 1]], together with complex arguments and callbacks, warrants taking the book seriously as composed literature. It raises the explanatory burden for simplistic claims of hurried patchwork, but not for capable oral composition or planned modern authorship.

- The independent weak lines in the ancient direction should be retained at their actual weight. [[Nahom]], manuscript syntax, and [[Alma 36]] do not prove antiquity, but their different data and failure modes mean they cannot fairly be collapsed into one coincidence. [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] nevertheless judges their convergence weak.

- Witness and manuscript evidence may make some forms of visible copying less natural. It does not justify saying that witnesses observed every stage of preparation or that their testimony proves the plates. The disputed Anthon episode should be handled through [[Embedded Secondhand Testimony in Joseph Smith—History 1]] and [[Source Date And Proximity in Joseph Smith—History 1]], not used as a shortcut to authenticity.

- No established nonbiblical exemplar means accusations should be stated precisely: nineteenth-century composition is a serious explanatory family, but calling Joseph a plagiarist from a named book requires evidence of dependence, direction, access, and transmission that broad parallels alone do not supply.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** the received English Book of Mormon is extensively mediated through biblical, often KJV-shaped, language; its final form displays deliberate literary and editorial coordination; and Joseph Smith and the canonical histories represented the work as a translation from plates rather than as ordinary composition. Those propositions describe the English text and historical claims, not their ultimate cause.

**Not established:** no supplied evidence demonstrates that Joseph copied the Book of Mormon from Spalding, *View of the Hebrews*, *The Late War*, or another particular nonbiblical book. Conversely, failure to identify a master source does not refute nineteenth-century KJV-informed composition. The present corpus has not adequately assessed all source-use, oral-dictation, collaboration, witness, archaeology, anachronism, or population evidence needed for an overall provenance verdict.

**Open:** an ancient substrate rendered through loose revelatory translation remains possible and receives limited support from several independent weak lines. An ancient-core/modern-expansion model is also possible but risks becoming ad hoc without criteria for its layers. A nineteenth-century KJV-informed production family presently explains the observed English mediation and final-form control economically, but the registry does not establish it as the exclusive history of the whole book.

**Matter of faith:** whether God revealed, authorized, or spiritually empowered the book is not settled by identifying its language, date, or historical substrate. Ancient provenance would not prove inspiration, and modern composition would not logically disprove it. The calibrated cumulative judgment is [[Book of Mormon Assessment]].
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text itself requires.** The final text presents itself as a translation of ancient records preserved and abridged by named custodians; Joseph's canonical history likewise claims plates and divine translation in Joseph Smith—History 1:34, 59, and 62. The text also openly quotes, interprets, and applies earlier scripture. It does **not** describe the English translation mechanism closely enough to require source-transparent wording, exclude KJV-shaped phrasing, or identify where translation ends and expansion begins. Conversely, its self-description is an internal historical claim, not independent proof that the represented plates, editors, and events existed. Arguments that “translation must contain no nineteenth-century language” and that “an elaborate editorial persona must be ancient” attach to assumptions rather than textual requirements.

- **Final-form literary work with pervasive biblical/KJV mediation — independently supported.** Extended quotations, distinctive phrase sequences, explicit biblical interpretation, editorial notices, and internal callbacks establish this origin-neutral baseline. It strongly challenges a KJV-independent or source-transparent English model. [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] treats this as the strongest present textual conclusion.

- **Ancient historical record rendered through a KJV-shaped loose or revelatory translation — plausible, but only weakly independently supported.** This accepts the book's represented ancient history while allowing familiar biblical English to mediate the translation. Nahom, if-and syntax, and [[Alma 36]] modestly raise the model. Its principal cost is that KJV compatibility is not itself prospective evidence for antiquity; the model needs principled criteria explaining why and where harmonization occurs.

- **Ancient core with substantial translation-level or modern expansion — possible.** A layered process could combine ancient narrative material with later Christian formulations and KJV wording. No surviving source-language Book of Mormon manuscript independently identifies the proposed layers. The model becomes **ad hoc** when expansion is invoked only wherever evidence causes difficulty rather than being identified by prior textual criteria.

- **Ancient record with ancient editorial layers — possible as one stage, not a complete production model.** The text portrays record succession and abridgment in [[1 Nephi 9]], [[Jacob 1]], and [[Words of Mormon 1]]. Those notices establish the final text's characterization of its history, not the external existence of the editors; a translation and English-mediation model is still required.

- **Nineteenth-century KJV-informed composition by Joseph Smith, including oral composition — plausible.** Known KJV availability, pervasive biblical English, nineteenth-century conceptual comparisons, and unified final-form control fit this family economically. It does not require a single copied book. The present registry has not sufficiently assessed oral-dictation capacity, advance preparation, source access, or all production testimony to establish this as the unique account.

- **Nineteenth-century synthesis with collaborators or multiple sources — possible.** This predicts environmental and textual affinities without requiring long verbatim dependence on one book. Specific collaboration claims require evidence connecting named people and sources to the production process. A generic collaborator can become an unfalsifiable placeholder if invoked without such evidence.

- **Spalding-Rigdon derivation — logically possible in a generalized form but presently unsupported; a lost-second-manuscript rescue is ad hoc.** The extant Spalding manuscript does not demonstrate the necessary textual relationship or transmission chain. Adding a materially different lost manuscript and undocumented delivery pathway preserves the theory only by multiplying unattested elements. See [[Solomon Spalding]], [[Sidney Rigdon]], [[Philastus Hurlbut]], and [[Eber D. Howe]].

- **Direct dependence on *View of the Hebrews* — possible but not independently supported.** Shared Israelite-origin interests make comparison legitimate, and indirect cultural influence is plausible. Direct copying requires distinctive correspondences and an access pathway that the supplied evidence does not establish. See [[Ethan Smith]] and [[B. H. Roberts]].

- **Dependence on pseudo-biblical works such as *The Late War* — possible but unestablished here.** Shared archaizing diction is predicted by a genre in which several authors imitate the KJV. A convincing dependence case would need rare shared sequences, directionality, access, and controls showing that the parallels exceed the background rate in comparable texts.

- **Inspired modern scripture without a substantially ancient historical substrate — possible as a theological model.** This allows genuine revelation or spiritual authority while treating much of the narrated history as modern revelatory composition. Historical methods can test its claims about date and dependence but cannot directly adjudicate divine inspiration.

- **Purely accidental biblical resemblance — set aside.** The extent and specificity of the English relationships make accident an inadequate explanation.

- **A single identified nonbiblical master source — set aside as presently unestablished, not declared impossible.** Neither the supplied corpus nor the named comparisons demonstrate such a source. This finding must not be expanded into the claim that cultural influence, preparation, or unrecognized sources were absent.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
