# Was Jesus born of a virgin?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Latter-day Saint canon's answer is yes. [[Matthew 1]] and [[Luke 1]] explicitly present Mary as conceiving Jesus without prior intercourse and attribute the conception to divine action. [[1 Nephi 11]] identifies a virgin as the mother of the Son of God; [[Mosiah 3]] names Mary as his mother without itself mentioning virginity; and [[Alma 7]] gives the most complete Restoration-scripture formulation. These texts and the supplied Latter-day Saint teachings settle what the tradition affirms. They do not independently demonstrate the biological event to every historical method: Matthew and Luke are the only New Testament writings that narrate it, their surrounding infancy accounts differ substantially, and their underlying sources cannot now be identified. Historical study can assess the texts and competing explanations, but acceptance that God caused the conception remains a reasoned matter of faith rather than a universally demonstrable conclusion.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Matthew's direct assertion:** Matthew 1:18-25 says Mary was pregnant before she and Joseph came together, attributes the conception to the Holy Ghost, and states that Joseph did not know her before Jesus's birth.

- **Luke's direct assertion:** Luke 1:27, 34-35 identifies Mary as a virgin, records her question about not knowing a man, and answers with the action of the Holy Ghost and the power of the Highest.

- **A shared narrative core:** Despite major differences in their infancy narratives, Matthew and Luke agree on Mary, conception before marital intercourse, Joseph's nonbiological role, divine causation, and the name Jesus. This establishes two distinct canonical presentations; it does not prove independent eyewitness sources.

- **Restoration-scripture affirmation:** [[1 Nephi 11]] supplies virgin motherhood, Mosiah 3:8 supplies Mary's name, and Alma 7:10 combines Mary's name, virginity, overshadowing, and conception by the Holy Ghost. This is strong evidence for what the canon teaches, while its independent historical force depends on judgments about Book of Mormon provenance.

- **Documented Latter-day Saint teaching:** The 1925 and 1926 conference reports and the cited 2008 and 2013 addresses explicitly affirm the virgin birth. They establish institutional teaching, not independent biological proof.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **Limited New Testament attestation:** Only Matthew and Luke narrate virginal conception. Mark begins with Jesus as an adult, John teaches incarnation without describing conception, and Paul's extant letters never explicitly mention Mary's virginity.

- **Davidic-descent tension:** Romans 1:3 describes Jesus as being of David's seed according to the flesh. In a patrilineal setting this can be read as tension with virginal conception, although the formula does not discuss conception mechanics and can be interpreted through legal descent or inherited traditional language.

- **Substantially different infancy narratives:** Matthew emphasizes Joseph, magi, Herod, Egypt, and later settlement in Nazareth; Luke emphasizes Mary, a census, shepherds, the temple, and return to Nazareth. Many differences are omissions rather than direct contradictions, but a full harmony requires supplying chronology and movements neither author states.

- **Isaiah's immediate context:** Isaiah 7 addresses Ahaz during an eighth-century political crisis, and the Hebrew almah does not unambiguously specify virginity. Matthew's citation establishes his Christological interpretation, not by itself Isaiah's sole original referent or the event's historicity.

- **Theological-development model:** The stories may have developed from reflection on divine sonship, scripture, and biblical birth patterns rather than from remembered biological history.

- **Historical inaccessibility:** The alleged conception was private, left no recoverable physical evidence, and is narrated decades later in theological works. Historical analysis can compare explanations but cannot directly inspect the event or identify divine causation.

- **Conditional Restoration corroboration:** Internal pre-Christian dates do not independently decide whether Book of Mormon passages represent ancient prophecy, inspired translation language, later shaping, or modern composition. Alma 7:10 also says Jesus would be born 'at Jerusalem,' a geographic formulation that critics regard as imprecise because the Gospel narratives identify Bethlehem; this does not refute its virginity statement but bears on claims that the verse is independent historical corroboration.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- Silence is not denial. Mark, John, and Paul have different purposes and omit many biographical details, although their silence still limits claims of broad early attestation.

- Romans 1:3 does not specify a father or conception mechanism. Legal Davidic descent through Joseph, descent through Mary, or use of a traditional formula are possible responses, but none is explicitly explained in the verse.

- Matthew and Luke's shared core is more specific than a generic miraculous-birth theme. Their different presentations may point to distinct traditions, but agreement cannot prove that the traditions were independent or historically accurate.

- Most narrative differences are not explicit denials. A combined chronology is possible, but 'can be harmonized' must not be confused with 'the authors state the harmony.'

- Matthew can employ Isaiah typologically or as fuller fulfillment even if Isaiah first addressed Ahaz's crisis. That defends Matthew's theological reading without making the Hebrew lexical question disappear.

- If divine action is possible, virginal conception is not intrinsically incoherent. That philosophical possibility does not verify this particular miracle; the testimony still requires evaluation.

- 'At Jerusalem' in Alma 7:10 may be defended as a regional designation or treated as geographic imprecision. Either way, it should temper claims of neutral historical corroboration without erasing what the verse plainly teaches about Mary.

- The Book of Mormon strengthens the canonical case. Its independent historical value remains conditional on larger judgments about provenance and revelation.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require:** Matthew requires pregnancy before Mary and Joseph's sexual union, excludes Joseph as the cause, and attributes conception to the Holy Ghost. Luke requires that Mary had not known a man and attributes conception to divine power. Neither gives a physiological mechanism. [[1 Nephi 11]] identifies a virgin mother, Mosiah 3:8 names Mary, and Alma 7:10 combines the full formulation. These texts require virginal conception within their narratives, not Mary's perpetual virginity or the Roman Catholic doctrine technically called the Immaculate Conception.

**Models permitted or raised by the evidence:**

1. **Literal miraculous conception — textually required within Matthew and Luke; plausible within theism; not independently demonstrated.** The natural narrative reading is that God caused Mary to conceive without intercourse. The texts strongly establish the authors' claim but do not supply neutral physical verification.

2. **Historical core with theological elaboration — plausible.** A family or early-community memory of extraordinary conception may underlie accounts later shaped with Isaiah, dreams, annunciation patterns, hymns, and distinct geographies. This explains agreement and difference but rests on unrecoverable sources.

3. **Shared early tradition, true or false — independently supported at the level of tradition.** Two distinct Gospel presentations support the existence of the belief by the time both works circulated. They do not establish whether the common tradition was historical memory or early legend.

4. **Theological or legendary development — historically possible and prominent in critical scholarship.** Reflection on Jesus's divine sonship, Greek Isaiah 7:14, and scriptural birth patterns may have generated the narratives. Limited New Testament attestation and literary construction support this possibility; no surviving source documents the proposed development directly.

5. **Ordinary conception by Joseph before cohabitation — physically possible but textually contradicted and weakly supported.** It explains pregnancy before formal household union, but both Gospel narratives deliberately exclude Joseph as biological cause.

6. **Conception by another man — physically possible but ad hoc on present evidence.** Later hostile allegations are too late and polemical to identify a father or establish an irregular conception.

7. **Isaiah's almah means only a young woman — plausible for Isaiah's immediate lexical question but insufficient for Matthew and Luke.** It challenges treating Isaiah alone as an unambiguous prediction; it does not remove Matthew's statements about Joseph or Luke's statement that Mary had not known a man.

8. **Ancient Restoration prophecy — possible within the Book of Mormon's claimed history and independently significant only if that provenance is established.** On this model, [[1 Nephi 11]] and [[Alma 7]] are pre-Christian witnesses.

9. **Inspired modern rendering — plausible within a revelatory model but historically underdetermined.** Ancient underlying teaching could be expressed through familiar New Testament language during translation without requiring identical ancient wording.

10. **Later redaction or modern composition — historically possible.** Either could produce explicit Christian language and internal convergence without pre-Christian prediction. Final-form coherence alone cannot choose among these Restoration models.

**Considered and set aside:** Perpetual virginity is a separate later doctrinal question. Pagan divine-birth comparisons were not assigned weight because no sufficiently specific comparative evidence was supplied, and many proposed parallels differ materially from the nonsexual causation narrated here. The Panthera allegation was set aside as late hostile polemic. Pure harmony and asserted contradiction were both rejected: many details can coexist, but neither Gospel supplies the combined itinerary.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established by the texts:** Matthew and Luke intentionally affirm conception without intercourse and attribute it to divine action. [[1 Nephi 11]] affirms virgin motherhood, Mosiah 3:8 names Mary, and Alma 7:10 gives an explicit Restoration-scripture virgin-conception statement. The selected Latter-day Saint teaching sources accept the doctrine.

**Historically supported but open:** Matthew and Luke demonstrate that the belief existed in two distinct canonical narrative forms. Whether they preserve independent family testimony, a shared tradition, theological elaboration of a historical core, or a nonhistorical sacred-origin tradition remains disputed. Isaiah 7:14 explains Matthew's theological presentation but is not independent proof. Book of Mormon passages provide strong canonical but conditional historical evidence because their provenance remains disputed.

**A matter of faith:** Surviving evidence cannot directly establish or falsify that God caused Mary to conceive while she remained a virgin. Historical inquiry can assess texts and rival explanations, so the matter is not beyond analysis; it simply does not yield demonstration of the supernatural cause. Neither canonical assertion alone nor silence, narrative difference, Isaiah's Hebrew, Romans 1:3, or Alma's geographic wording proves the larger conclusion for every reader.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Matthew 1]] and [[Luke 1]] side by side, listing what each states before attempting a harmony; then continue with Matthew 2, [[Luke 2]], and Luke 3:23. Read [[Isaiah 7]] in its Syro-Ephraimite setting before comparing Matthew 1:22-23. For Restoration scripture, study [[1 Nephi 11]], [[Mosiah 3]], and [[Alma 7]], noting both their distinct contributions and Alma 7:10's 'at Jerusalem' wording.

For critical scholarship, consult Raymond E. Brown, *The Birth of the Messiah* (updated edition, 1993); Joseph A. Fitzmyer, *The Gospel According to Luke I–IX* (1981); and John P. Meier, *A Marginal Jew*, volume 1 (1991). For Latter-day Saint reception, compare [[Conference Report, October 1925 (General Conference (full report), October 1925)]], [[Conference Report, April 1926 (General Conference (full report), April 1926)]], [[God Loves and Helps All of His Children (Bishop Keith B. McMullin, October 2008)]], and [[It's a Miracle (Elder Neil L. Andersen, April 2013)]].
