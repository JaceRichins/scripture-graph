# Why did so many early leaders leave the Church?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
There was no single exodus and no single cause. Some prominent leaders lost confidence during the 1837-1838 financial and governance crisis surrounding [[Why did the Kirtland Safety Society fail]]; some clashed with Joseph Smith over authority, property, discipline, or the violent atmosphere in Missouri; some later opposed plural marriage and Nauvoo government; some were expelled for alleged misconduct; and after 1844 some followed rival succession claims rather than abandoning the Restoration altogether. Contemporary records also show coercion against certain Missouri dissenters, so departure was not always wholly voluntary. The departures are serious evidence of instability and contested leadership, but they do not alone prove either fraud or divine authenticity. Individual cases—especially [[Oliver Cowdery]], [[David Whitmer]], [[Thomas B. Marsh]], [[W. W. Phelps]], [[William Law]], [[John C. Bennett]], and [[Sidney Rigdon]]—must be examined separately.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The defections clustered around identifiable crises.** D&C 112:10, 14-15, dated July 1837, warns the Twelve against pride, self-exaltation, and rebellion against Joseph Smith. Documentary histories then locate a major concentration of dissent amid the Kirtland Safety Society's collapse, financial losses, lawsuits, and competing claims about Joseph's leadership. See [[Why did the Kirtland Safety Society fail]], [[Thomas B. Marsh]], and [[Warren Parrish]].

- **The founding witnesses did not fit a simple fraud-confession narrative.** [[Oliver Cowdery]], [[David Whitmer]], and [[Martin Harris]] all experienced institutional estrangement, but continued in different settings to affirm their foundational witness. That persistence does not prove a supernatural event, yet it is important evidence against the claim that leaving automatically amounted to admitting conscious fabrication. See [[How did the Three Witnesses live out their lives]] and [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]].

- **Missouri dissent included coercion.** Contemporary and near-contemporary documentation indicates that Cowdery, the Whitmers, and Phelps received a threatening Danite notice and left Caldwell County. Their prior alienation and disciplinary proceedings remain relevant, but the physical departure cannot honestly be described as an entirely free choice. See [[Who were the Danites]] and [[Why were the Saints driven from Missouri]].

- **Later Nauvoo departures had different causes.** [[William Law]] had been elevated to the First Presidency under D&C 124:87, 91. His subsequent opposition drew on plural marriage, Church-state power, and disciplinary conflict and culminated in the Nauvoo Expositor. [[John C. Bennett]] represents a different case involving expulsion and allegations of sexual misconduct. See [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]] and [[Why was the Nauvoo Expositor destroyed]].

- **Some separations were reversed.** Phelps reconciled with Joseph Smith, Cowdery was rebaptized, Marsh returned, and Harris renewed fellowship and gathered west. Return does not erase their objections, but it shows that early estrangement was neither uniform nor always final.

- **The canon anticipates fallible leaders without deciding every dispute.** D&C 6:18-20 expects mutual admonition between Joseph and Oliver; D&C 23:1 warns an otherwise commended Oliver about pride; and D&C 121:34-42 says authority fails when exercised through unrighteous control. These passages rule out the assumption that senior calling guarantees either personal infallibility or permanent loyalty.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest critical objection is cumulative: a striking number of people with unusually close access to Joseph Smith—including witnesses, counselors, apostles, financial associates, and members of founding families—became dissenters. Their proximity may give their judgments more evidentiary weight than the dissatisfaction of casual members. The Kirtland failure, Missouri coercion, secrecy surrounding plural marriage, concentration of civic and ecclesiastical power, and destruction of the Nauvoo Expositor can be read as a recurring pattern in which criticism was attributed to apostasy and institutional power was used to suppress opposition. From this view, departures are not random weakness but insider evidence against Joseph's judgment or claims.

A second objection concerns the sources. Loyalist minutes commonly state formal charges, but a disciplinary record is not a neutral transcript of motive. Conversely, dissenting newspapers and later interviews were produced by aggrieved opponents and can also be partisan. Retrospective stories—especially simplified moral tales about [[Thomas B. Marsh]]—can replace complicated contemporary causes with memorable lessons about pride.

A third objection challenges witness apologetics. Continuing to affirm a visionary experience after leaving does not prove that plates were objectively present or divinely delivered. Identity, reputation, cognitive consistency, sincere spiritual interpretation, and reluctance to confess error can all explain persistent testimony.

Finally, the word many lacks a stated denominator. Counting every temporary suspension, excommunication, geographical departure, rival succession choice, and permanent rejection together exaggerates uniformity. Yet invoking a denominator can itself become minimization: repeated defections among top leaders during short periods remain historically important even if most members or leaders stayed.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believers can answer first by conceding the historical substance: senior defections, financial failure, coercive conduct by some Latter-day Saints, contested discipline, and grave Nauvoo disputes are not inventions. A defense that depends on denying them is brittle.

They can then resist the unsupported inference that one cause explains every case. [[Oliver Cowdery]] objected to developments in authority and governance but returned; [[David Whitmer]] retained an earlier Restoration identity without rejoining; [[W. W. Phelps]] reconciled; [[William Law]] opposed Nauvoo developments; and [[Sidney Rigdon]] claimed succession. Those trajectories are incompatible with both the blanket claim that every dissenter discovered fraud and the blanket claim that every dissenter merely became immoral.

Believers may also point to the canon's own demanding account of leadership. D&C 6:18-20 allows mutual correction, while D&C 121:34-42 condemns coercion by anyone who possesses office. Thus faith need not require believing that every action by Joseph Smith's associates—or every institutional response to dissent—was correct. The theological claim is that divine work proceeds through agents capable of pride, fear, error, repentance, and return; history alone cannot independently verify that divine interpretation.

Finally, the witnesses' continued testimony has limited but genuine force. It does not settle the nature of their experience, but it complicates the specific allegation that their later estrangement was a confession that the Book of Mormon story was knowingly invented. The proper conclusion is narrower than proof and stronger than irrelevance.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** The canon presents prominent callings and spiritual gifts as conditional rather than guarantees of infallibility or permanent loyalty. Oliver is honored in D&C 20:3 and D&C 21:10, warned in D&C 23:1, and placed in a mutually corrective relationship with Joseph in D&C 6:18-20. D&C 112:10, 14-15 warns the Twelve against self-exaltation and rebellion, while D&C 121:34-42 requires readers to consider unrighteous control by officeholders. These texts permit moral evaluation of both leaders and dissenters, but they do not narrate the later separations, assign one motive to everyone, or identify the correct party in each dispute.

- **Independently supported—compound institutional-crisis model.** Financial losses, disappointed expectations, legal conflict, leadership rivalry, rapid organizational change, and displacement interacted during 1837-1838. Financial and historical records establish the crisis and its clustering, though not one identical motive for every person. See [[Why did the Kirtland Safety Society fail]] and [[Why were the Saints driven from Missouri]].

- **Independently supported—case-specific grievance model.** The records identify differing combinations of property, authority, discipline, violence, theology, plural marriage, and personal conduct. The histories of [[Oliver Cowdery]], [[David Whitmer]], [[Thomas B. Marsh]], [[William Law]], and [[John C. Bennett]] resist a universal explanation.

- **Independently supported—doctrinal and governmental innovation model.** David Whitmer's later first-person account, *An Address to All Believers in Christ* (1887), attributes his opposition to what he regarded as unauthorized changes in doctrine, offices, and Church government. The document independently establishes Whitmer's stated retrospective rationale, not that his historical or theological judgment was correct or that it fully explains his 1838 separation.

- **Independently supported—coercive-boundary model.** In Missouri, formal discipline was followed by intimidation against dissenters, including a threatening notice associated with the [[Who were the Danites|Danites]]. This helps explain their physical departure from Caldwell County but does not establish the original cause of their alienation or settle Joseph Smith's knowledge and responsibility.

- **Independently supported—succession-fragmentation model.** After 1844, some leaders continued to affirm Restoration claims while recognizing [[Sidney Rigdon]], [[James Strang]], the Twelve, or another line of authority. Historical evidence establishes competing claims; deciding which claimant possessed divine authority remains theological. See [[How was Brigham Young chosen to succeed Joseph Smith]].

- **Plausible—disillusionment with prophetic performance.** Some insiders concluded that financial decisions, secrecy, changing teachings, or governance were inconsistent with Joseph Smith's prophetic claims. Contemporary and retrospective complaints establish this as a genuine explanation offered by dissenters, but not as a universal motive or proof that their conclusion was correct.

- **Plausible—agency, pride, rivalry, and endurance model.** D&C 23:1 and D&C 112:10, 14-15 permit the possibility that pride, ambition, rivalry, fear, or unwillingness to endure contributed in individual cases. Applying the model without case-specific evidence is circular because disagreement itself then becomes proof of spiritual failure.

- **Plausible—organizational learning and centralization model.** A rapidly growing movement had immature financial, disciplinary, and succession systems. Crises exposed unclear authority and weak safeguards, while later structures became more centralized. The institutional development is historically investigable; describing every crisis as providential training is a faith interpretation.

- **Possible—common concentration-of-authority model.** Critics may see a recurring cause beneath otherwise different episodes: increasing financial, ecclesiastical, civic, and doctrinal authority around Joseph Smith, followed by conflict when insiders challenged its exercise. Several cases make this a serious hypothesis, but a complete leadership roster and case-by-case causal comparison are still lacking.

- **Possible—deliberate-fraud-discovery model.** Some insiders may have concluded that founding claims were fraudulent. The model cannot be excluded by assertion. As a universal explanation it fits poorly with varied stated grievances, later returns, and the Three Witnesses' continued testimony; that testimony, however, does not independently prove a supernatural event. See [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]].

- **Possible—divine-sifting model.** Believers may interpret the crises as trials of covenant loyalty. Scriptural themes permit this reading, but it is not an independently supported historical mechanism and becomes unfalsifiable if either loyalty or departure is automatically counted as confirmation.

- **Ad hoc when universalized—all dissenters were wicked or all dissenters exposed fraud.** Both formulas ignore contrary cases and use the outcome as its own proof.

**Considered and set aside as single causes:** external persecution cannot explain internal conflict; plural marriage is central to some Nauvoo cases but cannot explain Kirtland; financial loss cannot explain every later rupture; and raw totals are unusable until leader, departure, period, and denominator are defined. Migration to build [[Zion]], loss of office, excommunication, coerced removal, temporary estrangement, permanent disaffiliation, and allegiance to a rival successor must not be counted as interchangeable events.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** several people holding the highest early offices became estranged, were disciplined, or joined competing movements. The defections were especially concentrated around the Kirtland and Missouri crises and later around Nauvoo plural marriage, governance, and succession. Contemporary evidence also establishes that some Missouri dissenters faced intimidation, and that some prominent defectors later returned. The Three Witnesses' institutional estrangement did not produce a simple collective recantation of their witness.

**Open:** the relative weight of conscience, personality, financial injury, theological change, ambition, coercion, and spiritual conviction differs by person and is often recoverable only imperfectly from partisan sources. Joseph Smith's knowledge of or responsibility for every coercive action remains debated. The exact number implied by so many also depends on whether the denominator is founding officers, all leaders, all members, or a particular period.

**Matter of faith:** history can test dates, offices, accusations, conduct, and documentary consistency; it cannot by itself determine whether departures were divinely permitted trials, whether Joseph Smith retained prophetic authority, or which post-1844 succession claim God recognized. The most defensible conclusion is neither that departure proves fraud nor that officeholders' criticism can be dismissed as apostasy. It establishes a Restoration history marked by consequential internal conflict, fallible human agency, and several substantially different kinds of separation.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with D&C 6, 18, 20-21, 23, 112, and 121, distinguishing what the revelations actually say from later biographical applications. Then study [[Organization of the Church]], [[Oliver Cowdery]], [[David Whitmer]], [[Thomas B. Marsh]], [[W. W. Phelps]], [[William Law]], [[Sidney Rigdon]], and [[John C. Bennett]] separately.

For the 1837-1838 cluster, read the Joseph Smith Papers introduction and documents for the Kirtland Safety Society, the Far West high-council minutes of April 12-13, 1838, and the contemporary Danite warning, alongside [[Why did the Kirtland Safety Society fail]], [[Who were the Danites]], and [[Why were the Saints driven from Missouri]]. Compare institutional narration in *Saints*, volume 1, with Richard Lyman Bushman's *Joseph Smith: Rough Stone Rolling* and D. Michael Quinn's *The Mormon Hierarchy: Origins of Power*.

For Nauvoo, read William Law's diary, the sole issue of the *Nauvoo Expositor* dated June 7, 1844, the Nauvoo City Council record, and [[Why was the Nauvoo Expositor destroyed]]. Study plural marriage separately through [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]] rather than projecting it backward onto Kirtland.

For the witness question, compare contemporary and lifetime statements in [[How did the Three Witnesses live out their lives]] and [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]]. For the succession question, use the August 8, 1844 meeting minutes and [[How was Brigham Young chosen to succeed Joseph Smith]]. Give contemporary records priority over late moral anecdotes, while remembering that minutes and newspapers were themselves interested sources.
