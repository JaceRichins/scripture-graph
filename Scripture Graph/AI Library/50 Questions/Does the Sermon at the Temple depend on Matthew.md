# Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Yes, in a carefully limited textual sense: the received English of [[Sermon at the Temple (3 Nephi)]] in [[3 Nephi 12]]–[[3 Nephi 14]] shows strong, sustained dependence on or mediation through Matthew 5–7 and its King James wording. The sequence is too extensive for coincidence, while local and post-resurrection adaptations show purposeful rewriting rather than undifferentiated copying. What remains unsettled is the mechanism: direct nineteenth-century adaptation, revelatory restatement through familiar biblical language, translation or editorial harmonization of an ancient discourse, and layered combinations of these processes can all produce the central observation. The evidence therefore establishes English textual interdependence much more strongly than it establishes who performed the adaptation, when it occurred, or whether an ancient sermon stands behind it. See [[Textual Dependence And Intertextuality in 3 Nephi 12]] and the cumulative judgment in [[Book of Mormon Assessment]].
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Extended order, not an isolated saying.** 3 Nephi 12:3–48, 13:1–34, and 14:1–27 move through substantially the same ordered teaching units as Matthew 5:3–48, 6:1–34, and 7:1–27. A common Jesus tradition might explain shared themes; it does not readily explain this sustained English-level sequence without textual mediation.

- **Close formulations occur throughout the sequence.** The relationship embraces the Beatitudes, salt and light, law sayings, prayer, fasting, treasures, judging, asking and knocking, the two ways, false prophets, and the builders. The evidentiary force comes from the cumulative pattern, not from counting each saying as an independent proof. [[Textual Dependence And Intertextuality in 3 Nephi 12]] judges this a strong but mechanistically contested relationship.

- **Adaptations fit the new narrative setting.** The sermon follows the risen Jesus's appearance at the temple in Bountiful (3 Nephi 11:1, 10–17), addresses newly commissioned disciples (3 Nephi 12:1–2; 13:25), and modifies the perfection saying to include the risen Jesus (3 Nephi 12:48; compare Matthew 5:48). These changes demonstrate deliberate contextualization. They do not decide whether the contextualizer was an ancient speaker/editor, a revelatory translator, or a nineteenth-century composer.

- **The discourse is integrated elsewhere in 3 Nephi.** The asking-and-knocking and strait-gate sayings return in 3 Nephi 27:29, 33. This supports deliberate final-form construction but, as [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] cautions, an internal reprise is not a second independent witness to provenance.

- **The broader corpus shows the same kind of mediation.** [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] weighs the sermon together with Isaiah blocks and shorter distinctive echoes and concludes that the received English Book of Mormon is pervasively, often specifically KJV-shaped. The sermon is one substantial component of that line, not a standalone arithmetic vote.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **Direct adaptation is the economical ordinary explanation.** A nineteenth-century writer with access to the KJV could preserve Matthew's long sequence, reproduce its wording, and insert Bountiful-specific material. That model requires no unattested ancient manuscript or unusually extensive translation harmonization.

- **Contextual changes do not prove an independent source.** An adapter normally alters a source to suit a new setting. The post-resurrection wording, references to Nephite disciples, and fulfillment-of-law framing therefore establish literary intelligence, not ancient independence.

- **A source-transparent ancient translation is strongly strained.** If the English closely represented an independent ancient sermon, sustained agreement with the wording and order of one familiar English Gospel would be unexpected. The broader KJV mediation assessed in [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] compounds this difficulty.

- **Unrestricted harmonization can become unfalsifiable.** A claim that God or the translator supplied Matthew's exact idiom whenever close correspondence appears can accommodate nearly any result after the fact. Without prior criteria identifying expected harmonization, compatibility should not be presented as equal predictive support.

- **No surviving Nephite-language source permits layer separation.** The available evidence does not independently identify which words, if any, belong to an ancient discourse and which arose in translation, revelation, editing, or composition.

- **The narrative's attribution is internal evidence.** 3 Nephi presents Jesus as the speaker, but that claim occurs within the text whose origin is disputed. It cannot independently authenticate the represented event.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- A thoughtful believer need not defend a source-transparent English translation. The canonical account claims translation by divine power but does not provide a sufficiently technical theory of linguistic control. [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]] is therefore more relevant than assumptions that every English phrase must map directly onto an ancient phrase.

- Jesus could repeat and adapt teachings for different audiences. That makes a historical parallel sermon possible, and the Bountiful-specific revisions are consistent with such repetition. It does not independently establish that the event occurred or explain why the English follows Matthew so closely.

- Joseph Smith's revelatory corpus elsewhere uses recognizable KJV language; D&C 10:57–59 is one accepted example. This makes revelatory restatement through scriptural vocabulary a historically relevant model, not a device invented only for 3 Nephi. It still cannot distinguish divine restatement from ordinary literary reuse.

- An ancient discourse plus translation-level harmonization can explain both local adaptations and Matthean English. This preserves a coherent believing model, especially if revelation is understood as communicating through the translator's familiar scriptural register. Its evidentiary cost is that the proposed ancient layer cannot presently be isolated or independently tested.

- Believers can accept the strong finding of Matthew/KJV mediation without conceding that inspiration is disproved. Historical provenance, translation mechanism, literary dependence, and divine authority are different questions. Equally, inspiration should not be used as though it were textual evidence selecting one historical mechanism.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** Within the narrated world of [[3 Nephi 11]]–[[3 Nephi 15]], the risen [[Jesus Christ]] speaks at the temple in Bountiful; the text does not say anyone consulted a written Gospel of Matthew. Comparison nevertheless requires the received English to have a strong relationship with Matthew 5–7. The text requires distinguishing its represented event from the recoverable history of its English wording. It does not require either the assumption that Jesus must use wholly novel language on a second occasion or the assumption that Matthew's physical text must exist inside the Nephite setting.

- **Final-form Matthew/KJV mediation — independently supported.** The extended order and English correspondence establish that the received text is mediated through the Matthean/KJV tradition. This is the origin-neutral baseline of [[Textual Dependence And Intertextuality in 3 Nephi 12]] and [[Book of Mormon Assessment]]. It does not specify the mediation stage.

- **Historical repeated sermon, rendered through KJV-shaped translation — possible, weakened by source criticism.** Jesus may have taught overlapping material to different audiences, while translation supplied Matthean English. The distinct Bountiful setting and local adaptations are consistent with this. But mainstream source-critical scholarship holds that Matthew's Sermon on the Mount is itself substantially a redactional compilation of sayings gathered from scattered occasions (compare Luke's Sermon on the Plain and parallel sayings elsewhere in Luke), not a single historical discourse. To the extent 3 Nephi's parallel tracks Matthew's specific redactional sequence and combinations rather than generic sayings of Jesus, this weakens a single-historical-sermon model and correspondingly favors models of direct dependence on Matthew's edited literary product.

- **Ancient discourse later harmonized by a translator or editor — plausible.** A genuine ancient discourse could have been brought into alignment with Matthew during translation or editing while retaining Nephite framing and post-resurrection changes. This needs principled criteria for identifying the proposed ancient substrate; invoked only wherever dependence is troublesome, it becomes ad hoc.

- **Revelatory restatement deliberately using Matthew — plausible.** Recognizable KJV reuse in D&C 10:57–59 gives this model a corpus precedent, establishing possibility, not divine causation or its application here.

- **Nineteenth-century KJV-informed composition adapting Matthew — plausible and economically supported.** Known access to Matthew, sustained correspondence, purposeful local revisions, and integration into 3 Nephi are all predicted by direct composition, without an unrecoverable source layer. This does not by itself establish fraud, motive, or absence of inspiration.

- **Ancient core with substantial modern or revelatory expansion — possible.** No surviving source manuscript independently locates the boundary between layers; responsible only if layers are proposed using prior textual criteria.

- **Shared Jesus tradition without English textual mediation — possible for themes, ad hoc for wording.** This may explain common content but not the prolonged KJV-shaped order and wording; the redactional-compilation point above further undercuts it as a complete account of the final English.

- **Coincidental convergence — set aside.** Too sustained and sequential to be accidental.

- **Dependence on Matthew the apostle personally — set aside as a conflation.** The evidence concerns the canonical Gospel's English textual tradition, not the apostle's personal authorship or participation.

The chief objection to an ancient model attaches most sharply to source-transparent translation; the principal believing response attaches to loose, revelatory, or harmonizing translation. Local adaptations challenge mechanical copying but not purposeful modern adaptation.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The received English of 3 Nephi 12–14 has a strong, sustained textual relationship with Matthew 5–7, normally described as dependence, intertextuality, or KJV-mediated harmonization. A substantially Matthew-independent English formulation is not credible. The discourse also contains purposeful adaptations to the Bountiful setting and is coherently integrated into 3 Nephi's final form.

**Open:** The comparison does not determine whether the underlying event occurred, whether an ancient discourse preceded the English text, or whether the mediation arose through translation, revelation, editing, memory, direct composition, or multiple stages. Direct nineteenth-century adaptation is an economical ordinary explanation of the English evidence. Ancient-discourse-plus-KJV-shaped translation remains possible, but it is not independently established by the parallel itself and becomes weak if allowed unlimited harmonization without predictive constraints.

**Matter of faith:** Accepting that the risen Christ delivered the discourse in Bountiful or that divine revelation governed its English form goes beyond what textual comparison can demonstrate. Conversely, literary dependence does not logically disprove inspiration. The cumulative [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] strongly establishes biblical/KJV mediation while expressly withholding an overall verdict on Book of Mormon historicity or inspiration.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
