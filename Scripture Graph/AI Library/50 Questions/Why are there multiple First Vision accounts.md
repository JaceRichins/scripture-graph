# Why are there multiple First Vision accounts?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
There are multiple First Vision accounts because [[Joseph Smith Jr]] described the claimed experience in several documentary settings between about 1832 and 1842. Joseph Smith Papers editors conventionally classify four accounts as recorded by Joseph or under his direction, while five additional reports preserve what hearers remembered; only the 1838/39 narrative was canonized as [[Joseph Smith—History 1]]. The accounts repeatedly present youthful religious distress, solitary prayer, a heavenly manifestation, and a divine response, but they differ in age, foregrounded motive, narrative detail, and how explicitly the heavenly beings are identified. Their genres and audiences plausibly explain some variation, but do not automatically explain every difference. Selective retelling, reconstructive memory, developing interpretation, sincere elaboration, and conscious embellishment remain live models. Textual comparison can weigh these possibilities, but cannot by itself establish or disprove that the underlying [[First Vision]] occurred.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## The accounts

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The documentary inventory is secure.** Four accounts are conventionally classified as recorded by Joseph Smith or under his direction: circa 1832, November 1835, 1838/39, and 1842. Five additional surviving reports came from people who heard him describe the experience. Only the 1838/39 narrative was canonized as [[Joseph Smith—History 1]].

- **The accounts contain both continuity and real variation.** Across 1832–1842 Joseph repeatedly presented youthful religious distress, solitary prayer, a heavenly manifestation, and a divine answer. This strongly establishes a recurring autobiographical claim by that period, but the accounts derive from one principal claimant and are not independent eyewitness corroboration of an 1820 event.

- **The differences are checkable in the primary texts.** The 1832 history emphasizes sin, forgiveness, and “the Lord”; the 1835 account explicitly describes two sequential personages and mentions angels; the 1838/39 history foregrounds the question of which church to join, two personages, and subsequent opposition; the 1842 public summary again describes two personages. The earliest account's “sixteenth year” also differs from the later about-fourteen/spring-1820 chronology.

- **Documentary setting has some explanatory value.** The penitential 1832 autobiography, recorded 1835 conversation, defensive institutional history, and compressed 1842 public summary have emphases that fit their genres and purposes. This moderately supports selective narration, but genre and chronology are confounded and the fit cannot prove that omitted details were already part of the earliest memory.

- **The evidentiary gap is also secure.** No presently known contemporaneous 1820 narrative survives; the earliest detailed account dates to about 1832 and was not then published. This is a genuine limitation, though absence of a record is not equivalent to a contemporary denial.

- **The full corpus is presently available for inspection.** The Joseph Smith Papers supplies manuscripts, transcripts, and editorial introductions, while [[First Vision Accounts (Gospel Topics)]] openly discusses differences. Present access does not establish uninterrupted historical transparency.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **No known 1820 record survives.** The earliest detailed account is about twelve years later and was unpublished. Early surviving Latter-day Saint origin narratives more often foreground the plates and angelic ministry. A critic can reasonably ask why an event now treated as foundational left so faint an early documentary footprint.

- **The story becomes more explicit over time.** The 1832 account says Joseph saw “the Lord,” whereas the 1835, 1838/39, and 1842 accounts explicitly present two personages. Later accounts also develop the church-choice, opposition, and institutional-origin themes. Critics read this as narrative and theological elaboration rather than simple abbreviation.

- **The motives do not align exactly.** In 1832 Joseph has already concluded through scripture that existing denominations have departed from true Christianity and seeks mercy for his sins. In the later accounts uncertainty over which church is right becomes a central reason for praying. Multiple motives can coexist, but the shift affects the story's causal structure.

- **Chronology is disputed.** [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] connects the prayer with unusual religious excitement and dates it to spring 1820 (Joseph Smith—History 1:5–14). Wesley P. Walters documented a much clearer Palmyra revival in 1824–25; believing historians have supplied evidence for earlier regional Methodist activity. Regional activity does not automatically prove that the precise revival sequence in the later narrative occurred in 1820.

- **Lucy Mack Smith's draft history complicates the received chronology.** As Christopher C. Smith emphasizes, Lucy's draft connects Joseph's question about the true church with the 1823 angelic visit and does not independently narrate an earlier Father-and-Son vision. Because Lucy wrote decades later and her narrative has its own editorial history, it cannot simply override Joseph's accounts, but neither should it be ignored.

- **“Different audiences” can become unfalsifiable.** If every omission is automatically assigned to audience or genre, harmonization stops testing the evidence. The actual texts identify their settings; they do not explicitly say that all omitted details were consciously withheld for audience-specific reasons.

- **The 1832 manuscript's handling raises legitimate trust concerns.** Its leaves were removed from their bound volume in the mid-twentieth century and the unpublished account was generally forgotten until historians brought it to public attention in the 1960s. The surviving physical evidence does not prove intentional suppression, but claims that the documents were always fully transparent are historically too simple.

- **The accounts ultimately depend on Joseph.** Five secondhand reports show that other people heard him tell versions, not that they witnessed the event. Multiple textual attestation should not be confused with multiple independent observation; compare [[Multiple Textual Attestation in 2 Chronicles 36]] and [[Multiple Pauline Textual Attestations in 1 Corinthians 9]].
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- A thoughtful believing response begins by conceding the real differences. [[First Vision Accounts (Gospel Topics)]] argues that repeated narration across years and settings naturally produces distinct emphases. The documentary settings do fit the observed emphases, making rhetorical selection a plausible explanation rather than a merely invented rescue.

- The 1832 account does not say that only one being appeared. Its focus is the Lord who addresses Joseph and forgives him. Later accounts can therefore supply a second personage without formal contradiction. Still, treating the two occurrences of “Lord” in 1832 as two distinct beings is only a possible reading, not something the manuscript clearly establishes.

- Personal forgiveness and inquiry about churches are compatible motives. A young seeker could be distressed by both his sins and the religious divisions around him. What remains uncertain is whether the changing foreground reflects ordinary selection, developing memory, or later reconstruction.

- A lack of contemporary documentation is not evidence of equal strength to a contemporary denial. Joseph's early record-keeping concentrated on revelations and the plates; the circa-1832 history was among his first sustained attempts at personal history. This can explain delay, but it cannot turn the absent 1820 record into positive corroboration.

- The revival objection should be stated geographically and chronologically with care. Evidence of religious excitement in the broader region before 1820 makes the canonical setting possible; evidence for a more conspicuous 1824 Palmyra revival keeps conflation or retrospective compression possible. Neither side has documentary grounds to claim that revival research alone settles the vision's historicity.

- Believers may see a genuine experience whose meaning became clearer as Joseph's vocation and theology developed. This model permits development without equating it with fraud. It also gives up the stronger claim that every later formulation was fully explicit in Joseph's earliest remembered narration.

- Present publication of the full corpus by the Joseph Smith Papers is valuable and permits accountable study. It demonstrates current documentary openness, not uninterrupted openness or proof that every past archival decision was benign.

- [[Acts 9]], [[Acts 22]], and [[Acts 26]] appropriately rebut the rule that any narrative variation entails fabrication. They should not be used as direct historical corroboration or as a shortcut around the particular tensions in Joseph's accounts.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** Four accounts are conventionally classified as recorded by Joseph Smith or under his direction, five additional reports preserve what hearers remembered, and only the 1838/39 narrative is canonized as [[Joseph Smith—History 1]]. The accounts share a recognizable narrative core and contain genuine differences. The 1832 manuscript is the earliest surviving detailed account, not an 1820 record.

**Open:** The original content and phenomenology of Joseph's experience; why no contemporary narrative survives; how much variation comes from genre, memory, theological development, scribal mediation, or conscious elaboration; and how the 1820 setting relates to the better-documented 1824 revival. The evidence permits responsible faithful, critical-middle, and skeptical models.

**Matter of faith:** Historical documents can establish that Joseph repeatedly testified of a divine manifestation and can test particular features of his narration. They cannot independently demonstrate that God the Father and Jesus Christ appeared. Accepting the [[First Vision]] as revelation and as the opening of the [[Restoration of the Gospel]] involves a religious judgment informed—but not compelled—by the historical record.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with the primary documents: *History, circa Summer 1832*; the journal entry for 9 November 1835; *History, circa June 1839–circa 1841* alongside [[Joseph Smith—History 1]]; and *Church History, 1 March 1842*. The Joseph Smith Papers page “Accounts of Joseph Smith's First Vision” links these documents, manuscript images, editorial introductions, and the five early reported accounts.

Then compare the institutional treatments [[First Vision Accounts (Gospel Topics)]], [[First Vision Accounts (Essay)]], and [[First Vision (Gospel Topics)]]. [[Sources Cited]] identifies Dean C. Jessee's foundational “The Early Accounts of Joseph Smith's First Vision,” BYU Studies 9, no. 3 (1969).

For contrasting interpretations, consult Steven C. Harper, *First Vision: Memory and Mormon Origins* (2019); Richard Lyman Bushman, *Joseph Smith: Rough Stone Rolling* (2005); Wesley P. Walters, “New Light on Mormon Origins from the Palmyra Revival” (Dialogue 4, no. 1, 1969); Dan Vogel, *Joseph Smith: The Making of a Prophet* (2004); and Christopher C. Smith, “First Vision Controversies: Implications for Accounts of Mormon Origins” (BYU Studies Quarterly 59, no. 2, 2020).

For a limited scriptural comparison, read [[Acts 9]], [[Acts 22]], and [[Acts 26]]. These passages demonstrate literary variation across retellings, not independent corroboration of Joseph Smith's experience.

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
### What the evidence itself requires

The canon requires only that [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] presents Joseph's later account of a spring 1820 prayer, two personages, an answer about the churches, and subsequent opposition (Joseph Smith—History 1:1–23). It does not mention the other accounts or prescribe how they must be harmonized. The historical manuscripts require recognition of both recurring features and development. Silence is not logically identical to denial, but silence can still have evidentiary weight when the omitted matter would seem relevant. First-person form establishes testimony; it does not by itself establish external historicity, as the calibrated method in [[Internal First-Person Narrative Testimony in Alma 56]] makes clear.

### Serious models permitted by the record

- **Plausible; mechanism independently supported — stable experience, selective retelling.** Joseph experienced substantially what the later accounts describe and selected details according to spiritual need, audience, genre, and available space. The alignment between documentary settings and emphases supports this model. General research on autobiographical memory supports selective reconstruction, but that general fact does not independently verify Joseph's experience.

- **Plausible — genuine experience with developing interpretation.** Joseph had a powerful religious experience but came over time to understand and narrate its participants and institutional meaning more explicitly. Richard Bushman and Steven C. Harper offer versions of this developmental approach. It explains both continuity and growth while leaving open whether later precision reproduces the original perception or mature interpretation.

- **Plausible — ordinary reconstructive memory without deception.** Joseph sincerely remembered an event, while elapsed time, repeated narration, later persecution, and current theological concerns reshaped details. This model does not require fraud, but it allows some later particulars to be historically inaccurate.

- **Plausible critical-middle model — sincere visionary experience later theologized.** Joseph may have experienced forgiveness or a manifestation understood initially in less differentiated terms, with the explicit Father-and-Son formulation and Restoration-origin meaning emerging later. The 1832 emphasis and later increasing specificity support considering this model; no independent source identifies the original phenomenology.

- **Possible — conscious embellishment of an earlier experience.** Joseph may have deliberately increased the story's authority or theological specificity. The documentary delay and expanding detail give the model some support. Recurring core elements and the private, unpolished 1832 account prevent the progression alone from proving conscious invention.

- **Possible and disputed — conflation or displacement of 1823–25 experiences.** Following evidence emphasized by Wesley P. Walters, Dan Vogel, and Christopher C. Smith, the later origin narrative may combine an 1824 revival, the family's church discussions, and Joseph's 1823 angelic experience, relocating them to 1820. Lucy Mack Smith's draft history and the stronger documentation for an 1824 Palmyra revival motivate this reconstruction. Its dependence on late and conflicting reminiscences keeps it from being independently established.

- **Possible — wholesale construction before 1832.** On this model no corresponding 1820 manifestation occurred and Joseph created the story by the time of the 1832 history, then developed it. The lack of an extant 1820 account permits the model. The 1832 manuscript is a firm latest boundary, and D&C 20:5–8 may contain an earlier remission-of-sins trace, though that possible allusion is too nonspecific to disprove construction.

- **Possible but partly ad hoc — exhaustive harmonization.** Every later detail is presumed present in 1820 but selectively omitted earlier; the two uses of “Lord” in 1832 may be assigned to two beings; all chronological differences are approximation. None is impossible, but the model becomes ad hoc when omission is invoked automatically without contextual evidence. The stronger harmonizing case rests on demonstrated genre and audience differences, not merely on logical possibility.

### Considered and set aside

- **Perfect consistency** was set aside because the texts genuinely vary in chronology, motive, explicit personages, and detail.
- **Fatal contradiction from the 1832 singular wording alone** was set aside because the account does not say that only one being appeared; omission and contradiction are different claims.
- **The two-“Lord” reading as established fact** was set aside because the grammar permits but does not clearly require two referents.
- **The Damascus-road parallel as authentication** was set aside because literary variation in Acts shows possibility, not the historicity of Joseph's vision.
- **Current publication as proof there was never concealment** was set aside because present transparency does not resolve the mid-twentieth-century excision or earlier access history.
- **Archival excision as proof of a suppression conspiracy** was set aside because the manuscript evidence establishes removal and custody but not remover or motive.
