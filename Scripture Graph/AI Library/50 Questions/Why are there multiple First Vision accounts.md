# Why are there multiple First Vision accounts?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Joseph Smith left four firsthand accounts (1832, 1835, 1838/39 — the
canonized [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] — and 1842 Wentworth letter), plus
several secondhand reports. They differ in emphasis and detail (his age, the
number of personages explicitly mentioned, the framing motive — forgiveness
of sins vs. which church is true). Multiple retellings over 12+ years to
different audiences are normal for memory and rhetoric; the accounts agree
on the core event: a theophany in answer to prayer in spring 1820-ish.
Whether the differences are innocent retelling variance or evidence of an
evolving story is the live debate.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## The accounts

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **1832** (holograph, earliest): emphasizes forgiveness; explicitly
  mentions "the Lord"; written in Joseph's own hand.
- **1835** (journal, to Robert Matthews): two personages, angels mentioned.
- **1838/39** (official history → [[Joseph Smith—History 1]]): fullest;
  two personages, "This is My Beloved Son"; persecution narrative.
- **1842** (Wentworth letter): concise public summary; "two glorious
  personages."
All are published in full by the Joseph Smith Papers, and the Church's
Gospel Topics essay treats them openly — the documents are not hidden.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- The 1832 account's singular focus (and no explicit second personage) is
  read by critics as the "original" story later expanded.
- No known contemporary (1820s) documentation of the vision; earliest
  telling is 12 years after the event.
- Details like the revival chronology have been challenged (Wesley Walters)
  and defended (Milton Backman's documentation of 1819–20 regional revivals).
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Defenders note: composite memory normally *adds specificity for audience and
purpose*; the 1832 account's "the Lord" appearing does not exclude the
Father (and its opening frame mentions two beings' roles); private
theophanies in the 1820s went untold for fear of ridicule (which the 1838
account itself reports); and the variations match well-documented patterns
in repeated autobiographical narration. The full-document transparency of
the JSP publication is itself a point in favor of good faith.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The documentary record is genuinely multiple and genuinely harmonizable;
neither "fatal contradiction" nor "perfect consistency" survives a careful
reading. The strongest honest statement: the accounts vary the way live
memory varies, and the theological weight the Restoration places on the
event rests on Joseph's consistent core claim, not on detail stability.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
