# Was the Bible changed by councils and copyists?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Not in the way popular accounts imagine, and yes in ways that matter to
Latter-day Saints in particular. The Council of Nicaea (325) did not decide
the canon; the books of the New Testament were in broad use by the second
century and formally listed by the fourth, by consensus rather than decree.
Copyists did introduce variants — hundreds of thousands across the
manuscripts — but the overwhelming majority are spelling and word order,
and the few substantive ones (the ending of Mark, the woman taken in
adultery, the "Johannine comma") are known and flagged in modern editions.
What Latter-day Saints add is a different claim: that before the
manuscripts we have, "plain and precious" teachings were lost or removed
([[1 Nephi 13]]), which textual criticism can neither confirm nor refute
because it happened, if it happened, before the earliest copies.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- The New Testament is the best-attested ancient text by far: over 5,000
  Greek manuscripts, some within a century of composition, against a
  handful (often a millennium later) for classical authors.
- The Dead Sea Scrolls showed the Hebrew Bible copied with remarkable
  fidelity over a thousand years — the Great Isaiah Scroll matches the
  medieval text closely.
- The canon's formation is documented (Muratorian fragment, Athanasius's
  367 letter) and shows debate at the margins (Hebrews, Revelation,
  2 Peter) but a stable core from early on; no council "voted out" books
  that had been scripture.
- The Book of Mormon's claim of loss is about *content* in the earliest
  transmission, and there is evidence of early theological diversity
  (Ehrman's own *Orthodox Corruption of Scripture*) that fits a period of
  contest.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- "Plain and precious things removed" is unfalsifiable by design — the
  loss is placed exactly where no manuscript can check it.
- The Joseph Smith Translation restores no readings attested in any
  manuscript; its changes look like expansion and clarification, not
  recovery.
- Some variants *do* touch doctrine (the Johannine comma is the clearest
  Trinitarian proof text and is a late insertion), so "no doctrine
  affected" is too strong.
- The Old Testament's textual history is messier than the Isaiah scroll
  suggests: the Septuagint and Dead Sea texts of Jeremiah and Samuel differ
  substantially from the Hebrew we have.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Latter-day Saints reply that Joseph Smith himself did not claim the JST
was a manuscript restoration in most cases — it is inspired commentary,
harmonization and, in places, restoration; that the claim of early loss is
about the first generations, when the New Testament's own letters describe
false teachers, altered letters ([[2 Thessalonians 2]]) and departing
traditions; that the Nephi passage says the losses caused people to
"stumble," not that the Bible is unreliable — the Church uses the KJV and
treats it as scripture; and that the eighth Article of Faith ("as far as
it is translated correctly") is a modest claim, not a suspicious one.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The Bible we have is substantially the Bible the early church had; the
copyist-corruption story is mostly myth, and Latter-day Saints should not
lean on it. The Restoration's real claim is different and earlier — that
something was lost before the record we can check — and it should be
stated as a claim of revelation, not of textual criticism. A believer can
trust the text and still hold that revelation restored what it lacked; a
critic is right that the second half of that sentence cannot be tested by
manuscripts.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
