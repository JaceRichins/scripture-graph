# Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The historical outline is substantially settled, but the claimed divine reason is not. Black men such as [[Elijah Abel]] and [[Q. Walker Lewis]] were ordained during [[Joseph Smith Jr]]'s lifetime; the general priesthood restriction began under [[Brigham Young]], who publicly announced it in 1852, and temple exclusion subsequently affected Black women as well as men. No canonized revelation instituting the restriction is known, and the Church's own [[Race and the Priesthood (Essay)]] says the record gives no clear explanation of its origin, disavows the curse-of-Cain and premortal-unworthiness theories once used to defend it, and condemns racism. In June 1978, [[Spencer W. Kimball]], the First Presidency, and the Twelve reported receiving revelation ending the restriction; Official Declaration 2 announced ordination without regard to race or color, and temple access followed worldwide. Current instructional material, [[December 8–14 “We Believe” Articles of Faith and Official Declarations 1 and 2]], states plainly that the Church does not know why the restriction existed. Historical evidence strongly supports nineteenth-century racial ideas and institutional precedent as part of how it developed; whether God commanded it, permitted a human error, or was not involved in it is not settled by the surviving documents.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The practice changed over time.** Black men, notably [[Elijah Abel]] and [[Q. Walker Lewis]], were ordained during [[Joseph Smith Jr]]'s lifetime, while [[Brigham Young]] publicly announced the general restriction in 1852. That sequence is documented in [[Race and the Priesthood (Essay)]] and weighs strongly against an uninterrupted rule received at the Church's founding.

- **There is an evidentiary asymmetry.** No known canonized revelation institutes the restriction, and the official essay says the records give no clear insight into its origin. By contrast, the removal is expressly presented as revelation in Official Declaration 2 and in the participant history summarized by [[The Life and Ministry of Spencer W. Kimball]], [[Chapter 22 Revelation ‘A Continuous Melody and a Thunderous Appeal’]], and Edward L. Kimball's 2008 study, “Spencer W. Kimball and the Revelation on Priesthood.”

- **The discarded rationales are identified, not merely softened.** [[Race and the Priesthood (Essay)]] disavows claims about a curse or divine disfavor attached to Black skin, premortal misconduct, interracial marriage as sin, and racial inferiority. Thus these theories are evidence about what some leaders and members once believed, not valid present answers to the question.

- **The canon supplies universalist pressure but no instituting rule.** 2 Nephi 26:33 says God denies none who come to him and includes “black and white” in its declaration that all are alike unto God. [[D&C 107]] describes priesthood structure without stating a Black racial disqualification. These texts challenge inherent-inferiority theories, although neither independently specifies every historical rule governing office or temple admission.

- **The 1978 event is unusually well attested as an institutional religious experience.** Participants described prolonged prayer, unanimity, and a shared spiritual confirmation in the Salt Lake Temple; Official Declaration 2 was then publicly accepted. This strongly supports sincerity and institutional consensus. It does not, by historical method alone, prove supernatural causation.

- **The affected members' experience is part of the evidence.** [[Witnessing the Faithfulness]] records that Black Saints continued in faith while being denied significant ordinances and offices. Their endurance does not make the restriction harmless; it documents both commitment and cost.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The duration and injury are morally grave.** A church claiming living [[Prophets]] maintained race-based exclusion across generations. Black Saints could be baptized and contribute to Church life while being denied ordinances the Church taught were central to eternal family life. Any account must confront that asymmetry rather than treating 1978 as a triumphant conclusion detached from the preceding harm.

- **Leaders did more than inherit a neutral administrative custom.** Some defended the restriction publicly with racial and theological assertions, sometimes presenting them authoritatively. Later calling these assertions "theories" does not erase the authority with which members heard them or the consequences imposed through them.

- **The missing instituting revelation is a serious problem.** If revelation was required to end the restriction, critics ask why no comparable revelation establishing it can be produced. The documentary asymmetry fits a human-origin model at least as readily as a temporary-divine-command model.

- **The timing permits a sociological explanation.** Worldwide growth, changing racial norms, public controversy over BYU's athletic programs (including boycotts by opposing teams), ancestry-classification problems in Brazil, and the approaching São Paulo Temple all increased the practical cost of the restriction. A critic can interpret the 1978 experience as sincere religious ratification of a change made necessary by institutional circumstances.

- **The dilemma for prophetic authority remains.** If the restriction was human error, senior leaders maintained and theologized a grave injustice for more than a century. If it was God's command, the moral problem shifts to divine racial exclusion. Current official sources reject former rationales but do not expressly choose between these alternatives.

- **Official repair remains contested.** [[Race and the Priesthood (Essay)]] disavows theories and condemns racism, but it does not issue a formal institutional apology or clearly assign responsibility. Critics reasonably distinguish repudiating explanations from acknowledging that the restriction itself was wrong.

- **Acts 10 can be an inadequate analogy.** [[Acts 10]] concerns the opening of the gospel to Gentiles within its own ancient setting. Invoking Peter's correction may illuminate prophetic learning, but it cannot establish the origin of the modern restriction and may become evasive if it substitutes analogy for the actual racial history.

- **Religious testimony is not public proof.** The convergent accounts of the 1978 temple experience establish that participants interpreted something powerful as revelation. Psychological convergence, prayerful consensus, later memory, and social pressure remain naturalistic alternatives.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believing responses begin by conceding rather than dissolving the historical problem. They note that Restoration scripture never promises that every administrative judgment or inherited assumption of a prophet is infallible. [[Acts 10]] shows [[Peter (apostle)]] requiring revelation to abandon a boundary he had previously treated as religiously binding; this makes prophetic correction scripturally possible, though it does not prove that the two cases have the same cause.

A second response distinguishes the reality of revelation in 1978 from an unwarranted claim that the original restriction was revealed. Official Declaration 2, [[The Life and Ministry of Spencer W. Kimball]], [[The Truth of All Things (Elder David F. Evans, October 2017)]], and [[The Blessing of Continuing Revelation to Prophets and Personal Revelation to Guide Our Lives (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2020)]] consistently present the removal as revelation, arrived at only after sustained seeking — including [[David O. McKay]]'s private prayers about the restriction and [[Harold B. Lee]]'s study of the question in the years before 1978. A believer may therefore conclude that God corrected a humanly developed restriction without pretending to possess a revealed explanation for its origin.

A third response holds social context and revelation together. Practical crises can prompt a prophetic question without predetermining the answer; scripture commonly portrays revelation as arising through concrete need. This preserves the reported spiritual experience while acknowledging that global expansion and racial injustice made the status quo increasingly untenable. It remains a faith interpretation, not a historical demonstration.

Finally, the official disavowal matters: curse and premortal-unworthiness theories should no longer be repeated as explanations. Yet an adequate believing response should also center Black Saints, including [[Jane Manning James]], acknowledge institutional responsibility and lasting injury, and permit calls for clearer repentance and repair. Defending prophetic fallibility cannot mean treating the harmed as acceptable collateral.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** Black men were ordained during [[Joseph Smith Jr]]'s lifetime; the general restriction arose under [[Brigham Young]]; it eventually denied priesthood ordination to Black men and temple endowment and sealing to Black women and men; former racial rationales are now disavowed; and the restriction ended in 1978 through an event Church leaders unanimously testified was revelation. Official Declaration 2, [[Race and the Priesthood (Essay)]], and [[Witnessing the Faithfulness]] are the essential anchors.

**Strongly supported but interpretive:** The documentary sequence and nineteenth-century context make human cultural influence and institutional development much better supported than any specific claimed divine rationale. The evidence does not justify saying that Joseph Smith instituted the general restriction, that Black people were premortally less worthy, or that scripture plainly required the restriction.

**Open:** The precise mixture of Brigham Young's motives, inherited American racial ideas, scriptural interpretation, and claimed inspiration; the stages by which temple restrictions hardened; why successive leaders did not reverse the rule earlier; and the causal weight of global and institutional pressures in 1978.

**Matter of faith:** Whether the June 1978 experience was revelation from God, whether God merely permitted an earlier human error, and how divine guidance operates through fallible leaders. A believer can affirm corrective revelation while acknowledging a profound prophetic and institutional failure; a critic can explain the same public evidence without supernatural causation. Neither interpretation should be misrepresented as historically proven.

The most defensible concise conclusion is therefore not “God imposed the restriction for an unknown wise purpose.” It is that the historical restriction developed under Church leadership in a racialized nineteenth-century setting, that no verified divine rationale is known, and that the Church now repudiates the theories formerly used to explain it. The moral and theological implications for [[Prophets]], [[Revelation]], and [[Sustaining Church Leaders]] remain serious rather than simple.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Read Official Declaration 2 alongside [[2 Nephi 26]] (especially 2 Nephi 26:33), [[Acts 10]], [[D&C 107]], and [[D&C 138]]. Track exactly what each text says and resist importing a racial rule into passages that never mention one.

- Begin the institutional history with [[Race and the Priesthood (Essay)]], then read [[Witnessing the Faithfulness]] so that policy history is not separated from Black Saints' lived experience. Compare [[Race and The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]] and [[December 8–14 “We Believe” Articles of Faith and Official Declarations 1 and 2]] for current official framing.

- For the 1978 decision, read Edward L. Kimball, “Spencer W. Kimball and the Revelation on Priesthood,” *BYU Studies* 47, no. 2 (2008), together with [[The Life and Ministry of Spencer W. Kimball]] and [[Chapter 22 Revelation ‘A Continuous Melody and a Thunderous Appeal’]]. Distinguish participant testimony, later recollection, and theological interpretation.

- For critical historical reconstruction, consult W. Paul Reeve, *Religion of a Different Color: Race and the Mormon Struggle for Whiteness* (2015); Russell W. Stevenson, *For the Cause of Righteousness: A Global History of Blacks and Mormonism, 1830–2013* (2014); and Matthew L. Harris and Newell G. Bringhurst, *The Mormon Church and Blacks: A Documentary History* (2015). Check their claims against the primary documents they reproduce or cite.

- Study the biographies of [[Elijah Abel]], [[Q. Walker Lewis]], and [[Jane Manning James]]. They illuminate different dimensions of priesthood, temple access, and Black belonging that an institution-only chronology can obscure.

- Compare this case with [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]], [[What does the Book of Mormon say about skin color and the Lamanites]], [[Premortal Life]], [[Priesthood]], [[Temples]], and [[Revelation]]. Keep the Book of Mormon's internal skin-language questions analytically distinct from the nineteenth-century priesthood-temple restriction.

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require.** Official Declaration 2 requires recognition that the 1978 First Presidency and Twelve testified of revelation and authorized ordination without regard to race or color. It does **not** disclose why the restriction began, say that Brigham Young received a revelation instituting it, or endorse any former racial theory. 2 Nephi 26:33 requires a universal divine invitation and rejects inherent exclusion from coming to Christ; it does **not** explicitly define eligibility for every priesthood office. [[Acts 10]] permits a model in which revelation corrects an apostolic boundary, but it does not identify the cause of the modern restriction. D&C 124:127 and D&C 138:53 honor [[Brigham Young]]'s calling and foreordination without declaring every decision of his presidency infallible. Arguments that turn these limited statements into either proof of divine authorization or proof that no administrative restriction was possible attach conclusions to assumptions rather than to the texts.

**1. Historical-institutional development — independently supported.** The restriction developed under [[Brigham Young]] after Black ordinations in the Joseph Smith period, amid nineteenth-century racial thought; later leaders inherited it and supplied changing rationales. The chronology, public statements, and absence of a known instituting revelation strongly support this account of *how* the practice became established. [[Race and the Priesthood (Essay)]] and mainstream historians (Reeve, Stevenson) support this reconstruction. It does not by itself adjudicate divine involvement.

**2. Human error corrected through revelation — plausible, with partial independent support.** On this believing model, prophets can carry cultural assumptions into governance, while God eventually corrects the institution when leaders seek revelation. The human-development portion is historically supported; the divine-correction portion rests on Official Declaration 2 and participant testimony interpreted through faith. [[Acts 10]] supplies an analogy, not proof. This model explains the missing instituting revelation and the explicit revelatory removal better than a simple temporary-command theory, but it leaves the hard question of why correction took so long.

**3. Accreted precedent sincerely treated as divine — plausible and independently supported in part.** The restriction may not have originated in one sharply defined decision. Public teaching, administrative precedent, racial classification, and deference to predecessors could have hardened into a rule that later leaders sincerely believed only revelation could change. This accounts for both institutional continuity and sincere prophetic conviction without requiring a lost founding revelation.

**4. Temporary divine restriction for an unrevealed purpose — possible but not independently supported.** Official Declaration 2's language about the long-promised day can be read consistently with a prior divinely permitted or commanded limitation. No known instituting revelation or accepted rationale supplies positive evidence for this model, and current material says the reason is unknown. Claims about a specific divine purpose are therefore ad hoc unless new evidence is produced.

**5. Divine accommodation or delayed correction — possible, largely theological.** God may have tolerated an unjust practice because of leaders' or members' limitations and corrected it when the institution could receive the change. This can combine with model 2. It avoids attributing racial inferiority to God, but explaining a 126-year delay as divine accommodation is not independently testable and risks excusing preventable suffering.

**6. Mixed social and revelatory causation in 1978 — plausible.** Global growth, Brazil, shifting racial norms, and internal pastoral concern helped force sustained reconsideration; prayer and the reported temple experience supplied religious resolution. Social prompting and sincere revelation are not logically exclusive. Documentary reconstructions support the presence of both practical concerns and prolonged spiritual seeking, but historical evidence cannot measure the divine component.

**7. Fully human restriction and fully human reversal — plausible, and on the documentary record alone the best-evidenced naturalistic model.** Nineteenth-century racism produced the rule; precedent and prophetic authority preserved it; social, moral, and organizational pressures eventually produced consensus for change; participants interpreted that consensus as revelation. Every point in this model except the reported 1978 spiritual experience itself has direct documentary support — no instituting revelation, explicit racial context, disavowed rationales, mainstream-historian consensus — a stronger evidentiary footing on the origin question than model 2 has for its correction claim. It cannot disprove participants' spiritual interpretation, since divine causation is not historically testable; the two claims (naturalistic origin, and whether 1978 was more than consensus) are logically separable.

**8. Curse-based explanations tied to Genesis/Abraham lineage traditions — the ban's actual historical anchor, now set aside as disavowed and contradicted.** Beyond folk 'curse of Cain' talk, nineteenth- and twentieth-century leaders also pointed to Abraham 1's account of Pharaoh's lineage — descended through Ham and Egyptus — as barred from priesthood 'by reason of the curse,' and used it to justify the restriction. This was the closest thing to a scriptural anchor the restriction's defenders had. It is not the reason given in Official Declaration 2, 2 Nephi 26:33, or the priesthood organization described in [[D&C 107]], and Alma 13:3-5 discusses foreordination without connecting race to premortal failure. [[Race and the Priesthood (Essay)]] explicitly disavows this reading and the theories built on it; it is documented history of what was once argued, not a live explanation.

**9. An uninterrupted restriction revealed through Joseph Smith — set aside for lack of fit with the evidence.** The documented ordinations of [[Elijah Abel]] and [[Q. Walker Lewis]] during Joseph Smith's lifetime and the official essay's placement of the general restriction under Brigham Young count strongly against this reconstruction.

**10. "It was only policy" as a complete solution — set aside as inadequate.** The label does not explain origin, longevity, theological defenses, temple consequences, or prophetic responsibility. Distinguishing administrative practice from eternal doctrine is useful; using the distinction to deny the restriction's religious and moral weight is not.
