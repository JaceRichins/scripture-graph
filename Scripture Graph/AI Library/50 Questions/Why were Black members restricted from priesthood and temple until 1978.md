# Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
From the presidency of [[Brigham Young]] until June 1978, men of Black
African descent were not ordained to the priesthood and Black men and women
were not admitted to temple endowment and sealing. The Church's 2013 Gospel
Topics essay states that the restriction began under Brigham Young (not
Joseph Smith, who ordained at least two Black men), that the explanations
offered for it over the years — the curse of Cain, premortal unworthiness —
are "disavowed," and that the Church "condemns all racism, past and present."
The restriction ended by revelation to Spencer W. Kimball and the Twelve,
announced in Official Declaration 2. Why it existed at all is the question the
Church itself now leaves open; that it reflected its time is not in dispute.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Joseph Smith ordained Elijah Abel (1836) and Q. Walker Lewis; there is no
  revelation instituting a ban, and the earliest restriction documents are
  Brigham Young's statements in 1852 in a political context (Utah slavery
  legislation).
- The 1978 revelation was received in the temple by the entire First
  Presidency and Twelve, who described a unanimous spiritual confirmation —
  the strongest institutional revelation claim in the modern Church.
- The Church has since published the history, disavowed the theories, and
  (in 2020) had its president call for members to "root out racism."
- Scripture the Church holds canonical teaches that God "denieth none that
  come unto him, black and white" ([[2 Nephi 26]]).
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- A church claiming prophetic leadership maintained a racial exclusion for
  126 years — decades after most American institutions abandoned theirs —
  and its leaders taught the "curse" explanations as doctrine, not opinion.
- If the ban was a mistake, prophets erred on something central for a very
  long time; if it was God's will, then God excluded a race, which is worse.
  The essay's silence on which is the problem.
- The timing (1978, amid pressure over BYU athletics, the São Paulo temple,
  and growing membership in Brazil and Africa) invites a sociological rather
  than revelatory explanation.
- The disavowal came in an unsigned essay, not an apology from the pulpit.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believing responses generally accept that the ban was a human error that God
permitted and eventually corrected, the way scripture shows prophets (Peter
and the Gentiles in [[Acts 10]]) needing revelation to overcome inherited
prejudice; that revelation in the Church's understanding comes when leaders
seek it, and it was sought earnestly and repeatedly before 1978 (David O.
McKay's private prayers, Harold B. Lee's study); and that the essay's
"disavowal" language is stronger than an apology in one sense — it says the
theories were never true. Some believing scholars and many members wish the
Church would say "it was wrong" in those words; the Church has come close
without saying exactly that.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The history is documented and the Church no longer defends the ban or its
rationales. What remains genuinely unresolved is theological: how prophets
can be wrong about something so grave for so long, and what that means for
prophetic authority. The most honest believing position is that the
Restoration's promise was never leaders who cannot err but a God who
corrects them — and that this correction came far too late, at real cost to
real people. Anyone who says the question is simple has not read the sources.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
