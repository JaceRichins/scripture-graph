# Why were the Saints driven from Missouri?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
There was no single Missouri expulsion or single cause. In 1833, rapid gathering to [[Jackson County]], the Saints’ unusual claim that [[Independence (Missouri)]] was the center place of [[Zion]], cultural and economic tensions, prospective political power, and especially slaveholding Missourians’ fears about abolitionism and free Black migration produced organized vigilante removal. In 1838, older hostility combined with disputes over settlement and voting, inflammatory rhetoric, mob attacks, Danite activity, Latter-day Saint retaliation, and armed clashes; Governor [[Lilburn W. Boggs]] then authorized state force to drive the Saints out. The Doctrine and Covenants adds a theological interpretation: God allowed chastening because many Saints had not lived Zion’s commandments, while explicitly condemning the enemies who oppressed them. That religious claim is central to believing interpretation but cannot be established by ordinary historical evidence, and it does not excuse dispossession or violence.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The episodes are distinct.** Church History Topics, “Jackson County Violence,” documents the county expulsion of 1833; “Mormon-Missouri War of 1838” documents the later armed conflict and statewide removal. Treating them as one undifferentiated persecution obscures important changes.

- **Contemporary tensions are specific and cumulative.** The 1831–33 gathering arose from the revealed [[Zion]] project (D&C 57:1–5; 84:2–5). Historical records identify rapid immigration, religious difference, fears of political and economic dominance, and slavery-related panic. W. W. Phelps’s article about legal restrictions on free Black migration was an immediate catalyst, not a sufficient single cause.

- **Force accomplished the 1833 removal.** Opponents destroyed the Church press, tarred and feathered [[Edward Partridge]] and Charles Allen, compelled an evacuation agreement, attacked settlements, and participated in a militia action that disarmed the Saints before their flight. See “Jackson County Violence” and David W. Grua, “Waiting for the Word of the Lord.”

- **The canon gives a dual diagnosis.** [[D&C 101]] 1–9 attributes divine permission to failures within Zion, but D&C 103:1–4 and 124:49–54 condemn those who drove, smote, hindered, and oppressed the Saints. D&C 101:41 says the transgressors were “many, but not all.”

- **The 1838 removal had an immediate governmental mechanism.** The Missouri State Archives preserves Boggs’s October 27 executive order; militia pressure following the surrender of [[Far West]] produced mass flight to Illinois. The period meaning of “extermination” included forced expulsion and could also contemplate lethal force; the document does not by itself settle Boggs’s private intent.

- **The record does not conceal Latter-day Saint force in 1838.** The Church’s own “Mormon-Missouri War of 1838” recounts [[Sidney Rigdon]]’s militant rhetoric, Danite organization, retaliatory burning and confiscation, and the Crooked River clash. Acknowledging these facts does not excuse the indiscriminate attacks, [[Haun's Mill]], or statewide collective expulsion.

- **Later revelation rejects a simple failure verdict.** [[D&C 124]] 49–54 says that God accepted the offering of those commanded to build in Jackson County because enemies hindered them.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The chastening explanation can become victim-blaming.** D&C 101 was produced after the expulsion within the persecuted community. A critic can reasonably classify its attribution of suffering to transgression as retrospective theodicy, not independent evidence that God caused or permitted the event.

- **A persecution-only narrative is incomplete.** By 1838 some Saints used threatening rhetoric, organized the Danites, raided settlements, burned property, and confiscated goods. Describing the entire conflict as passive believers attacked solely for worship suppresses relevant evidence.

- **Opponents perceived a real power shift.** A rapidly growing, cohesive religious bloc declared the county divinely designated, traded internally, and could eventually dominate elections. Those facts could be experienced as threatening even when the Saints purchased land legally. Explaining that fear is not endorsing its racist assumptions or violent remedy.

- **The slavery issue is easily oversimplified.** Many Saints came from nonslaveholding states, but the Church had no simple abolitionist program in 1833. Local allegations that the Saints intended to foment slave rebellion exceeded the evidence, while the Phelps article genuinely intensified fears in a slave state.

- **“Both sides” can distort as much as hagiography.** Reciprocal violence in 1838 does not mean equal responsibility for every event. The 1833 campaign was organized coercive removal, and the 1838 order punished an entire religious population, including noncombatants, for a conflict involving only some members.

- **Promised-land language had political consequences.** Even though the revelations instructed purchase rather than seizure, assertions that God had given the land to the Saints could understandably alarm existing residents. Theologically sincere speech can still be socially provocative.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- A thoughtful believer need not choose between repentance and historical accountability. [[D&C 101]] criticizes failures among the Saints, while D&C 103:1–4 and 124:49–54 condemn their persecutors. The text itself refuses the inference that chastening made expulsion just.

- The canon limits collective blame. D&C 101:41 says “many, but not all”; D&C 105:7 exempts some from its condemnation; D&C 124:54 remembers people pure in heart slain in Missouri. Claims that every victim deserved displacement go beyond the text.

- The revealed program includes lawful conduct: purchase land, seek judicial and executive redress, retain lawful claims, sue for peace, and make peace proposals (D&C 101:68–101; 105:24–29, 38–40). This does not erase militant imagery elsewhere, but it challenges the claim that immediate unlawful seizure was the governing instruction.

- Believing interpretation can acknowledge human error in 1838. The official historical account’s inclusion of Danite activity and retaliatory raids permits faith without a sanitized history. Self-defense, retaliation, vigilantism, and attacks on noncombatants must be judged incident by incident under [[War and Peace]].

- [[D&C 123]] 1–17 commands evidence-gathering, publication, and petitions to government. Endurance in [[Liberty Jail]] is therefore paired with accountable public action, not minimization of abuse.

- A believer may accept the chastening interpretation as revelation while admitting its epistemic category: its divine authority is a matter of faith, whereas the presence of the interpretation in the December 1833 text is historically observable.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** The Missouri revelations require a layered reading rather than one exclusive cause. They say that enemies persecuted and cast out the Saints (D&C 101:1; 103:1–4), that God allowed chastening connected with failures among many Saints (D&C 101:2–8, 41), that lawful redress and land purchase remained required (D&C 101:68–101), and that faithful workers could be hindered by enemies without forfeiting divine acceptance (D&C 124:49–54). They do not require the claim that every victim sinned, that persecutors were innocent instruments, or that historians can verify divine causation.

- **Social-conflict and vigilante-removal model — independently supported.** Rapid migration, concentrated voting potential, religious and cultural difference, economic anxiety, promised-land rhetoric, racial panic in a slave state, and organized local violence explain the 1833 expulsion at the historical level. Contemporary documents and the Joseph Smith Papers support this model. It explains human action without resolving providence.

- **Reciprocal escalation and state-suppression model — independently supported for 1838.** Settling beyond Caldwell County, voting conflict, militia and mob mobilization, militant rhetoric, Danites, retaliatory raids, and armed clashes culminated in Boggs’s order and the surrender of [[Far West]]. This is substantially supported, but applying it backward to make 1833 equally reciprocal is an overreach.

- **Religious-persecution model — plausible but incomplete when isolated.** Religious hostility and opposition by civic and religious leaders are well documented. As a total explanation it neglects race, slavery, political power, land, economics, and the Saints’ conduct during the later war.

- **Providential chastening model — textually required within the Doctrine and Covenants’ own voice; faith-dependent as a larger conclusion.** D&C 101:2–8 and 103:4 connect suffering with failure to heed commandments. The model is internally explicit, not an inference invented by later apologists, but divine permission cannot be independently tested by historical method.

- **Trial and preparation model — plausible within the canon.** D&C 101:4 compares chastening to Abraham’s trial; D&C 105:9–19 interprets delayed redemption and [[Zion's Camp]] as preparation and a trial of faith. This may provide meaning for suffering, but it does not identify every historical cause or prove that each violent act was divinely scripted.

- **Institutional unpreparedness model — plausible, partly text-supported.** Contentions, incomplete [[Consecration]], insufficient unity, and inadequate preparation may have weakened the Zion project (D&C 101:6–8, 47–54; 105:2–10). Evidence that these internal failures materially caused outsiders’ violence is less direct than evidence for their theological significance.

- **Saints-as-aggressors model — partly supported for particular 1838 incidents, inadequate as a total account.** Danite intimidation, raids, burning, and confiscation are documented and must not be hidden. The model becomes tendentious when it erases prior expulsions, mob attacks, denial of voting and legal protection, violence against noncombatants, or the collective character of the state order.

- **Purely innocent-victims model — possible only in a limited, individual sense; contradicted as a total model.** Some victims were noncombatants and the canon itself exempts some Saints from condemnation. But the documentary record does not permit the claim that no Latter-day Saint contributed to escalation, especially in 1838.

- **Divinely mandated seizure model — weak and largely set aside for 1833.** Neighbors could hear Zion rhetoric as a threat, but D&C 101:68–80 and 105:27–30 repeatedly specify purchase. Militant redemption language and 1838 conduct complicate the picture, yet a simple plan to steal existing farms is not demonstrated.

- **Inevitable westward-relocation model — ad hoc and set aside.** Later history may make expulsion look like a necessary step toward Utah, but the Missouri texts anticipate retaining claims, returning, purchasing more land, and building Zion. They do not say God expelled the Saints in order to send them to the Salt Lake Valley.

- **Eden-site explanation — set aside as later tradition.** [[Life of Heber C. Kimball]] reports Jackson County as reputed to be ancient Eden, but the relevant revelations expressly discuss New Jerusalem, gathering, and temple building. The Eden association neither appears as the stated reason for local hostility nor explains the expulsions.

The generic “driven” passages in Exodus 12:39, Deuteronomy 18:12, Joshua 24:12, Isaiah 8:22, Jeremiah 13:24, Mosiah 17:15–18, and Alma 25:9–12 were also considered and set aside: they concern different peoples, places, and events.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The Saints experienced a coercive county expulsion in 1833 and a statewide forced removal after the 1838 war. Religious difference, demographic growth, political and economic fears, slavery-related racial panic, vigilantism, reciprocal escalation in 1838, failure of civil protection, and Boggs’s executive order are documented parts of the explanation. Latter-day Saint force in 1838 is also documented and cannot honestly be omitted.

**Established about the canon:** [[D&C 101]] and D&C 103 criticize failures among the Saints while condemning their enemies; D&C 101 prescribes purchase and legal redress; [[D&C 105]] postpones redemption and emphasizes preparation and peace; [[D&C 123]] requires documentation of abuses; and [[D&C 124]] says the Jackson County builders were hindered by enemies and their offering accepted.

**Open:** The relative weight of economic competition, political fear, religious hostility, racism, particular leaders’ choices, rumor, and local honor culture cannot be reduced to a precise formula. Boggs’s exact intended range of meaning in “exterminated,” the responsibility of individuals for particular episodes, and counterfactual questions about whether different Latter-day Saint rhetoric or settlement practices could have prevented war remain debated.

**Matter of faith:** That God specifically allowed the expulsions as chastening, used them as a trial, or promised an eventual redemption of the Missouri Zion cannot be established by historical method. A believer may receive those propositions as revelation without mislabeling them as independently demonstrated history. Faithfulness does not require calling the expulsion deserved, lawful, or morally acceptable.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with [[D&C 57]], [[D&C 84]], and [[D&C 101]] for the Missouri project and the canon’s interpretation of the 1833 expulsion. Continue with [[D&C 103]] and [[D&C 105]] alongside [[What was Zion's Camp]] for the attempted redemption and its postponement. Read [[D&C 98]] and [[War and Peace]] for the demanding standards governing patience, defense, and peace. For 1838 and its aftermath, read [[D&C 109]] 47; [[D&C 121]]–[[D&C 123]]; [[Liberty Jail]]; [[Far West]]; [[Haun's Mill]]; and [[Who were the Danites]].

For documentary history, consult the Joseph Smith Papers, Documents, Volume 3: February 1833–March 1834 and Documents, Volume 6: February 1838–August 1839; the Joseph Smith Papers historical introduction to the December 16–17, 1833 revelation; W. W. Phelps’s “Free People of Color”; the Jackson County removal manifesto; petitions to Governor Daniel Dunklin; Sidney Rigdon’s July 4, 1838 oration; and Boggs’s October 27 executive order in the Missouri State Archives. Read Church History Topics, “Jackson County Violence,” “Mormon-Missouri War of 1838,” and “Extermination Order” as modern syntheses, then compare them with primary documents.

Use [[Conference Report, April 1926 (General Conference (full report), April 1926)]] and [[The Latter-day Saints' Millennial Star 1841-01 (Vol. 1 No. 9)]] to study later Latter-day Saint memory, not as substitutes for contemporary evidence. Treat the Eden statement in [[Life of Heber C. Kimball]] as a later tradition distinct from the explicit Missouri rationale in D&C 57 and 84.
