# What does the Book of Mormon say about skin color and the Lamanites?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Book of Mormon's received English text plainly says that early Lamanites received a divinely caused "skin of blackness," calls their dark skin a distinguishing mark associated with an ancestral curse, and later reports that the skin of one group became white when they united with the Nephites (2 Nephi 5:20-23; Alma 3:6-17; 3 Nephi 2:14-16). It also condemns Nephite reviling based on skin, calls the Lamanites more righteous in a crucial comparison, makes Lamanite and Nephite identity socially and religiously permeable, and teaches that "all are alike unto God" (Jacob 3:5-9; Alma 3:11; 4 Nephi 1:17-20; 2 Nephi 26:33). The nature of the mark is not settled by independent evidence. Current official teaching in [[Race and The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]] frames the curse principally as loss of the Spirit and covenant blessings "for a time," notes the skin distinguished the peoples "for a time," and teaches that standing with God depends on devotion, not skin color — without itself resolving what the mark physically was.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Explicit wording:** [[2 Nephi 5]] says God caused a "skin of blackness" after Laman and Lemuel's separation and gives non-attraction and non-mixing as part of its rationale (2 Nephi 5:20-23). [[Alma 3]] calls Lamanite skins dark and associates them with the ancestral mark and curse (Alma 3:6-10, 14-17). [[3 Nephi 2]] reports that the skin of an assimilated Lamanite group became white (3 Nephi 2:14-16). A complete account must acknowledge this bodily language.

- **An explicit rebuke of color-based contempt:** [[Jacob 3]] identifies Nephite hatred connected with the Lamanites' skins, declares those Lamanites more righteous in marital and family conduct, and commands, "revile no more against them because of the darkness of their skins" (Jacob 3:5-9). This rebukes prejudice without making the chapter's color vocabulary disappear.

- **Curse and complexion do not always move together:** Converted Lamanites in [[Alma 23]] are said to be followed no longer by God's curse, but no skin change is reported (Alma 23:16-18). That is strong textual reason not to reduce every use of "curse" to pigmentation, although [[3 Nephi 2]] connects curse removal and a reported skin change in one episode.

- **Lamanite identity is permeable:** [[Alma 3]], [[Alma 23]], [[3 Nephi 2]], and [[4 Nephi 1]] allow belief, conversion, union, and voluntary naming to change group classification (Alma 3:11; Alma 23:16-18; 3 Nephi 2:14-16; 4 Nephi 1:17, 20). "Lamanite" therefore cannot safely be translated into one immutable modern race.

- **The book's controlling universal statement:** [[2 Nephi 26]] says that the Lord denies none who come to him, "black and white," and that all are alike unto God (2 Nephi 26:33). [[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]] makes the present doctrinal application explicit: divine favor depends on devotion, not skin color.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The surface reading is racially troubling.** [[2 Nephi 5]] does more than employ a generic light-versus-dark metaphor: it says God changed skin so the Lamanites would not be attractive, discourages intermarriage, and extends consequences to descendants (2 Nephi 5:21-23). [[Alma 3]] repeats ancestry and separation language. Calling the curse merely spiritual can understate what these passages actually say.

- **Curse and mark cannot be separated perfectly.** Current Church teaching usefully identifies being cut off from God's presence as the curse in 2 Nephi 5:20, but Alma 3:6 itself describes the dark mark as "a curse upon them." The text sometimes distinguishes spiritual status from the visible mark and sometimes places them in the same explanatory complex.

- **A metaphor-only solution is textually strained.** Jacob's judgment-scene "whiter" language and the current "pure" reading of 2 Nephi 30:6 support symbolic color usage, but 2 Nephi 5:21, Alma 3:6, and 3 Nephi 2:15 repeatedly say "skin." Symbolism may be part of the rhetoric without exhausting its apparent referent.

- **Universal salvation does not automatically erase racialization.** A text can invite every group to salvation while still encoding a color hierarchy or prejudicial ancestry story. Therefore, 2 Nephi 26:33 and Jacob's rebuke are important counterweights, not simple cancellations of 2 Nephi 5.

- **The historical claim lacks independent confirmation.** No external evidence presently establishes that an ancient American population underwent the narrated color changes. Repetition inside one textual tradition shows literary consistency, not independent historical corroboration. A critic can plausibly read these passages as reflecting racial assumptions available in nineteenth-century America; the current registry, however, has not isolated a specific source or established that explanation for these verses.

- **Later clarification cannot retroactively erase the wording.** Joseph Smith's edit of 2 Nephi 30:6 from "white" to "pure" clarifies that verse but leaves the explicit skin passages unchanged. Likewise, modern antiracist teaching states how the Church now interprets and applies the book; it does not prove what every original narrator meant.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- A thoughtful believer can begin by conceding the difficult wording rather than euphemizing it. The book itself supplies an internal moral critique: Jacob condemns skin-based reviling, denies Nephite superiority, praises Lamanite marital faithfulness, and commands his people to confront their own filthiness (Jacob 3:5-9).

- The text repeatedly makes covenant response more important than inherited classification. The Anti-Nephi-Lehies cease being called Lamanites and are no longer followed by the curse; another converted group is numbered among the Nephites; ultimately there are no "-ites" among the united people of Christ (Alma 23:16-18; 3 Nephi 2:14-16; 4 Nephi 1:17). These passages do not undo 2 Nephi 5, but they resist an immutable-race theology.

- Believers need not claim knowledge the text does not supply. [[Race and The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]] frames the curse as loss of the Spirit and covenant blessings "for a time" and pivots directly to the teaching that standing with God depends on devotion, not skin color — a principled limit on how far the passage should be extended, though it does not itself settle what the mark physically was.

- Inspiration need not mean that every narrator's inherited social vocabulary is a timeless divine classification. One believing model allows ancient record keepers to describe real events through limited ethnocentric categories while the book's larger movement—repentance, incorporation, unity, and equality before God—corrects group pride. This remains an interpretation, especially because Nephi attributes the initial change directly to God.

- The firm modern doctrinal norm is supplied by [[Racial and Cultural Prejudice (Gospel Topics)]], [[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]], and [[The Need for Greater Kindness (President Gordon B. Hinckley, April 2006)]]: racial superiority and disparagement are incompatible with discipleship, and skin color does not determine divine favor. That norm should govern application without being used to sanitize the historical text.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** The received text requires acknowledging explicit skin terminology, a narrated divine cause, ancestry and separation language, a prohibition on reviling, porous political-religious identities, and universal access to God. It does **not** identify the skin change with Africans, Cain, Ham, the former priesthood restriction, or any particular modern Indigenous population. It also does not explain a physical mechanism.

- **Final-form mixed pattern — independently supported.** The text simultaneously contains ancestry-linked visible difference and a sustained dismantling of moral superiority through conversion, renaming, righteous Lamanite examples, abolition of "-ites," and equality before God (Jacob 3:5-9; Alma 23:16-18; 4 Nephi 1:17; 2 Nephi 26:33).

- **Literal miraculous pigmentation — possible; surface-text plausible; not independently supported historically.** "Skin of blackness," "skins ... were dark," and "skin became white" naturally suggest outward bodily color (2 Nephi 5:21; Alma 3:6; 3 Nephi 2:15). No independent evidence presently verifies the event or its biological meaning.

- **Natural population change through ancestry, intermarriage, or lifestyle — possible but ad hoc as a complete explanation.** Population mixing, or prolonged outdoor/wilderness living (cf. Alma 3:5), could change average appearance, but the narrative assigns the changes to God and 3 Nephi 2 presents the result within a short interval. Either naturalistic mechanism is invoked without direct textual support.

- **Moral or covenantal color symbolism — plausible locally; weak as a total model.** Jacob's throne-of-God comparison and the current "pure and delightsome" wording in 2 Nephi 30:6 make symbolic purity language plausible (Jacob 3:8; 2 Nephi 30:5-6; compare the KJV idiom in Jeremiah 13:23, "can the Ethiopian change his skin"). Yet the repeated noun "skin," comparison with Nephite appearance, and the distinction between inherited and self-applied marks resist making every passage purely figurative.

- **Non-genetic bodily or cultural marking — possible but weakly supported.** Alma 3 shows chosen marks could express allegiance, but it distinguishes the Amlicites' red forehead mark from the Lamanites' dark skins, and 3 Nephi 2 says skin became white — a garment-only reading is especially strained.

- **Spiritual curse plus uncertain visible mark — plausible, consistent with current official framing.** [[Race and The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]] frames the primary curse as loss of the Spirit and covenant blessings "for a time," with skin distinguishing the peoples "for a time." Alma 23:18 supports a spiritual distinction, since converted Lamanites lose the curse without a reported complexion change. The model must still acknowledge that Alma 3:6 calls the dark mark itself a curse, so the categories overlap in at least one passage.

- **Narrator-relative ancient ethnocentrism — possible within an ancient believing model.** The descriptions may preserve an ancient record keeper's hostile boundary-making rather than a timeless divine anthropology, with Jacob's rebuke as internal correction. This incurs a cost because Nephi and Alma's narrator explicitly attribute the mark to God.

- **Nineteenth-century racialized composition or expansion — plausible critical model.** The wording fits early American habits linking complexion, ancestry, and divine judgment. The corpus-wide [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] finds nineteenth-century KJV-informed production plausible and biblical mediation independently supported, but has not established a specific source or provenance verdict for these particular passages.

- **Layered ancient source with modern translation or expansion — possible.** No surviving source-language manuscript identifies which elements belong to which layer; becomes ad hoc if invoked only wherever difficulty appears.

- **Identity-status model — independently supported for the names, incomplete for the skin language.** Alma 3:11, Alma 23:16-18, 3 Nephi 2:14-16, and 4 Nephi 1:17-20 show "Lamanite" can be reassigned through belief, alliance, or naming; this challenges a fixed modern-race equation but does not alone explain what "skin" meant.

**Set aside:** A Cain/Ham explanation is set aside because neither these passages nor Moses 5:39-40 make that identification. A wholesale equation of Lamanites with all Indigenous Americans is set aside given the text's permeable group boundaries. A purely garment-based reading is set aside as textually strained. Treating all color language as harmless symbolism is also set aside, since it fails to account for the explicit bodily and intermarriage wording.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The received Book of Mormon contains explicit and morally difficult dark-skin and white-skin language about some Lamanite groups. It connects the early mark with ancestry, separation, and discouragement of intermarriage. The same book condemns skin-based reviling, portrays Lamanites as morally superior to Nephites in important episodes, makes group names permeable, and declares universal equality before God.

**Open:** The mark's physical nature, whether the color statements are wholly literal or partly symbolic, how curse and mark relate in every passage, and whether the narrated changes occurred historically are unresolved. The text supplies no valid basis for mapping the mark to Africans, Cain's descendants, all Indigenous Americans, or another modern race. [[Book of Mormon and DNA Studies (Essay)]] concerns population ancestry and genetic inheritance, not proof of the narrated skin changes.

**Official present teaching:** [[Race and The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]] frames the curse as loss of the Spirit and covenant blessings "for a time" and teaches that standing with God depends on devotion, not skin color. [[Let God Prevail (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2020)]] reinforces that skin color does not determine favor with God. These teachings authoritatively govern current Church application; they do not themselves specify the mark's physical nature, erase the historical wording, or settle every semantic question.

**Provenance and faith:** [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] establishes no overall historicity verdict. Its findings about biblical mediation and final-form design neither verify nor disprove these narrated skin changes. Accepting the narrators' divine attribution, an ancient source, or the book's inspiration remains a matter of faith informed—but not mechanically established—by the available evidence. Finding the passages racially troubling or historically uncorroborated does not by itself prove fraud or exhaust the book's ethical trajectory.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
