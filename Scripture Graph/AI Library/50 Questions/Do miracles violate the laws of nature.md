# Do miracles violate the laws of nature?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Scripture does not settle whether miracles “violate the laws of nature,” because its miracle accounts ordinarily attribute events to God without defining natural law or explaining physical mechanisms. Some later writers, including Parley P. Pratt in [[Key to the Science of Theology]], explicitly used violation language; other serious models treat a miracle as divine action through unknown processes, altered causal conditions, or an exceptional act within a world continually sustained by God. The texts establish what their narrators claim happened and how they interpret it; they do not establish which philosophical model is correct. Whether any reported miracle actually occurred remains a historical and theological question requiring more than the narrative's assertion.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The canon does not state the proposed rule.** [[Ether 3]], [[Ether 6]], [[1 Corinthians 15]], and [[D&C 84]] attribute extraordinary events to God but never say that God broke, suspended, or merely employed a higher physical law. Any such classification adds a philosophical framework to the text.

- **The word “law” can mislead.** Exodus 16:4, 28-30 and Exodus 18:16, 20 concern commandments and ordinances. Those normative laws are not interchangeable with scientific laws, which describe observed regularities.

- **Some narratives expressly exceed a natural-only reconstruction.** In [[Ether 3]] and [[Ether 6]], divine action supplies light, directional wind, and preservation. [[Could the Jaredite barges have worked]] accordingly judges that a natural-only engineering test evaluates a narrower model than the narrative presents. It also judges that unrestricted miraculous aid cannot demonstrate engineering feasibility.

- **The best resurrection evidence is historical testimony, not a physical mechanism.** In [[1 Corinthians 15]], Paul transmits an established proclamation and claims an appearance himself. This strongly establishes early resurrection belief and Paul's interpretation of his experience; it does not independently verify every listed appearance or decide how resurrection relates to physical law. See [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]].

- **Restoration scripture emphasizes religious function.** D&C 84:65-73 treats signs as gifts for profit and salvation and forbids boasting or displaying them before the world. That is a theological account of purpose, not a scientific theory of mechanism.

- **Latter-day Saint usage has not been philosophically uniform merely because one formulation is explicit.** [[Key to the Science of Theology]] calls miracles contrary to nature's laws. [[Conference Report, April 1926 (General Conference (full report), April 1926)]] records engagement with the same objection. These are historically relevant statements, not canonical resolution of what a natural law is.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The Humean objection:** David Hume's *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, section X, defines a miracle as a violation of natural law and argues that well-established regular experience normally outweighs testimonial reports of exceptions. Human error, legend, deception, and misinterpretation are independently familiar; violations of well-confirmed regularities are not.

- **“Higher law” may merely rename the problem.** Unless a proposed higher law makes independent predictions or has evidence apart from the miracle report, invoking it after the fact is an unfalsifiable rescue. Almost any failed natural explanation could then be relabeled hidden divine physics.

- **An empirical exception remains an exception.** If a dead body returns to embodied life or a physical object behaves contrary to every applicable causal model, saying that an external agent intervened does not erase the conflict with a closed natural description. It changes the proposed cause, not the observed anomaly.

- **Most scriptural evidence is mediated testimony.** Paul's appearance list is preserved through Paul; only his own experience is first-person within 1 Corinthians 15. The Gospel accounts are four canonical presentations, not automatically four independent depositions. Acts and Luke are connected literary works. The registry assigns the internal witness language of 3 Nephi 7 only 0.14 historical weight because all its witnesses are mediated through one narrator. [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]] likewise judges 3 Nephi's explicitness to supply almost no independent confirmation unless the Book of Mormon's antiquity and historical reliability are established separately.

- **Detailed miracle stories can still be literary constructions.** Narrative coherence, named witnesses, and practical details may occur in sincere history, communal amplification, legend, or deliberate composition. They cannot by themselves discriminate among those possibilities.

- **Selective intervention is morally difficult.** If God can override lethal processes, the rarity and apparent unevenness of healing demand explanation. [[Why does God allow suffering]] and [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]] prevent a simple equation of faith with predictable rescue.

- **Rare natural events are not automatically miracles.** Coincidence, spontaneous remission, unusual weather, psychological experience, and incomplete knowledge can produce events that observers sincerely interpret religiously. Calling an event improbable does not identify its cause.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- **Natural laws describe regularities under specified conditions.** An external agent can alter a system's causal conditions without making its ordinary regularities false—just as human action changes what happens in a physical system without abolishing physics. This makes divine intervention conceptually possible, though it supplies no evidence that a particular intervention occurred.

- **The text need not adopt Hume's definition.** Scripture's principal claim is that God acts purposefully. It does not ordinarily assert that an impersonal law is metaphysically ultimate or that God creates a logical contradiction. A believer may therefore understand miracles as exceptional divine agency rather than lawlessness.

- **Unknown mechanism and absent cause are different claims.** Failure to specify a physical mechanism does not logically prove that there is none. But intellectual honesty also forbids converting ignorance into positive evidence for a hidden law or divine act.

- **Historical claims are assessed cumulatively.** In the resurrection case, believers appeal to the early received tradition, Paul's claimed experience and changed allegiance, the named appearance traditions, and the empty-tomb and embodied-encounter narratives considered together. [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]] treats these as meaningful but disputed evidence, not laboratory proof.

- **Scripture itself resists spectacle-centered faith.** D&C 84:65-73 subordinates signs to profit and salvation, while [[Fourth Floor, Last Door (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, October 2016)]] says faith cannot violate another person's [[Agency]]. These teachings discourage treating miracles as mechanically demandable demonstrations.

- **A believer can affirm providence without claiming every anomaly as supernatural.** Investigation of ordinary causes is compatible with faith. [[How do faith and science fit together]] is strongest when neither scientific uncertainty nor theological commitment is misrepresented as more decisive than it is.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require.** Read straightforwardly, [[Ether 3]], [[Ether 6]], [[1 Corinthians 15]], and [[3 Nephi 11]] require their narrators to be claiming extraordinary divine action. They do not require the propositions that natural laws are exceptionless metaphysical constraints, that God “breaks” those constraints, or that every miracle operates through an undiscovered physical law. D&C 84:65-73 adds purpose and restraint, not mechanism. The objection that miracles violate nature therefore attaches partly to a philosophical definition of miracle; the supporting appeal to “higher laws” likewise adds a theory the passages do not state.

- **Violation model — possible; historically attested; philosophically disputed.** On Hume's definition and the wording in [[Key to the Science of Theology]], a miracle is an exception to a genuine law of nature. This model faces the objection that a genuine exception may show that the proposed law was incomplete or incorrectly scoped rather than literally violated.

- **External-agent or changed-boundary-conditions model — plausible and independently intelligible; not independently established for any cited miracle.** God acts on a physical system, changing its causal inputs while ordinary regularities continue to describe what happens given the new conditions. Human agency supplies an analogy for external input, but not evidence that divine input occurred. This model reconciles miracle and law conceptually without verifying a report.

- **Higher or unknown law model — possible; weakly supported unless predictive.** A miracle occurs through regularities unknown to present science. This is compatible with the texts, but they do not describe such laws. When invoked solely because an event lacks an explanation, the model is ad hoc.

- **Local suspension model — possible within some forms of theism; ad hoc without further evidence.** God temporarily prevents an ordinary regularity from operating. The model fits narratives of exceptional action but supplies no independent mechanism or test and risks making any counterevidence absorbable.

- **Continuous divine governance model — plausible within classical theism; metaphysical rather than scientific.** Natural laws describe God's usual governance, while miracles are unusual acts of the same sustaining agent. This removes the image of God fighting an independent machine, but its truth depends on broader arguments about [[Does God exist]] and [[Creation]], not on a miracle story alone.

- **Providential coincidence model — possible and sometimes plausible.** The event is naturally possible but religiously significant because of its timing or meaning. This avoids physical violation, though it must still address whether intentional divine coordination has evidence beyond perceived significance.

- **Transformative-new-creation model — textually plausible for resurrection; mechanism open.** First Corinthians 15:35-54 presents resurrection as transformation into incorruption, glory, and power. That can be read as divine creation of a new embodied condition rather than repair by ordinary biology. The text supports transformation but not a scientific account of it.

- **Natural misidentification model — independently supported as a general possibility; case-specific probability varies.** Unusual but natural events, altered states, memory development, mistaken diagnosis, or coincidence are interpreted as miracles. Such processes are well attested generally, but their availability does not establish that they explain every particular report.

- **Legendary or literary model — possible; plausible to differing degrees by document.** A miracle account serves theological or literary purposes without preserving a corresponding supernatural event. The mediated testimony of 3 Nephi 7, assigned only 0.14 historical weight by the registry, shows why internal witness language alone cannot exclude this model. The model still requires case-by-case literary and historical analysis; “miracle story” is not itself proof of invention.

- **Unrestricted-intervention model — logically possible but evidentially ad hoc.** God supplies every missing physical condition. [[Could the Jaredite barges have worked]] correctly observes that this can preserve theological coherence while ending engineering adjudication: no conceivable physical difficulty counts against an unrestricted hypothesis.

**Considered and set aside.** The claim that science has disproved every miracle was set aside because science cannot test unrestricted divine action as such and historical claims require case-specific evidence. The opposite claim that scientific ignorance supports miracle was set aside because missing explanation is not positive evidence of divine causation. The claim that all miracles must share one mechanism was also set aside: providential timing, healing, resurrection, visionary experience, and creation need not present the same causal question.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** Scripture reports events interpreted by its authors as divine acts; some accounts explicitly include continuing divine intervention. It does not supply a general theory of natural law or state whether miracles violate, suspend, supplement, or operate through it. The terms “God's laws” and “laws of nature” refer to different categories unless an argument connects them. Historical Latter-day Saint writers did sometimes employ violation language, as [[Key to the Science of Theology]] demonstrates.

**Open:** The correct philosophy of natural law; whether external divine agency should count as a violation; whether unknown processes could underlie particular events; and the historical reliability and independence of each miracle tradition. The resurrection evidence is stronger for the existence of early resurrection proclamation than for a recoverable physical mechanism. Jaredite narrative detail is insufficient for either a successful engineering validation or a decisive natural-impossibility proof.

**Matter of faith:** Affirming that God raised Jesus, preserved the Jaredites, or performed a reported healing goes beyond what textual observation or natural science alone establishes. Such faith may be reasoned and informed by cumulative evidence, but it should not be presented as a mechanically demonstrated conclusion. Conversely, methodological naturalism does not by itself prove metaphysical naturalism or God's nonexistence.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with [[1 Corinthians 15]] and [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]] to separate early proclamation, historical inference, and claims about transformed embodiment. Read [[Ether 2]], [[Ether 3]], and [[Ether 6]] alongside [[Could the Jaredite barges have worked]] for a disciplined comparison of narrated divine action and engineering underdetermination. Study D&C 84:65-73 with [[Miracles]], [[Spiritual Gifts]], and [[Faith]] for the stated purposes and limits of signs.

For philosophical foundations, read David Hume's *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, section X, followed by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry “Miracles,” which surveys objections to Hume and competing definitions. Compare these with [[Key to the Science of Theology]], [[Conference Report, April 1926 (General Conference (full report), April 1926)]], and [[Gospel Doctrine]] as distinct historical Latter-day Saint voices rather than interchangeable doctrinal authorities.

Then pursue the empirical and pastoral dimensions through [[Does intercessory prayer work]], [[How do faith and science fit together]], [[Why does God allow suffering]], and [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]].
