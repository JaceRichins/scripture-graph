# Did Jesus rise from the dead?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The historical core is unusually well established for antiquity: Jesus was
crucified under Pilate; his followers very soon afterward proclaimed that
he had been raised and that they had seen him; that conviction transformed
them from a scattered group into a movement willing to die for the claim;
and a persecutor of the movement, Paul, became its greatest missionary on
the strength of his own encounter ([[1 Corinthians 15]], written within
about twenty-five years, quotes a creed older still). What history cannot
do is verify a resurrection, since it lies outside what historians are
equipped to confirm. The Book of Mormon adds a second, independent witness
tradition: the risen Christ appearing to a people who had never met him
([[3 Nephi 11]]).
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- The creed in [[1 Corinthians 15]]:3–7 lists named witnesses, including
  five hundred at once, "most of whom remain alive" — an invitation to check
  that would be reckless if untrue.
- The empty tomb is reported in all four Gospels with women as the first
  witnesses — an embarrassing detail no one inventing the story in that
  culture would choose.
- The disciples' transformation is a datum critics must explain: men who
  fled the crucifixion preached the resurrection in the same city weeks
  later, and several were executed for it.
- Alternative explanations (theft, swoon, wrong tomb, hallucination) each
  account for part of the evidence and fail on the rest; group
  hallucinations of a physical, eating, touchable person are not a
  recognized phenomenon.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Miracles are, by definition, the least probable explanation of any
  evidence; a natural explanation, however strained, is always likelier
  than a suspension of nature (Hume's argument).
- The Gospel accounts differ in details (who went to the tomb, where the
  appearances occurred, whether the disciples were told to go to Galilee)
  and were written decades later by believers.
- Visions of a beloved dead leader are a known grief phenomenon; the
  "appearances" may have been experiences that later hardened into physical
  claims as the tradition developed.
- The earliest source, Paul, describes his own encounter as a vision and
  does not mention an empty tomb.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Defenders reply that Hume's argument assumes what it sets out to prove
(that miracles do not happen); that the Gospel differences are exactly the
variation independent testimony shows, while the core — the tomb empty, the
women first, the appearances — is stable; that Paul's "was buried … was
raised" implies the tomb, and his description of the risen body
([[1 Corinthians 15]]:35–49) is bodily, not ghostly; and that grief visions
do not produce empty tombs or convert enemies. The strongest believing
statement is not "history proves it" but "history is what you would expect
if it happened, and awkward for every alternative."
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The evidence is as good as ancient evidence gets and it is not proof.
Whether one concludes "he rose" or "something happened that they believed
was rising" turns on prior views about whether God acts in history — which
the evidence can inform but not decide. Latter-day Saints add that the
resurrection is not only a historical question but a present one: the
promise of [[Moroni 10]] is that the same Spirit that testified to the
first witnesses testifies still. That is a claim to be tested, not a
substitute for the history.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
