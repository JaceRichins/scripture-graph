# Did Jesus rise from the dead?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The canonical texts answer yes: Jesus truly died, God raised him, and he appeared alive. Historically, the most secure conclusions are narrower: Jesus was crucified; resurrection belief arose very early; Paul transmitted an established appearance tradition and personally believed that he had seen the risen Jesus; and the Gospels preserve empty-tomb and embodied-appearance narratives ([[1 Corinthians 15]], [[Luke 24]], [[John 20]]). Whether these facts are best explained by a divine bodily resurrection remains disputed because the sources are Christian testimony and the event is not independently observable. The Book of Mormon adds a detailed second scriptural witness in [[3 Nephi 11]], but it becomes independent historical evidence only if the record's antiquity and reliability are established separately. Thus resurrection is an historically serious and textually central faith conclusion, not a mechanically proved result.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The early Pauline tradition:** In 1 Corinthians 15:1-8 Paul says he received and delivered the proclamation that Christ died, was buried, was raised, and appeared. The formulation and named sequence strongly support a resurrection message that predates this letter and was not Paul's invention.

- **Paul's first-person claim:** Paul says that he saw Jesus and places his experience in the same appearance sequence as Cephas, the Twelve, James, and others (1 Corinthians 9:1; 15:8-10). Galatians 1:13-24 independently within Paul's letters connects his reversal with a claimed revelation. This strongly supports sincerity and a perceived encounter, though not one uncontested explanation of it.

- **A multiply preserved core:** [[Matthew 28]], [[Mark 16]], [[Luke 24]], and [[John 20]] all present the tomb as empty and women as the first discoverers or recipients of the news. The Gospels are literarily related and should not be treated as four wholly independent depositions, but their shared core is historically relevant.

- **Embodied rather than merely continued influence:** Luke 24:36-43 and John 20:24-29 emphasize wounds, touch, and eating. Paul likewise describes resurrection as transformation into an incorruptible spiritual body, not abandonment of embodiment (1 Corinthians 15:35-54). These passages establish what the authors meant to affirm, although they are still testimony rather than physical evidence.

- **Explanatory scope:** A bodily resurrection explains in one account both the empty-tomb tradition and the conviction that Jesus appeared. Natural models can explain these data, but commonly require more than one process—for example, visionary experiences plus removal, relocation, or later development of the tomb tradition. Simplicity alone is not proof, especially when the resurrection model also depends on the disputed premise of divine action.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The source problem:** No surviving neutral contemporary observer documents the resurrection. The extant sources were written by participants in, or advocates of, the movement and are intended to produce belief. Paul is early, but he personally reports only his own experience; the other witnesses reach us through him or later narratives.

- **Paul's limited description:** Paul does not narrate the discovery of an empty tomb and gives little sensory description of any appearance. He uses the same appearance sequence for his post-ascension encounter and the earlier witnesses (1 Corinthians 15:5-8), leaving room for the proposal that all were visionary experiences later narrated more physically.

- **Narrative differences and development:** The Gospels differ over the women named, the messengers, what the women did, and how Jerusalem and Galilee appearances are sequenced ([[Why do the Gospels differ from one another]]). Mark 16:1-8 ends without a narrated appearance, while the manuscript status of Mark 16:9-20 is disputed. A critic can interpret the increasing narrative detail as development rather than independent confirmation.

- **Religious experiences have natural analogues:** Bereavement experiences, visions, ecstatic states, group expectation, memory reconstruction, and social reinforcement are known phenomena. They can explain sincere conviction without fraud. A critic need not claim that five hundred people had identical clinical hallucinations; Paul's compressed report does not tell us what each person experienced.

- **The empty tomb is disputed:** Women at the tomb may support historicity, but the claim that no ancient author would invent female witnesses is too strong. Women could be included because they had roles in burial traditions, because male disciples had fled, or for theological and literary purposes. Even a genuine empty tomb would not identify its cause.

- **Prior probability matters:** If divine action is excluded in advance, some natural combination will always be preferred. Even if miracles are allowed, a unique resurrection is difficult to compare statistically with ordinary events ([[Do miracles violate the laws of nature]]). Historical evidence may shift prior judgment without eliminating it.

- **The Book of Mormon cannot simply be counted as independent:** [[3 Nephi 11]] is a distinct canonical witness tradition, but its historical independence depends on the contested origin of the Book of Mormon. Ancient-history, revealed-text, legendary, redactional, and nineteenth-century-composition models all predict an explicit risen-Christ narrative.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- The believer need not argue that historians must exclude every natural explanation. The narrower claim is comparative: the bodily-resurrection model fits the early proclamation, reported appearances, empty-tomb tradition, and embodied interpretation together. Whether that advantage outweighs the prior improbability of a miracle depends partly on the wider case for [[Does God exist]].

- Paul's lack of an empty-tomb story is an argument from silence, not a denial. His sequence of death, burial, and raising and his extended defense of bodily transformation are compatible with an emptied grave (1 Corinthians 15:3-4, 35-54). They do not, however, tell us whether Paul knew the later discovery narratives.

- Differences among the Gospels do not logically erase their common claims, and ancient authors routinely selected and arranged material. Yet differences are not automatically marks of independent eyewitness testimony either. Responsible defense argues from the stable core while allowing unresolved chronology.

- A visionary explanation is possible and may explain Paul's own experience, but merely applying the word vision does not reconstruct the diverse traditions. A complete natural account still must address why participants interpreted experiences as the resurrection of this particular crucified person, why bodily language became central, and—if the tomb tradition is historical—why the tomb was empty. Those questions create explanatory work, not a disproof of natural models.

- Latter-day Saints may treat [[3 Nephi 11]] and [[Moroni 10]] as cumulative revelatory evidence. Intellectual honesty requires distinguishing that confessional warrant from independent public historical evidence. Spiritual confirmation may rationally matter to the recipient while remaining open to psychological and comparative-religion explanations ([[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]]).

- The strongest defensible believing conclusion is therefore conditional rather than triumphalist: if God exists, acts in history, and vindicated Jesus, the surviving evidence looks much as one might expect. The evidence makes resurrection a responsible faith conclusion but does not force it on every investigator.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established with high confidence:** the surviving texts proclaim that Jesus rose; the proclamation was established before Paul composed 1 Corinthians; Paul sincerely claimed an appearance; resurrection was indispensable to his gospel; and the later canonical narratives present an empty tomb and embodied encounters. Jesus's crucifixion and the early existence of resurrection belief are historically firmer than any reconstruction of exactly what produced that belief.

**Historically plausible but open:** the empty tomb, the independence and age of particular Gospel traditions, the identity of the James in 1 Corinthians 15:7, the phenomenology of the appearances, and the extent to which later narratives preserve, arrange, or develop earlier memories. None can simply be assumed away, but none is established with the same confidence as early resurrection belief.

**A matter of historical-philosophical judgment and faith:** that God bodily raised Jesus. This conclusion has substantial explanatory power if divine action is possible, but it is not a result that the surviving documents compel regardless of worldview. The canonical and official Latter-day Saint answer is unequivocally affirmative; [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]], and [[Firstborn (Bible Dictionary)]] treat Christ as the first to rise in the Resurrection. Their doctrinal authority for believers should not be mislabeled as independent first-century evidence.

The most careful answer is therefore: there is strong evidence for an early, sincere, and embodied resurrection claim; meaningful but disputed evidence for the empty tomb; and no neutral historical procedure that turns those findings into either a demonstration or a refutation of divine resurrection.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read the primary texts before harmonizing them: 1 Corinthians 15:1-58; Galatians 1:11-24; [[Matthew 28]]; [[Mark 16]], noting the textual question surrounding verses 9-20; [[Luke 24]]; [[John 20]]; [[John 21]]; Acts 10:39-43; [[3 Nephi 11]]; 2 Nephi 25:12-13; and D&C 20:23-25. Compare each account for persons, locations, chronology, sensory details, and theological purpose.

For balanced modern scholarship, compare Dale C. Allison Jr., *The Resurrection of Jesus: Apologetics, Polemics, History* (2021); N. T. Wright, *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003); Michael R. Licona, *The Resurrection of Jesus: A New Historiographical Approach* (2010); and Bart D. Ehrman, *How Jesus Became God* (2014). These authors do not share the same conclusions or historiographical assumptions.

For external historical context, consult Tacitus, *Annals* 15.44, which supports Jesus's execution under Pilate and the early movement but does not attest the resurrection, and Josephus, *Antiquities* 18.63-64, whose explicitly Christian wording has a well-known textual authenticity dispute. For Latter-day Saint reception, read [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[The Mediation and Atonement of Our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ]], and [[Conference Report, October 1930 (General Conference (full report), October 1930)]]. [[Extra-Gospel Jesus Tradition in Acts 20]] is useful for studying how Acts may preserve Jesus traditions, though it is not direct resurrection evidence.

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require.** The relevant texts require recognition that their authors and speakers affirm more than Jesus's continuing influence: they proclaim death, burial, divine raising, appearances, and transformed embodiment (1 Corinthians 15:3-8, 35-54; Luke 24:36-43; John 20:24-29). They do not require the reader to assume that every Gospel detail comes from an independent eyewitness, that the five hundred were individually interviewed, or that every appearance had identical phenomenology. Nor does any single text supply a complete post-resurrection itinerary. Both apologetic harmonization and critical reconstruction add inferences to the textual minimum.

- **Divine bodily resurrection — possible; plausible within a theistic framework; not independently demonstrated.** This is the texts' own intended explanation and has the greatest direct textual fit: it explains the proclamation, appearances, transformed-body language, and empty-tomb narratives together. Its historical force depends substantially on whether divine action is antecedently credible. The absence of direct non-Christian observation prevents calling it independently established.

- **Bodily resurrection with selective or compressed narratives — plausible; textually supported; partly ad hoc only when over-harmonized.** Ancient writers could select different women, scenes, and locations without intending exhaustive chronology. This permits a coherent core across [[Matthew 28]], [[Luke 24]], and [[John 20]]. Harmonization becomes ad hoc when it invents repeated trips, duplicated messages, or unmentioned journeys solely to remove every tension.

- **Genuine appearance experiences plus an independently empty tomb, with natural causes — possible; plausible critical model; components independently supported only in general.** Visionary religious experiences and missing or relocated bodies are ordinary possibilities. Their combination can account for sincere belief and an empty tomb without miracle. No surviving source directly identifies a relocation, mistaken tomb, or other natural cause, so the specific conjunction remains reconstructed rather than independently attested.

- **Visionary experiences without a historical empty tomb — possible; plausible in critical scholarship.** Paul's silence about the tomb discovery, the limited phenomenology of 1 Corinthians 15, Gospel literary relationships, and possible narrative development support this model. Against it stand Paul's death-burial-raising sequence and the presence of an empty-tomb tradition in [[Mark 16]], commonly treated as the earliest canonical Gospel. It explains early conviction more readily than it explains why burial and embodied resurrection became integral to the tradition.

- **Legendary and theological development from an early resurrection conviction — plausible for some details; insufficient by itself as a total late-invention model.** Scriptural interpretation, liturgy, preaching, and storytelling could shape individual scenes and physical demonstrations. The received tradition in 1 Corinthians 15:3-7 makes a wholly late origin of resurrection belief difficult: development may explain form and detail but cannot simply erase the early proclamation.

- **Body removal, relocation, or mistaken tomb — possible; weakly supported and usually ad hoc as a complete model.** Such proposals explain an empty tomb but not appearance traditions or Paul's experience without additional hypotheses. No canonical source attests the proposed removal or mistake.

- **Survival or swoon — logically possible; historically weak and ad hoc.** It attempts to explain later encounters without resurrection, but must posit survival of crucifixion and subsequent presentation as victorious life while lacking positive source evidence. The narratives themselves plainly claim death, not near-death.

- **Deliberate conspiracy — possible in the bare logical sense; ad hoc and unsupported.** The sources show proclamation and controversy but furnish no positive evidence that the earliest witnesses knowingly fabricated the claim. Sincerity does not prove resurrection, yet fraud should not be asserted merely because it is naturalistic.

- **Book of Mormon second-witness model — possible within Restoration claims; textually explicit; not independently supported by the appearance narrative alone.** [[3 Nephi 11]] depicts numerous people approaching Jesus individually. If the Book of Mormon is ancient history, this would be major geographically independent testimony. If it is revealed devotional literature, later redaction, or nineteenth-century composition, the same detail is not external corroboration. The narrative itself cannot adjudicate those origin models.

- **Spiritual-confirmation model — possible as personal warrant; not publicly independent.** [[Moroni 10]] offers a revelatory means of knowing. Believers may regard such experience as divine testimony; critics can appeal to expectancy, affect, community formation, and analogous experiences in other religions. Historical and spiritual warrants may be cumulative for an individual without becoming interchangeable.

**Considered and set aside.** The dossier sets aside as overclaims: that 1 Corinthians 15:6 is demonstrably an invitation to investigate; that the four Gospels are four independent eyewitness accounts; that women could not possibly have been invented as witnesses; that all apostles are historically shown to have died specifically rather than deny the resurrection; that Gospel differences prove fabrication; and that calling Paul's experience a vision settles whether it had an external cause. Each assertion outruns the available evidence.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
