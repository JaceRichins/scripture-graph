# Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Joseph Smith taught that plural marriage was commanded by revelation as part
of the "restoration of all things" — the practice of Abraham, Jacob and David
restored under priesthood authority (recorded in [[D&C 132]], dictated 1843
but reflecting teaching from the early 1830s). He entered it reluctantly by
his own and others' accounts, practiced it privately, and it was publicly
announced only in 1852 under [[Brigham Young]]. The historical record shows
it was real, that it caused deep pain (not least to [[Emma Smith]]), that
some marriages were to women already civilly married, and that the Church
ended the practice with the 1890 Manifesto. Why God would command it is the
question the sources cannot settle; what Joseph believed about it, they can.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Joseph consistently framed it as commandment, not preference; several
  accounts (Mary Elizabeth Rollins Lightner, Benjamin Johnson) record him
  saying an angel required it and that he resisted for years.
- The Bible itself records plural marriage among covenant patriarchs
  without condemnation of the institution; [[Jacob 2]] treats it as
  permissible only when God commands it — a framework Joseph could point to.
- Secrecy is consistent with the practice being illegal and dangerous in
  1840s Illinois, not only with concealment for its own sake.
- The Church has published the documentary record (Gospel Topics essays,
  the Joseph Smith Papers), including the hardest parts.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Joseph publicly denied practicing plural marriage while practicing it, and
  married some women without Emma's knowledge or consent, contrary to the
  procedure [[D&C 132]] itself describes.
- Some wives were young (Helen Mar Kimball at 14) and some were already
  married to living husbands; critics see coercion in the promises of
  exaltation attached to proposals.
- The revelation's origin is hard to separate from Joseph's own interests;
  a skeptic reads the pattern as a man rationalizing desire.
- The practice was abandoned under legal pressure, which critics take as
  evidence it was never divine.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Defenders answer that the denials were carefully worded against the
"spiritual wifery" of John C. Bennett rather than plural marriage as such;
that many sealings (especially to already-married women) appear to have been
dynastic or eternity-only with no evidence of cohabitation; that Helen Mar
Kimball's own later writings defend the marriage as a sealing of families;
and that God commanding a hard thing and later withdrawing it has biblical
precedent. None of these responses makes the episode comfortable, and the
better defenders do not pretend it is.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The facts are largely settled and the Church now states them plainly. The
meaning is not: whether one reads Joseph as a prophet carrying a command he
did not want, or as a man who deceived himself, depends on prior judgments
about revelation that the documents cannot decide. A believer can hold that
God commanded it, that Joseph was imperfect in how he carried it out, and
that the pain it caused was real — all at once. What is not honest is to say
the record is simple in either direction.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
