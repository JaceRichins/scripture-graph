# Who were the Danites?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
“Danites” has two principal referents that must not be conflated. In the Bible, Danites are members of the Israelite tribe of Dan, descended from Jacob's son Dan; Judges 18 describes part of the tribe migrating, taking Micah's shrine objects and priest, destroying Laish, and rebuilding it as Dan. In Latter-day Saint history, the Danites were a paramilitary association organized at [[Far West]] during the 1838 Missouri crisis. [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] acknowledges that they intimidated dissenters and other Missourians and offers the qualified judgment that Joseph Smith approved the organization but probably did not know or sanction its full range of activity. Later claims about Utah “Danites” or “destroying angels” require separate evidence and should not be merged with blood-atonement rhetoric, the Utah War, or Mountain Meadows.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The biblical identity is explicit.** Judges 13:2 calls Manoah a member of a family of Danites, while Judges 18:1 calls the migrating group the tribe of the Danites. Genesis 30:5–6 identifies Dan as Jacob's son through Bilhah.

- **The migration narrative is concrete.** [[Judges 18]] reports five scouts, Laish's isolation, the seizure of Micah's cult objects and priest, Micah's retreat before superior force, the destruction of Laish, and its rebuilding as Dan (Judges 18:2–31).

- **Joshua preserves a close parallel.** [[Joshua 19]] reports Danites taking Leshem and renaming it Dan (Joshua 19:47). This strongly connects Leshem with Judges' Laish tradition, though it does not decide whether one account copied the other or whether the names were originally interchangeable.

- **The Missouri organization is acknowledged in a source that does not simply dismiss hostile evidence.** [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] identifies a paramilitary Danite group at Far West, acknowledges intimidation, and attributes qualified approval to Joseph Smith. [[27 We Proclaim Ourselves Free]] and [[28 Tried Long Enough]] direct readers to relevant Joseph Smith Papers material.

- **A contemporary record preserves an allegation of coercion with its attribution intact.** [[‘The Tithing of My People’]] cites Joseph Smith's July 27, 1838 journal for dissenters' claim that Danites used physical pressure where persuasion failed. The allegation is historically significant, although its hostile provenance must remain visible.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The biblical narrative itself is morally severe. The Danites target a settlement described as secure and isolated, kill its inhabitants, take Micah's religious property under armed superiority, and perpetuate an image-based shrine (Judges 18:7, 17–31). The text records no explicit divine command authorizing the massacre of Laish, so a reader should not turn the scouts' priestly oracle into blanket divine approval. Judges frames this entire complex — Micah's shrine and the Danite migration alike — within its recurring editorial refrain that 'there was no king in Israel: every man did that which was right in his own eyes' (Judges 17:6; compare 21:25), a frame that signals disapproval rather than endorsement of what follows, even though it is not attached explicitly to each individual act.

Joshua 19:47 and Judges 18:27–29 differ over Leshem versus Laish and occur within accounts that describe Dan's inheritance differently. A simple harmonization may conceal real literary or historical complexity. The parallel could represent variant tradition, editorial reuse, or dependence rather than two independent confirmations.

For the Missouri group, an apologetic account that treats the Danites as wholly invented by enemies is untenable against [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] and the primary-source pathways in [[27 We Proclaim Ourselves Free]], [[28 Tried Long Enough]], and [[‘The Tithing of My People’]]. Organization, intimidation, and Joseph Smith's approval cannot honestly be omitted.

The opposite maximal claim also exceeds the supplied evidence: neither Joseph Smith's approval nor later use of the name proves that he directed every Danite act or that a centrally controlled Danite murder organization continued into territorial Utah. Later sermons, retrospective histories, accusations, and denials have differing genres and interests. Repetition of the label is not proof of institutional continuity.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
A thoughtful believing reading need not defend every action of biblical Israel. [[Judges 18]] can be read as part of Judges' broader portrayal of religious and social disorder in the era before the monarchy — the book's own refrain, 'there was no king in Israel... every man did that which was right in his own eyes' (Judges 17:6; 21:25), frames the Micah-and-Dan complex as an example of that disorder rather than a model to emulate. The Danites exploit military superiority, appropriate a compromised shrine, and establish worship the narrative associates with an image; that negative reading fits both the episode and the book's own editorial stance, even though no single verse condemns each individual act.

Likewise, historical faith does not require denying the 1838 organization or its coercion. [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] models a more defensible response: acknowledge Joseph Smith's approval and Danite intimidation while distinguishing evidence for general authorization from evidence for knowledge of particular plans. That distinction limits both blanket exoneration and blanket attribution; it should be tested against the documents, not accepted merely because it is institutionally convenient.

The chronological distinctions also matter. [[Did Brigham Young teach blood atonement]], [[What was the Utah War]], and [[What happened at Mountain Meadows]] concern later questions under different circumstances. Connections are possible, but each requires affirmative evidence. [[Discourses of Brigham Young]] and later Journal of Discourses passages show that the Danite label remained rhetorically potent; they do not alone establish a surviving organization.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the sources require.** The biblical text requires recognition of an Israelite tribal group and reports its migration, coercive seizure of a shrine, conquest of Laish, and establishment of Dan and its cult (Judges 18:1–31). It does not require the conclusion that every Danite action had divine approval. The historical corpus requires recognition of a real 1838 Missouri organization, not merely a hostile legend. The supplied Church essay further requires taking Joseph Smith's approval seriously while preserving uncertainty about his knowledge of particular plans. Neither corpus requires institutional continuity between the biblical and Missouri groups or between Missouri and territorial Utah.

- **Independently supported: two-referent model.** Biblical Danites and 1838 Missouri Danites are distinct groups sharing a name. Scripture and dated nineteenth-century records independently establish the different settings. Any symbolic naming relationship does not erase that distinction.

- **Plausible: one conquest preserved in variant biblical accounts.** Joshua's Leshem episode and Judges' Laish episode share the people, action, renaming, and resulting city (Joshua 19:47; Judges 18:27–29). Variant naming, editorial shaping, shared tradition, or literary dependence could explain the differences. The correspondence is strong; independence and priority remain open.

- **Possible but ad hoc: two separate Danite conquests.** Leshem and Laish could theoretically be distinct places taken in closely analogous circumstances. The supplied evidence offers no independent second-city trail, and the shared renaming sequence makes this less economical than a variant-account model.

- **Worth noting, not settled: the shrine priest's genealogy.** Judges 18:30 names Jonathan's father as Gershom, 'the son of Manasseh' — but the word carries a raised letter (a suspended nun) in the Masoretic tradition, long read in Jewish textual tradition as a deliberate softening of an original 'son of Moses,' to avoid tying Moses' name to an idol shrine. If that reading is right, the chapter is doing something more pointed than general disapproval of the period — naming, then obscuring, whose descendant staffed the shrine. This is a text-critical observation, not an established fact, and neither proposal raised it; it belongs in further study rather than among the confirmed claims above.

- **Plausible historical synthesis: authorized defensive organization with partly uncontrolled or undisclosed coercion.** This is the model summarized by [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]]: Joseph Smith approved the group but probably lacked knowledge of all plans and likely did not sanction all activities. It accommodates both leadership involvement and limits in surviving evidence. Its details remain a historical reconstruction rather than direct access to every participant's intent.

- **Possible critical model: leadership knew or authorized substantially more.** Approval of a secretive paramilitary group amid escalating conflict could have created foreseeable coercion even without orders for every act. Witness testimony or additional documents might support greater responsibility. The supplied context, however, does not establish comprehensive operational control.

- **Set aside as contradicted: wholly fabricated Danite organization.** Contemporary and near-contemporary documentary trails, along with the Church essay's admission, make complete invention implausible. Later exaggeration does not erase the group's existence.

- **Possible but presently unsupported: continuing Utah Danite institution.** Later references could preserve memories of informal enforcement or revived usage. Yet the supplied Utah passages largely report accusations, denials, or rhetorical use. Without membership, command, or operational evidence linking them to 1838, institutional continuity is ad hoc.

- **Set aside as conflation: one continuous package comprising Danites, blood atonement, the Utah War, and Mountain Meadows.** These belong to different dates and evidentiary questions. Similar themes of violence or repeated labels cannot substitute for a demonstrated link.

The generic framework excerpts supplied from [[Gospel Doctrine]], [[Conference Report, April 1927 (General Conference (full report), April 1927)]], and [[A Framework for Personal Revelation (Elder Dale G. Renlund, October 2022)]] discuss theories or frameworks in other settings; they provide no Danite-specific historical evidence and therefore should not decide among these models.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** In scripture, Danites are members of Dan's tribe. [[Judges 18]] depicts a Danite migration that includes the armed appropriation of Micah's shrine, the destruction of Laish, its renaming as Dan, and the installation of an image and hereditary priests. [[Joshua 19]] preserves a closely corresponding Leshem account. In 1838 Missouri, a distinct Latter-day Saint paramilitary organization known as the Danites existed at [[Far West]], intimidated opponents, and had Joseph Smith's general approval according to the supplied Church historical synthesis.

**Open:** The literary relationship between Joshua 19 and Judges 18; the original relationship between the place-names Leshem and Laish; the exact Danite chain of command in 1838; what Joseph Smith knew about particular plans; the reliability and scope of individual hostile or defensive testimonies; and whether any later Utah activity had demonstrable organizational continuity with Missouri. Also open: the text-critical question at Judges 18:30, where the Masoretic tradition marks 'the son of Manasseh' with a suspended letter, read in Jewish tradition as an intentional softening of an earlier 'son of Moses' — relevant to how sharply the chapter frames the legitimacy of the Danite priesthood.

**A matter of moral and religious judgment:** Scripture's reporting of Danite conduct does not compel approval of that conduct. Likewise, conclusions about how the 1838 episode bears on Joseph Smith's prophetic calling or the truth claims of the Restoration involve broader theological judgments. Faith can coexist with an admission of institutional wrongdoing, but faith is not evidence that disputed historical acts did or did not occur.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with [[Judges 17]] and [[Judges 18]], then compare the Dan allotment and Leshem notice in [[Joshua 19]]. Judges 13:2 supplies another clear biblical use of 'Danites.' Attend separately to what the narrator reports, what characters claim in oracles, and what the narrative actually endorses. Note as well the textual crux at Judges 18:30, where the Masoretic tradition marks 'the son of Manasseh' with a suspended letter — a detail long discussed in Jewish textual tradition as an intentional softening of an earlier 'son of Moses.' It bears on how pointedly the chapter frames the legitimacy of the Danite priesthood, and rewards a look at a study Bible or other discussion of the suspended nun alongside the primary text.

For 1838, read [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] first as a synthesis, then follow its documentation rather than stopping with the summary. [[27 We Proclaim Ourselves Free]] points to Joseph Smith Papers, Documents, volume 6; [[28 Tried Long Enough]] points to the September 5, 1838 affidavit and Joseph Smith's August journal; [[‘The Tithing of My People’]] points to the July 27 journal entry and its dissenters' allegation.

For the later reception of the label, compare [[Nauvoo Neighbor 1843-07-26 (Vol. 1 No. 13)]] and [[Discourses of Brigham Young]]. Treat the multivolume History of the Church and Journal of Discourses as edited or reported sources whose provenance and date must be evaluated. Then study [[Why were the Saints driven from Missouri]], [[Did Brigham Young teach blood atonement]], [[What was the Utah War]], and [[What happened at Mountain Meadows]] separately before proposing connections.
