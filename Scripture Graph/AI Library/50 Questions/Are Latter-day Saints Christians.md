# Are Latter-day Saints Christians?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
In ordinary descriptive and historical usage, yes: Latter-day Saints are a Christian restorationist community whose canon, institutional name, baptism, sacrament, preaching, and hope of salvation center on Jesus as the divine Son of God, crucified and risen Lord (2 Nephi 25:23–30; 3 Nephi 11:9–40; D&C 20:21–31, 73–79). In a specifically Nicene-confessional use of *Christian*, they are not orthodox Christians: their account of [[The Godhead]], especially the embodied Father and distinct divine personages, rejects classical Trinitarian metaphysics (D&C 130:22). The most accurate short answer is therefore: **Christian in self-identification, devotion, scripture, historical genealogy, and ordinary religious classification; non-Nicene and outside the doctrinal boundaries recognized by many historic communions.** Whether Nicene orthodoxy or a claimed [[Restoration of the Gospel]] is the correct theological boundary is not settled merely by defining the word.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Explicit canonical confession:** 2 Nephi 25:26 says the community talks, rejoices, preaches, prophesies, and writes of Christ. D&C 20:21–31 confesses his divine sonship, crucifixion, resurrection, ascension, grace, justification, and sanctification.

- **The risen Christ is narratively central:** [[3 Nephi 11]] presents Jesus identifying himself, declaring that he bore the world's sins, displaying his wounds, receiving the multitude's worship, and defining his doctrine. His ministry continues through [[3 Nephi 28]].

- **Institution and ordinances:** D&C 115:4 gives the Church a name centered on Jesus Christ. Baptism and sacrament require taking his name, remembering his body and blood, and keeping his commandments (D&C 20:37, 73–79). These are constitutive practices, not occasional references.

- **New Testament continuity:** Latter-day Saints affirm the confession in [[Romans 10]] that Jesus is the risen Lord, the incarnation test in 1 John 4:2–3, Martha's confession in [[John 11]], and the resurrection proclamation of 1 Corinthians 15:3–8. That establishes recognizable continuity with central apostolic claims, though not agreement on every doctrine.

- **Historical genealogy:** The movement emerged from nineteenth-century Christian restorationism, retained the Bible, claimed to restore Christ's church, and continued to define salvation through him. Jan Shipps's *Mormonism: The Story of a New Religious Tradition* usefully stresses both this Christian origin and the movement's development into a distinctive tradition.

- **The contrary evidence is equally checkable:** D&C 130:22 teaches an embodied Father and Son, while D&C 20:27–28 both distinguishes Father, Son, and Holy Ghost and calls them one God. This is not the consubstantial Trinity formulated at [[Nicaea]] and developed in later creedal theology.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **Nicene identity is substantive, not a password.** Catholic, Orthodox, and many Protestant Christians hold that the Trinity identifies the God Christians worship. On this account, rejecting one divine essence eternally shared by Father, Son, and Holy Spirit changes something fundamental even where biblical names remain the same.

- **The objection extends beyond merely being non-Nicene.** Critics point to divine embodiment, premortal existence, exaltation, D&C 132:19–20's description of exalted persons as gods, and Joseph Smith's later plurality-of-gods teaching. They argue that the cumulative system is polytheistic or henotheistic and conflicts with biblical monotheism, not merely with later terminology.

- **Shared confession may be necessary but insufficient.** Christ-centered worship, biblical narratives, and sincere self-identification do not automatically settle religious identity. Critics argue that what matters is the theological identity attributed to Christ and God.

- **The Church claims a rupture in historic authority.** [[Apostasy]] and [[Restoration of the Gospel]] entail that existing churches lacked authority restored through [[Joseph Smith Jr]]. Critics respond that a movement rejecting the continuing authority of historic churches cannot simply assume doctrinal continuity with them.

- **The creeds may be faithful development.** Post-New Testament words such as *homoousios* do not by their date become corruptions. Nicene Christians argue that the councils supplied precise language to hold together scriptural monotheism, Christ's divinity, and the Spirit's divinity. Absence of the later formula from scripture does not prove the formula false.

- **Early diversity does not establish modern LDS theology.** Subordinationist, corporealist, and deification language existed, but isolated parallels may depend on metaphysical assumptions unlike modern LDS teaching.

- **Ecclesial judgments have concrete consequences.** The Roman Catholic Church's 2001 judgment that LDS baptism is invalid rests on a claimed difference in the meaning of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. That ruling does not govern every use of *Christian*, but it shows the disagreement is principled and substantial.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- Latter-day Saints answer with the content of their confession, not the institutional name alone: Jesus is the divine Son, incarnate Savior, atoning Lord, crucified and bodily risen; salvation, baptism, covenant, resurrection, judgment, and discipleship are mediated through him ([[3 Nephi 11]]; [[D&C 20]]).

- Their canon does not describe Father, Son, and Holy Ghost as unrelated gods. It distinguishes them while also calling them one God (2 Nephi 31:18–21; D&C 20:27–28). LDS theology interprets that unity differently from Nicene consubstantiality, but a fair critique must address the actual claim rather than substitute crude tritheism.

- In response to the polytheism charge, LDS interpreters distinguish worship of the one Godhead from the promised exaltation of God's children and deny that exalted beings become independent rivals to God. D&C 132:19–20 nevertheless uses stronger “gods” language than 2 Peter 1:4 alone, so this response does not erase the real metaphysical distance from classical theosis.

- The New Testament presents both distinction and divine unity: Jesus prays to the Father in [[Matthew 26]], is confessed as Lord in [[Romans 10]], and is presented as the divine Word made flesh in [[John 1]]. Nicene and LDS accounts are theological syntheses of these data; that observation does not make the syntheses equally well supported.

- Early Christian diversity and deification language in [[Irenaeus]] and [[Athanasius]] make claims of simple, fully articulated pre-Nicene unanimity difficult. They do not show that either writer taught the full modern LDS doctrine of exaltation.

- The Restoration response is internally coherent: creedal succession is not decisive if authority and teaching were lost and later restored. [[1 Nephi 13]] supplies an LDS canonical account of lost plain and precious teachings and later complementary records. The text establishes that this is the Book of Mormon's claim; it does not independently verify the history or the Restoration.

The most accurate public description is therefore positive and qualified: Latter-day Saints are non-Nicene restorationist Christians under broad descriptive and historical classification, while major communions exclude them from orthodox Christian boundaries.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** Latter-day Saints explicitly confess Jesus as divine Son, Savior, crucified and risen Lord; organize scripture, baptism, sacrament, covenant, and discipleship around him; call themselves Christian; and descend historically from Christian restorationism. It is also established that their teaching about [[The Godhead]] is non-Nicene and that major Christian communions regard important parts of it as incompatible with historic orthodoxy.

**Best descriptive judgment:** In ordinary language and historical religious classification, Latter-day Saints are Christians—more precisely, non-Nicene restorationist Christians. Within a church whose rule makes Nicene confession necessary, they are outside that church's orthodox Christian boundary. Those answers are not contradictory because the governing classifications differ, but the definitions are not interchangeable and should always be stated.

**Open historical and theological questions:** Whether Nicene doctrine is a faithful explication of apostolic scripture or an unwarranted development; how much doctrinal discontinuity changes a tradition's identity; and whether a restoration after apostasy occurred. Scripture relevant to the debate constrains proposed answers but does not mechanically settle the later taxonomy.

**Matter of faith:** That Jesus commissioned the Restoration, restored priesthood authority, and authorized additional scripture. Conversely, treating the ecumenical creeds as divinely protected or normatively final also involves theological commitments beyond the bare historical fact that councils adopted them. Intellectual honesty requires neither minimizing LDS differences nor denying the evident centrality of [[Jesus Christ]] to Latter-day Saint faith.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require.** Acts 11:26 applies *Christians* to disciples but gives no exhaustive later taxonomy. [[Romans 10]] associates salvation with confessing the risen Lord; 1 John 4:2–3 tests confession of Christ's coming in the flesh; [[Matthew 7]] rejects profession without obedience. Latter-day Saint scripture plainly centers teaching and ordinances on Jesus ([[2 Nephi 25]]; [[3 Nephi 11]]; [[D&C 20]]) while also presenting a Godhead ontology that differs from classical creeds. Christ-centered identity and non-Nicene divergence are observations. Deciding which controls the label is an additional judgment.

- **Ordinary discipleship model — independently supported.** A Christian is a person or community whose faith, salvation, worship, and discipleship center on Jesus as divine Savior and risen Lord. LDS canonical confession and sustained practice satisfy this model. Mere admiration of Jesus would be insufficient, but the LDS evidence is substantially stronger than admiration.

- **Apostolic-confession model — plausible and text-supported.** Romans 10:9, 1 John 4:2–3, and 1 Corinthians 15:3–8 supply central Christological affirmations that Latter-day Saints accept. No single passage says it is a complete boundary for every later doctrinal dispute.

- **Genealogical or religious-studies model — independently supported.** Mormonism arose within Christian restorationism, retained Christian scripture and narrative, and claims continuity with Christ's church. This strongly supports placement within the Christian family while permitting description as a distinctive new religious tradition.

- **Family-resemblance model — plausible and independently supported.** Classification weighs Christ-centered worship, scripture, salvation narrative, ritual, historical descent, self-identification, and doctrinal continuity. It yields a graduated finding: strongly Christian in devotion, narrative, and genealogy; markedly heterodox relative to Nicene doctrine.

- **Nicene-confessional model — independently supported as an ecclesial rule.** Christian orthodoxy requires confession of one divine essence shared by Father, Son, and Holy Spirit and the conciliar account of Christ. Latter-day Saints fail that standard. [[Nicaea]], [[Chalcedon]], and major communion judgments establish the rule's historical and institutional reality. Whether it should govern every public or academic use of *Christian* remains disputed.

- **Restoration model — possible, internally coherent, and faith-dependent.** Authentic Christianity is identified by revelation, covenants, ordinances, and authority restored after apostasy rather than by uninterrupted creedal succession. [[1 Nephi 13]] depicts lost teachings and later complementary records, while [[Apostasy]] and [[Restoration of the Gospel]] state the broader LDS model. These sources establish the claim within LDS scripture and belief, not that the claimed history or authority is independently verified.

- **Separate-new-religion model — possible and partly independently supported.** The cumulative doctrinal and institutional discontinuities may justify describing Mormonism as a new tradition descended from Christianity. This becomes overstrong if it treats Christian genealogy, Christ-centered devotion, and shared claims about Jesus of Nazareth as unreal.

- **Different-referent model — possible but philosophically disputed.** A sufficiently different divine ontology may lead theologians to say that shared words such as *God* and *Son* refer to different beings. Calling the traditions wholly unrelated is ad hoc unless the model also accounts for shared biblical narratives, the same claimed historical Jesus, crucifixion, resurrection, and historical descent.

**Considered and set aside.** Self-identification alone was rejected as sufficient because labels can be appropriated. Creedal conformity was rejected as the sole neutral lexical definition because the Christian label predates the councils, though it remains a legitimate ecclesial boundary. Antiquity alone cannot decide truth. Ancient corporealist or theosis language does not establish modern LDS theology, and the post-biblical date of creedal terminology does not by itself disprove the Trinity.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
