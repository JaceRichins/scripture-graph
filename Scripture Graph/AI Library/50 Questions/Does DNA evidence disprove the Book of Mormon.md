# Does DNA evidence disprove the Book of Mormon?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
DNA evidence decisively rejects the once-common model in which Lehi's family supplied most or all Indigenous American ancestry: ancient and modern genomes overwhelmingly trace Indigenous ancestry to populations formed from northeast Asian and Siberian sources long before Book of Mormon times, and no accepted study has detected a Levantine influx around 600 BC. It does not, however, logically exclude every small migration into an already populated region. The Book of Mormon narrates migrations but never explicitly says that its peoples were the continent's only inhabitants, and its labels can be political as well as genealogical (Jacob 1:13-14; Helaman 11:24). Drift, recombination, lineage extinction, and incomplete sampling can make a small contribution undetectable—but that is a model-dependent defense, not evidence that Lehi existed. DNA disproves a major-ancestry reading; by itself it neither proves nor universally disproves Book of Mormon historicity.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The established ancestry pattern:** Large genome-wide and ancient-DNA studies reconstruct Indigenous American ancestry chiefly from founding populations related to northeast Asia and Siberia, with later differentiation and movement within the Americas. They have not established a Book of Mormon-period Levantine migration. Posth et al., “Reconstructing the Deep Population History of Central and South America” (2018), is especially relevant because it directly studies pre-Columbian genomes.

- **The model matters:** If Lehites were the principal ancestors of Indigenous peoples across the hemisphere, a substantial West Asian-associated contribution should be visible. Its absence is powerful contrary evidence. If they were a very small group absorbed into a much larger population, nondetection is possible; its probability cannot be specified without population size, location, marriage patterns, growth, and sampling assumptions.

- **Ancient DNA strengthens the objection:** Post-contact mortality and admixture complicate living populations, but ancient genomes sample people before those disruptions. Coverage is still uneven, so ancient DNA narrows rather than closes every small-migration possibility.

- **The text does not explicitly require exclusivity:** The arrival narrative in [[1 Nephi 18]] does not call the land uninhabited. The internal map in [[Alma 22]], [[Alma 50]], [[Alma 52]], [[Alma 63]], [[Helaman 3]], and [[Ether 10]] is a regional network without recoverable modern coordinates; see [[Book of Mormon Geography]]. This permits but does not prove coexistence with other populations.

- **Group names are not genetic samples:** Jacob deliberately collapses several peoples into the political names Nephite and Lamanite (Jacob 1:13-14), while Helaman 11:24 distinguishes dissenters who adopted the Lamanite name from actual descendants. Consequently, a later person called a Lamanite need not represent an unmixed paternal line from Laman.

- **The institutional position changed:** The 1981 noncanonical introduction called Lamanites the “principal ancestors” of American Indians; the 2006 wording says “among the ancestors.” [[Book of Mormon and DNA Studies (Essay)]] acknowledges largely Asian ancestry and calls both affirmative and negative DNA arguments inconclusive when directed at the book's entire authenticity.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The limited model can look retrospective.** Early Saints broadly identified Native Americans as Lamanites, later leaders sometimes made expansive claims, and the 1981 introduction called Lamanites the “principal ancestors” of American Indians. Limited-geography proposals predate the modern DNA controversy, but that does not make them the original or dominant reading; see [[Why did the Lamanite introduction change in 2006]].

- **The narrative can sound demographically expansive.** It depicts multiplying peoples, persistent kingdoms, large wars, migrations, and lands between multiple seas. A lineage portrayed as socially central yet assumed to remain genetically negligible may be less natural than a nonhistorical explanation. Proposed incorporation of established populations is possible but must be demonstrated rather than assumed; see [[How large were Book of Mormon populations]].

- **The text retains strong lineage language.** Fluid political labels in [[Jacob 1]] and [[Helaman 11]] do not erase references to descendants, seed, and a latter-day “remnant of Jacob” in [[3 Nephi 21]]. Lamanite identity can be political, religious, and genealogical; emphasizing only the first two understates the genetic question.

- **Protected-land language resists an easy coexistence model.** In [[2 Nephi 1]], Lehi describes a choice land kept from the knowledge of other nations. Limiting “this land” to a region or allowing inhabitants already present is possible, but interpretive rather than explicit.

- **Ancient DNA has strengthened the test.** Appeals centered on post-contact mortality are incomplete now that pre-Columbian genomes can be studied directly. Existing samples support deep Indigenous continuity and provide no accepted evidence of a 600 BC Levantine influx. Coverage remains uneven, but it is not negligible.

- **An unspecified nondetectable migration risks becoming unfalsifiable.** If every null result is answered by changing the group’s size, location, ancestry profile, or survival, the model makes no risky genetic prediction. It may remain logically possible while explaining no positive observation.

- **There is no authenticated comparison population.** No remains, site, or genetic reference sample has independently been identified as Nephite, Lamanite, Mulekite, or Jaredite. This prevents a direct test and prevents defenders from identifying an otherwise unusual lineage as Lehite.

- **A nonhistorical model incurs no genetic surprise.** If the Book of Mormon is a nineteenth-century composition, the absence of a Book of Mormon-period Levantine signal is expected. DNA does not prove that production model, but it favors it over ancient models predicting substantial Lehite ancestry.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believing responses begin by conceding what genetics strongly establishes: a hemispheric principal-ancestor model is not viable. They then distinguish that model from the narrative’s minimum claims. [[1 Nephi 18]] describes an arriving party without saying it found an empty hemisphere; [[Book of Mormon Geography]] permits a regional setting; and [[Jacob 1]] and [[Helaman 11]] show that Nephite and Lamanite can function as political names as well as descent terms. A small immigrant group could therefore become culturally or politically important without supplying most ancestry. This is possible, not explicit.

Population genetics provides ordinary mechanisms by which a small contribution can disappear from sampled genomes: maternal or paternal lines can end, autosomal segments recombine and may be lost, and admixture can dilute ancestry. Perego, Whiting, and [[Book of Mormon and DNA Studies (Essay)]] develop this defense. The warranted conclusion is that nondetection may occur under some demographic models—not that science shows Lehite DNA once existed and disappeared.

Genealogical, cultural, covenantal, and detectable genetic descent are also different categories. A remote genealogical ancestor may contribute no identifiable DNA to a particular descendant. Yet this distinction must not erase the text’s real seed and remnant language, including [[3 Nephi 21]].

These responses preserve a narrower historical possibility; they do not confirm it. To become scientifically discriminating, the model needs an independently specified location, founder population, demographic history, and sampling expectation. Divine concealment or alteration of DNA is evidentially empty because no text here predicts it and it can accommodate any outcome.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** Indigenous American population history is overwhelmingly rooted in migrations and diversification beginning thousands of years before the Book of Mormon’s Lehite chronology. No accepted genetic evidence identifies a Levantine migration around 600 BC. This strongly disconfirms models in which Lehites supplied most or all Indigenous American ancestry. The text does not explicitly require such a model, although it does make historical migration and lineage claims.

**Open:** Present evidence does not logically exclude every small, localized migration. Ancient DNA makes the absence more informative than older discussions sometimes allowed, but coverage is incomplete and no Book of Mormon location or comparison population has been authenticated. The ancestry contribution reasonably predicted by the narrative remains disputed because its geographic scope, population figures, group incorporation, and descent terminology are disputed.

**Not established:** Drift does not confirm that Lehite ancestry once existed; limited geography does not identify a Book of Mormon site; political flexibility in the term Lamanite does not erase genealogical seed language; and compatibility with current DNA is not evidence of historicity. Conversely, failure of a major-ancestry model does not logically refute every smaller migration model.

**Larger assessment:** DNA should enter historical model comparison as an independent population line while leaving the corpus’s literary observations unchanged. [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] currently finds no overall verdict on historicity and identifies populations, archaeology, witnesses, anachronisms, and translation history as underdeveloped. DNA therefore decisively closes a major traditional demographic reading, leaves a narrower and increasingly parameter-dependent historical possibility open, and supplies no positive evidence for that possibility. Inspiration and spiritual authority remain distinct questions: an ancient migration would not prove revelation, and a nineteenth-century origin would not by itself adjudicate spiritual value.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** The narrative presents a Jerusalem-origin party reaching a promised land in [[1 Nephi 18]], another Jerusalem-associated group at Zarahemla, and an earlier Jaredite migration in [[Ether 6]]. It portrays descendants, seed, peoples, and enduring covenant promises; [[3 Nephi 21]] therefore prevents reducing all Lamanite identity to a purely political or spiritual label. The text does not state that Lehi’s party were the first, only, or principal inhabitants of the entire Western Hemisphere, provide modern coordinates, quantify admixture, or define every later Lamanite solely by descent. [[2 Nephi 1]] supplies genuine isolationist language, while [[Jacob 1]] and [[Helaman 11]] show that group names can also change through political alignment.

- **Hemispheric exclusive or principal-ancestor model — historically influential but scientifically strongly disfavored.** Expansive land and seed language and historical Church identifications explain why readers adopted it. Ancient and modern genomics contradict the major West Asian contribution it would ordinarily predict. Erasure becomes increasingly ad hoc as the proposed ancestral contribution grows.

- **Regional migration into an established Indigenous population — plausible and text-permitted, not independently supported by DNA.** The text does not declare the hemisphere empty, and its relative geography permits a regional reconstruction. Genetics independently establishes large pre-Lehite Indigenous populations, but not a Lehite component. The model needs a proposed location, founder size, admixture history, and testable expectations.

- **Small migration followed by extensive cultural influence and little surviving ancestry — possible.** Political adoption, unequal reproductive success, drift, recombination, and lineage extinction make this genetically coherent under some parameters. Without parameters or positive archaeological evidence, it is a compatibility model rather than an independently supported reconstruction.

- **Small migration with genealogical descendants but no identifiable genetic segments — possible.** Genealogical descent can outlast detectable DNA. This answers a narrow objection but becomes ad hoc if loss is asserted solely after every null result.

- **Large, comparatively endogamous Lehite expansion — scientifically disfavored.** The narrative’s large societies and persistent descent language can motivate this model, but the greater and broader the predicted ancestry, the more serious the conflict with ancient and modern genomes.

- **Ancient historical record rendered through loose or KJV-shaped revelation — possible and weakly supported by separate lines.** [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] identifies Nahom, conditional syntax, and Alma 36 chiasmus as independent but weak ancient-direction evidence while strongly establishing KJV-shaped mediation of the English text. A demographically small migration can remain compatible with DNA, but DNA supplies no positive support for this framework.

- **Ancient core with modern expansion — possible.** Modern revelatory or editorial expansion could explain some English and theological features, but it does not remove genetic expectations attached to whatever migrations remain historical. Without prior criteria identifying layers, invoking expansion only at points of difficulty is ad hoc.

- **Nineteenth-century KJV-informed composition — plausible.** It predicts no Lehite genetic signal and fits the strong English-biblical mediation and final-form control identified in [[Book of Mormon Assessment]]. DNA raises it relative to ancient models predicting substantial Lehite ancestry, but does not identify an author, source, motive, or complete production process.

- **Inspired but nonhistorical scripture — theologically possible.** This framework can affirm spiritual or revelatory value without treating the migrations as ordinary history. It fits the genetic absence but requires a nonliteral account of the narrative’s surface historical claims; DNA alone cannot determine inspiration.

- **Divine removal, alteration, or concealment of DNA — theologically possible but ad hoc and evidentially unusable.** No cited scripture predicts it, and an unrestricted miracle accommodates every possible observation.

**Considered and set aside as positive Lehite evidence.** Indigenous mitochondrial haplogroup X fits ancient northern Eurasian and Beringian history rather than establishing a 600 BC Levantine arrival. Modern West Eurasian ancestry is irrelevant unless securely dated before European contact. Possible Norse, Polynesian, Australasian-related, or other contacts would not identify Lehi’s party. Metaphorical references to spiritual DNA make no population-genetic claim.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
