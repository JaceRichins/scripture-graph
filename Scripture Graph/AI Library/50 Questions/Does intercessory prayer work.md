# Does intercessory prayer work?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The canon's answer is a qualified yes: it commands prayer for others, reports particular petitions as answered, and makes Jesus the central model of intercession; but it also records earnest requests that do not receive the requested outcome and places answers under God's will, moral judgment, and human agency. Thus scripture supports intercession as meaningful participation in God's work, not as a technique that guarantees control of events. Outside the narratives, randomized clinical research has not shown a clear average health benefit from assigned distant prayer. Whether God answered any particular prayer therefore remains a providential interpretation requiring more than temporal sequence or a favorable result.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Explicit narrated attribution:** In [[Mosiah 27]], the angel says that the prayers of the people and [[Alma the Elder]] are the reason for his intervention with [[Alma the Younger]] (Mosiah 27:14-16). The answer supplies confrontation and knowledge; Alma's repentance still unfolds personally (Mosiah 27:18-25).

- **A granted request with retained consequences:** In [[Numbers 14]], God answers Moses' plea for pardon but does not admit the rebellious generation into the promised land (Numbers 14:19-23). This is unusually useful evidence because the text defines both the answer and its limits.

- **Healing and reconciliation reports:** [[Genesis 20]] attributes the healing of Abimelech's household to Abraham's prayer (Genesis 20:17-18). [[Job 42]] says God accepts Job's prayer for his friends and associates it with Job's restoration (Job 42:8-10).

- **A community praying during deliverance:** [[Acts 12]] places the church's sustained prayer for Peter beside his angelic release (Acts 12:5-17). This is strong evidence for Luke's providential interpretation, although the shared narrator prevents independent causal verification.

- **Jesus' example:** [[John 17]] records [[Jesus Christ]] praying for the preservation, sanctification, unity, and love of present and future disciples (John 17:9-23). [[3 Nephi 19]] presents a related intercession at Bountiful (3 Nephi 19:20-29). The registry assigns their literary relationship moderate support, 0.60; that supports intertextuality, not independent proof of efficacy. [[Pray Always (Elder David A. Bednar, October 2008)]] and [[Lessons from the Lord’s Prayers (Elder Russell M. Nelson, April 2009)]] appropriately use these prayers as devotional models.

- **Explicit apostolic instruction:** [[James 5]] directs elders to pray over the sick and all believers to pray for one another (James 5:14-18). This establishes Christian practice and expectation, though not a universal recovery rate.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest textual objection is explicit non-granting, not merely perceived silence. In [[2 Samuel 12]], David fasts and seeks God for his child, yet the child dies. Jeremiah is forbidden to intercede for an unrepentant people (Jeremiah 7; 11; 14), and Ezekiel says that even Noah, Daniel, or Job could not deliver other condemned members of the community (Ezekiel 14). Intercession is therefore neither invariably permitted nor guaranteed to obtain its requested outcome.

Human agency and unequal intervention create another difficulty. If prayer could reliably cause another person’s conversion or conduct, it could appear to compromise responsibility and privilege people with stronger praying communities. [[Mosiah 27]] depicts prayer occasioning an extraordinary confrontation while leaving Alma to respond, but it does not explain why many similarly loved people receive no comparable intervention.

Narrative evidence is also vulnerable to attribution and selection effects. The same narrator commonly reports the prayer, outcome, and divine explanation. That strongly establishes the narrator’s theology but weakly distinguishes supernatural causation from coincidence, ordinary recovery, human help, or didactic shaping. Multiple internally narrated examples are not automatically independent corroboration.

Empirically, the 2006 STEP trial found no benefit of assigned intercessory prayer for complication-free coronary-bypass recovery. A 2009 Cochrane review of ten randomized trials involving 7,646 participants found no clear effect on death, general clinical state, coronary-care readmission, or rehospitalization. These findings weigh against a reliable average health benefit from tested distant-prayer protocols, although they do not adjudicate every theological or nonclinical claim.

Finally, selective-answer claims can become unfalsifiable. If every favorable event is counted as an answer and every unfavorable event is redescribed after the fact as a wiser answer, no conceivable result counts against the claim. Such trust may be religiously meaningful, but it is not publicly discriminating evidence.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
A thoughtful believing response begins by narrowing the claim. Scripture does not promise that every intercessor receives the requested event. It depicts God as an agent rather than a force, qualifies asking by divine will (1 John 5:14-15; Doctrine and Covenants 46:30), and openly preserves counterexamples such as David's child. Belief in selective answers is therefore closer to the text than belief in a guaranteed prayer mechanism.

Second, some answers operate through rather than around agency. In [[Mosiah 27]], the prayers bring Alma warning and an opportunity for repentance, not repentance without his participation. In [[Luke 22]], Jesus' prayer for Peter does not prevent denial; it can plausibly be read as seeking preservation and eventual service through the crisis (Luke 22:31-34, 54-62). This model reduces, but does not solve, the unequal-distribution problem.

Third, the canon joins prayer to responsible action. Nehemiah prays and then petitions the king ([[Nehemiah 1]]; [[Nehemiah 2]]); James summons elders to attend the sick ([[James 5]]); Amulek declares devotional practice spiritually empty when severed from [[Charity]] toward the needy (Alma 34:19-28). Intercession cannot justify withholding medicine, safety measures, repentance, advocacy, or material aid.

Fourth, clinical null findings constrain a believer's claims but do not test every scriptural model. Randomized trials address average measurable outcomes of assigned prayer under specified conditions. They do not directly measure forgiveness, unity, courage, an opportunity to repent, or the moral formation of a praying community. Contamination by outside prayer and the difficulty of standardizing religious intention also limit inference. These limits do not convert null results into positive evidence; they only define what the trials can and cannot establish.

Finally, believers may regard prayer as covenantal participation: asking, listening, acting, and entrusting the outcome to God. [[Pray Always (Elder David A. Bednar, October 2008)]] emphasizes patterned, intentional prayer, while [[Be One with Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2024)]] and [[Hearts Knit in Righteousness and Unity (Elder Quentin L. Cook, October 2020)]] draw from Christ's intercession for unity. Those are theological and pastoral readings, not controlled demonstrations of external causation.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** The canon treats prayer for others as legitimate and often commanded; some narrators explicitly attribute outcomes to it; some earnest requests are not granted as requested; and intercession remains subordinate to divine will, judgment, and human response. The text does not require the assumptions that every sincere petition changes events, that nonresponse proves deficient faith, that temporal sequence proves supernatural causation, or that one clinical null result settles every function of prayer.

- **Selective divine response — plausible and textually explicit, not independently established.** Genesis 20, Numbers 14, and Mosiah 27 say God acted in relation to intercession. This strongly establishes the canonical claim but does not independently distinguish divine action from literary interpretation, coincidence, or ordinary causation.

- **Genuine divine responsiveness — possible.** Some narratives naturally read as God responding to a petition rather than merely revealing a fixed outcome. Exodus 32 and Numbers 14 permit this reading, but their anthropomorphic and conditional language does not settle the metaphysics of whether God changed an otherwise fixed intention.

- **Prayer as an ordained means within providence — possible and historically serious.** God may will an outcome together with the prayer through which it is given, so prayer participates causally without informing or changing an unknowing divine mind. The narratives are compatible with this model but do not formulate it explicitly.

- **Conditional, non-coercive assistance — plausible.** Intercession may provide warning, strength, opportunity, or favorable circumstances without determining another person’s choice. [[Mosiah 27]] and [[Luke 22]] support this model, though they do not define the boundary between influence and coercion.

- **Petitioner-mediated action — plausible.** Prayer may change what petitioners notice and do, leading them to provide care, advocacy, reconciliation, or practical help. [[Nehemiah 1]], [[Nehemiah 2]], [[James 5]], and [[Alma 34]] fit this model. Such effects can benefit recipients without demonstrating an otherwise supernatural change in their circumstances.

- **Recipient expectancy and social support — possible and independently testable.** Knowing that others are praying may comfort some recipients, increase anxiety in others, or mobilize community support. These pathways differ from an effect on an unaware recipient and must not be treated as evidence for distant supernatural causation.

- **Relational and formative efficacy — plausible.** Prayer can express love, sustain attention, prompt service, and align a community with professed commitments even when the requested event does not occur. John 17, Alma 34, and 3 Nephi 18 support such functions, but benefits to petitioners cannot establish that an external event was supernaturally altered.

- **Ordinary-means providence — possible and text-compatible.** Answers may come through physicians, rulers, family, or changed decisions. Nehemiah’s prayer followed by his approach to the king fits this framework. Because ordinary causation predicts the same observable result, theological attribution usually remains independently underdetermined.

- **Naturalistic attribution — plausible and independently supported for the narrow clinical claim.** On this model, prayer has no supernatural effect on recipients; favorable cases reflect ordinary causes, coincidence, regression to the mean, selective memory, or narrative construction. STEP and the Cochrane review support this model against reliable average clinical benefit from assigned distant prayer, but do not settle every nonclinical claim or the existence of God.

- **God will not be experimentally tested — possible, but potentially ad hoc.** Deuteronomy 6 and Matthew 4 prohibit tempting God, and some believers infer that randomized assignment cannot compel divine action. The cited passages do not directly discuss clinical trials. Invoking this principle only after every null result would insulate the claim from evidence and supply no positive support.

- **Universal guaranteed outcomes — set aside as textually contradicted.** David’s child dies despite earnest petition, while Jeremiah and Ezekiel restrict intercession. This model cannot represent the canon without suppressing counterevidence.

- **Every outcome counts as success — possible as an expression of trust, ad hoc as evidence.** Submission to unknown purposes may be faithful, but declaring success regardless of outcome makes the efficacy claim non-discriminating and can trivialize loss.

- **Clinical experimentation settles the whole theological question — set aside as overbroad.** Trials can test specified average health outcomes and constrain therapeutic claims. They do not directly measure forgiveness, sanctification, unity, courage, divine-human relationship, or a selective agent not assumed to respond uniformly.

The moderate John 17–3 Nephi 19 literary parallel must not be multiplied into independent empirical corroboration. Shared or mediated language reinforces theology, not the historical verification of two separate answered prayers.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** Intercessory prayer is widespread, commanded, and theologically important within the canon. Scripture portrays [[Jesus Christ]] as an intercessor, reports particular answers, and also preserves clear non-granting, restrictions, and conditions. The strongest defensible canonical conclusion is therefore selective rather than automatic efficacy. Modern randomized clinical evidence reviewed here has not demonstrated a clear average health benefit from assigned distant prayer.

**Open:** Historical verification of individual scriptural answers; whether a favorable event was caused by prayer, ordinary means, or coincidence; how divine action relates to natural regularities and [[Agency]]; why interventions appear radically unequal; and whether better-designed studies could detect any narrowly defined effect. The clinical literature cited is relevant but does not exhaust every meaning of 'work.'

**A matter of faith:** That God hears intercession, sometimes acts because of it, and makes even ungranted petitions meaningful within providence. That belief is compatible with the canon and with the limits of current evidence, but it is not established by narrative repetition, personal testimony alone, or clinical trials. A responsible believer may pray with hope while refusing guarantees, refusing to blame sufferers for insufficient faith, and continuing every available form of practical care.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with [[John 17]] and [[3 Nephi 19]] for Christ's intercession; then compare the explicit answer reports in [[Mosiah 27]], [[Numbers 14]], [[Genesis 20]], [[Job 42]], and [[Acts 12]]. Read the counterevidence without skipping [[2 Samuel 12]], [[Jeremiah 7]], [[Jeremiah 14]], and [[Ezekiel 14]]. [[Luke 22]] is especially useful for distinguishing preservation through failure from prevention of failure.

For practice and ethics, study [[James 5]], [[Alma 34]], [[Mosiah 4]], [[3 Nephi 18]], [[2 Nephi 32]], and [[Mosiah 24]], alongside [[Prayer]], [[Fasting]], [[Charity]], and [[Agency]]. For the relation between petition and extraordinary causation, continue to [[Miracles]], [[Do miracles violate the laws of nature]], [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]], and [[Why does God allow suffering]].

Among the library sources, compare [[Lessons from the Lord’s Prayers (Elder Russell M. Nelson, April 2009)]], [[Pray Always (Elder David A. Bednar, October 2008)]], [[The Father and the Son (Elder Christoffel Golden Jr., April 2013)]], and [[Be One with Christ (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2024)]]. Treat these as official devotional interpretation, not independent empirical evidence.

For empirical study, read Benson et al., “Study of the Therapeutic Effects of Intercessory Prayer (STEP) in cardiac bypass patients,” American Heart Journal 151 (2006): 934-942, and Roberts, Ahmed, and Davison, “Intercessory prayer for the alleviation of ill health,” Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews (2009), CD000368. Attend closely to outcome definitions, contamination, heterogeneity, and the difference between absence of detected benefit and proof of universal ineffectiveness.
