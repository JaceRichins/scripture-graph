# Why has Church teaching changed over time?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Church teaching has changed for more than one reason: the Church claims continuing revelation; scriptures sometimes depict God changing covenantal practice or time-bounded assignments; teaching can be adapted to capacity and circumstance; councils interpret new experience; and fallible leaders and institutions can learn, correct errors, or respond to social and legal pressure. [[Acts 10]] and [[Acts 15]] establish that authorized change is possible within scripture, but they do not prove the divine origin of any modern change. The historical changes themselves—including plural marriage, racial restrictions, the enforcement of the [[Word of Wisdom]], temple procedures, and women's responsibilities—are well established; whether a particular change was new revelation, inspired adaptation, delayed correction, institutional survival, or some mixture remains a case-by-case judgment. The distinction among doctrine, policy, practice, and personal opinion is useful, but critics are right that it can become circular when categories are assigned only after a reversal.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The changes themselves are documented.** Official Declaration 1, Official Declaration 2, the Church's disavowal of earlier racial theories, the changing enforcement of the [[Word of Wisdom]], and acknowledged temple-procedure adjustments establish substantial historical change. They do not, by themselves, identify its divine or human causes.

- **Continuing revelation is a canonical premise.** Articles of Faith 1:9 expressly anticipates future revelation. This makes later disclosure coherent within Latter-day Saint theology, but it does not authenticate every later teaching or reversal.

- **Scripture depicts authorized change.** In [[Acts 10]], Peter changes his treatment of Gentiles after claimed revelation and witnessed spiritual manifestations. In [[Acts 15]], leaders resolve a dispute about Gentile obligations through testimony, scripture, debate, council judgment, and claimed guidance from the Holy Ghost. These are strong canonical examples, but neither proves that a particular modern change followed the same pattern.

- **Some canonical directions are explicitly contextual.** D&C 124:49–51 excuses a commanded work when faithful effort is forcibly prevented, and D&C 126:1–3 changes an authorized assignment. These passages establish conditional commands and assignments more clearly than they explain reversed truth claims.

- **The canon rejects automatic infallibility.** D&C 28 regulates who may direct the Church, D&C 107:27–32 places moral conditions on council decisions, and [[Jeremiah 23]], [[Jeremiah 28]], and [[Ezekiel 13]] warn against falsely attributing human words to God. Authorized channels matter, but claimed authority is not self-validating.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **Retrospective classification can be circular.** If a teaching is called doctrine while current and relabeled policy, speculation, or error after abandonment, “doctrine” risks meaning only “what the Church presently retains.” [[What was the Adam-God teaching]] and past racial explanations make this objection concrete.

- **Several changes coincide with external pressure.** The 1890 Manifesto came amid severe federal action against plural marriage. The 1978 change followed decades of criticism, civil-rights transformation, and increasing international growth. Organizational adaptation predicts such timing without invoking revelation. A believer may see providential preparation in the same circumstances, but chronology alone cannot choose between those explanations.

- **The scriptural analogies may be too generous.** [[Acts 10]] narrates visions, independently reported divine direction, and an observable outpouring of the Holy Ghost. Many later changes were announced with less public revelatory detail. Showing that Peter changed does not establish that every institutional reversal resembles Peter's.

- **Fallibility creates a present-tense problem.** A later correction may vindicate a self-correcting process, but it also proves that members can be asked to sustain harmful or mistaken teaching before correction arrives. [[Why were Black members restricted from priesthood and temple until 1978]] raises this problem with unusual moral force.

- **Some changes appear contradictory, not merely additive.** A practice cannot simultaneously be required and forbidden for the same people under the same conditions. Appeals to “line upon line” explain incomplete disclosure more readily than they explain reversed truth claims.

- **Institutional continuity can be explained naturalistically.** A critical model can treat revelation language as the community's sacred interpretation of negotiation, leadership conflict, moral learning, and survival. The existence of change does not discriminate strongly between divine guidance and ordinary institutional development.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- Continuing revelation makes correction and adaptation theologically possible, but a careful believer need not call every change revelatory. [[Acts 15]] suggests a more demanding pattern: experience, spiritual gifts, scripture, sustained deliberation, and communal judgment converge.

- Latter-day Saint scripture does not require prophetic impeccability. The warnings in [[Jeremiah 23]], [[Jeremiah 28]], and [[Ezekiel 13]], along with the stewardship dispute in D&C 28, make discernment part of religious life. Members may sustain [[Prophets]] as authorized stewards without treating every sermon, explanation, or prediction as canon.

- D. Todd Christofferson's “The Doctrine of Christ” offers a practical institutional rule: enduring doctrine is not established by one isolated statement but by the united First Presidency and Twelve. D&C 107:27–32 similarly describes council unity under demanding moral conditions. This reduces the weight placed on idiosyncratic remarks, although it does not eliminate ambiguity when united teaching itself later changes.

- Circumstances and revelation are not mutually exclusive explanations. In [[Acts 10]], Cornelius's request creates the immediate crisis in which Peter receives direction. Believers can therefore understand legal, global, or pastoral pressure as the occasion for revelation. That remains a faith interpretation unless independent evidence identifies divine causation.

- Correction can be morally meaningful even when late. The official disavowal of earlier racial theories, adjustments in women's responsibilities described in [[Women’s Service and Leadership in the Church (Gospel Topics)]], and the history behind [[How did the Word of Wisdom become a commandment]] show an institution capable of revising inherited positions. Honesty also requires conceding that correction does not erase harms suffered before it.

- The best believing defense is cumulative rather than automatic: test a teaching against the canon, the [[Gospel of Jesus Christ]], authorized united councils, its fruits, and later confirmation. This is a workable discipline of trust and discernment, not a mechanical guarantee.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
Established: Church teaching and practice have changed; continuing revelation is canonical; scripture permits changes in covenantal application, teaching depth, and time-bounded assignments; and the canon does not teach simple leader infallibility. Also established is that the modern doctrine-policy distinction does not classify every historical case without dispute. Open: which changes were direct revelation, which were inspired implementation, which corrected human error, and how much external pressure explains their timing. Those questions require case-specific primary sources, not a single slogan. A matter of faith: that God guides the Church through this visibly human process and that present united teaching merits religious trust. That faith can be intellectually responsible if it does not convert authorization into inerrancy, erase institutional harm, or treat later correction as proof that the earlier position was secretly correct. The strongest conclusion is therefore mixed: the Restoration's theology makes change coherent, the historical record makes fallibility undeniable, and no general theory removes the need for discernment in real time.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** The canon requires recognition that divine instruction can be continuing, contextual, and mediated through deliberation: Articles of Faith 1:9, [[Acts 10]], [[Acts 15]], D&C 124:49–51, and D&C 126:1–3 establish that much. It also requires caution about false claims to divine speech ([[Jeremiah 23]]; [[Jeremiah 28]]; [[Ezekiel 13]]) and places conditions on Church-wide stewardship and councils (D&C 28:1–13; D&C 107:27–32). It does **not** provide a comprehensive doctrine-policy taxonomy, promise that every authorized statement is correct, or tell readers which causal explanation fits every modern reversal. The assertion that change necessarily proves revelation, and the assertion that change necessarily disproves revelation, both add premises not supplied by the text.

- **Continuing or progressive revelation — plausible and text-supported.** God discloses or authorizes something new when the community is prepared or a new situation arises. [[Acts 10]], [[Acts 15]], and Articles of Faith 1:9 support this directly. It is independently supported as a genuine Latter-day Saint self-understanding, but a modern claimant must still show why a particular change belongs in this category.

- **Contextual command or changed assignment — plausible and independently supported.** The underlying purpose remains while its application changes with population, law, danger, or mission. D&C 124:49–51 and D&C 126:1–3 expressly permit this model; [[Diversity and Unity in The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Gospel Topics)]] supplies modern global context. This model fits programs and assignments better than contradictory historical propositions.

- **Pedagogical accommodation — possible, with limited textual support.** Different audiences receive different depth or emphasis according to capacity. [[1 Corinthians 3]] supports staged instruction. Using this model to explain every reversal is ad hoc because milk and meat can be complementary without being contradictory.

- **Revelation through council and interpretation — plausible and text-supported.** Divine guidance is mediated through reports, debate, scripture, and collective judgment rather than replacing those activities. [[Acts 15]] is the strongest model; D&C 107:27–32 provides a Restoration analogue. It permits genuine revelation and genuine human contribution, but therefore does not guarantee perfect reception or implementation.

- **Correction of prophetic or institutional error — plausible and independently supported.** Leaders can inherit assumptions, overstate opinions, or maintain harmful practices until correction occurs. Canonical false-prophecy warnings make error conceptually possible; official disavowal of past racial theories and the history connected with [[What was the Adam-God teaching]] give the model historical traction. Whether a correction itself is inspired remains a further claim.

- **Ordinary institutional adaptation interpreted religiously — plausible and independently supported.** Legal pressure, moral criticism, globalization, leadership succession, and organizational survival can generate change; revelation language can then provide sacred legitimation. The timing surrounding the Manifesto and the broader transitions surveyed by Thomas G. Alexander support this critical model. It explains timing well in some cases but cannot independently determine leaders' experiences or exclude providential influence.

- **Doctrinal development — possible to plausible.** A stable center—Jesus Christ, repentance, covenant, ordinances, and restoration—is articulated through expanding or revised conceptual frameworks. This can explain clarification without claiming that every earlier formulation was complete. It becomes ad hoc when “development” is used to conceal a direct contradiction or moral failure.

- **Retrospective doctrine-policy relabeling — possible, sometimes plausible, but ad hoc when criterionless.** Some changes genuinely concern administration; others concern propositions once presented with doctrinal force. Classification is evidentially useful when contemporaneous sources and stable criteria support it. Classification performed only after repudiation has weak explanatory force.

- **Comprehensive prophetic infallibility — set aside as textually unsupported.** The canon regulates competing revelation, condemns false prophetic claims, and depicts leaders learning through dispute. Sustaining [[Prophets]] does not textually require treating every utterance as irreformable.

- **“Any change proves the Church false” — set aside as overbroad.** Scripture itself depicts authorized change, so change alone cannot carry that conclusion. A narrower critical argument—specific contradictions, avoidable harms, or implausible revelatory claims undermine confidence—remains serious and must be evaluated case by case.

- **“Any change proves a living church” — set aside as equally overbroad.** Every enduring institution changes. The relevant question is not whether change occurred but whether the evidence distinguishes revelation, prudent administration, correction, and accommodation.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
