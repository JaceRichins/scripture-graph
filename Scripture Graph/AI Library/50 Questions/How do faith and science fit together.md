# How do faith and science fit together?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Faith and science fit together better than the popular "warfare" story suggests, and Latter-day Saint theology carries fewer built-in commitments than some other Christian traditions do. The Church has no official position on the age of the earth, the mechanism of creation, or evolution ([[Religion and Science (Gospel Topics)]]); it teaches that God created and organized the earth and that Adam and Eve were the first parents of the human family, and it leaves the "how" open. Its own scripture already resists a single procedural reading of origins: [[Genesis 1]] and [[Genesis 2]] differ in the order and vocabulary of creation, and God's creative work is repeatedly described as organizing existing materials ([[Abraham 3]]-[[Abraham 4]]; D&C 93:33) rather than calling matter from nothing. Independent lines of evidence converge on an earth about 4.5 billion years old and on common biological ancestry; neither is seriously disputed within mainstream science, and BYU itself teaches evolutionary biology. The sharper, unresolved friction is real: population genetics does not support all humans descending from a single recent couple, a global flood in the last several thousand years is contradicted by geology, and Church leaders have at times taught conflicting things about evolution. Honest belief here does not mean choosing scripture over evidence; it means holding these specific, hard questions open rather than pretending either side has already settled them.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Restoration scripture converges, from three independent passages, on organizing or forming existing materials rather than creation from nothing: Abraham 3:24 ("we will take of these materials... and we will make an earth"), Abraham 4:25 and 27 ("organized"/"organize"), and D&C 93:33 ("The elements are eternal"). This is a genuinely convergent pattern, not a single proof-text — a picture more hospitable to an ancient, lawful cosmos than creation-from-nothing theology strictly requires.

- D&C 88:42-45 describes the sun, moon, stars, and earth moving "in their times and their seasons" under "a law unto all things" — a lawful, orderly cosmos is a scriptural premise, not a concession to science. D&C 88:118 commands seeking "learning, even by study and also by faith," and D&C 93:24 defines truth itself as "knowledge of things as they are, and as they were, and as they are to come."

- Abraham 4 marks its creation intervals as the first through sixth "time" rather than quantifying them in hours — a real textual feature leaving the duration of creation unspecified, though it does not by itself prove a "day-age" reading.

- The Church's current Gospel Topics essay "Religion and Science" reports the roughly 4.5-billion-year scientific estimate of earth's age without contesting it, states that scripture reveals who created human bodies and why but not how, and says the Church takes no position on evolution; the Church History topic "Organic Evolution" documents the 1931 First Presidency instruction to leave geology, biology, archaeology, and anthropology to scientific research. BYU teaches evolutionary biology, and Church leaders including [[James E. Talmage]] and [[John A. Widtsoe]] held both scientific and ecclesiastical roles.

- The Church already models faith cooperating with well-supported science in practice: [[Healing the Sick (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2010)]] and [[Priesthood Blessing (Gospel Topics)]] explicitly pair medical treatment, prayer, and priesthood blessings rather than treating them as competitors.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Population genetics does not support all living humans descending from a single recent breeding pair; a strictly literal, sole-progenitor reading of Adam and Eve is difficult to reconcile with genomic evidence, and reading the Fall as the origin of all death conflicts with a fossil record showing predation, disease, and extinction long before humans existed.

- A global flood within the last several thousand years, as a straightforward reading of Genesis 7 and as some Latter-day Saint leaders taught, is contradicted by stratigraphy, radiometric dating, and continuous historical records from civilizations that show no corresponding gap.

- Church leaders have not spoken with one voice on evolution: [[Joseph Fielding Smith]] and [[Bruce R. McConkie]] taught that organic evolution was false doctrine, while [[John A. Widtsoe]] argued more explicitly for compatibility and [[James E. Talmage]]'s 1931 address accepted an old earth and pre-Adamite animal death while remaining notably reticent about human evolutionary descent specifically. Members received genuinely different signals from within the same institution, and later institutional neutrality does not erase that history.

- Reconciliation can look like it is chasing the evidence: as science ruled out six ordinary creation days, a young earth, and a global flood, readings of "day," "all things," and "the whole earth" broadened accordingly. A critic can reasonably ask whether these readings come from the text itself or were adopted mainly to avoid falsification.

- Miracles, answered prayer, and the resurrection are claims about events that, when they have alleged physical consequences, enter territory science can test; describing miracles as operating through "higher law" (as some read [[Alma 42]]) is a theological proposal, not a demonstrated mechanism, and risks becoming unfalsifiable if invoked to explain away every contrary observation.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believing scientists and scholars reply that Adam and Eve can be read as historical figures who are genealogically universal ancestors, or who inaugurate a covenant humanity within a larger population, without being the sole biological source of every human gene — a reading scripture does not rule out, since [[2 Nephi 2]]:22-25 makes a theological argument rather than describing a genetic mechanism, though the surrounding population is not itself something the text describes. Others read the Fall's "no death" language as bounded to Eden or to humanity rather than to all living things, a restriction the text's own sweeping "all things" language does not make automatic but does not forbid either. The Flood can be read as a real, catastrophic regional event described in the universal language ancient narrators used for their known world, consistent with independently documented regional floods, though Genesis 7's repeated universals keep this a considered interpretation rather than the text's plain sense. Individual leaders' scientific opinions were never presented as revelation, a point the First Presidency itself has clarified, and the Church's statements on evolution have consistently stopped short of a doctrinal ruling. And miracles are claims about whether God acted in a particular case, not claims that natural law is chaotic or false — a question science can investigate for testable physical consequences but cannot settle by itself, since it concerns agency rather than the existence of law.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** the Church has no official position on the age of the earth, the mechanism of creation, or evolution, while affirming God as creator/organizer and Adam and Eve as the first parents ([[Religion and Science (Gospel Topics)]]); the physical earth is approximately 4.5 billion years old and biological common ancestry is strongly supported by converging evidence; the canon's own creation language favors organizing existing matter over creation from nothing, and Genesis itself contains more than one, non-identical creation account. **Open:** how, or whether, a historical Adam and Eve relate to human population genetics; the scope of "death before the Fall"; the historicity and geographic extent of the Flood; and how a scriptural "spiritual" creation (D&C 29:31-32) relates to a physical, evolving universe. The Church has left these open, and thoughtful members hold a genuine range of views, including some (young-earth, single-couple, global-flood readings) in real tension with the physical evidence. **Faith:** that revealed truth and empirically discovered truth will ultimately converge, because both are claims about "things as they are" (D&C 93:24) — a theological hope, not yet a demonstrated result; and that miracles occur through law rather than against it even where the mechanism is unknown — a stance about God's mode of action that science can neither confirm nor exclude, since it concerns agency rather than law.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text itself requires, as distinct from what readers have assumed.** The canon requires that God is the author or organizer of the physical order ([[Genesis 1]]; [[Abraham 3]]-[[Abraham 4]]); that Adam and Eve are named, genealogically embedded participants in the Fall (Genesis 2-3; [[Moses 6]]; D&C 107:53-56), not merely symbols; that creation is repeatedly described as organizing or forming existing materials (Abraham 3:24; Abraham 4:25,27; D&C 93:33); that the cosmos is law-governed (D&C 88:36-45); and that truth is defined as objective reality (D&C 93:24). It does not fix an absolute creation date, quantify Abraham 4's creation "times" in earth-hours, specify a mechanism for Adam and Eve's origin, or describe the biology of the Flood. Claims that scripture requires a young earth, that no organism of any kind died before Adam, or that evolutionary common ancestry is inherently atheistic attach to interpretations, not to anything the text states outright.

**Models the evidence and text permit, in both directions:**

- *Evolutionary creation with a covenantal or genealogically-universal Adam and Eve* — plausible; the old-earth and common-ancestry science behind it is independently well supported, but the surrounding population and the covenant/genealogical distinction are not stated in the canon, so this remains a reconciling model, not doctrine.

- *Evolutionary creation with Adam and Eve as historical and representative* — plausible; consistent with how individual biblical figures often carry collective meaning, but does not resolve how their Fall relates to already-existing biological death.

- *Non-concordist / literary creation accounts* — plausible, and supported by the canon's own plurality of non-identical creation narratives ([[Genesis 1]], [[Genesis 2]], Moses 2, [[Abraham 4]]); treats "how" questions as outside the text's purpose rather than settling them.

- *Young-earth, single-couple, literal-day, global-flood concordism* — a defensible reading of the plain English text and the one most 19th- and early-20th-century leaders assumed; strongly disfavored as physical history by radiometric, genomic, and stratigraphic evidence, and not required by current Church teaching.

- *Appearance-of-age creation* (God created an old-looking cosmos with a young actual history) — logically possible but empirically untestable by design, and sits awkwardly beside D&C 93:24's definition of truth as "things as they are." Considered and set aside as unfalsifiable rather than refuted.

- *Intelligent Design* (design as scientifically detectable through specific biological markers) — a distinct claim from generic theism, since it asserts a testable empirical signature; its specific arguments are contested within mainstream biology itself, so it is possible but not independently established.

- *Miracles as suspension of law vs. miracles as action through higher, currently unrecognized law* — the text favors the second: Alma 42:13's "God would cease to be God" grounds even mercy in inviolable law, and D&C 88:42's "law unto all things" generalizes the point. Plausible and internally consistent, though extending a statement about moral necessity to physical law is an inference the chapter does not make explicit, and it risks becoming unfalsifiable if invoked only to explain away contrary observations.

- *Strict independence ("two magisteria")* — eases some tensions but fits Latter-day Saint theology poorly, since it insists on real physical divine action (an organized earth, a bodily resurrection); considered and set aside as incomplete rather than wrong.

- *Conflict / perpetual-warfare thesis* — considered and set aside as poor historiography; historians of science broadly reject it as an oversimplified 19th-century polemic, even though the specific tensions above are real and are not resolved merely by rejecting the general narrative.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
