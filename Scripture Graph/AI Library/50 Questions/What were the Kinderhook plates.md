# What were the Kinderhook plates?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Kinderhook plates were six small brass plates manufactured near Kinderhook, Illinois, planted in a mound, and staged as an archaeological discovery in April 1843. They were brought to [[Nauvoo]], where [[Joseph Smith Jr]] examined or discussed them. William Clayton contemporaneously reported a partial translation and a Ham-and-Pharaoh interpretation, while Parley P. Pratt reported related genealogical claims and comparison with Egyptian materials. No sustained translation or manuscript resulted. Fugate's later confession and forensic examination of the surviving plate establish that the objects were nineteenth-century, acid-etched forgeries. The remaining dispute concerns Joseph's exact words, method, confidence, and whether he regarded the brief interpretation as revelation. A late, secondhand account by David Whitmer claims use of a seer stone, while another reconstruction proposes ordinary comparison with Joseph's Egyptian papers; neither method is established with the certainty of the forgery itself.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Modern manufacture is established.** Fugate described the fabrication and use of acid; examination of the surviving plate found nineteenth-century brass and acid-etched markings consistent with his account and the 1843 facsimiles. See Stanley B. Kimball, “Kinderhook Plates Brought to Joseph Smith Appear to Be a Nineteenth-Century Hoax,” and the Church History Topics essay “Kinderhook Plates.”

- **The staged discovery was publicly documented in 1843.** The certificate printed in the *Times and Seasons* and [[Nauvoo Neighbor 1843-05-10 (Vol. 1 No. 2)]] described six oxidized, bell-shaped brass plates. It verifies the hoax's public presentation, not the truth of the excavation story.

- **Joseph's involvement and a brief interpretation are substantially supported.** Clayton's May 1 journal reports a partial translation and a Ham/Pharaoh lineage claim. Pratt's May 7 letter independently reports related genealogical content and comparison with Egyptian materials, although its details differ. Joseph's May 7 history records consultation concerning the plates.

- **The familiar first-person quotation is not Joseph's autograph statement.** The later *History of the Church* converted Clayton's third-person report into autobiographical first person. This weakens direct attribution but does not erase Clayton's contemporary evidence.

- **The method remains disputed.** Bradley and Ashurst-McGee propose a character comparison with Joseph's Egyptian-language papers. David Whitmer's 1887 account instead attributes the interpretation to a plate placed in a hat and examined through a seer stone. Whitmer wrote decades later and did not present himself as an eyewitness to the event, so his account is relevant but weaker than Clayton's contemporary report.

- **The effort was limited.** No dictated Kinderhook translation, completed work, canonized text, or continuing project is known.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest critical case begins with Clayton's contemporary report that Joseph obtained specific historical meaning from meaningless marks on a modern forgery. If substantially accurate, Joseph made a false translation-related judgment about an alleged ancient record, an activity relevant to evaluating his credibility because translation occupied a central place in his prophetic claims.

The objection becomes stronger if David Whitmer's 1887 account is credited. Whitmer claimed that Joseph placed a Kinderhook plate in a hat and used a seer-stone procedure associated with the Book of Mormon translation. That account is late and apparently secondhand, and it may conflate events, but it cannot be omitted merely because it complicates the nonrevelatory-comparison response.

Source criticism of *History of the Church* does not remove the problem. Its first-person wording is editorial rather than Joseph's autograph statement, but it derives from Clayton's earlier journal. The proper question is how accurately Clayton reported Joseph, not whether later editing made the underlying report disappear.

The absence of a completed translation limits the episode but does not refute a brief false interpretation. Conversely, Kinderhook does not directly prove that the Book of Mormon was fabricated. The artifacts, provenance claims, documentary histories, and resulting texts are distinct; any larger conclusion requires additional evidence.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
A careful believing response can concede that the plates were forged, that Joseph took them seriously enough to investigate, and that Clayton may preserve a mistaken interpretation. Latter-day Saint belief need not require prophets to detect every deception or be infallible in historical and linguistic judgment. The Kinderhook plates were never canonized, and no surviving source records a completed revelation or sustained translation project.

The proposed ordinary-comparison model is historically plausible: Pratt mentions Egyptian-material comparisons, and a Kinderhook character resembles an entry in Joseph's Egyptian-language papers associated with royal descent through Pharaoh. This could explain Clayton's reported result without a formal revelatory process. It remains inferential, however; the same-day request for a Hebrew Bible and lexicon may have been unrelated, and no contemporary source explicitly identifies the proposed character entry as Joseph's method.

Whitmer's later seer-stone account prevents the comparative model from being treated as settled. Believers may reasonably question it because it was written more than four decades later, appears secondhand, and may conflate Kinderhook with other translation narratives. Those weaknesses justify reduced weight, not silence.

The most defensible faithful position is therefore compatible with a fallible Joseph who made a brief erroneous judgment. That mitigates claims of a formally revealed failed translation but does not eliminate the credibility concern for readers who expect unusual reliability in Joseph's translation-related judgments. The separate Book of Mormon claims should be evaluated through [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]], [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]], and [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]].
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the sources require.** The canon says nothing about the Kinderhook plates. Joseph Smith—History 1 describes a different claimed record: gold plates disclosed by [[Moroni (angel)]], accompanied by interpreting instruments, and delivered in 1827. The historical evidence requires that the Kinderhook objects were modern forgeries, that Joseph encountered them, and that Clayton attributed a partial interpretation to him. It does not establish Joseph's exact words, method, confidence, or claimed authority.

- **Modern-hoax model — independently supported.** The plates were deliberately manufactured and planted in 1843. Fugate's account, forensic analysis, contemporary facsimiles, and the staged provenance converge. This is the settled answer to what the objects were.

- **Brief ordinary-comparison model — plausible, partly supported, partly inferential.** Joseph or associates compared the markings with Egyptian materials, and a proposed match in Joseph's Egyptian-language papers could account for the Ham/Pharaoh interpretation. Pratt's letter supports comparison generally. No contemporary source explicitly documents use of the proposed entry, and the Bible/lexicon request may be unrelated.

- **Brief seer-stone interpretation — possible, supported by late testimony.** David Whitmer's 1887 account says Joseph used a plate in a hat with a seer stone in a manner associated with the Book of Mormon translation. This directly supports a revelatory-method reading if accurate. Its weaknesses are its date, apparent secondhand character, and possible conflation; it is neither independently established nor legitimately ignored.

- **Brief claimed revelatory translation that failed — possible, not established.** Clayton's translation language and Whitmer's method claim permit this critical reading. Against certainty, no contemporary source says Joseph invoked revelation, used interpreters, dictated a formal text, or presented the result as scripture.

- **Accurate Clayton report plus a fallible Joseph — plausible theological framework.** Joseph made an erroneous human judgment while examining an artifact. This accepts the adverse evidence but leaves the implications for other prophetic claims to theological and comparative evaluation.

- **Clayton misunderstood or overstated an exploratory remark — possible but weakly supported.** Clayton did not identify his source or claim eyewitness status. His proximity to Joseph, specificity, and Pratt's partial corroboration make dismissal for apologetic convenience ad hoc.

- **Joseph suspected or exposed the fraud — ad hoc and set aside.** No contemporary source reports detection, divine warning, or a plan to expose the makers. Short-lived interest alone cannot establish suspicion.

- **Kinderhook directly disproves or authenticates the Book of Mormon — set aside as overreach.** Kinderhook supplies evidence about vulnerability to deception and translation-related judgment, but it cannot independently determine the origin of another text or artifact claim. See [[Were metal plates used for records in antiquity]] and [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]].
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** the Kinderhook plates were nineteenth-century brass forgeries; their apparent excavation was staged; they reached [[Nauvoo]]; Joseph Smith examined or discussed them; Clayton reported a partial interpretation; and no completed translation followed. The first-person version in *History of the Church* is an editorial recasting rather than Joseph's autograph wording.

**Probable:** Joseph made, endorsed, or at least entertained a short and erroneous interpretation. Clayton's contemporary report and Pratt's partially corresponding account make a flat denial less satisfactory than acknowledging a mistake.

**Plausible but disputed:** comparison with Joseph's Egyptian-language materials offers a concrete nonrevelatory mechanism. David Whitmer's late account instead attributes the activity to a seer stone. Neither method can presently be treated as established; the comparative model has circumstantial documentary support, while the seer-stone model has explicit but late and secondhand testimony.

**Open:** Clayton's immediate source, Joseph's exact language and confidence, whether he regarded the interpretation as revelation, and why the investigation ended.

**Evaluative or theological judgment:** how much this error should affect confidence in Joseph's other claims. Kinderhook genuinely challenges expectations of automatic prophetic discernment, but it does not by itself prove or disprove the Book of Mormon. Intellectual integrity requires conceding the error supported by the evidence while refusing both an undocumented apologetic escape and an unsupported larger conclusion.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with the contemporary record: William Clayton's journal for May 1, 1843; Parley P. Pratt's May 7 letter to John Van Cott; Joseph Smith's May 7 history entry in the Joseph Smith Papers; the *Times and Seasons* certificate; and [[Nauvoo Neighbor 1843-05-10 (Vol. 1 No. 2)]]. Compare these with the later first-person narrative in *History of the Church*, Volume 5.

For the hoax and artifact, read Fugate's 1878 and 1879 correspondence and Stanley B. Kimball, “Kinderhook Plates Brought to Joseph Smith Appear to Be a Nineteenth-Century Hoax,” *Ensign*, August 1981. For the comparative-method reconstruction, read Don Bradley and Mark Ashurst-McGee, “Joseph Smith and the Kinderhook Plates,” while separating documentary observations from inferred mechanism. Read David Whitmer, *An Address to All Believers in Christ* (1887), pages 32–33, for the competing seer-stone claim, assessing its lateness, source access, and possible conflation. The current institutional summary is the Church History Topics essay “Kinderhook Plates.”

Then consult [[Book of Mormon Translation (Gospel Topics)]], [[Moroni's visits and the plates]], [[Records and Sacred Writings]], [[Were metal plates used for records in antiquity]], and [[What were the Mark Hofmann forgeries]].
