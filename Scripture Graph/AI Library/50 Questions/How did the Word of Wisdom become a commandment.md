# How did the Word of Wisdom become a commandment?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Word of Wisdom did not become the modern commandment in one clean, fully documented moment. [[Doctrine and Covenants 89]] was revealed in 1833 as God’s “order and will” but expressly “not by commandment or constraint” and as “a principle with promise” (D&C 89:2–3). In 1851 [[Brigham Young]] invited the Saints in general conference to covenant to abstain from the substances mentioned in it; later leaders treated that action as the point at which it became binding. Actual observance and enforcement nevertheless remained uneven for decades. Under [[Joseph F Smith]] and especially Heber J. Grant, leaders progressively standardized abstinence and tied it to recommends and temple participation: an official Church History Library summary cites a 1919 First Presidency requirement, while Thomas G. Alexander identifies 1921 as the general temple-admission turning point and the 1933 handbook states the requirement explicitly. Current teaching in [[Word of Wisdom (Gospel Topics)]] unequivocally calls it a commandment. The most historically defensible answer is therefore a staged development—1833 revelation, 1851 covenant and commandment tradition, then early-twentieth-century administrative enforcement—not a later canonical verse or a single known new revelation.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The controlling text:** D&C 89:2–3 says both that the instruction reveals God’s “order and will” and that it was sent “not by commandment or constraint,” as “a principle with promise.” Any account must preserve both halves rather than quote only one.

- **The 1851 milestone:** Contemporary conference minutes report that [[Brigham Young]] called on the Saints to covenant to cease using tea, coffee, tobacco, whiskey, and the other things mentioned in the revelation. [[Chapter 29 Living the Word of Wisdom]] represents the later official understanding that the Word of Wisdom is a commandment from God.

- **Practice did not change overnight:** Thomas G. Alexander’s “The Word of Wisdom: From Principle to Requirement” documents disagreement and variable practice among leaders around 1900. The Church History Library likewise says implementation after 1851 was gradual and notes continued beer and wine use by some leaders as late as 1901. That convergence is strong evidence against an immediate-uniform-enforcement narrative.

- **The administrative standard became progressively explicit:** Records from [[Joseph F Smith]]’s administration show increasing recommend restrictions. A 1919 First Presidency letter required observance for the recommendations it addressed; Alexander dates a general temple-admission requirement to 1921; and the 1933 handbook expressly told recommend interviewers that applicants should keep the Word of Wisdom.

- **Current status is not ambiguous:** [[Word of Wisdom (Gospel Topics)]] calls it a commandment and explains that later servants of the Lord made obedience a requirement for baptism, missionary service, and temple ordinances. This establishes present doctrine and administration even though it does not erase the more complicated history.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
1. **The scripture appears to contradict the present claim.** D&C 89:2 does not merely omit the word “commandment”; it affirmatively says “not by commandment or constraint.” A critic can reasonably argue that calling it a commandment reverses the revelation’s stated mode of delivery.

2. **No discrete superseding revelation is known.** Alexander found no contemporary record of a separate revelation changing its status. The 1851 record describes a covenant and conference vote, while the strict worthiness regime emerged much later through deliberation, preaching, letters, and handbooks. A critic may therefore call the change human policy rather than new revelation.

3. **The 1851 turning-point account is too neat.** If that vote immediately made the modern standard binding on everyone, decades of tolerated noncompliance—including among senior leaders—require explanation. Later recollections may have read early-twentieth-century expectations back into 1851.

4. **Modern enforcement is selective.** D&C 89 counsels sparing use of meat, permits wine of the Saints’ own making for the sacrament, and assigns barley to mild drinks (D&C 89:5–7, 12–13, 17). Yet the worthiness standard concentrates on alcohol, tobacco, coffee, tea, and harmful drugs. This shows that the operative rule is an interpreted administrative standard, not simply every clause enforced alike.

5. **The development tracks surrounding culture.** The revelation arose amid temperance and health-reform debates, while strict enforcement grew during the prohibition and anti-tobacco era. A naturalistic account can explain both the original vocabulary and the later institutional emphasis without invoking new revelation.

6. **The meaning of “hot drinks” is supplied by interpreters.** D&C 89:9 does not name coffee or tea. Although Hyrum Smith identified them publicly by 1842 and that reading became authoritative, the distinction between text and interpretation remains real.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
A thoughtful believing response begins by conceding the historical development. The faithful claim need not be that the entire present interview standard appeared fully formed in 1833. D&C 89:2 can describe how the revelation was initially imposed, while still calling its contents God’s revealed “order and will.” On this reading, time for adaptation was mercy rather than evidence that the counsel lacked divine authority; [[Gospel Doctrine]] preserves [[Joseph F Smith]]’s version of that explanation.

Believers can also understand the 1851 covenant as a legitimate prophetic and communal act even if enforcement was initially lenient. Covenants, teachings, and administrative requirements are distinguishable: a rule may be declared binding before leaders apply identical sanctions to every member. [[Chapter 29 Living the Word of Wisdom]] and [[Conference Report, April 1926 (General Conference (full report), April 1926)]] articulate this authority-based understanding.

The lack of a separately recorded revelation is important but not decisive within Latter-day Saint theology. [[Revelation]] may be understood as occurring through sustained prophetic counsel, collective deliberation, and institutional reception rather than only through a new first-person divine text. History can document that leaders made and regularized the decisions; whether their deliberation was inspired remains a theological judgment.

Finally, cultural responsiveness and revelation are not logically exclusive. A revelation may address the live problems of its recipients in familiar vocabulary. That response, however, establishes compatibility, not divine origin; believers should not present historical context itself as proof of revelation.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text itself requires.** D&C 89 requires recognition that the 1833 instruction claims revelatory and divine status while initially disclaiming commandment or constraint (D&C 89:2). It contains counsel, prohibitions, permissions, and promises, but does not state a future date when its status will change, name coffee or tea, prescribe worthiness interviews, or say every provision carries an identical sanction. D&C 89:18 links keeping 'these sayings' with obedience to commandments, but its grammar does not conclusively reclassify every saying. Claims of an immediate modern code and claims of merely human advice both add more than the text says.

- **Gradual prophetic-development model — plausible; institutionally supported; divine authorization is a faith claim.** God gave an accommodating principle and later authorized prophets to make its central rules binding as the Church matured. Fits D&C 89:2-3, the 1851 covenant, later teachings, gradual enforcement, and current Church teaching. History supports the development; it cannot verify divine direction.

- **1851 formal-commandment model — possible and officially taught, but incomplete if treated as instantaneous.** Brigham Young's 1851 conference covenant supplied a formal communal act, later described as the moment the Word of Wisdom became law. Continued variation afterward means 1851 works better as a covenantal milestone than as the date of uniform modern enforcement.

- **Progressive administrative-routinization model — independently supported.** The modern rule emerged through accumulated interpretation, leadership advocacy, and administrative steps, especially under Joseph F. Smith and Heber J. Grant. Directly supported by Alexander's study; describes the record without deciding if the process was inspired.

- **Policy-with-prophetic-authority model — plausible.** 'Commandment' in current usage may describe an authoritative Church requirement rather than a textual amendment to D&C 89, explaining why canonical wording is unchanged while enforcement changed and why some provisions (e.g., sparing meat use) are not enforced identically.

- **Cultural-reform model — plausible and independently supported as a contributing cause.** Antebellum temperance/health reform illuminates 1833; Prohibition-era reform illuminates early-20th-century enforcement. Overconfident if taken to prove purely human origin — the same setting is compatible with revelation addressing contemporary conditions.

- **Purely institutional-construction model — possible.** Later leaders may have converted noncompulsory counsel into a worthiness test without a documented new revelation, driven by institutional needs and social reform. Cannot demonstrate leaders lacked sincere inspiration.

- **The text was always a full commandment despite verse 2 — possible but strained.** Appeals to 'the order and will of God' and D&C 89:18 to nullify 'not by commandment or constraint' is ad hoc unless 'commandment' is carefully redefined; it cannot explain the documented later shift in sanctions.

- **Canonization (1835) alone made it a commandment — possible but weak/ad hoc.** D&C 89 was already included in the canonized Doctrine and Covenants by 1835. Canonization conferred scriptural status but its accepted wording still said 'not by commandment'; canonization alone does not explain the later 1851 covenant or the 20th-century enforcement regime.

- **A lost or unrecorded discrete revelation changed the rule — possible but presently unsupported.** Absence of documentation isn't proof no revelation occurred, but it adds an unnecessary assumption when the gradual documented process already explains the policy history.

**Considered and set aside:** an immediate, universally enforced 1851 transformation is inconsistent with later variable practice; a sudden single doctrinal revelation around 1921 lacks any known contemporary record; and reducing the whole development to either health science or national Prohibition ignores the 1833 revelation, the 1851 covenant, internal religious reasoning, and the selective way leaders adopted surrounding reforms.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** D&C 89 was originally sent “not by commandment or constraint”; the 1851 conference covenant was a major formal milestone; observance and enforcement remained gradual; early-twentieth-century leaders made compliance an increasingly standardized worthiness requirement; and the Church now authoritatively teaches it as a commandment. The modern standard is an interpreted and administered form of the revelation, not a verbatim restatement of every dietary clause.

**Open:** whether 1851 should be called the exact moment it “became” a commandment depends on whether that word means covenantal obligation, leadership teaching, or enforceable worthiness rule. The scope of the 1919 instruction relative to the broader 1921 standard also deserves careful archival specification. No known separate canonized revelation settles the transition.

**Matter of faith:** history can show what leaders taught and required, but it cannot determine whether the gradual process was guided by God. Believers may reasonably understand continuing prophetic interpretation as [[Revelation]]; critics may reasonably view the same record as institutional policy development. Intellectual honesty permits the historical conclusion without pretending that it resolves the theological one.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
