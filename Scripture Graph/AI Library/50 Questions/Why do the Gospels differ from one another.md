# Why do the Gospels differ from one another?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Gospels differ because they are selective ancient narratives, not four verbatim transcripts. Luke acknowledges earlier accounts, eyewitness transmission, investigation, and ordered composition (Luke 1:1–4), while John says that many signs were omitted and that those included were chosen to foster belief (John 20:30–31). Matthew, Mark, and Luke are closely related literarily; Markan priority is the best-supported majority reconstruction, though no Gospel states it. John uses substantially different material, chronology, style, and theological presentation. Some variations can reasonably reflect selection, paraphrase, compression, oral tradition, or literary arrangement; others—especially the Passover chronology and the two genealogies—remain genuine tensions, as do the differing accounts of Judas in Matthew and Acts. These differences undermine strict transcript-like harmony, but they neither prove wholesale unreliability nor, by themselves, establish four independent eyewitness reports.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The writers disclose mediated composition.** Luke refers to earlier narratives, eyewitness transmission, investigation, and his own ordered account (Luke 1:1–4). John says many signs were omitted and identifies the purpose governing those selected (John 20:30–31). This directly rules out treating either work as an exhaustive transcript.

- **The Synoptics combine close agreement with editorial difference.** Comparisons such as Matthew 9:1–8, Mark 2:1–12, and Luke 5:17–26 show corresponding wording and episode structure alongside variations. This strongly supports literary or tradition-level relationship, although the exact direction and source map remain reconstructed.

- **Several tensions are directly observable.** Matthew 1 and Luke 3 trace different lines after David and name different fathers for Joseph. The Synoptics identify the final meal with Passover, whereas John 18:28 and 19:14 retain Passover language afterward. Matthew 27 and Acts 1 frame Judas's death and the field differently. Proposed harmonizations are possible, but none is narrated by the paired texts.

- **The resurrection narratives preserve both convergence and difference.** All four report crucifixion, an empty tomb, and women at the discovery or initial witness, while differing in visitors, messengers, sequence, appearances, and movement. The earliest recoverable text of Mark ends at Mark 16:8 without narrating a resurrection appearance; Mark 16:9–20 is a later, textually disputed ending.

- **Ancient genre explains some, not all, variation.** Richard A. Burridge's *What Are the Gospels?* supports comparison with Greco-Roman biography, where selection, paraphrase, compression, characterization, and some rearrangement were normal. Michael R. Licona applies these conventions to Gospel differences, but any proposed device still requires passage-specific support.

- **Restoration scripture offers a theological analogy, not historical proof.** First Nephi 6, 1 Nephi 9, and Words of Mormon 1 portray sacred writers selecting, omitting, distinguishing, and combining records. This supports an inspired-editor model within [[Records and Sacred Writings]] but does not establish which sources the evangelists used.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **Some differences look contradictory rather than complementary.** Matthew and Luke cannot both be read as straightforward, generation-by-generation biological genealogies of Joseph without additional assumptions. John's Passover markers resist the Synoptic chronology, and neither Matthew 27 nor Acts 1 narrates the combined death sequence proposed for Judas.

- **The earliest recoverable ending of Mark sharpens the resurrection problem.** Mark 16:8 ends with the women fleeing in fear and saying nothing to anyone; the familiar appearances in Mark 16:9–20 belong to a later, textually disputed ending. The four traditions therefore do not simply provide parallel appearance narratives.

- **Literary dependence weakens the popular claim of four independent witnesses.** Extensive verbal and sequential agreement makes shared written sources or direct literary dependence probable among the Synoptics. Repetition of inherited material is not equivalent to three independent confirmations.

- **Later evangelists appear to reshape inherited tradition.** Under Markan priority, Matthew and Luke sometimes clarify Mark, relocate material, alter wording, or change characterization. A critical model can understand this as theological redaction or tradition development rather than independent memory.

- **John differs at a deeper level.** It contains long first-person discourses, explicit preexistence theology, a multi-Passover framework, and signs absent from the Synoptics. Critics argue that its portrait reflects decades of theological interpretation as much as remembered speech.

- **Genre flexibility can become unfalsifiable.** Assigning every discrepancy to compression, transfer, paraphrase, or topical arrangement without passage-specific evidence turns a legitimate historical framework into an ad hoc rescue.

- **Inspiration does not settle historical questions.** Divine work through human authors can make variation theologically coherent, but it does not demonstrate which chronology is historically prior or whether a particular speech or event occurred exactly as narrated.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believers need not defend four identical transcripts. Luke 1:1–4 and John 20:30–31 already require a model involving sources, selection, ordering, and purpose. Articles of Faith 1:8 likewise does not require a perfectly transmitted or verbally inerrant Bible. [[Records and Sacred Writings]] in 1 Nephi 6, 1 Nephi 9, and Words of Mormon 1 depicts sacred history as genuinely edited while still offered as testimony.

Some apparent conflicts can be complementary: mentioning one messenger does not deny a second, and different summaries can compress different stages of an event. Ancient-biography research also gives independently attested reasons to expect paraphrase and nonchronological arrangement. These answers should be applied case by case, however. A second temple cleansing is possible but unconfirmed; legal-versus-biological genealogy proposals are possible but not identified by Matthew or Luke; a combined Judas narrative is coherent but reconstructed.

Believers may also distinguish exact quotation from faithful representation. John's discourses could preserve a distinct witness tradition expressed through mature theological reflection. That is plausible, especially given John's declared theological purpose, but it cannot be proved merely by invoking the beloved disciple. [[John the Apostle]] is the traditional identification of that unnamed disciple, not a name supplied by John 21:20–24.

The strongest faithful response is therefore cumulative and modest: the Gospels agree on the central proclamation of Jesus's ministry, crucifixion, and empty tomb while preserving real human and literary differentiation. [[Hosanna to the Most High God (Elder Ronald A. Rasband, April 2023)]] and [[And They Sought to See Jesus Who He Was (Elder James W. McConkie III, October 2022)]] articulate this fourfold-witness approach. Whether that proclamation corresponds to divine action remains a question requiring broader historical argument and faith, not a conclusion generated merely by explaining the differences.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The four Gospels overlap extensively but differ in selection, order, wording, chronology, and theology. Luke and John explicitly disclose composition and selection. The Synoptics are literarily related, and Markan priority is the best-supported majority reconstruction. Several differences are genuine unresolved tensions rather than mere changes of perspective. All four nevertheless share the crucifixion and empty-tomb center of the Christian proclamation.

**Open:** The exact Synoptic source relationships; how much material derives directly or indirectly from eyewitnesses; which proposed harmonizations represent actual history; the historical priority of John's or the Synoptics' Passover chronology; the origin of each genealogy; and how closely reported speeches preserve Jesus's wording. Ancient biography and oral-memory studies narrow the possibilities but do not eliminate them.

**Matters of faith:** That the evangelists were divinely inspired, that their common proclamation is saving [[Truth]], and that Jesus rose bodily are theological conclusions that historical and literary evidence may support but cannot compel. A responsible Latter-day Saint position can affirm inspiration and [[Testimony]] without pretending that every difference has been reconciled. The differences are damaging to strict transcript-like inerrancy; they are compatible with, but do not prove, an inspired-human-record model.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with parallel reading: Matthew 3, Mark 1, Luke 3, and John 1 for the baptism; Matthew 21, Mark 11, Luke 19, and John 2 for the temple cleansing; Matthew 26–28, Mark 14–16, Luke 22–24, and John 18–20 for Passover, crucifixion, and resurrection; then compare Matthew 1 with Luke 3 and Matthew 27 with Acts 1. Read Luke 1:1–4 and John 20:30–31 as the evangelists' clearest statements about composition and purpose. When reading Mark 16, consult a textual apparatus concerning the ending at 16:8 and the later 16:9–20.

For source criticism, read Mark Goodacre, *The Synoptic Problem: A Way Through the Maze*, and Raymond E. Brown, *An Introduction to the New Testament*. For genre and compositional practice, read Richard A. Burridge, *What Are the Gospels?*, and Michael R. Licona, *Why Are There Differences in the Gospels?* For an eyewitness-oriented reconstruction, read Richard Bauckham, *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, alongside critical reviews of its identification arguments. Thomas Wayment's *The New Testament: A Translation for Latter-day Saints* is useful for translation and textual notes; Bruce M. Metzger's *A Textual Commentary on the Greek New Testament* treats major manuscript variants.

For early Gospel attribution, study the reports associated with [[Papias]] preserved by [[Eusebius]] and [[Irenaeus]], *Against Heresies* 3.1.1, while distinguishing external reception from statements inside the Gospels. For Restoration comparisons, study 1 Nephi 6, 1 Nephi 9, Words of Mormon 1, [[Records and Sacred Writings]], and [[Why are there multiple First Vision accounts]]. [[Hosanna to the Most High God (Elder Ronald A. Rasband, April 2023)]] and [[And They Sought to See Jesus Who He Was (Elder James W. McConkie III, October 2022)]] present modern Latter-day Saint fourfold-witness approaches.

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require.** They require plurality, selection, and literary purpose. Luke knows of multiple narratives and constructs an ordered account (Luke 1:1–4); John omits material intentionally (John 20:30–31); Acts continues a prior volume addressed to Theophilus (Acts 1:1–3). They do not require four isolated eyewitness authors, exhaustive chronology, verbatim speech, or modern inerrancy. They also do not say that chronology is irrelevant, that every difference is deliberate, or that errors are impossible. The objection to perfect verbal harmony attaches to an imposed doctrine of scripture; the objection that every difference disproves the shared story likewise outruns the text.

- **Shared-source and redaction model — independently supported and highly plausible.** The Synoptic wording and order strongly support shared written or stable traditional material. Markan priority is the majority reconstruction; Matthew-first and more complex source models remain possible. This model explains simultaneous agreement and author-specific alteration but does not by itself decide historicity.

- **Ancient-biography model — independently supported and plausible.** Comparison with ancient lives supports selection, paraphrase, compression, characterization, and some rearrangement. It handles many differences without imputing fraud. A proposed device becomes ad hoc when invoked solely to remove a conflict without textual or comparative support.

- **Eyewitness-memory model — possible and partly supported, but disputed.** Named participants, claims of testimony, and John 21:24 permit connections to eyewitness memory; Richard Bauckham develops the strongest modern version. Yet the Gospels do not identify all four conventional authors as eyewitnesses, and Synoptic dependence means variation alone cannot prove independence.

- **Oral-tradition model — independently supported and plausible.** Repeated teaching could remain stable in substance while varying in wording, order, and application before and alongside written sources. Oral transmission can preserve material, reshape it, or do both; appealing to it does not automatically certify a saying as authentic.

- **Theological-literary portrait model — textually required in a limited sense and plausible more broadly.** John declares a belief-producing aim; each Gospel's arrangement creates a distinctive portrait. Theology and historical memory need not be opposites, but some scholars judge portions—especially Johannine discourse—to be theological composition rather than recoverable verbatim speech.

- **Case-by-case harmonization — possible and sometimes plausible.** Selective narration can explain one messenger versus two, and compressed accounts can describe different phases. Different calendars or broader festival usage may address the Passover problem. Two temple cleansings, a legal-versus-biological genealogy division, and a fall following Judas's hanging are logically possible; because the paired texts do not supply those explanations, they range from possible to ad hoc depending on the external support claimed.

- **Tradition-growth or partial-error model — possible and plausible for some details.** Memories and sources can diverge, and later writers can reshape earlier accounts. This model straightforwardly accommodates incompatible chronologies or genealogies. It does not follow that the shared crucifixion and empty-tomb proclamation is fabricated; each claim requires separate historical evaluation.

- **Thoroughgoing literary-fiction model — possible in the bare logical sense but weak as a global explanation.** Literary shaping is undeniable, yet wholesale invention must still explain early proclamation, common narrative anchors, and the works' engagement with remembered persons and places. Recognizing literary artistry is not sufficient evidence for total fiction.

- **Inspired-human-witness model — confessionally plausible, not independently established by the differences.** God may communicate through writers who select, translate, remember, and arrange. This accords with Articles of Faith 1:8 and the editorial self-awareness of Restoration scripture. It interprets the theological significance of the evidence rather than settling each historical question.

**Considered and set aside as governing assumptions:** strict dictation, because the texts display selection and authorial shaping; automatic independence from variation, because literary dependence is strongly evidenced; automatic collusion from agreement, because common sources and stable tradition predict agreement; and automatic harmonization, because logical compatibility is weaker than a textually supported reconstruction. The manuscript question treated by [[How reliable is the biblical text]] and [[Was the Bible changed by councils and copyists]] is related but distinct: transmission variants do not account for most differences among the four compositions.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
