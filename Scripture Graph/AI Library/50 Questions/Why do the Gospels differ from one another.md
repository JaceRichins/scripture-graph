# Why do the Gospels differ from one another?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Because they are four portraits, not one transcript. Matthew, Mark and
Luke share material (Mark is probably the earliest and a source for the
other two) yet arrange, word and frame it differently; John stands apart in
chronology, style and content. Differences range from trivial (one angel or
two at the tomb) to substantial (the day of the crucifixion relative to
Passover; the cleansing of the temple at the start or the end of the
ministry). Ancient biography permitted compression, rearrangement and
paraphrase in ways modern readers do not expect; the Gospels are faithful
to that convention. The differences are a problem for a theory of scripture
that requires verbatim harmony; they are evidence of independent memory for
a theory that does not.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Independent accounts of any real event differ in exactly this way;
  identical testimony is what collusion produces. The core (baptism,
  Galilean ministry, conflict with authorities, Jerusalem, crucifixion,
  empty tomb) is stable across all four.
- Each Gospel has a discernible purpose — Matthew for a Jewish audience,
  Luke for Gentiles, John explicitly "that ye might believe"
  ([[John 20]]:31) — and its selections and emphases follow from it.
- Greco-Roman biography (Plutarch's parallel lives of the same figure are
  the best comparison) shows the same kinds of variation without anyone
  calling Plutarch a liar.
- The Book of Mormon's own multi-witness structure ([[3 Nephi 11]] and the
  small plates' overlapping accounts) treats plural testimony as strength.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Some differences are contradictions, not perspectives: Jesus dies before
  Passover in John and after the Passover meal in the Synoptics; the
  genealogies in Matthew and Luke do not agree; Judas dies two different
  ways ([[Matthew 27]], [[Acts 1]]).
- Later Gospels appear to "improve" earlier ones — Matthew and Luke soften
  Mark's rougher portrait of the disciples and Jesus — which looks like
  theological editing.
- John's Jesus speaks in long discourses about his own divinity that the
  Synoptic Jesus never gives; one of the portraits is doing something other
  than reporting.
- If God inspired the texts, why not inspire them to agree?
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believers distinguish inspiration from dictation: the Latter-day Saint view
holds scripture to be the word of God through human writers, "as far as it
is translated correctly" (Articles of Faith 8), and expects human
fingerprints. On the hard cases, harmonizations exist (Judas's death, the
Passover chronology) but the more honest response is that ancient authors
compressed and arranged for meaning, and that a genealogy tracing legal
descent and one tracing biological descent can both be true. John's
discourses may reflect a different memory tradition (the Beloved Disciple)
and decades of reflection; that they are interpretive does not make them
false. Restoration scripture is comfortable with this — the Joseph Smith
Translation revises the Gospels freely.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The Gospels differ because they are what they claim to be: testimonies,
selected and shaped. Some differences resist harmonization and a believer
should say so. The choice is between a doctrine of scripture that the texts
themselves do not support (verbal inerrancy) and one they do (inspired,
human, reliable on what matters). The Restoration chose the second in 1830,
which is why this question is less threatening to Latter-day Saints than it
is to some of their neighbors.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
