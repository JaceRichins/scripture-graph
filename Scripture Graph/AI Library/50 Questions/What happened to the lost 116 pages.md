# What happened to the lost 116 pages?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
What is settled is limited: 116 pages of an early Book of Mormon translation manuscript, historically called the Book of Lehi, disappeared while entrusted to [[Martin Harris]] and have never been recovered; [[Book of Mormon Translation (Essay)]] cautiously says they were lost or stolen. Precisely who took them, whether they were misplaced, withheld, or destroyed, and where they ended up are unknown. D&C 3 rebukes [[Joseph Smith Jr]] for surrendering the writings, while D&C 10 says hostile persons altered them and directs him not to retranslate the same portion but to translate a parallel record extending to King Benjamin. That is the canonical explanation; the alleged alteration plot and providential preparation of the replacement remain religious claims not independently established by the missing manuscript.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The loss itself:** [[Historical Summary]] and [[Book of Mormon Translation (Essay)]] agree that a 116-page translation manuscript disappeared while in [[Martin Harris]]'s possession. The latter uses the appropriately cautious description "lost or stolen."

- **Joseph's responsibility and interruption:** D&C 3:4-15 portrays [[Joseph Smith Jr]] as yielding to persuasion, violating instructions about the writings, losing privileges temporarily, and needing repentance. D&C 10:1-3 says his translating gift was later restored.

- **The instruction actually given:** D&C 10:30-45 says not to translate the missing portion again and instead directs translation of an account on the plates of Nephi through King Benjamin. This is a checkable textual fact regardless of whether one accepts the revelation's divine origin.

- **A parallel-record structure:** 1 Nephi 9:2-5 distinguishes records with different purposes, and Words of Mormon 1:3-11 describes Mormon finding a record from Nephi to Benjamin and joining it to his compilation. Together they fit the believing explanation that the surviving opening books replace rather than reproduce the lost narrative.

- **The limit of the evidence:** [[The Contributions of Martin Harris]] expressly says the precise manner of loss is speculative. It reports both the rumor that Lucy Harris burned the pages and her denial; no supplied source turns that suspicion into an established fact.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
1. **The conspiracy claim is internally sourced.** D&C 10:8-19 and 10:29-33 say adversaries altered the manuscript, but the revelation is the claim under examination, not independent corroboration. No surviving original or altered copy is available for comparison.

2. **Avoiding retranslation has a straightforward critical explanation.** If a second dictation differed materially from the first, critics could expose the discrepancy. A naturalistic model therefore reads D&C 10 as a way to avoid a test Joseph might fail, without needing an actual altered manuscript.

3. **The replacement can look post hoc.** The two-record explanation in 1 Nephi 9 and Words of Mormon 1 is internally coherent, but internal coherence does not establish divine foreknowledge. A critic may argue that the literary structure was composed or emphasized after the loss to solve the problem it created.

4. **The physical story is underdetermined.** Lost, stolen, deliberately withheld, and destroyed all fit the surviving evidence. Naming Lucy Harris as culprit exceeds that evidence, especially because [[The Contributions of Martin Harris]] records her denial and calls the circumstances speculative.

5. **The missing text prevents decisive testing.** Its exact contents, literary quality, relationship to the present opening books, and reproducibility cannot now be examined. Arguments claiming either a perfect correspondence or a demonstrated contradiction go beyond the evidence.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believers can answer that D&C 10 does not promise a verbatim recreation: it explicitly substitutes a different record with overlapping chronology and a different emphasis. Accordingly, ordinary variation between two dictated texts would not by itself refute the translation claim; the proposed hostile comparison depended on presenting altered wording as though it were the original.

The Book of Mormon also contains a real two-record architecture rather than only a bare excuse: 1 Nephi 9:2-5 distinguishes records by purpose, and Words of Mormon 1:3-11 narrates Mormon's decision to join the Nephi-to-Benjamin record to his compilation. That makes the replacement model textually substantial. It does not, however, independently prove ancient composition or divine foreknowledge.

D&C 3's severe criticism of [[Joseph Smith Jr]] is relevant to literary posture: the account preserves his error, fear of others, loss of privilege, and need to repent rather than depicting an infallible founder. This may increase its candor, but candor is not proof that every explanatory claim in D&C 10 is historically exact.

Finally, believers need not defend the rumor against Lucy Harris. [[Book of Mormon Translation (Essay)]] says lost or stolen, and [[The Contributions of Martin Harris]] acknowledges uncertainty. Faith in providential preservation can stand without pretending to know the manuscript's physical fate.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require, versus what readers assume.** D&C 3 requires only that writings Joseph entrusted to another were lost after he yielded to persuasion, and that he was disciplined (D&C 3:4-15). D&C 10 requires, as that revelation's own claim, an adversarial plan involving altered wording and an instruction not to retranslate (D&C 10:8-19, 29-45), plus a substitute account through King Benjamin. Neither text names Martin Harris, states '116,' identifies Lucy Harris, or supplies external confirmation of any plot — those come from later historical framing, not the verses themselves.

**Plausible — accidental loss or unplanned destruction:** Martin may have misplaced or failed to secure the manuscript. Fits the disappearance and the acknowledged uncertainty, but doesn't explain D&C 10's alteration claim.

**Plausible — intentional removal by an unknown person:** Someone with access may have taken or destroyed the pages. Suspicion reported in the sources gives this general model context, but no culprit is independently supported.

**Possible but not independently supported — Lucy Harris burned the manuscript:** Reported specifically as rumor; she denied it. Treating this as settled history is unwarranted.

**Possible within the revelation; historically uncorroborated — altered-manuscript conspiracy:** D&C 10 states adversaries changed the wording to make a new translation look inconsistent. This explains the non-retranslation instruction on its own terms, but no surviving altered pages or independent witness confirms it.

**Plausible critical model — naturalistic risk management:** A second dictation might not have matched the first, so Joseph substituted a new narrative rather than risk a failed comparison. Doesn't itself prove deception, since the missing manuscript makes the model untestable.

**Plausible critical model, distinct from risk management — compositional dating of D&C 10's explanation:** The alteration narrative in D&C 10 may have been articulated, or its details sharpened, only after the loss was already known and its consequences already being managed — meaning the explanation could be shaped by hindsight rather than contemporaneous knowledge of an actual plot. This is a distinct claim from risk-management (which grants the explanation is contemporaneous and only questions motive); it questions the explanation's timing and origin instead, and is neither confirmed nor ruled out by the sources here.

**Plausible within the Book of Mormon's theological framework — providential parallel record:** D&C 10:38-45, 1 Nephi 9:2-5, and Words of Mormon 1:3-11 jointly permit the model that God anticipated the loss and preserved overlapping history on another record, prepared within the narrative's own chronology centuries before 1828. The textual fit is strong; the claim of divine foreknowledge remains a faith conclusion.

**Possible but speculative — the pages survived in private hands:** Non-recovery doesn't prove destruction, but no authenticated fragment or provenance supports continued secret survival either.

**Considered and set aside:** a verbatim-restoration model, contrary to D&C 10's own replacement language; confident assignment of blame to Lucy Harris, which the sources treat as rumor; and equating this episode with 1 Nephi 13's separate plain-and-precious-losses claim, a different textual-history question entirely.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** An early translation manuscript identified by historical sources as 116 pages and the Book of Lehi disappeared while in [[Martin Harris]]'s custody; [[Joseph Smith Jr]] did not retranslate the missing portion; D&C 3 records a rebuke and temporary loss of privileges; and D&C 10 supplies the non-retranslation and replacement instructions. The manuscript has not been recovered in the evidence available here.

**Open:** The person responsible, the mechanism of disappearance, the manuscript's final disposition, its exact contents, and whether anyone altered it remain unknown. The Lucy Harris burning story should not be presented as fact.

**A reasonable but unproved critical inference:** Avoiding a second version protected Joseph from comparison. This explains the practical decision but does not demonstrate fraud, because the absent manuscript makes the proposed discrepancy untestable.

**A matter of faith:** That God foresaw the loss, prepared the parallel record, revealed an actual adversarial plot, and preserved the intended gospel material. The internal alignment among D&C 10, 1 Nephi 9, and Words of Mormon 1 supports this interpretation within the canon but cannot independently establish its supernatural premises.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
