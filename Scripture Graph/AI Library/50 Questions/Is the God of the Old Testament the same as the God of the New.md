# Is the God of the Old Testament the same as the God of the New?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
At the level of the received Christian canon, yes: the New Testament repeatedly identifies the God proclaimed by Jesus and the apostles with the God of Abraham, Israel's prophets, creation, and the covenants (Matthew 22:31-32; Hebrews 1:1-12). It also distinguishes the Father from the Son, so “the same God” does not mean that every occurrence of “God” or “LORD” names the same person. Classical Christianity expresses this as one God in distinct persons; Latter-day Saint scripture more specifically presents Jesus Christ as Israel's divine lawgiver acting in unity with, yet personally distinct from, the Father (3 Nephi 15:1-10; D&C 76:20-24). Real differences in laws, portrayals, and moral problems remain. They challenge simplistic claims of an unchanged administration or perfectly uniform biblical voice, but they do not overturn the canon's explicit claim of divine continuity.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The canon explicitly claims continuity.** Hebrews presents the same God speaking formerly through Israel's prophets and now through the Son ([[Hebrews 1]]). Jesus affirms Israel's confession of one Lord and treats the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as the living God ([[Mark 12]]; [[Matthew 22]]; [[Exodus 3]]).

- **New Testament authors apply Israel's divine functions and scriptures to Jesus.** John presents the Word as preexistent, divine, active in creation, and made flesh ([[John 1]]). Hebrews applies Psalm 102's creator language to the Son, and Romans joins confession of Jesus as Lord with Joel's promise concerning those who call upon the LORD ([[Hebrews 1]]; [[Romans 10]]; [[Joel 2]]). These are strong evidence for high Christology, although divine-identity and principal-agent interpretations remain disputed.

- **Restoration scripture answers more explicitly.** The risen Jesus says that he gave Israel's law and made covenant with Israel ([[3 Nephi 15]]), while [[1 Nephi 19]] identifies the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob as the one who would be crucified. Other Restoration texts distinguish Christ from the Father: Christ prays to the Father, and Joseph Smith and Sidney Rigdon report seeing him at God's right hand ([[3 Nephi 19]]; [[Doctrine and Covenants 76]]). This establishes the Restoration canon's internal position; accepting its historical authority is a separate faith and evidence question.

- **The wrathful-Old-Testament/gentle-New-Testament caricature fails.** The Old Testament calls the LORD merciful and gracious and commands love of neighbor and resident stranger ([[Exodus 34]]; [[Leviticus 19]]). The New Testament includes final judgment and destructive divine action ([[Matthew 25]]; [[Revelation 16]]). This establishes overlapping moral attributes, not a complete solution to the conquest and violence problems.

- **The canon acknowledges covenantal change.** Jesus speaks of fulfilling the law, Acts does not impose the whole [[Law of Moses]] on Gentile converts, and Hebrews describes a new covenant ([[Matthew 5]]; [[Acts 15]]; [[Hebrews 8]]). Continuity of God therefore does not require identical administration in every period.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **Moral discontinuity is real.** Commands of total destruction in Deuteronomy, Joshua, and 1 Samuel stand in severe tension with Jesus's commands to love enemies and renounce retaliation. Naming both justice does not resolve questions about victims, moral agency, genre, or whether every attributed command accurately reveals God's will. See [[Why did God command the conquest of Canaan]], [[Does the Bible endorse slavery]], and [[Justice and Mercy]].

- **The Old Testament is not a single undifferentiated authorial voice.** Historical-critical models treat it as a collection formed from texts and traditions with different dates, settings, and theological emphases. Divine-council language, covenant traditions, and later exclusive-monotheism polemic can therefore be read as evidence of development within the Old Testament itself. The question may presuppose more internal uniformity than the documents display.

- **Divine language applied to Jesus need not settle ontology.** Jewish principal-agent, Wisdom, Word, and heavenly-intermediary traditions offer a model in which an exalted agent bears divine authority or language while remaining distinguishable and subordinate. Hebrews 1 and Romans 10 securely establish what their authors apply to Jesus, but interpreters dispute whether this entails shared divine being or delegated agency.

- **Father-Son distinction resists a rigid name key.** Acts distinguishes the God of Abraham from Jesus; Jesus prays to the Father; and Paul names one God, the Father, and one Lord, Jesus Christ. Thus the LDS formula identifying Jehovah with premortal Jesus can express a coherent doctrinal synthesis while exceeding what every biblical LORD occurrence independently establishes.

- **Laws and institutions genuinely change.** Food laws, circumcision, sacrifice, purity regulations, and civil penalties do not continue unchanged into the Gentile church. If divine constancy means issuing identical commands in every age, the canon contradicts that definition.

- **Canonical continuity is not independent proof of metaphysical reality.** Later authors can appropriate earlier scriptures and claim fulfillment. That strongly establishes intended literary and theological continuity, but not that one transcendent being actually spoke in every narrated event.

- **A historic different-gods model exists.** [[Marcion]] separated Israel's creator from the Father proclaimed by Jesus. The model takes moral and legal discontinuity seriously but can be maintained only by rejecting or revising substantial portions of the received New Testament that affirm Israel's God and scriptures.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- The strongest believing response begins with the texts' explicit self-identification, not merely with similarities of character. Jesus and the apostles repeatedly claim Israel's God, prophets, covenants, and scriptures as their own history (Matthew 22:31-32; Luke 24:25-27; Hebrews 1:1-3). A different-gods model therefore conflicts with the final form of the canon it is trying to explain.

- Thoughtful believers distinguish God's identity from a covenant's administration. Scripture itself permits differentiated commands: Jesus says a Mosaic divorce provision responded to human hardness of heart (Matthew 19:7-8), Acts gives Gentile converts a different legal burden (Acts 15:5-29), and Hebrews speaks of a new covenant (Hebrews 8:6-13). [[Covenants]] can change without implying that the covenant-maker has been replaced.

- The moral evidence should be compared in both directions. Exodus 34:6-7 and Psalm 103:8-13 prevent reduction of the Old Testament God to wrath; Matthew 25:31-46 and Revelation 19:11-21 prevent reduction of the New Testament Christ to nonjudgmental kindness. This establishes overlap, not a complete moral solution.

- Classical Trinitarian readers understand Father and Son as personally distinct while sharing the one divine identity; Latter-day Saints understand them as distinct divine persons perfectly united in purpose, with the premortal Christ functioning as Israel's Jehovah. Both can accommodate the distinction texts. They differ on divine ontology, and neither framework should be presented as though it were stated in full by a single biblical verse. See [[How did the doctrine of the Trinity develop]] and [[The Godhead]].

- Believers may interpret historical development as progressive revelation or divine accommodation to ancient societies. That is possible and has textual analogies, but it becomes ad hoc if invoked only to excuse each difficulty without criteria. Conquest, slavery, and severe penalties still require case-by-case moral and historical study; continuity of identity does not automatically vindicate every interpretation of every attributed command.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require.** The final-form New Testament deliberately claims continuity with Israel's God, scriptures, prophets, and covenants. It also distinguishes Father and Son while applying divine functions and scriptural language to Jesus. The texts do not require identical laws in every covenant, perfect uniformity among biblical authors, or the rule that every Old Testament use of LORD refers exclusively to the Son. The further metaphysical correlation is interpretive.

- **Canonical continuity with progressive covenantal revelation — plausible and strongly text-supported.** The same God acts through successive covenants while commands and the clarity of revelation change ([[Matthew 5]]; [[Hebrews 1]]; [[Hebrews 8]]). This best fits the canon's explicit self-presentation but does not prove that every attributed command came from God.

- **Classical Trinitarian continuity — plausible; historically and textually supported as a synthesis.** The one God is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, allowing YHWH texts to be applied to the Son without making him either the Father or a fourth deity. [[John 1]], [[1 Corinthians 8]], and [[Hebrews 1]] support the synthesis, whose technical formulation developed through controversies associated with [[Arius]], [[Athanasius]], and [[Nicaea]]. [[Chalcedon]] principally addresses Christ's two natures rather than completing the doctrine of the Trinity. See [[How did the doctrine of the Trinity develop]].

- **Latter-day Saint Jehovah-Christ continuity — plausible and explicitly supported within Restoration scripture.** Jesus is Israel's lawgiver and covenant-maker, personally distinct from the Father but united with him ([[3 Nephi 15]]; [[3 Nephi 19]]; [[Doctrine and Covenants 76]]). This is a direct internal answer for readers who accept Restoration scripture. The Bible alone does not require assigning every LORD passage exclusively to the premortal Christ. See [[The Godhead]] and [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]].

- **Jewish principal-agent or heavenly-intermediary Christology — possible and independently supported as historical context.** Jesus may receive divine authority, functions, worship, or scriptural language as God's supreme agent while remaining ontologically subordinate. Father-Son distinction texts support this possibility; creation and YHWH-text applications create pressure against reducing Jesus to an ordinary messenger. This model explains significant data without either Nicene identity or Marcion's opposed deities.

- **Unitarian or exalted-Messiah continuity — possible but textually strained in its simpler forms.** Israel's God is the Father alone, while Jesus is the uniquely commissioned and exalted human Messiah. John 17:3 and Acts 3:13 support the distinction, but [[John 1]], [[Hebrews 1]], and creation language require substantial explanation.

- **Arian or ontological subordinationist continuity — historically significant and possible as a synthesis.** The Son is a preexistent, generated, lesser divine being through whom the supreme God acts. It accommodates preexistence, creation through the Son, and statements such as the Father being greater, but conflicts with Nicene readings of shared divine titles and worship. [[Arius]] is therefore a substantive model, not merely background.

- **Canonical polyphony — plausible and independently supported at the literary level.** Later authors claim continuity with earlier scripture while preserving diverse genres, voices, and tensions. This can coexist with inspiration, accommodation, or a critical account of composition and explains why identity continuity may be clearer than uniformity of portrayal.

- **Evolving human understanding of one claimed deity — plausible and independently supported by diachronic study.** Israelite and Christian communities may have revised their conceptions of God. A believer may interpret development as accommodation or progressive revelation; a critic may interpret it as human theological evolution. Historical development alone does not decide whether one divine reality stands behind the texts.

- **Marcionite different-deities model — logically possible but ad hoc and poorly supported as a reading of the received canon.** It gives full weight to moral and legal discontinuity but must discard, reedit, or override numerous passages joining Jesus to Israel's God. Early responses by [[Irenaeus]] and [[Tertullian]] show that this was a substantive ancient dispute.

- **Simple modal identity of Father and Son — set aside as inadequate to all the texts.** Prayer, sending, glorification, and Christ at God's right hand require a real distinction, even though traditions dispute its nature.

- **A wholly violent Old Testament deity replaced by a wholly gentle New Testament deity — set aside.** Mercy and concern for outsiders occur in the Old Testament, while severe judgment occurs in the New.

- **Strict univocality with no development or tension — set aside.** Covenant changes, diverse voices, Father-Son distinctions, and morally difficult texts are too explicit to treat every difference as merely apparent.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established from the texts:** The received New Testament intentionally identifies its God and gospel with Israel's God and scriptures. It incorporates Jesus into divine creation, authority, judgment, and scriptural language while distinguishing him from the Father. The Book of Mormon states directly that the risen Christ gave Israel's law and covenant. Both Testaments contain mercy and judgment, and the canon itself acknowledges covenantal change.

**Open:** The exact metaphysics relating Father, Son, and Israel's divine name; whether each Old Testament LORD passage should be assigned to a particular divine person; how much theological development occurred within Israel and early Christianity; and how particular violence, slavery, and penalty texts should be understood morally and historically. Continuity of identity does not settle those questions.

**A matter of faith:** That the God described by these records actually revealed himself, that Jesus truly shares divine identity and authority rather than merely receiving those claims from later communities, and that Restoration scripture gives authentic clarification. The textual case establishes what the canon claims far more strongly than it independently establishes the transcendent truth of those claims.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with [[Exodus 3]] for the divine name and patriarchal identification; [[Exodus 34]] for the Old Testament declaration of divine character; [[Deuteronomy 6]] and [[Mark 12]] for the confession Jesus affirms; [[John 1]], [[John 8]], and [[Hebrews 1]] for New Testament Christology; and [[3 Nephi 15]] and [[3 Nephi 19]] for the Restoration canon's combination of Christ as Israel's lawgiver with Father-Son distinction.

For covenantal change, read [[Matthew 5]], [[Acts 15]], [[Romans 9]], [[Romans 10]], and [[Hebrews 8]]. For the moral comparison, study [[Leviticus 19]], [[Psalm 103]], [[Ezekiel 18]], [[Matthew 5]], [[Matthew 25]], and [[Revelation 16]], alongside [[Why did God command the conquest of Canaan]], [[Does the Bible endorse slavery]], and [[Justice and Mercy]].

For historical frameworks, compare [[Marcion]], [[Irenaeus]], and [[Tertullian]], then follow the development through [[Nicaea]], [[Arius]], [[Athanasius]], and [[Chalcedon]]. Mark S. Smith's *The Early History of God* presents a major historical-critical reconstruction of Israelite divine-language development. [[Gospel Doctrine]] and [[Chapter 2 The Everlasting Gospel]] illustrate later Latter-day Saint teaching about continuity of gospel and ordinances; they are tradition witnesses, not independent proof of identity.
