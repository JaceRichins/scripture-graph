# When was the book of Daniel written?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The book is set in the sixth century BCE and presents visions in the voice of [[Daniel (prophet)]], but the strongest cumulative historical and literary evidence indicates that it reached substantially its present form during the crisis under [[Antiochus IV Epiphanes]], about 167–164 BCE. Daniel 11 follows Hellenistic events with exceptional detail through Antiochus's persecution, then appears to expect an end for him that does not match the usual historical account; Daniel 7–9 and 12 reflect the same crisis. Many scholars nevertheless judge some court tales or traditions in Daniel 1–6 to be older, perhaps Persian or early Hellenistic. A traditional sixth-century-authorship view remains theologically possible if detailed predictive prophecy is granted, but it is not the conclusion favored by most historical-critical scholarship. The supplied Church sources affirm Daniel's scriptural and prophetic value without defining an official composition date; see [[Daniel, book of (Bible Dictionary)]].
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
1. **Daniel 11's historical horizon.** Daniel 11:2–35 moves from Persia through Alexander's divided empire into the Ptolemaic-Seleucid wars and the persecution under [[Antiochus IV Epiphanes]]. The density and order of the correspondences are weighed in [[Cumulative Historical Correspondence in Daniel 11]], [[Historical Identification in Daniel 11]], and [[Event Correspondence in Daniel 11]]. This is substantially stronger than a single verbal parallel.

2. **The change after Daniel 11:39.** Daniel 11:40–45 expects further actions and an end for the northern king near the holy mountain; the normal historical account places Antiochus's death on an eastern campaign. Daniel 12:1–3 immediately associates that time with deliverance and resurrection. The ordinary historical inference is that the author stood inside the crisis, knew its preceding events, and still awaited its conclusion. Meir's 2023 literary reading and Coşkun's 2019 redactional reconstruction caution that this inference should not be turned into an exact date for every chapter.

3. **Daniel 8 identifies its own empires.** Daniel 8:20–22 names Media-Persia and Greece and describes the first Greek king followed by four kingdoms; Daniel 8:9–14, 23–25 then depicts a persecuting ruler and a violated sanctuary. See [[Historical-Correlation in Daniel 8]] and [[Historical Identification in Daniel 8]].

4. **The visions converge on one crisis.** The horn and persecuted holy ones of Daniel 7, the sanctuary crisis of Daniel 8, the final week of Daniel 9, and the northern king of Daniel 10–12 are mutually reinforcing, not four independent proofs. Their cumulative alignment is assessed in [[Internal Chronological Correspondence in Daniel 10]], [[Historical-Context Alignment in Daniel 12]], and [[Textual-Doctrinal Evidence in Daniel 12]].

5. **The book interprets earlier scripture.** Daniel 9:2 explicitly reads Jeremiah's seventy years (Jeremiah 25:11–12; 29:10) and Daniel 9:24–27 transforms the horizon into seventy weeks. That establishes literary posteriority to Jeremiah, though not by itself a second-century date.

6. **External boundaries.** Daniel manuscripts at [[Qumran]] and allusions in 1 Maccabees show that Daniel circulated by the later second century BCE. They establish a terminus ante quem, not a sixth-century autograph. The 2025 radiocarbon result for 4Q114 dates its parchment probabilistically and does not directly date the book's first composition.

7. **Language and historical notices are secondary controls.** The Hebrew, Aramaic, Persian vocabulary, and Greek musical terms fit post-exilic transmission but cannot fix 164 BCE by themselves; see [[TextualLinguistic Scholarship in Daniel 2]]. Belshazzar is genuine limited corroboration ([[Historical Corroboration in Daniel 5]]), while the opening chronology and Darius the Mede remain disputed ([[Historical Chronology in Daniel 1]]).
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objection to the Maccabean conclusion is the book's own presentation: Daniel 7:1–2, 8:1–2, 9:2, and 10:1–2 attribute visions to a named sixth-century courtier. If God can reveal the future, detailed knowledge of later history is not evidence against the stated date. A believer may also argue that apparent errors arise from incomplete modern records, ancient regnal conventions, broad kinship language, symbolic prophecy, or mistaken modern identifications.

The early-date case has positive points. Daniel preserves Belshazzar's name and authority when later Greek accounts foreground Nabonidus; its Aramaic is not a simple second-century vernacular; its Greek vocabulary is small and concentrated in musical terminology; and the Qumran copies show relatively early and rapid reception. Conservative treatments such as Stephen R. Miller's *Daniel* and Zdravko Stefanovic's *The Aramaic of Daniel* argue that these observations fit a sixth-century work later transmitted by scribes.

Against a narrowly fixed 167–164 BCE date, the book may have several layers. Daniel 10–11 could preserve an expectation written before Antiochus's death while other visions or chronological notices were revised afterward. Coşkun proposes such a reconstruction, and Meir argues that Daniel 11:45 may be a deliberate literary portrayal of imperial downfall rather than merely a failed forecast.

The objection to the traditional model is cumulative: it must explain why the book becomes exceptionally specific in the second century, why its apparent knowledge horizon changes precisely during Antiochus's crisis, why Darius the Mede lacks a secure identification, why the opening chronology is difficult, and why linguistic and literary evidence fits prolonged post-exilic growth. Invoking supernatural prediction answers the first question within faith, but does not independently explain the compositional seams or historical difficulties.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believers need not choose between scripture's religious authority and an artificially certain claim about a single sixth-century writing session. The text requires that its visions be heard as Danielic revelation situated in exile; it does not explicitly say that the entire twelve-chapter collection was written and edited at one time by one hand. Ancient inspired books can preserve earlier traditions, adopt a revered voice, receive inspired interpretation, and address a later covenant crisis.

A believer who retains sixth-century authorship can reasonably stress that historical method is not equipped to disprove revelation, that absence of evidence for Darius is not evidence of absence, that father can denote dynastic relationship, and that languages and loanwords travel across centuries. Those responses keep an early date possible. They become weak when possibility is presented as historical demonstration or when every difficulty receives a different untestable rescue.

A believer who accepts a Maccabean final form can understand Daniel's retrospective visions as apocalyptic proclamation: past empires are narrated as prophecy so suffering readers can see history under God's sovereignty and remain faithful. On this reading, pseudonymous voice is an ancient literary convention rather than necessarily a modern-style fraud. That interpretation should still acknowledge the theological difficulty created by unfulfilled expectations in Daniel 11:40–12:3 rather than pretending there is no problem.

Most importantly, dating does not erase the book's canonical message. [[Daniel, book of (Bible Dictionary)]] emphasizes fidelity to God under imperial pressure, and [[November 9–15. “There Is No Other God That Can Deliver” Daniel 1–7]] uses the narratives to teach prayer, courage, gratitude, and trust. Those claims do not depend on demonstrating a particular compositional date.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** Daniel requires a sixth-century narrative setting, a seer named Daniel, successive foreign empires, and a final horizon dominated by persecution, desecration, deliverance, and resurrection. It does **not** explicitly require that Daniel personally penned every third-person court tale, that all chapters originated simultaneously, that later scribes made no additions, or that the book's final editor lived in the sixth century. Conversely, it does not announce that its visions are retrospective literary prophecy. Both unitary authorship and wholesale pseudonymity add assumptions beyond what the text directly states.

- **Single sixth-century author with predictive revelation — possible within faith; not independently supported as the best historical reconstruction.** First-person attribution and the book's setting support it; Belshazzar provides limited corroboration. It must account for the Antiochian knowledge horizon, Daniel 11:40–45, the chronology of Daniel 1, Darius the Mede, language, and literary growth. Supernatural prediction can explain foreknowledge but is not an independently testable dating instrument.

- **Sixth-century Danielic core, followed by substantial inspired updating — plausible in broad outline; details disputed.** An exilic person or traditions could lie behind some narratives, while later storytellers, visionaries, and editors shaped the collection. This model respects possible early memories and the evidence for a Maccabean final form. Claims that every difficult word or verse is a later interpolation become ad hoc unless supported by textual or literary seams.

- **Older court tales plus Maccabean visions and final editor — plausible and independently supported; the mainstream compositional model.** Daniel 1–6 likely grew from diaspora court tales, while Daniel 7–12 was composed or decisively reshaped around 167–164 BCE. The mismatched language and genre boundaries, the differing narrative forms, and the convergent Antiochian horizon support this model. The exact age of each tale remains open; see [[Literary-Theological Pattern in Daniel 1]], [[TextualInterpretive in Daniel 1]], and [[Explicit Textual Identification in Daniel 2]].

- **Essentially unitary Maccabean pseudonymous composition — possible and historically economical in some respects, but less adequate than a growth model.** It directly explains the second-century horizon and Danielic voice as apocalyptic pseudonymity. It has more difficulty explaining signs that some court tales had independent histories and differing forms.

- **Maccabean core with limited post-164 revision — plausible for particular passages; independently argued but a minority reconstruction.** Coşkun dates Daniel 10–11 before Antiochus's death but places portions of Daniel 7–9 and 12 shortly afterward. Meir's literary account likewise weakens the claim that Daniel 11:45 must reflect ignorance. These models refine rather than overturn the second-century setting.

- **Futurist break within Daniel 11 — theologically possible; textually disputed and often ad hoc as a dating defense.** Some Christian interpreters move from Antiochus to a future adversary at Daniel 11:35, 36, or 40. This can preserve literal fulfillment, but the passage does not name a new king and Daniel 11:40 continues the existing north-south framework. It needs independent textual justification rather than being introduced solely where correspondence with Antiochus stops.

- **Purely symbolic, nonchronological reading — possible as reception, weak as an account of original context.** Daniel's images can legitimately acquire later typological applications, but Daniel 8:20–22 explicitly identifies historical empires and Daniel 11 follows an ordered succession. A reading that ignores those anchors cannot explain the text's historical specificity.

**Considered and set aside.** Ben Sira's failure to name Daniel is an argument from silence and receives little weight. Daniel's placement among the Writings rather than the Prophets may fit a late date, but canon formation is too uncertain for it to carry the case. Greek musical words and Qumran manuscript dates are useful controls, not decisive proofs. Ezekiel 14:14, 20 and 28:3 were also set aside as secure early attestations because Ezekiel's Danel/Daniel may be a different legendary wise man.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** Daniel is set during the [[Babylonian captivity of Judah]] and early Persian rule; it combines court tales and visions, Hebrew and Aramaic; Daniel 8 explicitly identifies Media-Persia and Greece; and Daniel 7–12 is intensely aligned with the persecution of [[Antiochus IV Epiphanes]]. Daniel circulated by the later second century BCE, as its reception and [[Qumran]] manuscripts demonstrate.

**Best-supported historical conclusion:** The book reached substantially its canonical form in the Maccabean crisis, approximately 167–164 BCE, probably by combining and adapting earlier material. The date is strongest for the final visions and editorial form, not for the first telling of every narrative. A modest extension after 164 BCE for some material remains possible.

**Open:** The age of the individual court tales; whether a historical sixth-century Daniel stands behind any of them; the identity of Darius the Mede; the precise stages of redaction; and whether Daniel 11:40–45 was written before Antiochus's death, deliberately idealizes it, or was later reapplied.

**Matter of faith:** That Daniel's revelations are inspired, that God disclosed future events to an exilic prophet, or that later inspired writers legitimately spoke in Daniel's voice cannot be proved or disproved by ordinary historical dating alone. Faith should not be made to depend on claiming that disputed linguistic or archaeological evidence proves more than it does.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
