# How reliable is the biblical text?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The biblical text is broadly recoverable but not perfectly preserved, and the answer depends on what “reliable” means. Thousands of manuscripts, ancient translations, quotations, and the Dead Sea Scrolls permit unusually close comparison and reveal extensive continuity; they also expose real variants, competing ancient editions, and a few substantial passages such as Mark 16:9-20 and John 7:53-8:11. No autographs survive, so textual criticism ordinarily reconstructs the earliest attainable wording with varying degrees of confidence rather than offering photographic access to an original. That conclusion concerns transmission, not automatically historical accuracy, canon, translation, interpretation, or inspiration. Latter-day Saint scripture holds affirmation and qualification together: Articles of Faith 1:8 calls the Bible God's word “as far as it is translated correctly,” while [[1 Nephi 13]] claims plain and precious material was lost and later records would restore and confirm truth. Neither passage requires wholesale skepticism, manuscript inerrancy, or identification of one historical institution as the culprit.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest evidence

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Multiple, geographically dispersed witnesses:** Greek and Hebrew manuscripts, ancient versions, and quotations allow readings to be compared rather than accepted from a single controlled copy. This makes both continuity and corruption observable. Metzger and Ehrman, *The Text of the New Testament* (4th ed.), and Emanuel Tov, *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* (3rd ed.), are standard starting points.

- **The Judean Desert manuscripts:** Material associated with [[Qumran]] predates the principal medieval Masoretic codices by many centuries. Some witnesses agree closely with the later Masoretic tradition; others preserve genuine textual plurality. The balanced finding is substantial continuity without letter-for-letter uniformity; see [[Dead Sea Scrolls and Old Testament reliability]].

- **Variant patterns can be inspected:** Most differences are small, while substantial cases are identifiable rather than hidden. The Longer Ending in [[Mark 16]] and the woman accused of adultery in [[John 8]] are marked in responsible modern editions because their early manuscript support is weak or their location varies.

- **Ancient translations supply independent lines of comparison:** The Septuagint, Latin, and Syriac traditions sometimes witness a source reading older than surviving Hebrew or Greek copies. Translation freedom and later revision limit this evidence, but comparison can still constrain reconstructions.

- **Restoration scripture expects both value and imperfection:** Articles of Faith 1:8 affirms the Bible conditionally, while 1 Nephi 13:38-41 portrays additional records as establishing the truth of the first record and making lost teachings known. The model is converging witnesses, not replacement of the Bible.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Real textual issues to know

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The originals are unavailable.** Every reconstruction depends on later copies. Especially where the earliest evidence is sparse, an error or edition predating all surviving witnesses may be unrecoverable.

- **Some variants are substantial.** Mark 16:9-20, John 7:53-8:11, and the expanded Trinitarian wording associated with 1 John 5:7-8 show that passages could be added, relocated, or enlarged. Saying that most variants are minor does not erase the important ones.

- **The Old Testament was textually plural.** Qumran evidence and differences among the Masoretic Text, Septuagint, Samaritan Pentateuch, and other witnesses sometimes reflect more than spelling mistakes. Books such as Jeremiah circulated in materially different editions; Samuel also contains difficult competing readings.

- **Manuscript abundance is not the same as early access.** Many New Testament manuscripts are medieval, and raw totals can be used apologetically without accounting for date, genealogy, dependence, or quality. More copies also produce more recorded variants.

- **Transmission does not establish truth.** Even perfect copying would not prove miracles, authorship, chronology, prophecy, or inspiration. Those belong to [[Is the Old Testament historically reliable]], [[Why do the Gospels differ from one another]], and related historical questions.

- **The Restoration claim is not independently mapped.** [[1 Nephi 13]] alleges important losses but names no missing passage, manuscript family, date, or responsible institution. Known textual variants do not by themselves demonstrate the full theological claim, and the broad allegation can become difficult to test if every missing Restoration teaching is assigned to an otherwise unattested loss.

- **Canon is a separate vulnerability.** A copied book can be transmitted accurately while the larger collection remains disputed. Questions about excluded, lost, or contested writings belong especially to [[Who decided which books are in the Bible]].
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Restoration perspective

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believers need not defend manuscript inerrancy. Articles of Faith 1:8 already combines confidence with qualification, and [[1 Nephi 13]] anticipates correction through additional witnesses. That framework can accommodate textual criticism's finding that the Bible is substantially recoverable while some readings remain uncertain.

The existence of variants is not evidence that every reading is equally doubtful. Manuscripts form patterns: their age, relationships, geographical distribution, scribal habits, and the emergence of readings can often be evaluated. The same evidence that reveals corruption also prevents many alterations from passing unnoticed. Modern critical editions openly flag serious cases instead of depending on them silently.

Believers may also read 1 Nephi 13 more broadly than a prediction of detectable copyist errors. Its language can encompass lost writings, canon selection, suppressed covenants or teachings, translation, and interpretive obscuring, although actual removal 'from the book' in 1 Nephi 13:28 should not be reduced to interpretation alone. This broader model is compatible with some historical phenomena, but it becomes ad hoc when attached to particular missing doctrines without evidence.

Finally, textual uncertainty can be localized. A reader can treat Mark 16:9-20 or John 7:53-8:11 cautiously without concluding that the Sermon on the Mount, Paul's principal letters, or the Hebrew prophetic corpus is therefore unknowable. The Joseph Smith Translation offers a related but distinct response to lost plainness: many Latter-day Saint scholars understand its readings as inspired commentary and restoration of meaning rather than as verified recovery of ancient manuscript wording, so it should not be cited as textual-critical evidence.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The biblical manuscripts contain real variants; no known autographs survive; most variation is comparatively small; some passages and readings are substantial or unresolved; ancient versions and the Judean Desert manuscripts permit meaningful reconstruction; and both continuity and textual plurality are facts that a responsible account must retain. Textual reliability is graded by passage and book, not answered by one percentage.

**Open:** The exact wording at some locations, the relationship among early editions of certain Old Testament books, whether particular secondary passages preserve authentic events, how much material disappeared before surviving witnesses, and precisely how the losses in 1 Nephi 13 correspond to recoverable history remain open. Textual criticism alone cannot settle canon, historicity, inspiration, or correct interpretation.

**A matter of faith:** Calling the Bible the word of God, accepting 1 Nephi 13 as revelation, attributing preservation to providence, and believing that additional scripture restores divine truth exceed what manuscript comparison can prove. They may coexist responsibly with the evidence so long as they are identified as theological commitments rather than presented as conclusions forced by textual criticism.

For ordinary study, broad confidence with local caution is better supported than either word-for-word certainty or wholesale distrust. Use a modern edition with textual notes, compare translations where wording matters, and investigate disputed passages individually.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Read [[1 Nephi 13]] in full, especially 1 Nephi 13:20-29 and 38-41, alongside Articles of Faith 1:8 in [[Articles of Faith 1]]. Track separately what each text explicitly says and what later readers infer.

- Use [[Dead Sea Scrolls and Old Testament reliability]] and [[Qumran]] for the strongest Old Testament manuscript test. Then consult Emanuel Tov, *Textual Criticism of the Hebrew Bible* (3rd ed.), comparing examples where Qumran agrees with and differs from the Masoretic Text.

- For the New Testament, consult Bruce M. Metzger and Bart D. Ehrman, *The Text of the New Testament* (4th ed.), David C. Parker, *An Introduction to the New Testament Manuscripts and Their Texts*, and the apparatus of Nestle-Aland 28 or UBS5.

- Examine the primary witnesses and apparatus for Mark 16:9-20, John 7:53-8:11, and 1 John 5:7-8. Thomas Wayment's *The New Testament: A Translation for Latter-day Saints* provides accessible textual notes from an Latter-day Saint scholar.

- Separate the neighboring questions by reading [[Was the Bible changed by councils and copyists]], [[Who decided which books are in the Bible]], [[Why do the Gospels differ from one another]], and [[Is the Old Testament historically reliable]].

- For research method, compare the source-evaluation counsel in [[Truth (Gospel Topics)]] and [[Gospel Topics Essays (Essay)]] with actual critical editions rather than relying on claims about manuscript counts alone.

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the Restoration texts require:** Articles of Faith 1:8 requires both affirmation of the Bible as God's word and a qualification concerning correct translation. It does not define the full scope of "translated." [[1 Nephi 13]] requires, within its own prophetic narrative, an initially pure Jewish-apostolic record, subsequent removal of plain and precious material and covenants, resulting religious stumbling, and later records that restore and confirm truth (1 Nephi 13:24-29, 38-41). It does not use the word *Bible*, delimit a modern canon, name a manuscript, identify a council, or equate the unnamed great church exclusively with one denomination. Claims of perfect preservation and claims that one named institution rewrote everything are assumptions added by readers.

- **Critical reconstruction model (reasoned eclecticism / Alexandrian priority) — independently supported.** The surviving witnesses preserve most wording with high confidence while leaving localized uncertainty. Its support is the agreement and patterned variation among manuscripts, versions, and citations; this is the working model behind Metzger/Ehrman and Tov, and the basis for regarding Mark 16:9-20 and John 7:53-8:11 as later additions.

- **Byzantine-priority / majority-text model — plausible, minority scholarly view.** A minority of textual scholars (e.g., Maurice Robinson, Wilbur Pickering) argue the numerically dominant, geographically dispersed Byzantine tradition — which includes Mark's long ending and the pericope adulterae — reflects a text-type at least as old as the Alexandrian one, and that earliest-surviving-manuscript need not equal earliest-text. This is a real countermodel in the opposite direction from the page's default framing; it has not displaced mainstream consensus but should not be silently omitted.

- **Textual plurality and developing-edition model — independently supported.** Some biblical books circulated in more than one literary or textual form before later standardization. Qumran, Septuagintal, Samaritan, and Masoretic comparisons support this especially for parts of the Old Testament.

- **Providential sufficiency without perfect preservation — plausible and text-supported as theology.** God may have preserved enough for covenantal witness and salvation while allowing human copying, translation, and canon processes to remain imperfect. Manuscript evidence can support textual sufficiency in a limited practical sense but cannot independently prove providence.

- **Broad Restoration-loss model — plausible, partly historically supported, but underdetermined.** "Taken away" may include lost compositions, competing editions, canon exclusion, deliberate alteration, and suppression of teachings or covenants. History independently supports examples of textual loss and plurality but does not identify the complete set of losses envisioned in 1 Nephi 13.

- **Narrow copyist-corruption model for 1 Nephi 13 — possible but incomplete.** Known scribal changes illustrate how wording can change, yet the chapter's references to covenants and many plain teachings may contemplate more than ordinary copying.

- **Large-scale unrecoverable corruption model — logically possible, weakly supported as a general history.** Changes predating all witnesses could evade comparison; invoking this for a specific lost teaching without independent trace is ad hoc.

- **Strictly inerrant-transmission model — set aside as a universal historical claim.** It conflicts with demonstrable additions, omissions, and competing editions.

- **Radical unreliability model — possible as abstract skepticism, poorly fitted to the evidence globally.** Widespread agreement and identifiable manuscript relationships constrain this conclusion for the corpus as a whole, though it may be justified for individual unresolved readings.

- **Purely interpretive-loss model — considered and set aside as sufficient by itself.** 1 Nephi 13:28 specifically depicts things taken from the book; interpretation may be part of the loss but does not exhaust the passage's language.

- **Single-institution conspiracy model — set aside as textually and historically overdrawn.** The chapter names no institution, and manuscript history reflects many communities, languages, accidents, revisions, and canon choices rather than one coordinated rewrite.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
