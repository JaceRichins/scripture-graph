# Could the Jaredite barges have worked?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The most defensible answer is **possibly, but not demonstrated**. [[Ether 2]], [[Ether 3]], and [[Ether 6]] describe eight small, light, tightly constructed vessels with closable air openings, provisions, families and animals, episodic submersion, and a 344-day voyage. That account is too sparse to validate or decisively disprove a natural reconstruction: it gives no usable hull geometry, material properties, stability data, loading plan, ventilation rate, fresh-water system, sanitation arrangement, or route. It also does not say that every ordinary maritime device was absent. Miraculous light, unusually directed wind, divine protection, and resurfacing are integral to the story's own explanation. Engineering can test particular reconstructions and identify serious constraints, but it cannot establish that these vessels existed or completed the voyage; unrestricted miraculous aid is a theological claim, not an engineering demonstration.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The narrative recognizes real hazards.** The brother of Jared raises air, light, and steering; the account also addresses water ingress, provisions, storms, and propulsion (Ether 2:16-25; Ether 6:4-10). This is strong evidence of deliberate practical awareness, but only weak evidence that the solutions would work.

- **The builders have prior watercraft experience within the story.** The final vessels are built after the manner of barges used for earlier crossings (Ether 2:6,16). That makes the narrative less dependent on complete novice construction, though it does not independently establish either competence or historicity.

- **Some exaggerated objections exceed the text.** Ether requires periods under water but does not describe controlled submarine navigation or end-over-end tumbling. It does not describe sails, rudders, keels, ballast, or restraints, but neither does it declare them absent. This preserves possible reconstructions without validating any particular one.

- **The design is underdetermined.** Width, height, materials, thickness, displacement, center of gravity, righting behavior, loads, ventilation, and route are unspecified. That strongly supports a limited conclusion: no confident engineering verdict follows from the text alone.

- **Miracle belongs to the stated model.** Divine light, directional wind, protection, and resurfacing are explicit. This explains how the narrative understands survival, but it is not independent evidence that the voyage occurred.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
### Strong objections

- **The specification is radically incomplete.** “Small,” “the length of a tree,” “peaked,” and “tight like unto a dish” do not yield displacement, scantlings, hull strength, freeboard, center of gravity, reserve buoyancy, righting moment, or dynamic behavior. Eight full-scale vessels cannot be evaluated from those phrases alone (Ether 2:16-17; Ether 3:1).

- **Life support is the hardest cumulative problem.** The voyage lasts 344 days and includes men, women, children, and at least some flocks, herds, animals, or birds (Ether 6:3-4,11). Intermittent ventilation must manage oxygen, carbon dioxide, humidity, heat, odors, and potentially animal gases. Fresh water, food preservation, feed, waste removal, disease, exercise, births, and storm-time care compound one another. “All manner of food” answers provisioning only generally; it does not document a workable system.

- **The lower opening is obscure.** A simple open hole below the waterline admits water. A protected well, trunk, valve, air lock, or opening usable only in certain orientations could be proposed, but none is described. Treating the verse as a complete mechanical drawing is unwarranted; treating every proposed mechanism as though the verse states it is apologetic overreach (Ether 2:20-21).

- **Storm survival is not established by watertightness alone.** A closed hull can still capsize, remain inverted, suffer structural failure, lose stability through shifting people, animals, water, or cargo, or experience dangerous accelerations. The text reports survival but gives no stability or restraint design (Ether 6:5-10).

- **Propulsion and navigation remain unexplained in ordinary terms.** The story attributes progress to an unceasing wind toward the destination (Ether 6:5,8). It names no route and describes no sail, rudder, keel, or celestial navigation. Conversely, it does not explicitly prohibit those devices. A claim that favorable winds alone guarantee a 344-day arrival is therefore not an engineering reconstruction.

- **A central component is openly miraculous.** Sixteen stones shine continuously after being touched by the Lord (Ether 3:1-6; Ether 6:2-3). Natural luminescence proposals do not match the text's stated cause unless they substantially rewrite the episode.

- **The story admits a literary explanation.** Its explicit comparison to Noah's ark, preservation of families and animals through divinely controlled floods, and providential arrival can be read as a sea-deliverance narrative rather than travel reportage (Ether 6:7). [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] independently finds strong biblical/KJV mediation and extensive final-form design. Those corpus findings make literary composition a serious alternative, though they do not by themselves prove this episode fictional.

- **No barge-specific external corroboration is supplied here.** The corpus registry offers no identified wreck, securely associated artifact, departure site, route, landing site, technical manuscript, or successful full-scale replication of the described system. Internal narration is evidence for what the book says, not independent confirmation that the event occurred.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
### Careful believing responses

- **Do not make the text more vulnerable than it is.** “Buried in the deep” and “under the water” require episodic submersion, but not a powered submarine, controlled deep diving, or tumbling. “Driven before the wind” does not say that sails, rudders, keels, anchors, ballast, or cargo restraints were absent. Ether 2:19 even speaks of steering. Critiques that require all of those extra assumptions refute only one visualization.

- **An abridgment need not inventory routine technology.** Moroni presents himself as transmitting an abbreviated record, and the passage concentrates on theological turning points. Omitted water containers, husbandry, rigging, drainage, or sanitation are not thereby proven absent. This response preserves possibility, however; it cannot supply evidence for the missing systems.

- **The load is unknown.** Popular art may place implausibly large crowds and animal collections in each vessel, but Ether does not give the embarkation total, per-vessel allocation, dimensions, or species list. Even the bees named earlier are not explicitly named in Ether 6:4. Reducing imagined loads may answer some objections, but a reconstruction becomes ad hoc if every unknown is simply assigned its most favorable value.

- **Experimental voyages caution against armchair impossibility claims.** Kon-Tiki and other traditional-craft voyages show that apparently simple vessels can exploit winds and currents. They justify testing rather than ridicule, but they do not validate this different design.

- **The narrative claims extraordinary providence.** A believer need not pretend the voyage was ordinary. [[Consistent and Resilient Trust (Elder L. Todd Budge, October 2019)]] and [[Spiritual Capacity (Michelle Craig, October 2019)]] appropriately draw lessons about obedience and divinely varied solutions. [[Holy Scriptures The Power of God unto Our Salvation (Elder Robert D. Hales, October 2006)]] likewise calls the barges divinely designed. These are faithful readings, not technical demonstrations.

The strongest honest believing position is therefore not “engineering has proved the barges.” It is that the sparse text permits workable configurations, that several popular objections depend on added assumptions, and that the narrated voyage ultimately rests on claimed divine intervention. Historical confidence must come from a broader case, not from declaring an untested reconstruction solved.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
### What the text requires—and what readers add

The text requires eight vessels; prior barge-building experience; small, light, tight hulls with peaked ends and closable doors; two closable air openings; people and provisions; at least some transported creatures; miraculous lights; episodic submersion; wind-driven progress; divine resurfacing; and 344 days upon the water (Ether 2:6,16-25; Ether 3:1-6; Ether 6:2-11). It does **not** specify hull cross-section, material, width, height, ballast, deck layout, passenger distribution, route, maximum submersion depth or duration, sails, rudders, keels, anchors, tanks, drains, waste systems, or cargo restraints. It also does not explicitly say the vessels rolled, functioned as controlled submarines, remained sealed for days at a time, carried every earlier-mentioned species across the ocean, or lacked ordinary maritime equipment.

- **Wave-overwashed surface craft — plausible, text-supported.** The vessels are light “upon the water,” tossed “upon the waves,” and buried because mountain waves break upon them (Ether 2:16; Ether 6:5-7). “Above the water or under the water” still requires temporary immersion, but this model need not turn them into deep-diving submarines. It reduces some popular objections, while leaving structural stability, ventilation, load restraint, and long-term life support unresolved.

- **Self-righting enclosed pods — possible, not independently supported.** Peaked ends, overall tightness, and ambiguous top-and-bottom language can be reconciled with a hull intended to survive changing orientations. Actual self-righting would depend on ballast and center of gravity, neither of which the text supplies. Claims that this was certainly the design are speculative.

- **Protected vent trunk or moon-pool-like lower opening — possible but ad hoc at present.** A shaft, well, raised coaming, valve, or geometry tied to vessel orientation might explain the lower opening without uncontrolled flooding. Such devices are mechanically conceivable, but Ether 2:20 does not describe one and no barge-specific artifact or test is supplied. This is a rescue possibility, not established exegesis.

- **Conventional ancient craft described in loose English — possible.** Under [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]], “barge,” “window,” “top,” and “bottom” might be approximate English labels rather than source-transparent technical terms. That could accommodate an unfamiliar but ordinary vessel. The model becomes weak if lexical flexibility is invoked without constraints whenever a difficulty appears, and the explicit tightness and under-water language still require explanation.

- **Natural construction plus limited miracles — plausible within the narrative, not independently engineering-supported.** Human builders supply the hulls and provisions; divine action supplies light, unusually directed wind, protection, and resurfacing. This most closely follows the story's own allocation of agency (Ether 2:16-25; Ether 3:4-6; Ether 6:5-10). It can be religiously coherent, but the miraculous components prevent a purely natural test from validating the whole event.

- **Unrestricted miraculous preservation — possible within theism, empirically non-discriminating.** Omnipotent intervention could preserve any vessel and occupants. This answers possibility at the theological level but makes hull design largely irrelevant and cannot function as independent evidence that the voyage happened. It is ad hoc if introduced only to cover every physical difficulty without separate grounds for believing the revelation.

- **Fully natural literal reconstruction — possible in the abstract, presently unestablished.** A design might eventually satisfy buoyancy, stability, ventilation, structural, provisioning, and husbandry requirements. No dimensions or complete operating model in the supplied record allow that calculation. Confident declarations of either success or impossibility outrun the available inputs.

- **Ancient historical record rendered through KJV-shaped revelatory translation — plausible but only weakly independently supported at corpus level.** This framework accepts an ancient voyage while allowing biblical English, including the Noah comparison, to shape the received account. [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] says the whole registry gives only weak independent support to an ancient-source family and no overall historicity verdict. The barges add no calibrated independent archaeological line here.

- **Ancient core with later revelatory or editorial expansion — possible.** An older migration memory could have acquired the luminous-stone episode, Noahic phrasing, technical elaboration, or Christian interpretation during transmission or translation. No surviving source manuscript identifies those layers, so the proposal becomes ad hoc unless independent textual criteria predict them.

- **Nineteenth-century KJV-informed literary composition — plausible.** This model explains the Noahic comparison, miracle-centered problem-solution sequence, under-specified technology, and providential plot as features of final-form storytelling. It also fits the pervasive biblical/KJV mediation established in [[Book of Mormon Assessment]]. Yet no specific pre-1830 source for the complete barge narrative is established here, and literary intelligibility alone is not proof of invention.

- **Short inland-water voyage — set aside as poor fit.** It can reduce oceanic engineering burdens, but “the great sea which divideth the lands,” “this great deep,” severe prolonged conditions, and 344 days upon the water make a short inland crossing difficult to reconcile with the final text (Ether 2:13,25; Ether 6:5-11).

- **Glowing minerals or ordinary lamps as a complete explanation — set aside.** Ether explicitly excludes fire and attributes the stones' light to the Lord's touch (Ether 2:23; Ether 3:4-6). Natural luminous-material analogies may illuminate motifs, but they do not explain the narrated event without revising its causal claim.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** [[Ether 2]], [[Ether 3]], and [[Ether 6]] present a coherent miracle narrative in which eight specially prepared vessels transport Jared's company to a promised land over 344 days. The text expressly recognizes air, light, water, storms, food, and propulsion as problems. It does not require several exaggerated popular images, including powered submarines or continuous tumbling.

**Open:** Natural seaworthiness remains undetermined. Missing geometry, structure, load, ventilation, supplies, sanitation, route, and stability data prevent a responsible engineering verdict. Some components are physically conceivable, and experimental traditional-craft crossings defeat sweeping claims that ancient transoceanic travel is intrinsically impossible. None of that validates this specific fleet. Conversely, serious cumulative problems—especially life support and stability with animals over 344 days—justify skepticism but do not amount to a mathematical impossibility proof from the sparse text.

**A matter of faith:** The account expressly depends on miraculous light, divinely directed weather, protection, and resurfacing. Accepting those events depends on judgments about [[Revelation]], [[Miracles]], and the Book of Mormon's provenance, not naval architecture alone. [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] finds strong evidence for biblical/KJV mediation and deliberate final-form design, only weak independent ancient-provenance lines, and no overall verdict on historicity or inspiration. The barges should not be promoted as scientific proof of the Book of Mormon, nor should one disputed visualization be treated as a scientific disproof of the entire book.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with [[Ether 2]], [[Ether 3]], and [[Ether 6]], reading construction, luminous stones, loading, voyage conditions, duration, and arrival as one account. Compare [[Genesis 6]], [[Genesis 7]], and [[Genesis 8]] because Ether explicitly invokes Noah's ark, and compare [[1 Nephi 17]] and [[1 Nephi 18]] for a different Book of Mormon sea-crossing narrative.

For technical method, consult the International Maritime Organization's material on ship design and stability, especially watertight integrity, center of gravity, righting behavior, openings, cargo effects, and severe-weather performance. Experimental voyages such as Kon-Tiki are useful only as limited demonstrations that a tested low-technology design can cross an ocean; they do not test Ether's enclosed vessels, animal loads, submersion, or 344-day duration.

For modern devotional reception, read [[Spiritual Capacity (Michelle Craig, October 2019)]], [[Consistent and Resilient Trust (Elder L. Todd Budge, October 2019)]], and [[Holy Scriptures The Power of God unto Our Salvation (Elder Robert D. Hales, October 2006)]]. These illuminate religious use of the account, not naval feasibility.

Finally, consult [[Book of Mormon Assessment]], [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]], [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]], [[Book of Mormon Geography]], and [[How do faith and science fit together]] so that textual possibility, translation theory, historical corroboration, engineering, and faith remain distinct questions.
