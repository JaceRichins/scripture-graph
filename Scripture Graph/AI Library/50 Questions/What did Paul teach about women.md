# What did Paul teach about women?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Paul taught that women and men are equally included in Christ and inherit the promise together (Galatians 3:26-29); he addressed women as responsible disciples, gave wives and husbands reciprocal sexual obligations, assumed women prayed and prophesied, and commended women as coworkers, laborers, patrons or servants, teachers, and house-church participants (Romans 16:1-7; 1 Corinthians 7:2-5; 11:5; 16:19; Philippians 4:2-3; Titus 2:3-5). He also used headship and creation-order arguments, told wives to submit within marriage, commanded women to be silent in one Corinthian passage, and—according to 1 Timothy—restricted a woman from teaching or exercising authority over a man (1 Corinthians 11:3-12; 14:34-35; Ephesians 5:21-33; 1 Timothy 2:11-15). Exactly how the participatory and restrictive texts fit together is not settled: context, vocabulary, textual history, and disputed authorship permit several serious readings. Any honest answer must preserve both sides of the record rather than letting one slogan erase the other.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Equal covenantal inclusion:** [[Galatians 3]] says that male and female are one in Christ and belong to Abraham's promised family (Galatians 3:26-29). The direct claim is equal Christian identity and inheritance; an identical allocation of every role is a further inference.

- **Actual female participation:** [[1 Corinthians 11]] assumes women pray and prophesy (1 Corinthians 11:5). [[Romans 16]] commends Phebe, Priscilla, Junia, Mary, Tryphena, Tryphosa, Persis, and other women in Paul's network (Romans 16:1-7, 12-15). Philippians 4:2-3 says Euodias and Syntyche labored with Paul in the gospel. Acts 18:24-26 adds that Priscilla and Aquila instructed Apollos, though that report comes from Acts rather than Paul's own pen.

- **Reciprocity in marriage:** [[1 Corinthians 7]] gives husband and wife matching obligations and authority over one another's bodies (1 Corinthians 7:2-5). [[Ephesians 5]] begins its household discussion with mutual submission and commands husbands to love sacrificially (Ephesians 5:21, 25-33), even while retaining asymmetrical language for wives.

- **Real restrictions:** [[1 Corinthians 14]] tells women to keep silence and ask questions at home (1 Corinthians 14:34-35). [[1 Timothy 2]] prohibits a woman from teaching or exercising the authority expressed by authentein over a man and appeals to Adam and Eve (1 Timothy 2:11-15). These cannot responsibly be omitted from Paul's canonical reception.

- **A constraint on absolute readings:** The same 1 Corinthians that commands silence has already assumed female prayer and prophecy. That makes an unqualified prohibition of every female utterance difficult, but it does not identify the correct limitation automatically.

- **Authorship affects the historical answer:** Romans, 1 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, and Philemon are broadly accepted as Paul's; Ephesians and Colossians are disputed, and the Pastorals are widely judged post-Pauline in critical scholarship while Pauline authorship continues to be defended. Canonical authority and historical authorship are distinct questions.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objection to an egalitarian reconstruction is that the restrictive texts are not incidental. First Corinthians 11 invokes headship and creation; 1 Corinthians 14 issues an apparently categorical silence command; Ephesians and Colossians direct wives to submit; and 1 Timothy 2 connects its restriction to Adam's priority and Eve's deception rather than explicitly to a temporary local emergency. Galatians 3:28 concerns salvation and baptismal identity and does not itself abolish differentiated functions. Calling Phebe a diakonos need not mean she held a later formal office; Junia may have been well known to the apostles rather than counted among them; and coworker language does not prove ordination.

The strongest objection to a universal restriction is equally substantial. Paul assumes women pray and prophesy in congregational material, praises women who labored with him in the gospel, addresses Phebe with consequential ministry and patronage language, and gives reciprocal marital obligations. An absolute-silence reading makes 1 Corinthians conflict internally. Applying every ancient household asymmetry unchanged can also confuse first-century social accommodation with an eternal command. First Timothy's authorship, the meaning of authentein, and the situation at Ephesus are disputed.

Authorship arguments can be abused in either direction. Dismissing every inconvenient passage as non-Pauline is circular; treating a canonical attribution as though historical authorship had never been questioned suppresses genuine linguistic, ecclesial, and historical evidence. Modern readers on both sides may also import present-day concepts—egalitarian office, complementarian 'roles,' or modern priesthood categories—that the ancient texts do not name.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
A thoughtful restrictive reading can acknowledge women's prophecy, teaching of other women, patronage, missionary labor, and substantial leadership while distinguishing these activities from authoritative instruction or oversight of the gathered church. It can point to 1 Timothy's creation appeal as evidence that at least that author did not understand the restriction as merely a concession to Greco-Roman custom. This response is textually serious, but it must define the restricted authority without retrojecting later offices and must explain 1 Corinthians 11 alongside 1 Corinthians 14.

A thoughtful egalitarian reading can argue that the silence command concerns a specific disorderly kind of speech, that 1 Timothy addresses false teaching in Ephesus, and that authentein may denote improper or domineering authority rather than ordinary teaching leadership. It can also treat the women of Romans 16 as evidence of Paul's enacted ministry rather than an abstract exception. This reading has real contextual support, but the link between particular Ephesian women and false teaching is inferential, and the creation argument remains difficult.

Latter-day Saint readers can affirm equal spiritual worth and extensive female teaching, revelation, leadership, and administration, consistent with [[Women’s Service and Leadership in the Church (Gospel Topics)]], [[A Plea to My Sisters (President Russell M. Nelson, October 2015)]], and [[“Daughters in My Kingdom” The History and Work of Relief Society (Julie B. Beck, October 2010)]]. Current Church practice, however, is an institutional and revelatory claim; Paul's vocabulary alone neither maps the modern priesthood structure nor fully answers [[Why do women not hold the priesthood]].
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** Galatians presents women and men as equally included in Christ and Abraham’s promised family. First Corinthians gives spouses reciprocal obligations, assumes women pray and prophesy, uses headship and creation language, and later commands women to keep silence. Romans names consequential female collaborators. The wider canonical Pauline collection adds household submission, a restriction concerning teaching and authority in 1 Timothy, and a positive teaching assignment for older women in Titus. The texts do not themselves map these activities onto modern offices or supply one explicit reconciliation.

- **Differentiated but substantial ministry — plausible.** Women pray, prophesy, teach women, labor in the gospel, assist missions, and participate in house churches, while some authoritative teaching or congregational functions remain restricted. This fits much of the canonical evidence, but the texts never define one stable boundary called a “role,” and prophecy’s relation to authority remains contested.

- **Situational ordered-speech restriction — plausible.** Because 1 Corinthians 14 repeatedly regulates when tongue-speakers and prophets must keep silence, verses 34-35 may prohibit a narrower kind of questioning, interruption, or prophetic evaluation rather than every utterance. The chapter’s order theme supports this model; the prohibited activity is not explicitly identified.

- **Context-limited Ephesian restriction — plausible but partly inferential.** First Timothy repeatedly opposes false teaching, so 2:11-15 may address unlearned or disruptive teaching in that setting. The chapter does not say that the women under discussion were the false teachers, and its appeal to Adam and Eve remains a substantial difficulty.

- **Honor, shame, and social-respectability accommodation — possible and externally informed.** Instructions about coverings, household order, quietness, and public reputation may adapt Christian conduct to first-century Greco-Roman expectations so that communities avoid scandal. Ancient social context supports considering this model, but context alone cannot prove that the commands were temporary or exhaust their theological rationale.

- **Broad transcultural restriction — plausible.** First Timothy’s creation appeal can ground a continuing restriction on women authoritatively teaching men, with 1 Corinthians 14 as corroboration. This model is stronger when it permits prayer, prophecy, and teaching women, since 1 Corinthians 11 and Titus 2 rule out absolute female silence. Mapping the boundary onto a particular modern office is additional interpretation.

- **Egalitarian trajectory — plausible but not conclusive.** Galatians 3:28, reciprocal marital duties, women’s prophecy, and Paul’s coworkers may disclose principles whose implications exceed surviving social accommodations. These are substantial observations in widely accepted Pauline letters, but “trajectory” is interpretation and Galatians does not expressly revoke every differentiated arrangement.

- **Development within Paul or Pauline schools — plausible and independently supported by disputed authorship research.** Differences between Romans and 1 Corinthians on one hand and the Pastorals or household codes on the other may reflect changing circumstances, later organization, secretarial composition, or post-Pauline development. Authorship studies provide external evidence, but no authorship conclusion commands universal agreement, and restrictive material already appears in 1 Corinthians.

- **Quotation and refutation in 1 Corinthians 14 — possible minority model.** Verses 34-35 may quote a Corinthian position challenged in verse 36. This can reconcile chapters 11 and 14, but manuscripts do not mark the words as a quotation and the rhetoric remains disputed.

- **Interpolation in 1 Corinthians 14 — possible and disputed.** Placement after verse 40 in some Western witnesses demonstrates textual displacement and gives the theory external support. Surviving witnesses retain the verses somewhere, so transposition does not establish that a later writer composed them.

- **Lexical and relational narrowing — possible but insufficient alone.** Authentein may denote improper or domineering conduct; “woman/man” may in some contexts be read as “wife/husband”; diakonos and prostatis may imply substantial responsibility; and Junia may be notable among or well known to apostles. Each question deserves lexical study, but disputed words cannot bear the whole conclusion.

- **Canonical-authority model — independently supported as a confessional approach, not a historical reconstruction.** A reader may regard all canonically Pauline books as authoritative while remaining uncertain who composed their final wording. This distinguishes canonical reception from the historical question of what Paul personally wrote.

- **Current Latter-day Saint synthesis — institutionally supported as present practice.** Women and men are equal covenant participants, and women teach, lead, pray, testify, and administer in many settings while priesthood ordination is currently restricted. [[Women’s Service and Leadership in the Church (Gospel Topics)]] documents that arrangement. Paul’s vocabulary alone neither proves nor uniquely generates it.

**Considered and set aside.** An absolute ban on all female speech is incompatible with the canonical evidence of prayer, prophecy, and teaching women. Complete interchangeability of every function goes beyond Galatians 3 and leaves restrictive texts unexplained. Treating every restriction as merely pagan culture is too general to account for creation appeals; treating every difference as an unchanged rule for every era overlooks Paul’s occasion-specific counsel. Claims that Phoebe or Junia held an office identical to a later institution, or that they held no meaningful authority, both exceed the available evidence.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** Paul taught equal inclusion of women and men in Christ; addressed women as morally responsible disciples; gave wives and husbands genuinely reciprocal obligations; assumed female prayer and prophecy; and worked alongside numerous women. The canonical Pauline collection also contains genuine asymmetry and restrictions concerning headship, household submission, congregational speech, and teaching authority.

**Open:** the scope of the Corinthian silence, the force of authentein, Junia's exact relation to the apostles, Phebe's precise ecclesial status, and the authorship and historical setting of the disputed letters. No single harmonization commands consensus. The evidence makes both 'Paul simply silenced women' and 'Paul erased every functional distinction' inadequate summaries.

**A matter of faith and ecclesial judgment:** whether all canonically Pauline instructions are equally direct revelation for every age, how prophetic authority may adapt their application, and how they relate to present Latter-day Saint priesthood organization. Current Church teaching strongly affirms women's leadership and spiritual gifts, but the historical exegesis should not be made artificially certain to defend either current practice or a preferred reform.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

Keener, *Paul, Women & Wives*; Eldon Jay Epp, *Junia: The First Woman Apostle*; Gordon D. Fee's commentary on 1 Corinthians; and Andreas J. Köstenberger and Thomas R. Schreiner, eds., *Women in the Church*. These sources disagree and should be compared rather than cited as a manufactured consensus.
