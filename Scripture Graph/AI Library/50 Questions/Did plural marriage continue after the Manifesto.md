# Did plural marriage continue after the Manifesto?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Yes—but not as a single, unchanged practice. Existing plural families continued after Wilford Woodruff issued the Manifesto in 1890, because it did not order spouses to separate. The official historical account in [[The Manifesto and the End of Plural Marriage (Essay)]] also acknowledges that a limited number of new plural marriages occurred during the post-Manifesto transition. The 1904 Second Manifesto under [[Joseph F. Smith]] made entering a new plural marriage grounds for excommunication and supplied a much clearer institutional boundary. Thus 1890 was the decisive beginning of cessation, not the date on which every plural household and every new plural marriage instantly disappeared.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The Manifesto's actual scope:** Official Declaration 1 announces submission to anti-polygamy laws and advises against marriages forbidden by law. It does not direct existing families to dissolve.

- **Official acknowledgment of later marriages:** [[The Manifesto and the End of Plural Marriage (Essay)]] acknowledges limited entry into new plural marriages after 1890. That makes an absolute claim that every new plural marriage stopped immediately untenable.

- **Surviving households:** [[Conference Report, April 1907 (General Conference (full report), April 1907)]] reported 2,451 plural families in 1890, 1,543 nine years later, and 897 four years after that. Whatever the limitations of those counts, they directly document gradual rather than instantaneous disappearance.

- **The Second Manifesto:** [[Plural Marriage in The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] identifies the April 1904 statement of [[Joseph F. Smith]] as making new plural marriage punishable by excommunication. Its issuance is strong evidence that the first Manifesto had not created uniform understanding or enforcement.

- **Contemporary interpretive dispute:** [[Conference Report, April 1911 (General Conference (full report), April 1911)]] acknowledged that some people read the first Manifesto as geographically limited to the United States, even while the report denied that the Church had authorized illegal marriages.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **A blanket “yes” can mislead.** Continued support of pre-1890 wives and children is not the same act as contracting a new plural marriage. Nor is an unauthorized ceremony by an individual necessarily an act of “the Church.” Any answer that collapses these categories overstates the case.

- **Contemporary institutional denials must be taken seriously.** [[Conference Report, April 1911 (General Conference (full report), April 1911)]] asserted that the Church had not performed plural marriages or authorized violations after the Manifesto, and [[Conference Report, October 1918 (General Conference (full report), October 1918)]] described the practice as positively abandoned. These statements complicate claims of a simple, centrally directed continuation.

- **The critical objection is stronger than mere administrative confusion.** Public assurances of cessation existed alongside later-acknowledged marriages. Critics can reasonably argue that geographic distinctions, informal approval, or narrow uses of the term “the Church” allowed leaders to communicate one impression publicly while tolerating something different privately.

- **The believing account also faces a moral question.** Calling the transition difficult does not itself justify secrecy, inconsistent instructions, unequal consequences, or statements that listeners could reasonably understand as broader than the practice actually followed.

- **Records do not answer every authorization question.** Surviving evidence may establish a marriage without conclusively establishing who approved it, what an approving leader believed his authority to be, or whether the First Presidency acted collectively.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- A careful believing response concedes the central history: cessation was gradual, some post-1890 marriages occurred, and public statements did not always describe the transition with sufficient precision. Faith does not require defending an instantaneous-cessation account contradicted by [[The Manifesto and the End of Plural Marriage (Essay)]].

- Existing families presented obligations that differed from authorization of new unions. Requiring immediate abandonment could have imposed severe costs on wives and children. The Manifesto's failure to command dissolution therefore helps explain continued plural households without explaining every later ceremony.

- Different legal jurisdictions and differing interpretations of “the law of the land” explain some contemporary conduct. [[Conference Report, April 1911 (General Conference (full report), April 1911)]] confirms that this geographic reading actually existed. Explanation, however, is not the same as full moral vindication.

- The 1904 Second Manifesto can be understood as an explicit disciplinary clarification after an inadequately uniform transition. [[Plural Marriage in The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] supports this institutional account, though it does not prove that every earlier ambiguity was innocent.

- Believers may accept the account in [[The Life and Ministry of Wilford Woodruff]] that the cessation was divinely directed while also holding that leaders and members implemented that direction imperfectly. The historical evidence can support the implementation claim; the divine source of the direction remains a matter of faith.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require.** Official Declaration 1 requires recognition of Woodruff's public intention to obey the law and his advice not to contract marriages forbidden by law. It does not itself require dissolution of existing families, explain foreign jurisdictions, catalogue private authorizations, or specify disciplinary consequences. D&C 132:7,61-63 shows that plural marriage and authorization were joined in the earlier canonical framework; its continued canonicity does not independently answer who possessed permission after 1890. The historical question therefore cannot be settled merely by observing that D&C 132 remained canon or that the Manifesto existed.

- **Independently supported — phased institutional cessation.** The public turn began in 1890; plural households declined rather than disappearing; limited new marriages continued; and the 1904 Second Manifesto imposed a clearer worldwide disciplinary rule. This model best accommodates [[The Manifesto and the End of Plural Marriage (Essay)]], [[Conference Report, April 1907 (General Conference (full report), April 1907)]], and [[Plural Marriage in The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Essay)]].

- **Possible but limited — legal or geographic cessation.** Some participants understood the Manifesto to prohibit marriages only where civil law prohibited them, especially within the United States. [[Conference Report, April 1911 (General Conference (full report), April 1911)]] independently attests that the interpretation existed. It cannot explain every disputed case, and later Church policy expressly rejected geographic exceptions.

- **Independently supported in some cases — decentralized or unauthorized continuation.** Some people could continue because they believed earlier authority remained operative or because local officers acted without full institutional approval. This helps reconcile later Church denials with some marriages, but it cannot be assumed for every case without marriage-specific evidence.

- **Plausible critical model — managed ambiguity or equivocation.** Leaders publicly pursued legal survival while some marriages continued through narrow jurisdictional readings or informal channels. The tension between the Manifesto, acknowledged post-1890 marriages, and categorical later denials gives this model real evidentiary footing. A coordinated policy of deception, however, requires more case-level evidence than the supplied sources establish.

- **Plausible within faith but not independently established — revelation followed by imperfect implementation.** [[The Life and Ministry of Wilford Woodruff]] presents cessation as revealed; [[The Messenger and the Manifesto]] supplies contemporary context for the decision. Human confusion and resistance are compatible with that model, but the historical facts do not independently demonstrate the revelation's divine origin.

- **Ad hoc as a claim to present Church authority — secret continuing authorization.** D&C 132's presence in the canon can be invoked to claim that plural marriage secretly remains authorized. That inference conflicts with D&C 132:7's emphasis on appointed authority and with the explicit post-1904 institutional denial recorded in [[Plural Marriage in The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Essay)]]. It remains a theological claim of independent groups, not evidence of present authorization by The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints.

- **Considered and set aside — instantaneous universal cessation in 1890.** This model cannot account for the documented survival of plural families, official acknowledgment of limited new marriages, or the need for a Second Manifesto. It can describe the desired public direction only if it is not mistaken for a complete description of practice.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** Existing plural families continued after 1890, a limited number of new plural marriages also occurred during the transition, and the Church established a more categorical disciplinary prohibition in 1904. The record therefore does not support saying that plural marriage ended everywhere and in every sense on the day of the first Manifesto.

**Open or case-dependent:** The precise number of post-1890 ceremonies, the authorization chain for each one, how consistently senior leaders interpreted the Manifesto, and whether particular public denials reflected narrow institutional terminology, incomplete knowledge, or intentional equivocation require case-level primary sources. [[Conference Report, April 1911 (General Conference (full report), April 1911)]] and the later official essay should be weighed together rather than using either to erase the other.

**Matter of faith:** That the original practice and its cessation were commanded by God is an official religious interpretation represented by [[Plural Marriage in Kirtland and Nauvoo (Essay)]] and [[The Life and Ministry of Wilford Woodruff]]. Historical documentation can test actions, dates, and statements, but it cannot by itself prove divine authorization.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with Official Declaration 1 and distinguish its operative declaration from the explanatory materials printed with it. Then read [[The Manifesto and the End of Plural Marriage (Essay)]] alongside [[The Messenger and the Manifesto]] and [[The Life and Ministry of Wilford Woodruff]]. For the households inherited from the pre-1890 period, consult [[Plural Marriage and Families in Early Utah (Essay)]]. For changing public descriptions and demographic claims, compare [[Conference Report, April 1907 (General Conference (full report), April 1907)]], [[Conference Report, April 1911 (General Conference (full report), April 1911)]], and [[Conference Report, October 1918 (General Conference (full report), October 1918)]]. Read D&C 132:7,61-63 with [[Eternal Marriage]] to distinguish canonical plural-marriage provisions from later institutional authorization. For the 1904 disciplinary boundary and subsequent official position, use [[Plural Marriage in The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints (Essay)]].
