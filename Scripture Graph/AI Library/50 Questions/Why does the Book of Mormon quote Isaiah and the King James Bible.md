# Why does the Book of Mormon quote Isaiah and the King James Bible?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Book of Mormon itself explains why its speakers use Isaiah: Nephi says he read Isaiah from the brass plates to persuade his family to believe in the Redeemer and to apply earlier scripture to themselves (1 Nephi 19:22-24); he later treats Isaiah as a witness of Christ and interprets the prophet in relation to Israel's judgment and restoration ([[2 Nephi 25]]). Abinadi and the risen Jesus likewise use Isaiah within explicit teaching settings ([[Mosiah 14]]; 3 Nephi 23:1-3). That answers the narrative and theological question, but not why the received English closely follows the King James Version. The sustained Isaiah blocks and the Matthean wording of [[3 Nephi 12]]–[[3 Nephi 14]] strongly establish pervasive KJV-shaped mediation. They do not, by themselves, distinguish direct nineteenth-century adaptation from an ancient source rendered or harmonized through biblical English, revelatory restatement, or a layered ancient-and-modern process. [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] therefore treats mediation as established and its mechanism as unresolved.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The dependence is extended, ordered, and verbal.** The sequence from [[2 Nephi 12]] through [[2 Nephi 24]] follows Isaiah 2–14, while [[Mosiah 14]] closely corresponds to [[Isaiah 53]]. This is much stronger evidence of textual dependence than a shared religious phrase. [[Mosiah 14 quotes Isaiah 53]] reaches the same narrow conclusion without pretending that dependence identifies the mechanism.

- **The pattern is broader than Isaiah.** [[3 Nephi 12]]–[[3 Nephi 14]] follows much of Matthew 5–7 in sequence and KJV-shaped English, and the registry separately finds strong biblical-English mediation elsewhere in the corpus. Together these establish a corpus-level pattern, weighed as a single line in [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] rather than as independent votes.

- **The quotations do real literary work.** Nephi identifies Isaiah as a witness of the Redeemer (2 Nephi 11), acknowledges that Isaiah is difficult for his audience, and supplies an interpretation ([[2 Nephi 25]]). Abinadi moves from the priests' Isaiah question through [[Mosiah 14]] into a christological exposition in Mosiah 15. This strongly supports deliberate final-form design, though final-form design is origin-neutral — it does not by itself argue for an ancient source.

- **The text supplies in-world reasons for quotation.** Nephi says he read Isaiah from the brass plates to promote belief and likened scripture to his people ([[1 Nephi 19]]); the risen Jesus commands his audience to search Isaiah (3 Nephi 23). These explain why the narrative values Isaiah; they do not identify how Isaiah's English wording came to match the King James Version so closely.

- **The variants matter, but only case by case.** Royal Skousen's critical text work allows individual readings to be checked against manuscripts and editions. A raw count of differences from the KJV, or a few claimed agreements with the Dead Sea Scrolls or Septuagint, is not by itself diagnostic — each candidate variant needs directionality, textual support, and comparison against ordinary editing before it counts as evidence either way.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **Direct modern use predicts the English naturally.** An author or revelatory producer working in the nineteenth century had access to the KJV, and extensive KJV sequence, diction, supplied words, and translation-specific readings are straightforward under direct adaptation. A hypothetical source-transparent translation independent of the KJV predicts these features poorly. See [[Why does the Book of Mormon reproduce King James italics]].

- **The later-Isaiah problem is substantial.** Mainstream historical-critical scholarship places Isaiah 40–55 principally in the Babylonian-exilic setting. Yet material corresponding to Isaiah 48–49 appears in 1 Nephi 20–21 and Isaiah 53 in [[Mosiah 14]], while the narrative places Lehi's departure in Zedekiah's reign. This objection rests on historical setting, vocabulary, literary relationships, and the addressed audience—not merely on an assumption that predictive prophecy is impossible. Joseph Blenkinsopp's *Isaiah 40–55* represents the critical setting; David P. Wright's essay in *American Apocrypha* presses its Book of Mormon implications.

- **The Matthean sermon compounds the problem.** The close sequence and English phrasing shared by Matthew 5–7 and [[3 Nephi 12]]–[[3 Nephi 14]] are naturally explained by access to Matthew's received English text. The Book of Mormon's post-resurrection setting explains why Jesus teaches the Nephites, but it does not independently explain why the English tracks Matthew so closely. See [[Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew]].

- **Purposeful adaptation is not evidence of antiquity.** Nephi's editorial explanations and Abinadi's exposition show literary intelligence, but a nineteenth-century composition can construct narrators, sources, and interpretive frames. Internal source claims cannot serve as independent external authentication.

- **A Bible need not have been visibly open during dictation.** Even if retrospective witnesses denied that Joseph read from a book, KJV-shaped language could enter through memory, earlier preparation, intermittent consultation, scribal harmonization, or the proposed revelatory process. The textual pattern and the physical production scene are related but separate evidentiary questions.

- **Variants do not automatically rescue antiquity.** Many deviations from the KJV are nonspecific: they can arise from contextual revision, grammatical editing, dictation, scribal transmission, or printing. Claims of agreement with the Dead Sea Scrolls or Septuagint must be demonstrated individually and compared against the rate of coincidental or editorial agreement.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believers generally answer at the level of translation rather than denying the KJV-shaped English. One model holds that an ancient record was rendered in a scriptural register familiar to an 1829 audience, much as translators often reuse received biblical diction; [[Translation of the Book of Mormon]] and [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]] are therefore more relevant than a source-transparent model the text does not explicitly require. This response is plausible, but compatibility is not positive evidence that such harmonization occurred, and it needs criteria for predicting where KJV wording should and should not appear.

On Isaiah's date, believers may defend substantial pre-exilic authorship, allow genuine predictive prophecy, posit earlier materials incorporated into later Isaiah, or place some familiar wording at the modern revelatory stage. The first two positions must still engage the critical arguments from historical audience, vocabulary, and literary development; simply accusing critical scholars of disbelief in prophecy is inadequate. Translation-stage insertion reduces the brass-plates chronology problem but yields a more layered account than a straightforward quotation of a pre-600 BC document.

Believers can also point to the quotations' interpretive integration. Nephi connects Isaiah with the Messiah and Israel's covenants ([[2 Nephi 25]]); Abinadi builds an argument around [[Mosiah 14]]; and Jesus makes Isaiah relevant to the covenant future (3 Nephi 23:1-3). Grant Hardy and Joseph Spencer show that Isaiah is structurally important to the book. This rebuts the claim that the biblical material is merely random padding, but it does not distinguish ancient exegesis, revelatory composition, and skilled modern adaptation.

Finally, witness accounts and dictation history deserve assessment rather than dismissal, but they should not be made to prove more than they report. Absence of a visibly consulted Bible would challenge one narrowly specified production scenario, not every naturalistic mechanism; conversely, KJV dependence does not prove deception or exclude inspiration.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The received English Book of Mormon contains extensive, deliberate biblical intertextuality and is pervasively mediated through biblical, often specifically KJV-shaped, language. Its Isaiah material is not merely decorative: Nephi, Abinadi, and Jesus give it theological and covenantal functions. The extended correspondences strongly challenge a source-transparent English translation substantially independent of the KJV.

**Open:** The evidence summarized here does not determine whether KJV wording entered through direct nineteenth-century composition, conscious or remembered source use, revelatory translation, editorial harmonization, an ancient core with modern expansion, or more than one mechanism. Critical dating of Isaiah 40–55 is a serious pressure on a simple pre-600 BC brass-plates model, but layered and translation-stage models remain possible at differing costs. Variants must be judged individually; neither their mere existence nor their total count settles provenance.

**Matter of faith:** Inspiration and spiritual authority are not synonyms for ancient provenance or source-transparent translation. A believer may responsibly affirm an ancient record delivered through KJV-shaped revelation while acknowledging that the textual evidence does not establish that mechanism. A skeptic may responsibly regard nineteenth-century KJV-informed production as the most economical ordinary explanation of the assessed English evidence while acknowledging that the present registry does not prove fraud, identify every production step, or decide spiritual value. The broader, deliberately limited judgment belongs in the required [[Book of Mormon Assessment]].
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with the primary comparisons: [[1 Nephi 19]] and [[2 Nephi 25]] for Nephi's stated purpose; [[2 Nephi 12]] through [[2 Nephi 24]] beside Isaiah 2–14; 1 Nephi 20–21 beside Isaiah 48–49; [[Mosiah 14]] beside [[Isaiah 53]] and then Mosiah 15; [[3 Nephi 12]]–[[3 Nephi 14]] beside Matthew 5–7; and [[3 Nephi 15]] for the discourse's narrative setting. Follow the focused questions [[Why does the Book of Mormon reproduce King James italics]], [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Malachi before Malachi wrote]], and [[Does the Sermon at the Temple depend on Matthew]].

For textual history, consult Royal Skousen, *The Book of Mormon: The Earliest Text* and *Analysis of Textual Variants of the Book of Mormon*. For literary function, consult Grant Hardy, *Understanding the Book of Mormon*, and Joseph M. Spencer, *The Vision of All: Twenty-five Lectures on Isaiah in Nephi's Record*. For a critical provenance argument, read David P. Wright, “Isaiah in the Book of Mormon: Or Joseph Smith in Isaiah,” in *American Apocrypha*. For the later-Isaiah framework, compare a modern critical commentary such as Joseph Blenkinsopp's *Isaiah 40–55*. Nicholas J. Frederick's *The Bible, Mormon Scripture, and the Rhetoric of Allusivity* broadens the inquiry beyond explicit quotation.

Finally, read [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]], [[Translation of the Book of Mormon]], [[How reliable is the biblical text]], and the required corpus synthesis [[Book of Mormon Assessment]]. These prevent a local Isaiah observation from being inflated into an unsupported verdict about the whole book.

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text itself requires.** The final text presents Nephi as possessing scriptural records, reading Isaiah, selecting material, and applying it to his audience (1 Nephi 19:22-24; 2 Nephi 11:2-8; [[2 Nephi 25]]). It presents Abinadi and Jesus as quoting or teaching scriptural material ([[Mosiah 14]]; [[3 Nephi 12]]–[[3 Nephi 14]]; 3 Nephi 23:1-3). It therefore requires Isaiah and related discourse to have purposeful narrative functions. It does **not** describe whether matching English was translated independently, supplied by revelation, drawn from memory or a Bible, or harmonized during editing. Both the objection that the KJV wording must have been on ancient plates and the apologetic assumption that every difference must preserve an ancient variant add mechanisms not stated by the text.

- **Final-form literary work with pervasive biblical/KJV mediation — independently supported.** Extended blocks, shared order, distinctive English phrasing, callbacks, and integrated exposition establish this origin-neutral baseline. [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] judges the line strong. Accidental resemblance is not a serious explanation for the extended passages.

- **Ancient historical record rendered through KJV-shaped loose or revelatory translation — plausible, weakly independently supported.** This fits the book's narrated ancient records and can accommodate familiar English as translation language. The independent ancient-provenance lines currently weighed in [[Book of Mormon Assessment]]—Nahom, if-and syntax, and Alma 36 chiasmus—offer only weak cumulative support. Believing scholarship debates this under a 'tight control' model (the English, including KJV translators' own supplied wording, was given essentially word-for-word) versus a 'loose control' model (Joseph Smith received the sense and supplied his own KJV-influenced English); these make different predictions about where BofM English should track the KJV, and the registry has not tested between them. The model remains underconstrained unless it supplies prior criteria for when translation follows the KJV, revises it, or introduces later biblical material.

- **Ancient core with substantial translation-level, revelatory, or modern expansion — possible.** This can accommodate ancient narrative material alongside later Christian and KJV formulations, including passages difficult to place on pre-exilic plates. Layered transmission is historically possible in principle, but no surviving Book of Mormon source-language manuscript independently identifies the layers. The model becomes **ad hoc** when expansion is invoked only wherever a difficulty occurs, without linguistic or textual criteria that can be tested elsewhere.

- **Ancient record with ancient editorial layers — possible as one stage, not a complete solution.** Nephi, Mormon, and Moroni are portrayed as record keepers and abridgers; see [[Records and Sacred Writings]]. Those self-presentations establish the final narrative framework, not the historical existence of the editors. Ancient redaction also does not explain KJV-shaped English without a separate translation or harmonization model.

- **Revelatory reuse or modernization without a source-transparent quotation process — possible as theology; not independently established by textual resemblance.** God could, within a believing framework, reveal familiar biblical wording and adapt it to a new audience. This model is consistent with [[Mosiah 14 quotes Isaiah 53]] and the temple sermon. Because revelation can accommodate many textual outcomes, it gains historical discrimination only if it makes risky predictions that differ from ordinary modern reuse.

- **Nineteenth-century KJV-informed composition or adaptation — plausible and economical for the assessed English evidence.** Contemporary access to the KJV, pervasive biblical diction, extended sequence agreements, and one final text's control over quotation and interpretation fit this family directly. It does not follow that a particular nonbiblical source book has been identified, that every feature was consciously copied, or that inspiration and spiritual value are excluded. See [[Did Joseph Smith copy the Book of Mormon from other books]].

- **Mixed nineteenth-century mechanisms — memory, consultation, oral recomposition, and later editing — plausible.** A physical Bible continuously on the table is only one direct-use scenario. Oral memory or intermittent consultation may explain looser passages, while close extended blocks may demand stronger source access or revelatory reproduction. Multiple mechanisms are legitimate only if production evidence or textual patterns distinguish them; otherwise the model risks becoming ad hoc.

- **Source-transparent ancient translation substantially independent of the KJV — considered and strongly disfavored.** It does not predict sustained KJV order and translation-level wording well. It can explain the presence of Isaiah content but not the received English pattern without adding harmonization, at which point it collapses into an earlier model above.

- **Variants as proof of an ancient non-KJV Isaiah text — considered but not established.** Some variants may eventually be diagnostic, but many alternatives generate change. The claim should be accepted only for readings supported by early Book of Mormon witnesses, securely ancient biblical witnesses, demonstrated directionality, and matched controls.

- **Obvious borrowing as evidence against forgery — considered and set aside as weak psychology.** A modern producer might conceal dependence, display scriptural mastery, or regard reuse as legitimate revelation. Openness does not reliably discriminate sincerity, fraud, inspiration, or literary strategy.

- **One mechanism for every biblical passage — not required.** Isaiah on the brass plates, Abinadi's exposition, Jesus' sermon, and shorter echoes occupy different narrative settings. A unified mechanism is simpler, but the evidence should test rather than assume uniformity.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
