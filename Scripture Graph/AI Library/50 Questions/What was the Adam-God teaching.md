# What was the Adam-God teaching?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Adam-God teaching was a cluster of ideas publicly associated with [[Brigham Young]] beginning in 1852. The surviving records indicate that Young taught that [[Adam]]—whom Latter-day Saint scripture identifies with [[Michael (archangel)]]—had a celestial or exalted condition before Eden, participated in organizing the earth, came with [[Eve]] to establish the mortal human family, and stood as humanity's immediate Father and God; related statements connected Adam with spirit parentage and the mortal begetting of Jesus. The sources do not form one perfectly systematic creed, but independent contemporary notes and later reaffirmations make the central teaching difficult to dismiss as a single reporting error. It was disputed during Young's lifetime, does not appear in this developed form in canon, and was later publicly rejected. The Church today retains Adam's identity as Michael and his exceptional patriarchal authority while distinguishing him from Heavenly Father and Jesus Christ.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Explicit canonical identity:** D&C 27:11 and D&C 107:54 directly identify [[Adam]] with [[Michael (archangel)]]. They give Adam elevated titles but do not state the developed Adam-God teaching.

- **Multiple historical attestations:** The published report of [[Brigham Young]]'s April 9, 1852 discourse and Wilford Woodruff's contemporary journal independently attest the central teaching. Young's later public reaffirmation shows that the idea was not merely an isolated phrase. This is strong evidence that Young taught it, not evidence that it was metaphysically true.

- **Contemporary disagreement:** Records of the conflict between Young and [[Orson Pratt]] show that the teaching was disputed within senior leadership. It was therefore neither a later hostile invention nor a unanimously received doctrine.

- **Canonical relational pattern:** [[Moses 5]], [[Moses 6]], and D&C 78 portray Adam as commanded, taught, redeemed, and entrusted with authority under distinct divine beings. This strongly challenges the developed teaching as a natural reading of the canon, while not logically excluding every hierarchical use of the title "God."

- **Documented institutional discontinuity:** Young publicly advanced the teaching, whereas later Church leadership rejected Adam-God and current instructional materials distinguish Heavenly Father, Jesus Christ, and Adam-Michael. This establishes a real change in authoritative teaching, although whether it is best described as correction, clarification, or reversal is interpretive.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
### Objections to minimizing the historical teaching

- [[Brigham Young]] did not confine the idea to private speculation. The central propositions appear in a reported public discourse, are independently reflected in Wilford Woodruff's contemporary notes, and recur in later material. Treating the whole record as one stenographic mistake is therefore weak.
- Lack of canonization does not prove that nineteenth-century hearers regarded forceful presidential preaching as unimportant or unofficial. A canon-only authority test can impose a later boundary on an earlier setting.
- A purely symbolic headship reading does not naturally explain the entire documentary complex, especially claims about pre-Eden celestial status, human parentage, and Jesus's mortal begetting.

### Objections to treating it as uniform Church doctrine

- The records do not form a single perfectly consistent creed, and Young's use of terms such as Father, God, Elohim, Jehovah, and Michael varied.
- [[Orson Pratt]] and others resisted elements of the teaching. Its historical presence therefore does not establish unanimous acceptance or formal ratification by the Church as a whole.
- The developed propositions do not appear in canon, whose ordinary narrative distinguishes Adam from the beings who create, command, and redeem him.

### Objections to a simple believing reconciliation

A later Church president's rejection of a teaching publicly advanced by an earlier president creates a genuine problem for any model of comprehensive prophetic infallibility. Calling the episode continuing revelation may accommodate the change theologically, but it does not erase the historical discontinuity or provide an automatic contemporaneous method for knowing which confident teachings will later be rejected.

### Objections to present-day fundamentalist acceptance

Accepting Young and rejecting later Church correction preserves Young's authority but conflicts with current institutional teaching and still must reconcile the theory with canonical passages distinguishing Adam, the Father, and Christ. Evidence that a belief was sincerely taught is not independent evidence that the belief is true.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
A historically responsible believing response need not deny the documents. One model distinguishes prophetic calling from comprehensive personal infallibility: [[Brigham Young]] could have exercised genuine prophetic leadership while being mistaken in an expansive theological reconstruction. This response fits the later institutional rejection, though it bears the real cost of acknowledging serious public prophetic error.

A second response distinguishes the accepted canonical core from the rejected extension. Scripture identifies [[Adam]] with [[Michael (archangel)]], makes him the first patriarch, gives him priesthood keys, and assigns him an important role in [[Creation]]. None of those teachings requires identifying him as humanity's Heavenly Father or accepting the additional parentage claims.

A limited semantic response is also possible. Terms such as father and god can sometimes describe patriarchal headship or delegated authority beneath higher Gods. Adam's canonical titles give this interpretation some basis for isolated statements, but it does not naturally explain the whole historical record and should not be presented as a complete solution.

Believers may finally understand the later rejection as correction through continuing revelation. That is a coherent faith interpretation, but the historical documents alone cannot establish that God directed either the original teaching, the correction, or both.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
### What the texts require

The canon identifies the individual [[Adam]] with [[Michael (archangel)]], grants him exceptional patriarchal and priesthood authority, and places him within a hierarchy of delegated authority (D&C 27:11; D&C 78:16; D&C 107:53–57). It also presents Adam as formed, commanded, fallen, called to repentance, and taught redemption through the Only Begotten ([[Moses 5]]; [[Moses 6]]). It does not state that Adam is Heavenly Father, the father of human spirits, or the physical father of Jesus. Conversely, the canon does not require every use of the word god to denote the highest divine being; a delegated title is conceptually possible.

### Models permitted by the evidence

- **Literal historical reconstruction — independently supported as a reconstruction of Young's belief.** [[Brigham Young]] appears to have understood Adam as a previously celestial or exalted being who participated in organizing this world, entered Eden with [[Eve]], initiated the mortal human family, and stood as its immediate Father and God. Related records add spirit-parentage and Jesus-begetting claims. Multiple reports support that Young taught a substantive core; they do not establish that it was true.

- **Prophetic error followed by institutional correction — plausible and historically supported.** Young sincerely taught theological propositions later Church leadership rejected in favor of the canonical distinction among Heavenly Father, Jesus Christ, and Adam. The historical change is strongly supported; why God permitted it is a theological question rather than a historical finding.

- **Delegated-God or patriarchal-headship reading — possible and partly supported.** Adam's canonical titles as father, prince, ancient of days, and key-holder support a subordinate governing role. This may explain some language, but as a complete reconstruction it is weak because it does not naturally account for all the parentage and pre-Eden claims.

- **Reporter error or misunderstanding — possible for individual wording, ad hoc for the whole record.** Reported sermons require source criticism and particular phrases may be inaccurate. Independent contemporary notes and later recurrence make a corpus-wide reporting-error explanation implausible.

- **Doctrinal development rather than a single fixed system — plausible.** Young may have maintained a stable core while changing or never resolving parts of the surrounding divine genealogy. This better fits the evidence than either treating every record as a perfectly harmonized creed or dismissing the teaching as casual exploration.

- **Progressive revelation or a withdrawn mystery — possible but weak.** This model treats Young's teaching as additional truth later leaders were not meant to teach. It lacks independent canonical confirmation and fits poorly with later treatment of the theory as false rather than merely withdrawn.

- **Fundamentalist continuity and later apostasy — possible as a sectarian faith model, not independently supported.** It preserves Young's authority by rejecting later institutional authority. It conflicts directly with present Church teaching and retains canonical tensions.

- **Two-Adam or reusable-title model — logically possible but ad hoc.** It assigns difficult statements to a heavenly Adam distinct from the Edenic Michael. The principal historical records connect the disputed figure with Michael, Eden, Eve, and this human family, while no clear canonical two-Adam scheme supplies independent support.

### Considered and set aside

The claim that [[Joseph Smith Jr]] originated the developed teaching remains unestablished: Young's retrospective attribution is evidence of his memory or claim, not independent corroboration. Also set aside is the assertion that Adam-God was either uniformly binding Church doctrine or merely an invention of later critics. The evidence instead shows forceful presidential teaching, meaningful support, internal dissent, nonappearance in developed canonical form, and later repudiation.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** [[Brigham Young]] repeatedly taught a substantive Adam-God theology extending beyond the canonical identification of [[Adam]] with [[Michael (archangel)]]. Independent contemporary evidence supports the historical core. The teaching encountered senior-level dissent, never entered the canon in its developed form, and is rejected by the modern Church. Current instruction preserves Adam's elevated patriarchal and eschatological roles while distinguishing him from Heavenly Father and Jesus Christ.

**Open:** Young's terminology and the complete architecture of his divine genealogy were not perfectly stable. The exact relationship among all surviving sermons and the 1877 documentary material remains interpretive, as does the breadth of ordinary nineteenth-century acceptance. Young's attribution of the teaching to [[Joseph Smith Jr]] remains possible but independently uncorroborated.

**Matter of faith:** Historical method cannot determine whether Young's claim to revelation was genuine, whether later rejection was divinely directed correction, or what the episode ultimately implies about prophetic authority. A fallibilist believer, a critic describing doctrinal reversal, and a fundamentalist rejecting later authority interpret the same discontinuity differently; none of those theological conclusions follows mechanically from the historical record.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with the primary record rather than summaries:

- Brigham Young’s April 9, 1852 discourse, Journal of Discourses 1:46–53, especially 50–51.
- Wilford Woodruff’s journal entry for April 9, 1852, which independently summarizes the sermon.
- Young’s June 8, 1873 discourse, printed in the Deseret News on June 18, 1873, p. 308, col. 4, and in Journal of Discourses 16:46.
- L. John Nuttall’s journal for February 7, 1877, for the late “lecture at the veil”; treat it as documentary history and avoid projecting its contents onto current temple practice.
- Spencer W. Kimball, “Our Own Liahona,” October 1976, for the explicit modern repudiation.
- David John Buerger, “The Adam-God Doctrine,” Dialogue 15, no. 1 (Spring 1982), and Gary James Bergera, “The Orson Pratt-Brigham Young Controversies,” Dialogue 13, no. 2 (Summer 1980), for contrasting sources and institutional history.

For canonical comparison, read [[Moses 5]], [[Moses 6]], [[D&C 27]], [[D&C 78]], [[D&C 107]], and [[D&C 116]]. Then compare current Church treatments of [[Creation]], [[Fall of Adam and Eve]], [[The Godhead]], and the history-oriented question [[Why has Church teaching changed over time]]. [[Gospel Doctrine]] is useful for the later doctrinal synthesis. [[Latter-day Saints' Southern Star 1899-02-04 (Vol. 1 No. 10)]] supplies limited evidence of contemporary public controversy, not a definition of the teaching.
