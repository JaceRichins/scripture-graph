# Why does God seem silent when I pray?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Because he often is, by the testimony of the very people scripture holds
up as models: David ("how long wilt thou forget me," [[Psalm 13]]), Job,
Jeremiah, Jesus on the cross ([[Matthew 27]]:46), Joseph Smith in Liberty
Jail ("O God, where art thou?" [[D&C 121]]). Scripture treats silence as a
recognized part of faith rather than a failure of it. The reasons it offers
are several and partial: answers come in forms we do not recognize (a
thought, a peace, a person; [[D&C 6]], [[D&C 8]]); timing is God's
([[D&C 121]]'s answer came, but not soon); some silence is the space in
which faith is exercised rather than replaced ([[Ether 12]]:6); and some is
simply unexplained. The Restoration's claim is not that God always
answers audibly but that he answers — and that the pattern of how he does
can be learned.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- The Psalms of lament are a third of the Psalter; the Bible canonized the
  complaint, which means the experience is expected, not aberrant.
- Restoration scripture is unusually specific about the *forms* of answers
  — "I did enlighten thy mind" ([[D&C 6]]), a "burning in the bosom" or a
  "stupor of thought" ([[D&C 9]]), peace ([[D&C 6]]:23) — which reframes
  many silences as unrecognized replies.
- Liberty Jail is the paradigm: the plea in [[D&C 121]]:1–6 is answered in
  the same section, and the answer is presence and perspective, not
  rescue.
- Alma's account of faith as an experiment ([[Alma 32]]) describes growth
  by degrees, which implies that early on the signal is faint by design.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- "The answer was a feeling you didn't notice" is unfalsifiable and can
  turn any outcome into confirmation.
- A loving parent does not stay silent for years while a child pleads;
  divine hiddenness is a real argument against a personal God, not only a
  pastoral problem.
- Many people pray sincerely for decades and report nothing; telling them
  they missed it adds guilt to grief.
- Feelings of peace are produced by prayer whether or not anyone is
  listening, as studies of meditation show.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believers grant the unfalsifiability worry and answer that the test
scripture proposes is not a single prayer but a life ([[Alma 32]]: does the
seed grow?), which does yield evidence over time; that the "loving parent"
analogy cuts both ways, since parents also withhold to let children grow;
that the Church's own teaching now says explicitly that not everyone
receives dramatic spiritual experiences and that quiet faith is not
second-class (Elder Bednar's "patterns of light"); and that the peace of
prayer being *also* physiological does not make it *only* physiological.
The pastoral truth comes first: the scriptures' own heroes were not spared
the silence.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
Silence is a real experience and scripture does not deny it; it gives it a
place. The Restoration adds a practical theology of how answers come that
is genuinely useful and genuinely hard to disprove. A believer in the
silence is in good company and owes no one an apology; a critic is right
that the argument from hiddenness is serious. What the vault can offer is
what the tradition offers: the lament, the pattern, and the promise —
"be still, and know" ([[Psalm 46]]).
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
