# Does God exist?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
No argument settles it, and honest believers and honest atheists both say
so. What exists is a set of considerations that make theism reasonable to
many careful thinkers — why there is anything at all, why the universe is
ordered and life-permitting, why there are moral facts and conscious minds,
and the testimony of religious experience across cultures — and a set of
considerations that weigh the other way, chiefly suffering and divine
hiddenness. Scripture does not argue for God's existence; it assumes it and
invites a test: "prove me now herewith" ([[Malachi 3]]), "ask" ([[James 1]],
[[Moroni 10]]). Latter-day Saints hold that the decisive evidence is
available to anyone willing to seek it, and that it is personal rather than
public.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- The universe had a beginning and its constants fall within the narrow
  ranges that permit stars, chemistry and life; "why this rather than
  nothing, and why so finely balanced" is a question naturalism answers by
  multiplying universes or declining to answer.
- Moral realism — that cruelty is actually wrong, not merely disliked — is
  hard to ground in a purely physical world, and most people cannot help
  believing it.
- Consciousness, reason and the intelligibility of the cosmos to minds are
  at least as expected on theism as on materialism, arguably more so.
- Religious experience is nearly universal, often transformative, and
  reported by people with nothing to gain; the Book of Mormon's promise
  ([[Moroni 10]]) treats it as evidence to be tested, not asserted.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- The scale and randomness of suffering — children with cancer, animal
  pain across millions of years — fits an indifferent universe better than
  a loving creator (see the question on suffering).
- If God wants to be known, why is his existence not obvious? Divine
  hiddenness is a serious argument, not a complaint.
- Religious experience is culturally shaped: people find the God of their
  upbringing, which is what psychology would predict if it were internal.
- Science has steadily explained what was once attributed to God; "God of
  the gaps" arguments have a poor track record.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believers answer that the cosmological and fine-tuning arguments are not
gap arguments but questions about the whole framework science presupposes;
that hiddenness is what one would expect if God values freely chosen faith
over compelled assent — a theme scripture makes explicit ([[Alma 32]] on
faith as a seed, [[Ether 12]] on witnesses coming *after* the trial of
faith); that culturally shaped experience is still experience of something;
and that suffering is an argument against a certain picture of God's
purposes rather than against God. None of this is proof, and the tradition
does not claim it is.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
Theism is intellectually respectable and so is its denial; the arguments
shift the probabilities, not the verdict. The Restoration's distinctive
claim is not a better argument but a better *method*: that God answers
sincere inquiry, and that the answer — while not transferable to a skeptic
— is real knowledge for the one who receives it. A believer should not
oversell the arguments; a skeptic should not pretend the question is closed.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
