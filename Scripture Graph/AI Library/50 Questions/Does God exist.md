# Does God exist?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Neither scripture nor philosophy supplies a conclusion that compels every reasonable person. The canon straightforwardly teaches that God exists and records creation, revelation, covenant, and transformative encounters, but those texts are testimony from within a believing tradition rather than religiously neutral demonstrations. Cosmological, fine-tuning, moral, consciousness, and experiential considerations can make theism reasonable; suffering, divine hiddenness, religious diversity, and successful naturalistic explanation can make disbelief or suspension of judgment reasonable. The Restoration's distinctive invitation is participatory—ask, act, observe, and persist ([[James 1]], [[Alma 32]], [[Moroni 10]])—but its results remain personally interpreted and cannot simply be transferred to another person as proof.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Canonical testimony and participatory inquiry:** Genesis 1 and Moses 2 attribute cosmic order and life to God; [[Moses 1]] and [[D&C 76]] depict revelatory encounters; [[Alma 32]], [[Ether 12]], [[James 1]], and [[Moroni 10]] invite forms of asking, acting, observing, and persevering. These are strong evidence for what the canon teaches and offers, but not independent demonstrations of their supernatural cause. Alma's immediate experiment tests the word, not bare theism, and Moroni initially asks about the record's truth.

- **Contingency:** Cosmological arguments ask why contingent reality exists and whether explanation terminates in something necessary. This is a serious argument, classically developed by Leibniz and critically examined by Graham Oppy, but it depends on disputed principles of sufficient reason, necessity, and explanatory termination. Even success would not by itself establish a loving, revelatory God, and an impersonal terminus remains possible.

- **Fine-tuning:** Some physical constants and initial conditions appear sensitive to life-permitting outcomes. Robin Collins develops the design inference; Oppy and others dispute its probability assumptions. Design, multiverse and observer selection, deeper necessity, unknown physics, and brute contingency remain live comparisons. There is no agreed probability distribution over all possible universes.

- **Moral experience:** Objective duties and values may fit naturally within theism, as Robert Merrihew Adams argues, but Erik Wielenberg and other secular realists defend objective morality without God. Constructivism, contractualism, naturalist realism, and error theory are additional alternatives. Romans 2:14-15 supports only the narrower textual point that moral awareness is depicted among people without revealed law.

- **Consciousness and reason:** First-person experience, rational normativity, and the mathematical intelligibility of nature remain difficult explanatory problems. David Chalmers establishes the seriousness of the consciousness problem without establishing theism; J. P. Moreland argues for a theistic inference. Physicalism, emergentism, panpsychism, neutral monism, and non-theistic idealism remain live alternatives.

- **Religious experience and transformation:** Experiences may be immediate, coherent, and transformative, giving substantial first-person warrant. [[Alma 36]] depicts enduring moral reversal, while [[D&C 76]] gives explicit visionary testimony. William James documents the variety and fruits of religious experience; William Alston defends its possible epistemic rationality. Cultural shaping, incompatible interpretations, expectancy, neurological processes, and limited public accessibility sharply reduce its power to establish one theology for outsiders.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **Evidential evil:** Intense apparently pointless suffering—including infant disease, natural disaster, predation, and animal pain before human moral agency—looks more expected in an indifferent world than under unlimited power and perfect love. Appeals to growth or agency do not cover every case, especially suffering that destroys agency or occurs outside human choice. See [[Why does God allow suffering]] and [[Adversity]].

- **Divine hiddenness:** If a perfectly loving God seeks relationship, sincere and nonresistant seekers who receive no recognizable answer are surprising. Saying that every nonbeliever secretly resisted God contradicts credible testimony and makes the claim unfalsifiable. [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]] addresses the lived form of this argument.

- **Religious diversity:** Experiences interpreted as divine occur in incompatible traditions and commonly reflect the seeker's inherited concepts. This is predicted by cultural and psychological formation and weakens any direct move from “I experienced something” to “my complete theology is true.” See [[Why do religious experiences occur in every religion]].

- **Natural explanations:** Cognitive science, social learning, expectancy, absorption, grief, neurological states, and community reinforcement can explain important features of religious experience. Science's success counsels against filling an explanatory gap with God merely because no present natural account is complete.

- **Underdetermination:** Cosmological arguments may reach an impersonal foundation; fine-tuning may reach a designer unlike the scriptural God; moral arguments may reach necessary values; visionary testimony may establish sincerity without establishing external cause. Combining underdetermined arguments does not automatically remove their shared ambiguity.

- **Scriptural circularity and textual complexity:** A book's declaration that God speaks is direct evidence of the book's theology, not independent proof of its source. Textual history also matters: the expanded wording of 1 John 5:7 is late according to the dominant manuscript judgment and should not be used as if it were secure earliest Christian evidence.

- **Conceptual ambiguity:** “God” may mean a necessary ground, a deistic creator, the personal covenant God, a finite divine being, or the Father within [[The Godhead]]. Evidence against one model does not automatically refute all others, but shifting definitions whenever objections arise can immunize the claim rather than explain it.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- Thoughtful believers treat cosmological and fine-tuning arguments as questions about the framework within which scientific explanations operate, not substitutes for scientific work. This avoids a crude gap argument, although it still requires contested philosophical premises.

- A believer may regard spiritual experience as genuine perception: perception normally gives prima facie justification unless defeated, even though it is not infallible. Alma 32:27-43 encourages repeated attention to consequences rather than reliance on one emotion. [[A Framework for Personal Revelation (Elder Dale G. Renlund, October 2022)]] adds a needed caution: claimed revelation operates within constraints, and sincerity alone does not prevent deception.

- Ether 12:6 provides a textual reason that witness may follow faithful action. Appeals to [[Agency]], moral development, and noncoercive relationship can make some hiddenness intelligible. They do not explain every case of nonresistant nonbelief, so believers should not use them to judge another person's sincerity.

- Religious diversity need not show that every experience is illusory. A real transcendent object could be interpreted through different conceptual schemes, just as ordinary perception is interpreted. Yet that model reduces what experience alone can establish: additional reasons are needed to choose among incompatible doctrines.

- Stable natural law, meaningful agency, soul-making, solidarity, and an afterlife can answer portions of the problem of evil. None presently demonstrates why every apparently gratuitous instance was necessary. “God has reasons beyond us” is possible but becomes ad hoc if used without limits against every conceivable observation.

- A cumulative case may rationally matter even when no component proves the conclusion. Its honest form compares how well a specified theistic model and its alternatives jointly predict existence, order, consciousness, morality, experience, evil, hiddenness, and diversity; it does not count every unexplained fact as a separate victory.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The canon teaches that God exists and depicts God as creator or organizer, covenant partner, moral governor, and revealer. It invites practices of inquiry—asking, acting, observing, and persevering—and contains reports of vision and transformation. Alma 32 is demonstrably a sustained experiential method, but its object is the word and its procedure is not a controlled test. The textual problem in 1 John 5:7 is real and warns against resting theology on one insecure proof-text.

**Reasonably supported but open:** Cosmological, fine-tuning, consciousness, moral, and experiential arguments give theism serious intellectual support, especially cumulatively. Evil, hiddenness, diversity, and naturalistic explanations give atheism or agnosticism serious support. None of these considerations uniquely selects the full Restoration account of [[The Godhead]], and their comparative weights depend on disputed background commitments.

**A matter of faith and personal warrant:** Identifying a prayer response, vision, moral transformation, or the fruit of Alma's experiment as communication from God goes beyond what an outsider can verify from the report alone. Such identification may be rational and even experienced as knowledge by the recipient, but intellectual honesty requires fallibility, testing, and respect for sincere nonbelief. The responsible conclusion is not that both propositions are true, but that the public evidence does not force a universal verdict while committed belief or disbelief may rest on broader personal and philosophical judgments.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require.** As presented, the cited canon assumes a personal agent who creates or orders, speaks, commands, judges, covenants, and can be encountered. It also presents several forms of inquiry: Hebrews 11:6 begins with belief; [[Alma 32]] tests the word through cultivation; [[Ether 12]] places witness after trial; [[James 1]] prescribes asking for wisdom; and [[Moroni 10]] prescribes prayer concerning the record. These texts do not require that such practices are controlled experiments, that every sincere seeker receives an unmistakable answer on demand, that creation means creation from absolute nothing, or that a spiritual impression interprets itself.

- **Restoration personal-organizer model — textually supported in part; externally disputed.** The canon strongly presents God as personal and revelatory. [[Abraham 3]] depicts divine agents proposing to take existing materials and make an earth, while leaving those materials' ultimate origin unstated. Broader Latter-day Saint teaching often joins organization from existing reality to claims about an embodied God and eternal intelligences, but those fuller propositions should not be inferred from Genesis 1 or Moses 2 alone. This model is not interchangeable with every classical argument for an immaterial ground of all contingent being.

- **Classical necessary-ground theism — philosophically supported but not required by these texts.** Cosmological reasoning may conclude that contingent reality requires a necessary terminus. The argument remains disputed, its terminus might be impersonal, and its standard classical form may fit Restoration metaphysics imperfectly.

- **Cumulative-case theism — plausible.** Contingency, fine-tuning, consciousness, moral experience, religious encounter, and transformation may jointly favor a personal-source model. The case is strongest when a specified model's predictions are compared with alternatives and weakest when dependent observations are counted as independent or counterevidence is excluded.

- **Warranted basic belief — possible and independently developed.** Plantinga's model permits rational belief in God without inferential proof when suitable faculties function properly. It resembles the canon's emphasis on encounter and witness, but disputed faculty reliability, negative outcomes, and religious diversity prevent automatic public warrant.

- **Genuine but culturally mediated encounter — possible.** One transcendent reality could be interpreted through divergent inherited concepts. This takes experience seriously and explains some diversity, but cannot by itself vindicate incompatible doctrinal details and becomes ad hoc if every contradiction is assigned to culture without criteria.

- **Deism or a non noninterventionist creator — possible but poorly fitted to these texts.** Cosmological or fine-tuning arguments might support an originating or organizing intelligence that does not reveal itself. That model can reduce some hiddenness problems but conflicts with the canon's central depictions of covenant, speech, intervention, and encounter.

- **Nonpersonal ultimate reality — possible.** Contingency, order, consciousness, or religious experience might be grounded in an impersonal necessary reality, idealist structure, or neutral foundation. This may capture part of the philosophical evidence while failing to match the canon's personal agent.

- **Naturalism — plausible and independently supported as a research program.** Physical, biological, psychological, and social explanations account for much without divine action and predict cultural dependence and religious diversity reasonably well. Unresolved questions about contingency, consciousness, normativity, or intelligibility are not by themselves refutations of naturalism.

- **Psychological or social construction — plausible for many experiences.** Expectation, absorption, crisis, memory, and community can generate or shape experiences and directly compete with supernatural interpretations of [[Alma 36]] and modern testimony. This model does not establish that every experience lacks an external object or explain why anything exists.

- **Agnostic suspension — plausible.** Because the arguments are underdetermined and much experiential evidence is not publicly transferable, a person may reasonably judge that neither theism nor atheism has cleared the evidential threshold.

- **Evidential atheism from evil and hiddenness — plausible and independently supported.** Apparently gratuitous suffering and nonresistant nonbelief bear directly against an all-powerful, perfectly loving God. They do not deductively exclude every deity, but they can substantially lower the probability of particular perfect-being models unless credible explanations are supplied.

- **Positive metaphysical naturalism — possible.** A person may judge naturalism simpler and more predictively successful overall. This conclusion bears a greater burden than rejecting one theistic argument because it must explain why insufficient theistic evidence warrants denial rather than suspension.

- **Bounded skeptical theism — possible; universal rescue is ad hoc.** Human limitations may conceal reasons for some suffering. If “God has unknown reasons” is made compatible with every conceivable outcome, however, the model loses predictive and evidential content.

**Considered and set aside:** “Science has proved God,” because scientific models do not identify a revelatory deity; “science has disproved every God,” because science tests specified claims rather than every metaphysical model; scripture alone as neutral proof, because that assumes the authority in question; Malachi 3:10 as a general existence test, because its subject is covenantal giving and blessing; James 1:5 as a neutral existence experiment, because it asks for wisdom in faith; John 5:31-39 as a direct proof of theism, because it argues for Jesus within an assumed theology; 1 John 5:7 as secure earliest wording, because the expanded Trinitarian formula is textually secondary; morality as impossible without belief, because Romans 2:14-15 and ordinary experience contradict that claim; multiverse theory as merely an atheist dodge, because it has independent motivations even though its fine-tuning use remains disputed; and free agency as a complete answer to evil, because it does not explain natural, prehuman, or non-agent suffering.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
