# Why is the Church so wealthy?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Church is wealthy chiefly because generations of members have paid recurring tithes and offerings, leaders have repeatedly retained part of the resulting surplus, and professionally managed investments have compounded over decades. Business interests, property appreciation, centralized administration, donated labor, and favorable nonprofit tax treatment plausibly reinforce that process, although their exact contributions are unknown. The SEC's 2023 order reports that Ensign Peak managed about $7 billion at its creation in 1997 and that its reportable Section 13(f) securities reached about $37.8 billion by 2020; those figures describe only part of the Church's assets. Because the Church publishes no consolidated worldwide financial statements, precise current net-worth claims remain estimates. Scripture does not prescribe a reserve amount: it requires generosity, humility, care for the poor, and distrust of riches. Consequently, explaining how the wealth accumulated does not settle whether its present scale, disclosure, and use constitute prudent stewardship.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Recurring receipts and deliberate surplus.** D&C 119:3–4 establishes recurring tithing, and the Church says it budgets operating expenses below anticipated receipts and invests part of the remainder. The [First Presidency's 2019 statement](https://newsroom.churchofjesuschrist.org/article/first-presidency-statement-church-finances) says a portion is safeguarded as a future reserve.

- **Regulatory confirmation of accumulation.** The [SEC's 2023 order](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2023/34-96951.pdf), paragraphs 3 and 5, says Ensign Peak managed about $7 billion at its 1997 inception and that its Section 13(f) securities reached about $37.8 billion by 2020. It identifies excess tithing, income, returns, and assets of other Church auxiliaries as sources. This independently supports the surplus-and-compounding explanation.

- **A broad asset base.** The Church's [financial overview](https://newsroom.churchofjesuschrist.org/article/church-finances-and-a-growing-global-church) acknowledges stocks, bonds, taxable businesses, real estate, and agricultural holdings. Temples, meetinghouses, schools, farms, and welfare facilities may be valuable without being equivalent to freely spendable cash; see [[Temples]] and [[Work and Self-Reliance]].

- **Long institutional time horizon.** The official [Latter-day Saint Economic History](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/history/topics/church-finances?lang=eng) records earlier debt and distress as well as later reserve building. Present wealth is the result of a historical reversal, not an uninterrupted condition from 1830.

- **Tax and reporting environment.** The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/churches-integrated-auxiliaries-and-conventions-or-associations-of-churches) explains that qualifying churches are generally tax-exempt, may receive deductible donations, and ordinarily do not file Form 990. These general rules facilitate long-term capital preservation but do not by themselves explain the Church's financial discipline or prove wrongdoing.

- **Documented deployment.** The Church's [2025 Caring for Those in Need report](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/serve/caring/report?lang=eng) reports $1.58 billion in welfare, self-reliance, humanitarian, and related expenditures. That is substantial activity, but it is a self-report rather than a consolidated audited statement and cannot yield a reliable charity-to-wealth ratio.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The reserve may be morally excessive.** Even if lawfully accumulated, the verified multibillion-dollar portfolio represents substantial opportunity cost while people lack food, shelter, medicine, and education. [[Jacob 2]] and D&C 104:15-18 make relieving suffering a purpose of abundance. Critics may reasonably ask what defined contingency requires the retained amount and why present suffering receives less priority.

- **Donors cannot independently evaluate the full stewardship claim.** The Church does not publish consolidated statements of revenue, expenses, assets, liabilities, reserve targets, or related-party transactions. Legal exemption from Form 990 filing does not settle whether an institution receiving sacrificial donations has a stronger moral duty of accountability.

- **The SEC findings validate a serious transparency concern.** The SEC found that Ensign Peak and the Church used thirteen LLCs to prevent disclosure of the Church's portfolio and filed forms that misstated investment and voting control. Reliance on legal counsel may affect judgments about individual intent, but it does not make the inaccurate filings compliant or erase the prolonged institutional effort to conceal ownership.

- **Poor members are asked to tithe despite the reserve.** [[Luke 21]] emphasizes the cost of the widow's gift, and its placement after the warning about scribes who devour widows' houses permits scrutiny of the receiving religious order as well as admiration for the donor. Critics argue that requiring ten percent from financially precarious households while preserving vast investments transfers risk toward vulnerable members.

- **Reported charitable spending does not resolve proportionality.** The Church's reported $1.58 billion in 2025 welfare, self-reliance, humanitarian, emergency-relief, and related expenditures is substantial. Without consolidated accounts, however, its cash, in-kind, volunteer, fast-offering, internal-program, and reserve-funded components cannot be fully reconciled or compared reliably with total capacity.

- **Mission categories can become too elastic.** If temples, education, commercial development, farms, media, investments, missionary work, and humanitarian aid all count as serving one mission, almost any retention may be defended after the fact. Critics therefore request measurable objectives, a stated reserve policy, and independent financial reporting.

- **Broader whistleblower tax allegations remain unresolved.** They warrant investigation but are not established by the SEC action, which concerned securities reporting. Treating the allegations as proven—or treating the SEC settlement as irrelevant to institutional trust—would both overstate the record.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- **A reserve is not inherently contrary to scripture.** [[Alma 1]] 1:26–31 depicts a church becoming wealthier while its members labor and distribute generously; the narrative condemns setting hearts on riches, not all prosperity. [[1 Timothy 6]] 6:17–19 likewise instructs the rich how to use wealth rather than declaring possession alone sinful.

- **A global church has durable obligations.** Meetinghouses, [[Temples]], missionary work, universities, family-history infrastructure, disaster response, and welfare systems impose recurring costs across economic cycles. A reserve can prevent debt, stabilize these commitments during recessions, and fund expansion where local donations cannot. The Church's financial overview presents this as its governing rationale.

- **Institutional assets are not all idle cash.** Worship buildings, schools, farms, and welfare facilities directly support religious or charitable functions; selling them would also curtail those functions. Even diversified investments can be understood as an endowment supporting future operations rather than as private consumption.

- **Real assistance is documented.** The 2025 caring report describes $1.58 billion in relevant expenditures, thousands of projects, food distributions, education initiatives, and self-reliance programs. This rebuts the absolute claim that the Church does nothing for present need, though it does not decide whether the amount is proportionate.

- **No cited evidence establishes personal ownership by leaders.** Large institutional assets and objectionable secrecy do not by themselves prove that senior leaders personally own the reserve or may distribute it as private wealth. The SEC describes Ensign Peak as a nonprofit manager of Church assets.

- **The Church changed the specific SEC reporting practice.** Its [settlement statement](https://newsroom.churchofjesuschrist.org/article/church-issues-statement-on-sec-settlement) acknowledges mistakes and says aggregated reporting began after the SEC raised concerns in 2019. That is relevant corrective action, although it is not a substitute for broader transparency.

These answers make prudent stewardship possible; they do not demonstrate that the current reserve is optimally sized. A thoughtful believing response can affirm tithing and institutional foresight while still requesting clearer reporting, a publicly articulated reserve policy, and greater deployment for the poor.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the texts require.** D&C 119:3–4 requires recurring tithing, and D&C 120:1 assigns its disposition to a council. [[Jacob 2]] 2:17–21, Mosiah 4:21–26, D&C 42:30–34, and D&C 104:15–18 require humility, generosity, equality of worth, and care for the poor. [[Mark 10]] 10:17–27 and [[1 Timothy 6]] 6:17–19 warn against trust in riches and join wealth to costly discipleship and distribution. The texts do **not** specify a reserve ceiling, investment mix, humanitarian percentage, public-audit regime, or answer to U.S. tax law. Claims that scripture plainly commands either the existing reserve or its rapid liquidation attach to added assumptions.

- **Independently supported — recurring-surplus and compounding model.** Durable tithing receipts exceeded at least some operating budgets; surpluses entered Ensign Peak; investment returns and asset appreciation compounded. The SEC order and Church publications independently converge on this mechanism. This best explains *how* wealth grew without deciding whether the outcome is righteous.

- **Plausible — prudent endowment model.** Leaders preserve capital so worship, education, welfare, and missionary operations can survive recessions and expand globally without debt. Institutional longevity and past financial crises support this model, as do the Church's published policies. What remains undisclosed is the target reserve and the modeled contingency that justifies its size.

- **Plausible — global cross-subsidy model.** Donations and returns from wealthier regions finance facilities and programs in places unable to support them locally. The worldwide infrastructure and caring reports support the possibility, but no consolidated regional accounts permit measurement.

- **Possible, faith-dependent — eschatological stewardship model.** The Church says reserves prepare for future growth and the work preceding Christ's return. This coheres with its theology of the [[Second Coming of Jesus Christ]], but the projected financial need and divine approval are not independently testable.

- **Plausible — excessive accumulation or mission-drift model.** A financially successful institution can make reserve preservation an implicit end, allowing compounding to outrun present charitable deployment. The immense verified portfolio, absence of a published reserve cap, and scriptural emphasis on the poor support this concern. It remains a moral interpretation because comprehensive inflows, expenses, restrictions, and future obligations are undisclosed.

- **Independently supported in limited scope — deliberate confidentiality model.** The SEC established that Church-approved entities were used to prevent public identification of Ensign Peak's holdings and that filings misstated control. This supports criticism of past securities disclosure. Extending it into a claim that every financial operation is deceptive is not supported by that order.

- **Possible but unestablished — tax-law abuse model.** Whistleblower allegations argue that reserve accumulation or transfers to commercial projects violated tax-exempt purposes. Those allegations warrant scrutiny, but the SEC case did not adjudicate them, and the cited record supplies no final IRS ruling establishing that broader claim.

- **Possible — donor-protection confidentiality model.** Leaders may believe that publishing vast reserves would discourage tithing, invite pressure, or impair investment strategy. The SEC record independently shows concern about “negative consequences,” but treating inaccurate filings as necessary protection is an ethically weak and legally failed extension of this model.

- **Ad hoc if asserted without accounts — every retained dollar is presently necessary.** Because no public reserve target or consolidated forecast tests this assertion, it merely restates the institutional conclusion. It should not be treated as established stewardship.

- **Ad hoc if asserted from size alone — wealth proves corruption or divine favor.** Financial scale neither demonstrates personal enrichment nor validates prophetic authority. Both conclusions add premises absent from the financial and scriptural evidence.

**Set aside.** The equation “total assets = spendable cash” is rejected because operating properties and securities have different functions and liquidity. The equation “reported caring expenditures = external humanitarian grants” is rejected because the report aggregates several kinds of welfare, self-reliance, donations, and service. The claim that legal tax exemption settles moral stewardship is also set aside: legality answers neither [[Charity]] nor opportunity cost.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The Church possesses very large financial and property resources. Recurring donations, operating surpluses, centralized investment, compounding returns, appreciation, and nonprofit tax treatment explain the accumulation. The SEC record establishes both multibillion-dollar growth and a serious, long-running disclosure failure. The Church also operates costly worldwide religious, educational, welfare, and humanitarian programs and reports substantial current assistance.

**Open:** The consolidated net worth, annual tithing receipts, total investment income, liabilities, liquidity, internal reserve target, category-by-category spending, and proportion drawn from investment reserves are not publicly established. Consequently, claims that the Church is worth a precise current amount, spends a precise percentage on charity, or possesses a demonstrably optimal reserve exceed the evidence. Whether present accumulation represents admirable foresight, excessive caution, or mission drift remains a partly moral judgment constrained by missing accounts.

**Matter of faith:** That tithing is divinely commanded, that the governing council in D&C 120 acts under revelation, that reserves prepare for divinely foreseen events, or that prosperity evidences God's approval cannot be demonstrated from balance sheets. Conversely, the existence of wealth or regulatory failure does not by itself disprove the Church's religious claims. The canon's clearest test is functional and spiritual: whether resources remain subordinate to God and are used generously for people in need ([[Jacob 2]]; [[1 Timothy 6]]), a test that supports neither complacent defense nor automatic condemnation.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Alma 1]] 1:26–31 beside [[Jacob 2]] 2:12–21 for the Book of Mormon's contrast between prosperous liberality and wealth-driven hierarchy. Then compare [[Mark 10]] 10:17–27, Luke 12:13–21, [[Luke 21]] 21:1–4, James 2:1–7, and [[1 Timothy 6]] 6:6–19. Restoration foundations are D&C 42:30–39, D&C 56:16–18, D&C 104:11–18, [[D&C 119]], and [[D&C 120]]; [[King Benjamin's address]] adds a sustained theology of giving without judgment.

For primary institutional evidence, read the [SEC administrative order](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2023/34-96951.pdf), not only commentary about it; then read the Church's [SEC response](https://newsroom.churchofjesuschrist.org/article/church-issues-statement-on-sec-settlement), [2019 First Presidency statement](https://newsroom.churchofjesuschrist.org/article/first-presidency-statement-church-finances), [financial Q&A](https://newsroom.churchofjesuschrist.org/article/church-finances-and-a-growing-global-church), [economic history](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/study/history/topics/church-finances?lang=eng), and [2025 caring report](https://www.churchofjesuschrist.org/serve/caring/report?lang=eng). Check current Ensign Peak disclosures directly in the [SEC EDGAR database](https://www.sec.gov/edgar/browse/?CIK=0001454984), remembering their limited scope, and consult the [IRS church-filing rules](https://www.irs.gov/charities-non-profits/churches-integrated-auxiliaries-and-conventions-or-associations-of-churches).

For historical Church framing, compare [[Conference Report, April 1921 (General Conference (full report), April 1921)]], [[Gospel Doctrine]], and [[Chapter 14 ‘Thou Shalt Have No Other Gods before Me’]]. Treat early conference rhetoric as historical evidence, not audited data about current finances.
