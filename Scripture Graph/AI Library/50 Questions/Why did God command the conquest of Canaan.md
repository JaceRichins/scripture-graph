# Why did God command the conquest of Canaan?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The biblical texts answer that Canaan's peoples were judged for grave wrongdoing, Israel had to be protected from adopting their worship, and the land promise to Abraham was being fulfilled (Genesis 15:13–21; Leviticus 18:21–30; Deuteronomy 9:4–6; 20:16–18). They simultaneously insist that Israel was not righteous and could itself be expelled for comparable sin. That establishes what the narrators claim, not independently that God issued every reported command or that every totalizing phrase is literal. Joshua's explicit killing language keeps the moral problem real, while its survivors, incomplete occupation, and named exceptions make a simple extermination account inadequate. Believers therefore divide among a unique-divine-judgment reading, a conventional-war-rhetoric or limited-campaign reading, and an accommodation reading in which inspired but fallible writers attributed their culturally conditioned understanding to God. None is demonstrable from the text alone, and none turns these passages into permission for later violence; [[Luke 9]] and [[Alma 48]] point readers toward mercy and strictly defensive restraint.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The text supplies several reasons, not one:** delayed judgment on the Amorites (Genesis 15:13–16), condemned practices and reciprocal warning to Israel (Leviticus 18:21–30), prevention of religious assimilation (Deuteronomy 20:16–18), fulfillment of the ancestral oath, and an emphatic denial that Israel deserved the land (Deuteronomy 9:4–7). [[Deuteronomy 9]] is especially important because it forbids converting victory into evidence of Israelite virtue.

- **The disturbing wording cannot honestly be removed:** Joshua 6:21 includes young and old, men and women; Joshua 8 and 10–11 also use sweeping destruction formulas. Hyperbole may qualify their demographic scope, but it cannot make every narrated action nonviolent.

- **The final narrative also resists a literal, comprehensive extermination:** Joshua 10:20 mentions survivors reaching fortified cities; Joshua 13:1–6 says much land remains; Joshua 23:4–13 addresses remaining nations; and [[Judges 1]] repeatedly reports peoples whom Israel did not drive out. This strongly supports literary compression, victory rhetoric, a limited campaign, multiple traditions, or some combination of them.

- **Exceptions matter but should not be exaggerated:** Rahab's household is incorporated into Israel (Joshua 6:22–25), and the Gibeonites survive under an oath (Joshua 9:15–27). These cases challenge a purely racial interpretation, but neither passage says that all other inhabitants received and rejected an equivalent offer.

- **External comparison gives qualified support to a rhetorical reading:** K. Lawson Younger Jr., *Ancient Conquest Accounts*, shows that sweeping claims were conventional in ancient victory accounts. Archaeological syntheses by William G. Dever and Ann E. Killebrew likewise favor a complex, regionally varied emergence of Israel over one uniform exterminating invasion. Neither line of evidence proves that no massacre occurred or decides whether a command was divine.

- **Later scripture supplies a norm against reenacting conquest:** Jesus rebukes the proposed destruction of a Samaritan village (Luke 9:51–56), while [[Alma 48]] praises defenders who do not delight in bloodshed or initiate aggression. These passages do not explain away Joshua, but they make Joshua unusable as an open-ended mandate.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The moral objection survives rhetorical qualification.** Even if regional summaries are hyperbolic, Joshua 6:21 expressly narrates the killing of women and children. A God of perfect justice ordering infants killed for a society's sins appears incompatible with individual moral responsibility and with Jesus's treatment of enemies.

- **The stated rationale is not independent verification.** Deuteronomy says the inhabitants were wicked, but the accused do not speak for themselves, and ancient conquerors commonly portrayed enemies as depraved and their own territorial claims as divinely authorized. Repetition shows what Israel's literature asserts, not that the assertion is true.

- **Collective punishment remains central.** The narratives generally do not distinguish each individual's guilt. The warning that Israel can receive similar judgment makes the theology less ethnically partial, but equal application of collective destruction does not by itself make that destruction just.

- **The text can legitimate dangerous reasoning.** If an asserted revelation can suspend ordinary prohibitions against killing, later claimants can imitate the logic. Saying the event was unique does not provide a public test for distinguishing a genuine exception from fanaticism.

- **Internal inconsistency may indicate competing traditions rather than benign hyperbole.** Sweeping conquest summaries and continuing Canaanite populations may reflect different sources or later theological editing. That model can explain the evidence without assuming a restrained historical campaign.

- **Critical historical reconstruction can deepen the problem.** If early Israel largely emerged from within Canaan, conquest literature may retrospectively turn related populations into condemned outsiders and sacralize territorial conflict. On that reading, the divine command is ideological legitimation rather than remembered revelation.

- **Appeal to later revelation creates a continuity problem.** Reading Joshua through Jesus may guide Christian practice, but it can also imply that earlier portrayals of God were morally deficient. The question then becomes how such portrayals function as scripture, not merely how to soften their wording.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believers give several answers, none of which should be mistaken for a conclusive proof.

1. **Unique divine judgment:** God, as giver of life and judge of nations, may authorize a historically bounded judgment that humans could not authorize themselves. Genesis 15:13–16 depicts delay rather than impulsive destruction, and Leviticus 18:24–30 subjects Israel to the same standard. This response takes the narrators seriously, but it depends on faith that the attribution is accurate and must still address children, collective responsibility, and how a claimed exception could ever be safely recognized.

2. **Conventional war rhetoric and limited objectives:** Joshua's sweeping formulas can be read as ancient victory language about the defeat of political-military resistance rather than a census of literal extinction. Surviving populations in [[Joshua 13]], [[Joshua 23]], and [[Judges 1]] support this qualification. The response is substantial but partial: Jericho and other individual episodes still narrate killing, so genre cannot be used as a universal solvent.

3. **Accommodation through fallible witnesses:** God may have worked with ancient Israel while its writers and leaders understood revelation through their own violent culture. Latter-day Saint acceptance of the Bible subject to textual qualification (Articles of Faith 1:8), together with Book of Mormon acknowledgments of human weakness in records, makes some form of fallibility doctrinally available. It does not prove corruption or misattribution in Joshua; invoking an otherwise unattested alteration only where a text is troubling would be ad hoc.

4. **Canonical and Christological reading:** Christians can treat Jesus's enemy-love and refusal of retaliatory destruction as the controlling norm for discipleship (Matthew 5:43–48; Luke 9:51–56). [[Alma 48]] similarly praises reluctant defense rather than aggression. This establishes how later believers should act more securely than it reconstructs what happened in Canaan.

Across these responses, the safest shared conclusion is negative: no modern person, church, or state may infer authorization for violence from resemblance to ancient Israel. The conquest commands are narrated as a bounded episode attached to named peoples, land, and covenant circumstances—not a reusable policy.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established from the text:** the biblical narrators attribute the conquest to divine command, ground it in judgment, covenant, and protection from apostasy, deny Israel's moral superiority, describe real killing in at least some episodes, preserve exceptions, and acknowledge surviving peoples and incomplete possession. The commands are presented as bounded to a particular land and historical-covenantal setting.

**Strongly supported but not exhaustive:** conventional victory rhetoric and literary compression help explain the tension between totalizing summaries and continued populations. A complex history involving gradual settlement and localized warfare fits much archaeological scholarship better than a simple continent-wide extermination. Neither conclusion proves that every violent episode is figurative.

**Open:** the historical scale and sequence of the campaigns; the number and status of noncombatants affected; how different sources or traditions contributed to Joshua and Judges; and whether accommodation, human misattribution, or an accurate exceptional judgment best explains the commands. The supplied evidence does not adjudicate these questions decisively.

**A matter of faith:** that God truly authorized any particular conquest action, that such authorization was perfectly just despite the fate of children and other noncombatants, or that later revelation preserves the same God while correcting rather than contradicting earlier human understanding. These conclusions cannot be obtained simply by repeating the narrator's attribution.

The most defensible believing stance is neither denial nor easy vindication: take the texts' own explanations seriously, admit that they do not independently prove their divine attribution, refuse to erase their victims, and let the clearest teachings of [[Jesus Christ]] govern present conduct. Moral discomfort is warranted. It is not evidence by itself that scripture is worthless, nor is loyalty to scripture evidence that every proposed harmonization succeeds.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with the primary sequence: Genesis 15:13–21; [[Leviticus 18]]; Deuteronomy 7; [[Deuteronomy 9]]; [[Deuteronomy 20]]; Joshua 2 and 6–11; [[Joshua 13]]; [[Joshua 23]]; [[Joshua 24]]; and [[Judges 1]]. Compare the later ethical texts in [[Luke 9]], Matthew 5, and [[Alma 48]]. Numbers 31 should also be read because the moral problem of divinely attributed total warfare is broader than Canaan alone.

For genre and ancient historiography, read K. Lawson Younger Jr., *Ancient Conquest Accounts*. For archaeology and Israelite origins, compare William G. Dever, *Who Were the Early Israelites and Where Did They Come From?*, with Ann E. Killebrew, *Biblical Peoples and Ethnicity*. For substantially different believing responses, compare Paul Copan, *Is God a Moral Monster?*; Christopher J. H. Wright, *The God I Don't Understand*; John H. Walton and J. Harvey Walton, *The Lost World of the Israelite Conquest*; and Eric A. Seibert, *The Violence of Scripture*. Susan Niditch's *War in the Hebrew Bible* and Lori L. Rowlett's *Joshua and the Rhetoric of Violence* provide important literary and critical counterweights.

For method, consult [[Literary Command-Fulfillment Correspondence in Exodus 38]] and [[Narrative Command-Fulfillment Correspondence in Genesis 17]], asking at every stage whether an argument establishes literary design, historical occurrence, moral justification, or divine origin. Those are four different claims.

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** The final form of Genesis-Joshua requires readers to recognize that its narrators attribute the land promise, judgment, and military commands to God; that they deny Israel earned the land; that they name assimilation and apostasy as a reason alongside judgment; and that some episodes include civilians in destruction while others report exceptions, survivors, and incomplete occupation. It does not require that every individual Canaanite was personally guilty, that every victory formula is a literal demographic count, that Israel was morally superior, or that later readers may reenact the commands.

**1. Unique divine judgment — possible; internally well-supported; not independently established.** Genesis 15:13-16, Leviticus 18:24-30, Deuteronomy 9:4-6, and Deuteronomy 20:16-18 support this model's stated reasons. Its weakness: the divine origin of the commands is the point under dispute, and it must still account for noncombatants and collective punishment.

**2. Hyperbolic conquest rhetoric — plausible, independently supported as genre comparison.** K. Lawson Younger Jr.'s comparative work on ancient conquest accounts, together with Joshua's own coexistence of totalizing summaries and surviving peoples (Joshua 10:20, 40; 13:1-6), supports reading some formulas as conventional victory claims. It becomes ad hoc if stretched to explain away explicit killing scenes like Joshua 6:21.

**3. Limited attacks on fortified centers — possible, with partial textual support.** References to kings, armies, and gates permit a model of disabling resistance centers rather than exterminating rural populations, but claims that civilians always evacuated first lack textual support.

**4. Gradual settlement plus compressed memory of conflict — plausible, independently supported in part.** William Dever and Ann Killebrew's archaeological syntheses favor continuity between Canaanite and early-Israelite populations alongside localized violence; [[Judges 1]] preserves a more incremental picture than Joshua's summaries.

**5. Identity displacement rather than ethnicity-based extermination — possible but disputed.** Rahab and the Gibeonites show ancestry was not an absolute barrier to survival; John and J. Harvey Walton's non-ethnic, identity-oriented reading of the ban develops this, though explicit death reports prevent treating it as a complete explanation.

**6. Progressive revelation or divine accommodation — plausible theologically; historically underdetermined.** The record may preserve real revelation mediated through an ancient people's limited categories, with Jesus's enemy-love as the clearer norm — though Joshua itself never announces its commands as accommodations.

**7. Human attribution and ideological legitimation — plausible critical model; independently supported by ancient parallels.** Israelite writers may have cast territorial conflict as divine command using conventions shared with surrounding royal literature; internal contradictions and the archaeological record fit this reconstruction, though it cannot by itself reconstruct every event.

**8. Composite-tradition model — plausible literary-critical model.** Joshua's sweeping summaries, its localized narratives, and [[Judges 1]]'s incremental picture may preserve different sources or theological programs rather than one consistent campaign report.

**9. Specific textual corruption — possible in principle, ad hoc on the evidence available.** Latter-day Saint teaching permits defects in transmission, but no manuscript evidence here identifies a missing negation or interpolation in the conquest commands specifically.

**10. Different-god or purely allegorical readings — considered and set aside.** A Marcionite split between the God of Israel and the God revealed in Christ conflicts with the canon's own identification of the two (see [[Is the God of the Old Testament the same as the God of the New]]); a purely spiritual allegory fails as the primary reading because Joshua names real places, kings, and casualties.

Joshua's own command-and-fulfillment pairs (6:2-5 against 6:6-20; 8:1-2 against 8:18-29) show deliberate narrative design; such correspondence establishes narrative shaping, not historicity or divine origin. Unrelated registry findings on Abrahamic chronology, Alma 54, 2 Nephi 24, Mosiah 13, and Moses 3 do not bear on this question.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->
