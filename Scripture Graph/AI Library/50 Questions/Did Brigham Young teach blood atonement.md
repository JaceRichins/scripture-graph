# Did Brigham Young teach blood atonement?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Yes—in the historically responsible sense that published reports of multiple public sermons attribute to [[Brigham Young]] the teaching that certain grave sins could require the willing sinner's own blood to be shed for forgiveness or exaltation. The clearest texts are Journal of Discourses 4:53–54 (September 21, 1856) and 4:219–220 (February 8, 1857), and the Church's own [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] acknowledges the teaching and cites the first sermon. Important qualifications remain: the Journal of Discourses is an edited, sometimes inaccurate transcription rather than canon; much of the preaching may have functioned as Reformation-era intimidation or hyperbole; evidence for actual implementation is limited and disputed; and the Church today explicitly rejects blood atonement as doctrine, teaching that redemption comes through [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]].
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **September 21, 1856:** The published sermon report says some offenders would willingly have their blood spilled, that the resulting offering would atone for their sins, and that some sins could not be remitted by Christ's blood but required the person's own blood (Journal of Discourses 4:53–54). The discourse's printed title itself includes “Atonement By the Shedding of Blood.”

- **February 8, 1857:** A second published sermon report develops the same idea. It imagines a person who knows that having his blood shed will atone for a grave sin, asks whether the Saints would love such a person enough to shed that blood, and predicts that the principle would someday operate in full force (Journal of Discourses 4:219–220). Repetition makes dismissal as one stray phrase difficult.

- **Institutional historical acknowledgment:** [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] says early leaders taught that some sins were so serious that the perpetrator's blood would have to be shed for forgiveness, citing Young at Journal of Discourses 4:53–54. Thus the careful dispute is over meaning, status, and implementation—not over whether such rhetoric appears in the historical record.

- **Context:** Both sermons belong to the 1856–57 Mormon Reformation, a campaign of stringent repentance preaching. Paul H. Peterson's “The Mormon Reformation of 1856–1857: The Rhetoric and Reality” argues that the language was calculated to alarm hearers into reform. That contextual interpretation explains a function of the sermons; it does not make their theological assertions disappear.

- **Canonical comparison:** The verses that actually name Young—D&C 124:127; 126:1; 138:53—say nothing about this teaching. [[Alma 34]] locates the final, infinite sacrifice in Christ (Alma 34:10–14), Mosiah 3:17 identifies no other saving means, and [[Alma 36]] portrays forgiveness following an appeal to Jesus (Alma 36:17–20).
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The reports are not flawless transcripts.** The Journal of Discourses was produced through shorthand reporting and editing, and documented discrepancies require wording such as “the published report says.” General source problems, however, do not by themselves show that the central argument of both sermons was invented.

- **The rhetoric may have been revivalist intimidation rather than an executable program.** The sermons arose during the Mormon Reformation, when leaders used severe language to provoke repentance and conformity. That context may qualify intended application, but hyperbole can still communicate a sincerely held theological proposition.

- **Young may have envisioned voluntary and lawful execution in a future theocratic order.** The reports imagine an informed offender accepting death, while D&C 42:79 assigns murder cases to civil law. This can distinguish the teaching from private vigilantism, but ordinary capital punishment does not itself explain the reported salvific efficacy assigned to the offender's blood.

- **Evidence for practice is disputed.** The Church's [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] rejects many sensational allegations but considers at least one instance of action on the rhetoric likely. Some non-Church historians argue from territorial court records, trial testimony, press reports, and patterns of extralegal violence for broader but diffuse implementation. That middle position is stronger than an unsupported theory of a systematic secret tribunal, yet it cannot be reduced to the essay's single conceded instance without examining the underlying cases individually.

- **Mountain Meadows does not settle the question.** Major archival scholarship finds no order from Young for the massacre while concluding that inflammatory leadership rhetoric contributed to the hostile climate. Absence of a direct order does not eliminate indirect responsibility; contextual influence does not prove that the massacre was conducted as blood atonement.

- **Current doctrine is different.** Present Church material rejects blood atonement and centers redemption in Christ. That establishes current teaching, but it cannot be retrojected to claim that Young never preached the historical idea.

The strongest critical conclusion is therefore limited but serious: noncanonical status, uncertain transcription, and disputed implementation qualify the record, yet none supports an unqualified denial that Young taught the substance attributed to him.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
A thoughtful believing response can concede the historical answer without adopting either denial or maximal accusation. First, prophets need not be treated as verbally infallible in every sermon; D&C 124:127 establishes Young's leadership without canonizing every later opinion. Second, source criticism rightly lowers confidence in exact wording, but repeated reports and the Church's own acknowledgment make complete denial implausible. Third, Reformation context can explain why Young used extreme language while leaving room to judge the language morally hazardous: the official essay expressly says historical context does not excuse violence.

Believers may distinguish an asserted theoretical principle from authorized practice. D&C 42:79 places accused killers under the law of the land, not private ecclesiastical execution. The evidence does not establish a standing Church tribunal or general program of blood-atonement killings. Even so, that distinction should not minimize the official essay's judgment that violent preaching increased tension and may have encouraged some extralegal acts.

The strongest doctrinal response is to make the canon and current teaching normative. [[Alma 34]] teaches an infinite and eternal sacrifice (Alma 34:10–14); [[Alma 36]] makes Christ the source of relief from guilt (Alma 36:17–20); and Mosiah 3:17 names no other saving means. Present Church teaching therefore rejects any idea that human beings should kill a sinner as a saving ordinance. Whether that correction represents prophetic fallibility, development, clarification, or repudiation is a further theological judgment, not something the historical record alone decides.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the sources require.** Multiple published sermon reports represent Young as teaching that an exceptional sinner's blood could have atoning or exaltational efficacy. They do not require treating every printed word as verbatim, the teaching as canon, or its implementation as widespread. Canon treats murder and innocent blood as exceptionally grave but supplies no ordinance for killing a sinner to save that sinner; D&C 42:79 instead directs murder cases to civil law. Neither the assumption that every presidential sermon is binding doctrine nor the assumption that an unofficial report has no historical value is warranted by the texts.

- **Historical-teaching model — independently supported.** Young taught the substance later called blood atonement. Two extended 1856–57 sermon reports and the acknowledgment in [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] strongly support this narrow conclusion.

- **Hyperbolic-revival model — plausible and independently supported.** Severe language was used principally to frighten hearers into repentance during the Mormon Reformation. Historical context supports this rhetorical function, but it does not erase the propositions communicated.

- **Literal theoretical-theocracy model — plausible.** Young may have believed the principle literally valid in an ideal divine order while regarding contemporary conditions as unsuitable for its general enforcement. References to consent and limited present applicability support this model, although the exact placement of the latter qualifier among the sermons requires verification.

- **Lawful-capital-punishment model — possible but incomplete.** Bloodshed may have meant an informed murderer's lawful execution rather than vigilantism. D&C 42:79 and later distinctions offer some support, but civil punishment alone does not explain the reports' language of atonement and exaltation.

- **Pure-metaphor model — ad hoc as a complete explanation.** Reformation rhetoric allows figurative coloring, but repeated and developed discussion of actually shedding the offender's blood fits poorly with a wholly metaphorical reading.

- **Transcription-distortion model — possible.** Journal of Discourses reporting and editing justify uncertainty about exact wording. A total-invention theory remains weak unless passage-specific shorthand or manuscript evidence shows that the central teaching was inserted or substantially altered.

- **Isolated-implementation model — plausible, moderately supported.** A small number of local Saints may have acted on the rhetoric without a regular institutional procedure. The Church essay's cautious concession supports this possibility.

- **Broader diffuse-implementation model — possible and seriously disputed.** Some historians interpret territorial records, testimony, and extralegal violence as evidence that the rhetoric influenced more incidents than the institutional essay concedes. This model does not require a centrally administered tribunal, but it must be tested case by case because hostile labeling, mixed motives, and weak records can produce false attribution.

- **Systematic secret-killing model — not independently supported by this dossier.** The sermons and violent climate make the allegation conceivable, but the supplied evidence documents no regular tribunal, reliable victim count, or set of identified orders from Young.

- **Development, correction, or repudiation model — descriptively supported, theologically open.** The historical sermon teaching is rejected by the Church today, while present materials center salvation in Christ. A critic may call this contradiction or reversal; a believer may call it correction of noncanonical speculation. The change is observable, but its theological meaning is not established by the historical documents.

**Set aside:** A truncated John Taylor fragment was not treated as corroboration because its argumentative context is unknown. Mountain Meadows was not treated either as automatic proof of institutional blood atonement or as irrelevant merely because no order from Young was found.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** Brigham Young is reported in more than one public sermon as teaching the core idea later called blood atonement. The official historical essay accepts that early leaders taught it and cites Young directly. It is therefore inaccurate to answer the title question with an unqualified no.

**Also established:** These sermons are not part of the standard works; Journal of Discourses transcription is imperfect; and the Church today says blood atonement is not its doctrine. The canonical profile of [[Brigham Young]] in D&C 124:127; 126:1; and 138:53 neither teaches nor endorses it. Canonical teaching about [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], especially Alma 34:10–14 and Mosiah 3:17, supplies the controlling present doctrinal framework.

**Open or disputed:** Young's exact spoken wording; the balance of literal belief and calculated hyperbole; what sins he consistently included; whether he envisioned only voluntary lawful execution; how directly the sermons caused particular acts; and the number and identity of any actual blood-atonement victims. Evidence for a centrally ordered or widespread program is not comparable in strength to evidence for the sermons themselves.

**Matter of faith:** Whether a prophet can teach a grave error in an unofficial sermon while retaining divine authority, whether later rejection is correction or unacceptable contradiction, and whether present teaching reflects continuing revelation cannot be settled merely by comparing historical documents. A faithful answer need not deny the record: it can acknowledge prophetic fallibility, condemn violent rhetoric, and give normative priority to Christ-centered canon and current teaching.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with the two primary published reports: Brigham Young's September 21, 1856 discourse in Journal of Discourses 4:51–57, especially 53–54, and his February 8, 1857 discourse in 4:215–221, especially 219–220. Read each sermon in full and describe its wording as a published report unless passage-specific shorthand evidence permits stronger attribution.

Next read the Church's Journal of Discourses overview and LaJean Purcell Carruth's research on discrepancies between preaching and publication. Then read [[Peace and Violence among 19th-Century Latter-day Saints (Essay)]] alongside both institutional and non-Church scholarship on the Mormon Reformation and territorial violence. Compare their handling of court records, contemporary correspondence, press accounts, and named incidents rather than accepting either minimizing or maximal claims wholesale.

For the surrounding crisis, study [[What was the Utah War]] and the documentary history behind [[What happened at Mountain Meadows]], including the testimony and trial record associated with [[John D. Lee]] and the local leadership role of [[Isaac C. Haight]]. These materials bear on implementation and influence but should not be assumed to prove a direct order from Young or a single motive for the massacre.

For doctrinal comparison, read [[Alma 34]], [[Alma 36]], Mosiah 3–4, D&C 42, D&C 98, and D&C 132. Alma 34:11's rejection of a merely human sacrifice is an especially pointed comparison. Read [[Chapter 5 Accepting the Atonement of Jesus Christ]] as evidence of later curated instructional emphasis, not as a complete inventory of Young's historical views. For biblical background, compare [[Exodus 12]], [[Leviticus 4]], Leviticus 16–17, and [[April 27–May 3. “Holiness to the Lord” Exodus 35–40; Leviticus 1; 4; 16; 19]].
