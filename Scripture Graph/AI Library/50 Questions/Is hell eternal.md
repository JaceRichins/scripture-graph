# Is hell eternal?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The canon does not use 'hell' as one univocal category, so no flat yes/no answer is available. For nearly everyone, what is called hell is temporary: Alma 40:11-14 places the wicked spirit's darkness within the interval 'until the time of their resurrection,' and D&C 76:81-89 describes telestial persons as not redeemed from the devil until the last resurrection, before they receive telestial glory. D&C 19:6-12 further cautions that 'eternal' and 'endless' punishment are explained in the text itself by reference to God as the author of the punishment, not simply asserted as claims about infinite duration — though the revelation never says the torment it discusses in fact ends, so this is a caution against one kind of inference, not a promise. Postmortem teaching (D&C 138) and provision for the unevangelized (D&C 137:7) show the intermediate state remains open to change for most people. Against this, D&C 76:31-38 reserves the 'second death' — non-redemption through the resurrection — for a narrowly named class, 'sons of perdition'; how much additional duration-language the text attaches to that class beyond exclusion from redemption is less certain than confident paraphrases suggest. New Testament and Book of Mormon passages (Matthew 25:46; Mark 9:43-48; Jacob 6:10; Revelation 20:10) use unqualified 'forever,' 'endless,' and 'unquenchable' language in judgment scenes distinct from Alma 40's pre-resurrection setting, and these are not resolved by appeal to D&C 19 alone. So: hell is not eternal for the vast majority — the temporariness of the ordinary intermediate state is well established in the text. Whether, and in what sense, the excluded remnant's exclusion is 'eternal' is affirmed for that narrow class, but is carried more by trust in the doctrine's internal logic than by a single decisive verse.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Explicit temporariness for the ordinary wicked.** Alma 40:12-14 twice bounds the wicked spirit's "awful state" as lasting only "until the time of their resurrection" — a stated terminus, not an open-ended sentence.
- **A direct scriptural redefinition of the key words.** D&C 19:6-12 states plainly, "it is not written that there shall be no end to this torment," and explains that "Eternal punishment is God's punishment" — i.e., a name, not necessarily a duration claim.
- **A narrow, named exception rather than a universal rule.** D&C 76:31-38, 44 confines the "second death" and the absence of forgiveness "in this world or the world to come" to "sons of perdition" specifically, while the bulk of the chapter (D&C 76:50-113) assigns the rest of humanity to one of three kingdoms of glory.
- **Postmortem teaching, not postmortem stasis.** D&C 138:7, quoting 1 Peter 3:18, grounds a vision in which Christ organizes missionary work among spirits in darkness (D&C 138), and D&C 137:7 promises celestial inheritance to the unevangelized dead who would have received the gospel — both incompatible with hell as an automatically endless, unchangeable state for most people.
- **Leadership acknowledges and addresses the tension directly.** Elder Quentin L. Cook's April 2009 talk names the exact difficulty — reconciling a loving Father with "the incorrect doctrine that most of mankind would be doomed to eternal hell" — and locates the answer in the kingdoms-of-glory framework rather than defending the traditional view.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
1. **Matthew’s grammatical parallel appears decisive.** Matthew 25:46 applies the same duration adjective to punishment and life. If eternal life is unending, treating eternal punishment as temporary can look lexically selective.

2. **The imagery repeatedly resists limitation.** Mark 9:43-48 speaks of unquenchable fire; Jacob 6:10 and Mosiah 3:27 use endless or forever-and-ever imagery; Revelation 20:10 explicitly says torment continues forever and ever. These are not minor or isolated warnings.

3. **D&C 19 does not actually say torment ends.** It says only that scripture does not state there is no end and explains punishment through God’s name. Divine authorship and endless duration are not mutually exclusive, so using D&C 19 to prove universal temporary punishment overreads it.

4. **Postmortal preaching does not equal eventual universal repentance.** D&C 138:57-59 conditions redemption on repentance and obedience. It neither promises that everyone accepts nor describes opportunities as temporally unlimited.

5. **Release from hell need not erase permanent loss.** D&C 76 assigns telestial persons glory, but not celestial life. A critic can therefore argue that temporary punitive confinement coexists with irreversible judgment and that calling the whole outcome “not eternal” understates its finality.

6. **The Restoration answer depends on later revelations.** Alma 40, D&C 19, D&C 76, and D&C 138 can be read coherently together, but that coherence is internal to an expanded Latter-day Saint canon. Readers who accept only the Bible may reasonably reach a different conclusion, and biblical scholarship remains divided among eternal-conscious-punishment, conditionalist, and universalist readings.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Latter-day Saint teaching distinguishes an intermediate spirit-world condition from a final post-judgment state. Matthew 25:46, Mark 9:43-48, Jacob 6:10, and 2 Nephi 9:16's severe warnings are read as addressed to the pre-resurrection darkness of ordinary unrepentant sinners — the condition Alma 40:12-14 places 'until the time of their resurrection,' and D&C 76:81-89 places 'until the last resurrection' before assigning telestial glory. On this reading, 'no end' and 'unquenchable' language conveys the certainty and severity of consequence while a person remains unrepentant, not literal endlessness for that population. D&C 19:6-12 supports treating 'eternal'/'endless punishment' as a title (God's own name for the punishment) rather than a bare duration claim, though the revelation is explicit only about its own immediate subject and never reapplies that gloss to Matthew, Jacob, or D&C 76. D&C 76:31-38 keeps a real, narrower exception: sons of perdition alone are not redeemed through the resurrection and are subject to the second death. This exclusion should rest on 'second death,' 'no forgiveness,' and continuation 'with the devil and his angels' (v.32) rather than on the claim that D&C 76 repeats the specific 'eternal/endless/everlasting punishment' vocabulary for that class — that exact phrase-matching is disputed and not clearly present in the verse range. D&C 137:7 and D&C 138 answer the fairness objection for the unevangelized by describing judgment according to what a person would have received and by depicting organized postmortem teaching, without promising universal acceptance. Revelation 20:10's 'for ever and ever' is explicitly attached to the devil, beast, and false prophet; extending it to every occupant of the lake of fire in verses 14-15 is a plausible but interpretive step, not a stated conclusion. None of this proves the traditionalist reading wrong on its own terms — a reader working from the Bible alone may reasonably reach a different conclusion — but it shows the Restoration canon's harmonization is coherent on its own premises.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text itself requires:** universal resurrection with no annihilation; a pre-resurrection intermediate state differentiated into paradise and a state of darkness/misery, explicitly bounded 'until...resurrection' (Alma 40:11-14); a post-resurrection judgment (D&C 76) sorting people into kingdoms of glory; and one explicitly named class, 'sons of perdition,' excluded from redemption through the resurrection and subject to the 'second death' (D&C 76:31-38). It also requires reading 'eternal'/'endless' language with some caution: D&C 19:6-12 explains that these terms can name the punishment's divine author rather than assert its duration — a caution, not a general decoder ring for every other occurrence.

**What is commonly assumed beyond this:** that every occurrence of 'hell' names the same postmortem state (Amos 9's use as the deepest hiding place and Alma 54's rhetorical military use show otherwise); that D&C 19's gloss automatically controls every other 'no end' passage, including Jacob 6:10, 2 Nephi 9:16, or D&C 76's language for perdition (the text never states this); and that D&C 76 repeats the specific 'eternal/endless/everlasting punishment' vocabulary for sons of perdition — closer reading finds mainly 'second death,' 'no forgiveness,' and continuation 'with the devil...in eternity' (v.32), which supports lasting exclusion without needing that disputed phrase.

**Serious models the text permits, both directions:**
- **Two-stage/polysemous hell — independently supported.** Alma 40 gives an intermediate wicked state; D&C 76:81-89 gives telestial hell an explicit endpoint; D&C 76:31-38 separately names a final, non-redeemed class. This best preserves all three without flattening them. See [[Death and the Spirit World]], [[Final Judgment]].
- **Near-universal graded salvation with a narrow final exception — independently supported.** D&C 76:39-113 extends resurrection and some glory broadly; D&C 137:7 and D&C 138 extend mercy to the unevangelized; only sons of perdition are excluded. See [[Kingdoms of Glory (True to the Faith)]], [[Plan of Salvation]].
- **Divine-title semantics for 'eternal'/'endless' — plausible, but locally bounded.** D&C 19:6-12 blocks a naive inference from the label alone to infinite duration for its own subject; silently extending it to Matthew 25:46 or D&C 76 is an interpretive choice the text does not make explicit.
- **Classical eternal conscious torment for the wicked generally — plausible on a plain reading of several passages taken alone.** Matthew 25:41-46, Mark 9:43-48, Jacob 6:10, 2 Nephi 9:16, Mosiah 3:27, and Revelation 20:10 use unqualified duration language; as a reading of the whole Restoration canon it must explain why D&C 76 gives telestial hell an endpoint.
- **Enduring exclusion for a limited class (sons of perdition) — independently supported, modestly stated.** D&C 76:31-38 repeatedly separates this class and denies it redemption through the resurrection; the text supports lasting exclusion more securely than it supports any particular account of what that exclusion consists of or how many it includes.
- **Strict universal reconciliation, including sons of perdition — speculative/ad hoc against this text.** Nothing in the corpus states this class is later restored; D&C 76:31-38 reads as a stated exception.
- **Annihilationism — considered and set aside.** Universal, unconditional resurrection for all (Alma 11:45 and parallels) excludes non-existence as anyone's final destiny.
- **All hell-language is purely rhetorical — considered and set aside as a totalizing claim.** Amos 9, Alma 54, and D&C 122-123 are clearly rhetorical, but Alma 40, D&C 76, and D&C 138 describe an actual condition of departed spirits, not only a figure of speech.
- **Hell as subjective incompatibility with holiness — a partial experiential gloss, not independently sufficient.** A Journal of Discourses sermon imagines a corrupt person finding even a pure society hellish; illuminating for self-alienation, but later homiletic material, not a substitute for the canon's judgment/resurrection/kingdoms/second-death vocabulary.

**Considered and set aside:** Amos 9:2-4 was set aside as evidence about postmortal duration — its heaven-to-hell contrast is about the reach of judgment, not a map of the afterlife. The registry's calibrated finding on Literary Intertextuality in 3 Nephi 11 (Biblical/KJV mediation, including 'gates of hell' language) was also set aside here: its strong-band support concerns New Testament textual mediation in 3 Nephi 11's English wording, not the duration of punishment, and should not be recruited into this question.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** no one is annihilated; the ordinary pre-resurrection wicked state is explicitly temporary, ending at resurrection (Alma 40:11-14); telestial hell has a stated endpoint at 'the last resurrection' before telestial glory (D&C 76:81-89); the intermediate state remains open to teaching and possible change (D&C 138; D&C 137:7); and one explicitly named class, sons of perdition, is excluded from redemption through the resurrection and subject to the second death (D&C 76:31-38). **Open even within scripture:** whether D&C 19's redefinition of 'eternal'/'endless' as titles of God is meant to reach the Book of Mormon's own unqualified 'no end' language (Jacob 6:10; 2 Nephi 9:16) or Matthew 25:46; and whether D&C 76 itself repeats that same duration vocabulary for sons of perdition or instead relies only on 'second death' and non-redemption language — the more cautious reading favors the latter, and confident claims that the canon calls perdition's punishment 'eternal/endless/everlasting' in so many words should be checked against the actual verse range. **A matter of faith beyond the text:** how rare sons of perdition actually are (the canon does not quantify this), what their exclusion is like experientially, and whether the internal Restoration harmonization reflects genuine revelation rather than later theological development — these rest on trust in the coherence of continuing revelation more than on any single additional verse.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read the passages in conceptual order rather than searching the word “hell” indiscriminately:

1. [[Alma 40]]—especially Alma 40:6-14—for the intermediate state.
2. [[D&C 19]]—especially D&C 19:4-20—for the revealed explanation of endless and eternal punishment and the continuing seriousness of repentance.
3. [[D&C 76]]—D&C 76:31-49 and 81-112—for second death, temporary hell, and differentiated glory.
4. [[D&C 137]] and [[D&C 138]]—especially D&C 137:7-9 and D&C 138:18-35, 57-59—for the unevangelized and redemption among the dead.
5. [[Matthew 25]], [[Mark 9]], [[Jacob 6]], [[Mosiah 3]], and [[Revelation 20]] for the strongest texts favoring enduring punishment.
6. [[Amos 9]] as a control showing that a reference to hell need not describe the postmortal world.

Then compare the canonical synthesis with [[Kingdoms of Glory (True to the Faith)]], [[Our Father’s Plan—Big Enough for All His Children (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2009)]], and [[Conversion to the Will of God (Elder Quentin L. Cook, April 2022)]]. The latter reports Frederic Farrar’s critique of English damnation terminology; treat that as a historical-linguistic argument requiring examination of particular Hebrew and Greek expressions, not as a shortcut that dissolves every judgment passage.
