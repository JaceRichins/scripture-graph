# Why does God allow suffering?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Scripture does not give a single answer, and the book that faces the
question most directly — Job — ends with God declining to explain. What the
canon does offer: suffering is bound up with agency (a world where choices
matter is a world where they can hurt), with mortality as a school of
growth ([[2 Nephi 2]]: "opposition in all things"), and with a God who does
not stand outside it — the Restoration's most distinctive claim is that God
weeps ([[Moses 7]]) and that Christ descended below all things
([[D&C 122]], [[Alma 7]]) precisely so that no suffering is unaccompanied.
Whether that is an answer or a companionship depends on the sufferer.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Meaningful freedom requires real consequences; a world in which no
  choice could cause harm would be a world without moral agents.
  Latter-day Saint theology places agency at the center of the plan
  ([[2 Nephi 2]], [[Moses 4]]), which makes this argument structural, not
  incidental.
- Much good is impossible without hardship: courage, compassion,
  endurance, forgiveness. The Book of Mormon frames adversity as the
  condition of growth ([[Ether 12]], [[Alma 62]]), not as punishment.
- Restoration doctrine limits God's responsibility in ways classical
  theism does not: God organizes eternal intelligences rather than creating
  them from nothing ([[Abraham 3]]), and works within a moral order he did
  not invent ([[Alma 42]]).
- The Christian answer is finally Christological: God does not explain
  suffering, he enters it ([[Isaiah 53]], [[Mosiah 14]], [[Alma 7]]).
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- Free will explains moral evil but not natural evil: earthquakes,
  childhood cancer, and animal suffering across deep time involve no human
  choice.
- The *quantity* of suffering vastly exceeds what soul-making would need;
  a child who dies of famine at two is not being educated.
- "God works within constraints" limits God's power in ways many believers
  are unwilling to accept, and "God weeps" describes empathy, not rescue.
- Appeals to a future compensation do not make present horror good; they
  only promise that it will be outweighed.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believers concede that no theodicy fully works and that Job is the model:
lament is faithful, and demanding an explanation is not a sin. They add
that Restoration theology is unusually willing to say God is *not*
omnipotent in the philosopher's sense — he cannot make agents free and
guarantee their choices, cannot make growth costless — and that this
lowers the bar the problem of evil sets. On natural evil, the "opposition"
doctrine holds that a lawful, physical, risky world is the only kind in
which embodied agents can exist and learn. And the promise of compensation
([[D&C 122]]: "all these things shall give thee experience") is meant not as
an argument but as something to hold onto.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
This is the strongest argument against God and every honest tradition
says so. The Restoration does not solve it; it changes the picture — a God
of limits, a God who suffers, agents who chose this — in ways that make
faith more livable without making the pain smaller. The most truthful thing
a believer can say to someone in it is not a theodicy but "I do not know
why, and I know he is there" — which is what Job was given.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
