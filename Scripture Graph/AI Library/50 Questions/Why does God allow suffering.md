# Why does God allow suffering?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Scripture supplies several partial answers, not one explanation covering every case. It connects some suffering to morally consequential [[Agency]] and a mortal order containing real opposition ([[2 Nephi 2]]; [[Moses 7]]), presents mortality as probation ([[Abraham 3]]), and sometimes shows adversity becoming an occasion for endurance or growth ([[Mosiah 24]]; D&C 122:5–9). Yet [[Job 1]] rejects a simple guilt theory, [[Alma 62]] shows that affliction does not automatically sanctify, and [[Job 38]] never gives Job the case-specific explanation available to the reader. The clearest Christian response is therefore not a complete causal theodicy but the claim that [[Jesus Christ]] enters suffering, succors sufferers, and promises redemption ([[Alma 7]]; [[D&C 122]]; Romans 8:18–23). Why this person suffered this horror, why natural and animal suffering are so extensive, and why God intervenes in some cases but apparently not others remain open.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The canon itself blocks a universal punishment theory.** Job is called upright before his losses, and the narrative locates their immediate cause in a heavenly challenge rather than an identified sin (Job 1:1, 8–12; [[Job 1]]). [[Alma 62]] further reports that the same broad affliction hardened some and softened others (Alma 62:41).

- **Opposition and agency are genuinely integrated, but only as a partial explanation.** [[2 Nephi 2]] moves through law, opposition, enticement, the Fall, redemption, and consequential choice as one argument (2 Nephi 2:5–11, 15–16, 21–27). [[Moses 7]] explicitly juxtaposes agency and commands to love with hatred and violence (Moses 7:32–33). This strongly bears on moral evil, but not directly on earthquakes, disease, or animal pain.

- **Divine compassion is central to the final form of Moses 7.** God, heaven, Enoch, and the earth participate in a distributed pattern of grief (Moses 7:28–29, 40–49). The registry rates this literary pattern strong at 0.85. It supports compassionate grief as a central theme, not a complete metaphysics of divine emotion and not an explanation for divine nonprevention.

- **The Christological texts clearly promise solidarity and succor.** Alma says Christ undergoes pains, sicknesses, death, and infirmities so that he may know how to succor his people (Alma 7:11–13; [[Alma 7]]). In [[D&C 122]], the Son of Man's descent below the ordeal catalogue immediately grounds the command to continue (D&C 122:5–9). These are strong textual answers to whether sufferers are abandoned; they do not tell why each event occurred.

- **Some texts distinguish sustaining grace from immediate rescue.** In [[Mosiah 24]], strength to bear burdens precedes deliverance (Mosiah 24:13–16, 20–22). D&C 121:7–8 offers Joseph Smith future perspective without calling the persecution itself trivial; the [[Liberty Jail]] setting matters.

- **The hope is restorative and eschatological.** Romans 8:18–23 depicts both creation and believers groaning while awaiting redemption. The passage recognizes suffering as creation-wide and unfinished rather than pretending that every present loss is already intelligible.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The evidential problem remains severe.** Even if free choice explains some cruelty, the scale, intensity, and apparently uneven distribution of suffering can make an all-knowing, perfectly good, maximally powerful God less likely. A merely possible unknown reason answers a strict logical contradiction more readily than it answers this cumulative evidential challenge.

- **Natural and prehuman suffering are not explained by human agency.** Earthquakes, congenital disease, childhood cancer, predation, and animal suffering long before human moral choice require a different account. [[2 Nephi 2]] states a need for opposition but does not demonstrate that this amount or distribution of pain is necessary.

- **Some suffering destroys rather than develops agency.** Torture, profound trauma, dementia, infant death, and deprivation can remove opportunities to learn or choose. Calling all such cases 'soul-making' risks treating victims as means and is contradicted by Alma 62:41, where affliction has divergent effects.

- **Permission still carries moral responsibility.** Job 1 portrays God as permitting the adversary to harm an upright man. Saying that another agent directly caused the harm does not by itself explain why a capable protector allowed it.

- **Selective intervention sharpens the question.** Scriptural narratives describe particular rescues, healings, and answers. If intervention is sometimes compatible with agency and stable natural order, it is fair to ask why similarly situated sufferers receive radically different outcomes.

- **Foreknowledge complicates the free-will defense.** If God knowingly creates or initiates a world containing foreseeable horrors, critics argue that creaturely responsibility does not exhaust divine responsibility for choosing that world or declining to intervene.

- **Compensation is not justification.** Future healing or glory might repair a victim's loss, but it does not automatically make preventable abuse a morally acceptable means. The victim's eventual good and the perpetrator's wrongdoing must not be conflated.

- **Divine empathy is not rescue.** [[Moses 7]] may present a God who grieves, and [[Alma 7]] a Christ who suffers and succors, but compassion alone does not answer why an omnipotent being does not prevent more harm.

- **Job's ending can appear morally incomplete.** Divine wisdom and creative power do not straightforwardly justify permitting the deaths and losses of Job 1. Job is never told the narrative's heavenly explanation, and replacement blessings do not make lost persons interchangeable.

- **A limited-God solution has costs.** Reading [[Abraham 3]] and [[Alma 42]] as strict limits on divine power may reduce one form of the problem, but those chapters do not spell out a complete finite-God theology. The proposal can conflict with stronger traditional claims about omnipotence and providence.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believing responses are strongest when offered cumulatively and modestly. First, [[Agency]] provides a substantial explanation for moral evil: love, trust, responsibility, and covenant would be hollow if choices could never produce real consequences. Moses 7:32–33 supports that connection, but believers should admit its limited reach.

Second, a regular embodied world may require stable processes that permit both life and injury. This is a **possible philosophical extension**, not an explicit teaching of [[2 Nephi 2]], and it still must confront excessive, apparently gratuitous, and prehuman suffering.

Third, opposition and probation can make courage, compassion, repentance, and endurance possible ([[2 Nephi 2]]; [[Abraham 3]]). Yet [[Alma 62]] prevents the overclaim that every affliction was individually assigned or inevitably improves its victim. [[Is Faith in the Atonement of Jesus Christ Written in Our Hearts (Linda K. Burton, October 2012)]] and [[Opposition in All Things (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2016)]] articulate this growth framework as modern Church teaching, not as a mechanically complete explanation of every tragedy.

Fourth, Latter-day Saint scripture can be read as placing God within an order containing preexistent persons, materials, and nonarbitrary justice ([[Abraham 3]]; [[Alma 42]]). That may qualify some classical assumptions behind the problem, but the texts do not specify exactly what God cannot prevent. It is more defensible as a limited possibility than as a settled solution.

Fifth, Christian scripture shifts from explanation to divine participation. [[Alma 7]] teaches that Christ's suffering equips him to succor; [[D&C 122]] frames endurance through the Son of Man's descent; [[Moses 7]] depicts divine grief. This can support trust that sufferers are neither unseen nor finally abandoned, while leaving the permission question intact.

Sixth, future redemption may transform or restore what present life leaves unresolved (Romans 8:18–23). Whether such restoration is real, sufficient, and freely welcomed is a matter of faith; it should never be used to call present horror good or to excuse a perpetrator.

Finally, scripture authorizes lament and action. [[Job 38]] does not condemn every demand for understanding, and D&C 123:1–17 requires documentation and public exposure of abuse. [[“You Are My Hands” (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, April 2010)]] rejects victim-blaming, while [[The Sanctifying Work of Welfare (Bishop H. David Burton, April 2011)]] emphasizes bearing others' burdens. The faithful response to suffering is therefore not only interpretation but protection, relief, truth-telling, and companionship.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established by the cited texts:** Scripture recognizes innocent or non-retributive suffering; treats agency and opposition as real features of mortality; shows that affliction can have opposite effects; portrays faithful lament and active resistance as permissible; and teaches that Christ suffers, succors, and promises redemption. The strong literary pattern in [[Moses 7]] establishes compassionate grief as a central textual theme, not a complete account of God's emotional metaphysics.

**Open:** The texts do not disclose why each person suffers, why natural and animal pain have their observed scale, what interventions are compatible with divine purposes, or whether every apparently gratuitous evil has an unknown justification. They do not demonstrate that this world contains the minimum suffering required for agency or growth. [[Job 38]] supports humility about human knowledge, but ignorance is not positive evidence for a particular hidden reason.

**Matter of interpretation:** Agency, probation, stable natural law, character formation, cosmic conflict, and divine constraints can each explain part of the data. None covers the whole without qualifications. Reading [[Abraham 3]] as substantially revising classical omnipotence is possible but disputed; reading every affliction as personalized curriculum is unsupported.

**Matter of faith:** That God is perfectly good, that Christ's succor reaches every sufferer, that losses can finally be healed without trivializing them, and that no innocent suffering will be meaningless or final are theological commitments rather than conclusions established by the occurrence of suffering itself. The most defensible believing answer is therefore cumulative and modest: some reasons are textually identifiable, divine companionship and redemption are promised, practical relief is commanded, and many particular 'whys' remain unanswered.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Job 1]] alongside [[Job 38]] to preserve the difference between what the narrator knows and what Job is told. Then compare [[2 Nephi 2]] on opposition and agency, [[Moses 7]] on divine and created grief, [[Alma 7]] on Christ's succor, [[Mosiah 24]] on strength before deliverance, [[Alma 62]] on divergent responses to affliction, [[Abraham 3]] on probation, [[Alma 42]] on justice and mercy, [[D&C 122]] in its [[Liberty Jail]] setting, and Romans 8 on creation-wide groaning and redemption.

For modern Latter-day Saint teaching, compare [[Opposition in All Things (Elder Dallin H. Oaks, April 2016)]], [[The Atonement Covers All Pain (Elder Kent F. Richards, April 2011)]], [[Remember Thy Suffering Saints, O Our God (Elder Anthony D. Perkins, October 2021)]], and [[“You Are My Hands” (President Dieter F. Uchtdorf, April 2010)]]. Treat these as pastoral and doctrinal interpretation rather than additions to the scriptural wording.

For broader philosophical comparison, read Eleonore Stump, *Wandering in Darkness*; Marilyn McCord Adams, *Horrendous Evils and the Goodness of God*; C. S. Lewis, *The Problem of Pain* together with the less systematic *A Grief Observed*; Terryl and Fiona Givens, *The God Who Weeps*; and David Bentley Hart, *The Doors of the Sea*. Compare their different assumptions about omnipotence, freedom, natural law, formation, divine passibility, and future restoration rather than treating them as one solution.

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot
<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
### What the texts require—and what they do not

The cited texts require a world of consequential choice and opposition ([[2 Nephi 2]]), acknowledge innocent or at least non-retributive suffering ([[Job 1]]), portray both human and creation-wide groaning (Romans 8:18–23), permit suffering to have divergent moral effects (Alma 62:41), and center divine compassion, Christ's participation, succor, and hoped-for redemption ([[Moses 7]]; [[Alma 7]]; [[D&C 122]]). They do **not** require that God individually causes each tragedy, that every affliction is punishment, that every victim consented premortally to a particular ordeal, that suffering always improves character, or that readers can identify God's reason in a given case. Objections to guaranteed soul-making attach to an extrapolation, not to Alma 62:41; objections to universal retribution attack a model that Job itself contests.

### Serious reconciling models

- **Independently supported, but limited — agency and consequential freedom.** [[2 Nephi 2]] and Moses 7:32–33 directly connect agency, alternatives, accountability, love, and violence. This is strong for moral harms caused by agents. It is incomplete for natural evil, animal suffering, involuntary illness, and God's selective nonintervention.

- **Independently supported as a general setting — opposition and probation.** Second Nephi 2:11–27 and Abraham 3:24–26 present mortality as involving opposition, choice, and proving. They support a general mortal framework. The stronger assertion that every particular horror is necessary or individually calibrated is **ad hoc** unless additional evidence is supplied.

- **Plausible — formative or soul-making adversity.** Mosiah 24:13–16, Ether 12:27, and D&C 122:5–7 permit some hardship to become an occasion for humility, strength, experience, or good. Alma 62:41 supplies an internal control: suffering can also harden. The model is plausible case by case, not a universal law.

- **Possible — stable-law embodied world.** A dependable physical order may make embodied action, prediction, responsibility, and community possible while also permitting collision, disease, and disaster. This can extend the opposition framework to some natural evil, but the cited passages do not state the model or show why the actual severity and distribution are necessary. Presented as a full solution, it becomes **ad hoc**.

- **Plausible in specified cases, rejected as universal — consequence or judgment.** Scripture sometimes interprets suffering as the consequence of sin or covenant breach, and [[Alma 42]] preserves accountability. But [[Job 1]] and Alma 62:41 rule out inferring personal guilt merely from suffering. Universal retribution was considered and set aside because the canon itself supplies counterexamples.

- **Plausible within the narrative world — cosmic conflict.** Job 1, Moses 4, and Revelation 2:10 portray adversarial agency operating under divine sovereignty or permission. This can locate some evil in hostile agency, but it intensifies rather than removes the question of permission and should not be used to diagnose an individual sufferer's unseen cause.

- **Independently supported as response, not cause — divine solidarity and atoning succor.** Moses 7:28–49, Alma 7:11–13, and D&C 122:8 strongly support divine grief, Christ's participation, and succor. This model answers abandonment and final healing more directly than causal permission. Treating empathy alone as a sufficient theodicy was considered and set aside.

- **Independently supported as canonical hope; metaphysical fulfillment remains faith — eschatological restoration.** Romans 8:18–23 places suffering within an unfinished movement toward redemption and glory. This may answer whether evil has the final word. It does not follow that later compensation retrospectively makes cruelty good or morally necessary.

- **Possible and disputed — constrained divine action.** Abraham 3:18, 22–25 and Alma 42:13, 22–25 can be read as locating God amid eternal persons, available materials, and nonarbitrary justice. Such a model may reduce the force of objections aimed specifically at unrestricted classical omnipotence. The texts do not enumerate God's limits, however; claiming that God could not prevent any specific tragedy is **ad hoc** without further support.

- **Plausible but nonexplanatory — epistemic humility or skeptical theism.** [[Job 38]] supports a large disparity between divine and human knowledge. That makes undiscovered reasons possible, but indiscriminate appeal to mystery risks becoming unfalsifiable and cannot license confidence that a particular tragedy was necessary.

- **Plausible pastoral model — protest without total explanation.** Job's complaints, God's engagement with Job, and the action program of D&C 123 permit lament, unanswered questioning, resistance, and practical solidarity to coexist with faith. This declines a comprehensive theodicy rather than solving one.

### Serious critical models

- **Plausible — the evidential argument from apparently gratuitous suffering.** The quantity, severity, randomness, and destruction of agency caused by suffering may count against the conjunction of exhaustive foreknowledge, perfect goodness, and unrestricted power. The scriptural frameworks make some reasons possible but do not demonstrate that every apparently gratuitous instance has a morally sufficient reason.

- **Possible — a powerful but limited or morally opaque deity.** The texts could be read as showing genuine divine opposition, grief, permission, and delayed victory rather than effortless control. This interpretation explains nonprevention more directly, but may conflict with other canonical claims about divine sovereignty and does not by itself establish whether the limitation is metaphysical, moral, strategic, or merely apparent.

- **Possible as an external explanation — indifferent natural processes.** On a non-theistic model, suffering follows from biology, geology, scarcity, and competing organisms without a providential reason. This fits the apparent randomness of many events but does not adjudicate the separate evidence for or against [[Does God exist]], nor does it answer the human search for redemption.

### Models set aside

A universal punishment model is set aside because of Job. Guaranteed character formation is set aside because of Alma 62:41 and suffering that destroys developmental capacity. Individual premortal consent to every ordeal is set aside because Abraham 3 teaches probation but not such individualized agreements. The claim that God causes evil so that he can heal it is set aside because the cited texts distinguish hostile agency, mortality, succor, and redemption rather than requiring that inference. The claim that replacing Job's losses erases the moral problem is set aside because persons are not interchangeable goods.
