# Why did Emma Smith deny plural marriage?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
No surviving source establishes with certainty why [[Emma Smith]] denied that [[Joseph Smith Jr]] practiced plural marriage. In the 1879 “Last Testimony,” recorded by her sons [[Joseph Smith III]] and Alexander Hale Smith, she denied other wives and denied that Joseph taught or received a revelation authorizing polygamy. Those statements conflict with D&C 132:51–56 and with cumulative historical evidence that Joseph entered plural relationships and that Emma knew of and temporarily approved at least some before opposing them. Possible explanations include sincere later memory or reconstruction, rejection of the sealings as legitimate marriages, incomplete knowledge of their extent, protection of Joseph or her children, institutional loyalty, or conscious strategic denial. The evidence supports historical inaccuracy more strongly than it supports any conclusion about Emma's intent.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Emma's denial is genuine evidence of her public position.** The 1879 interview records her denying both other wives and a revelation authorizing plural marriage. It is late, mediated testimony, but it cannot simply be discarded.

- **A contemporary document places Emma inside the conflict.** D&C 132:51–56 names Emma, tells her to receive women given to Joseph, commands her to cleave to him, threatens destruction for failure to abide the command, and tells her to forgive Joseph's unspecified trespasses. The document does not record her response or prove its divine origin.

- **The wider record supports temporary knowledge and acceptance.** [[Plural Marriage in Kirtland and Nauvoo (Essay)]] concludes that Emma approved four marriages for a time before again opposing the practice. [[Mercy Thompson and the Revelation on Marriage]] and [[41 God Must Be the Judge]] provide documentary context, although important details rely partly on Clayton's later, interested testimony.

- **The cumulative case for Joseph's plural relationships is much broader than one interview.** The 1843 revelation, contemporary records, sealing documentation, and testimony from participating women make Emma's categorical denial historically unreliable as evidence that no practice occurred. They do not establish her intent, complete knowledge, or the character of every relationship.

- **The evidence for motive is sparse.** No surviving contemporaneous diary or explanatory statement from Emma tells why she later answered as she did. Post-1844 conflict with the apostles and the anti-polygamy setting of [[Joseph Smith III]]'s church supply context, not proof of motive.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objection to a sympathetic reconstruction is straightforward: Emma knew about some plural marriages and later categorically denied them, so the ordinary description is that she gave false testimony. D&C 132:51–56, the 1843 manuscript, reports of her temporary approval, and testimonies of participating women make complete ignorance historically implausible. Family loyalty and the anti-polygamy position of [[Joseph Smith III]] supplied an evident incentive to conceal damaging facts.

A second objection concerns source criticism. Many details about Emma’s knowledge come from Utah-based plural-marriage participants speaking years later while defending a contested institution. Later affidavits can preserve authentic memory, but they can also standardize stories, exaggerate certainty, or serve institutional needs. The 1879 interview is likewise interested testimony: it was conducted by Emma’s son and supported his church’s position. Neither side’s late evidence should receive automatic privilege.

A third objection challenges believing appeals to D&C 132. The revelation gives Joseph authority for relationships that directly benefited him, commands Emma’s compliance, acknowledges Joseph’s unspecified trespasses, and threatens Emma with destruction. Critics reasonably see a strong appearance of self-authorization and coercion. Establishing that the document existed in 1843 does not establish that God authored it.

Finally, proposed semantic reconciliations can become special pleading. Emma’s reference to what she knew may limit some answers, and she may have denied that the sealings were valid marriages; nevertheless, her reported statement that Joseph had no other wife is broad. “She meant something else by wife” is possible but lacks a surviving explanation from Emma and cannot simply be asserted.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believers need not defend Emma’s denial as literally accurate. They can acknowledge that the cumulative evidence overwhelmingly supports Joseph’s plural-marriage practice while refusing to infer Emma’s subjective intent from contradiction alone. A false statement can arise through conscious concealment, reconstructed memory, narrowed definitions, trauma, loyalty, or several causes at once.

They can also distinguish three questions often collapsed together: whether Joseph entered plural sealings, how Emma understood or recognized those relationships, and why she later denied them. The first is strongly established historically; the second is established only in part; the third remains open. Emma’s temporary acceptance of some sealings does not mean she knew all of them or continued to regard any as valid.

Believers may regard D&C 132 as revelation and Joseph’s plural marriages as divinely authorized, but faith in that claim does not erase the text’s severity or Emma’s suffering. D&C 132:54 contains an explicit threat, while Jacob 2:31–35 treats women’s grief under abusive plural practice as morally serious. Honest belief can therefore include sympathy for Emma’s resistance and recognition that secrecy, uneven disclosure, and conflicting marital obligations created profound harm.

Conversely, rejecting the divine origin of D&C 132 does not prove Emma knowingly lied in 1879. It strengthens a critical account of the Nauvoo conflict but still leaves her late psychology and wording underdetermined.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the sources require.** D&C 132:51–56 portrays Emma as Joseph's wife, addresses her concerning women given to him, commands compliance and forgiveness, and records no response. It does not identify the women, establish what Emma was actually told, narrate delivery of the revelation, or explain her 1879 testimony. The later interview establishes that Emma publicly denied the practice. Its separate answers must not be collapsed: her response about other wives was knowledge-qualified in common transcriptions, while her denial that a revelation on polygamy or spiritual wives existed was more categorical. Neither source directly discloses her motive.

- **Historically inaccurate denial — independently supported.** Multiple evidence streams support Joseph's plural relationships and Emma's awareness of at least some. This best fits the cumulative record, but establishes inaccuracy more readily than conscious deception.

- **Conscious family or institutional protection — plausible.** Acknowledgment could have harmed Joseph's reputation, Emma's identity as his wife, and [[Joseph Smith III]]'s anti-polygamy position. The context supplies incentive, but Emma left no admission of this motive.

- **Rejection-of-validity model — possible.** Emma may have regarded concealed, repudiated, or unauthorized sealings as invalid and therefore not as true marriages. Her opposition makes this intelligible, but no surviving definition from Emma shows that it governed her answers, especially her categorical denial of a revelation.

- **Partial knowledge — independently supported only in limited form.** Secrecy makes uneven knowledge likely, and knowing some relationships would not mean knowing all. This can explain knowledge-qualified answers but cannot by itself explain denial of every other wife or of the revelation.

- **Sincere memory reconstruction — possible.** Thirty-five years, grief, conflict, and a settled later identity could have shaped recollection. This is a general psychological possibility, not a documented diagnosis of Emma.

- **Semantic distinctions among sealing, marriage, and sexual relations — possible.** The relationships were not uniform, so distinctions may explain parts of the testimony. Using an undocumented definition to neutralize every denial would be ad hoc.

- **Interview mediation — possible.** Joseph Smith III and Alexander Hale Smith selected questions and transmitted the testimony in an anti-polygamy institutional setting. That warrants caution; alleging invention or alteration without manuscript evidence would be ad hoc.

- **Challenged revelation provenance — serious critical model.** A critic may read D&C 132 as a human document that gives Joseph authority benefiting him, commands Emma's compliance, and threatens her. Its existence in 1843 does not prove divine authorship or perfect representation of Emma's knowledge. This model explains resistance but still does not establish why she spoke as she did in 1879.

- **Complete sincere ignorance — set aside as historically implausible.** The revelation's direct address and evidence of temporary approval make ignorance of the whole practice difficult to sustain, although ignorance of particular relationships remains plausible.

- **Simple liar model — set aside as underdetermined.** It explains contradiction economically but treats an inaccurate statement as direct evidence of intent. The sources do not reveal Emma's mental state.

- **Perfect harmonization — set aside as ad hoc.** Claims that Emma meant only sexual polygamy, only Utah polygamy, or only marriages she regarded as valid lack an explanatory statement from Emma capable of reconciling all her answers.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** [[Emma Smith]] publicly denied Joseph's plural marriage in 1879. D&C 132:51–56 places her within a represented plural-marriage conflict, and the cumulative historical record strongly establishes that [[Joseph Smith Jr]] entered plural relationships. Evidence that Emma knew of and temporarily approved some relationships is substantial, though details rely partly on retrospective testimony. Her denial is therefore not a reliable guide to whether Joseph practiced plural marriage.

**Open:** The full extent of Emma's knowledge, her definitions of wife, marriage, and sealing, and the relative influence of memory, legitimacy judgments, personal identity, family loyalty, institutional interests, or conscious strategy remain unresolved. No single motive should be presented as established.

**Matter of faith and moral judgment:** Historical inquiry can evaluate the revelation's date, provenance, use, and relationship to the practice. It cannot establish divine authorship or authorization, nor determine God's ultimate judgment of Joseph or Emma. Belief in D&C 132 should not erase its coercive language or Emma's suffering; criticism of the revelation should not be converted into proof of her private intent.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Read D&C 132:7, 37–44, and 51–66, distinguishing the text's claims from later identifications of individual women.
- Compare D&C 25, D&C 42:22, and Jacob 2:23–35 for the canon's differing portraits of Emma and its marriage norms and claimed exceptions.
- Read the complete 1879 “Last Testimony of Sister Emma,” noting separately the questions about other wives and about a revelation on polygamy or spiritual wives.
- Examine the July 12, 1843 revelation manuscript and the Kingsbury copy, then compare William Clayton's July 12 journal with his 1874 affidavit and 1886 statement rather than treating them as one evidentiary layer.
- Consult [[Plural Marriage in Kirtland and Nauvoo (Essay)]], [[Plural Marriage (Gospel Topics)]], [[Mercy Thompson and the Revelation on Marriage]], [[40 United in an Everlasting Covenant]], [[41 God Must Be the Judge]], and [[46 Endowed with Power]].
