# Who decided which books are in the Bible?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
No one person or council chose every book in “the Bible.” The Jewish scriptures developed through the preservation and recognition of the Torah, Prophets, and other writings; early Christians inherited those scriptures and gradually recognized apostolic and widely received Christian writings. Disagreement remained around several books: [[Eusebius]] still recorded acknowledged and disputed writings in the early fourth century, [[Athanasius]] supplied the earliest surviving exact list of the now-common twenty-seven-book New Testament in 367, and regional North African councils later listed the same New Testament. [[Nicaea]] did not leave a canon decision. Much later, Roman Catholic and Protestant authorities defined different Old Testament boundaries for their own communions, and Christian canons still differ. Historical evidence can describe this process; whether divine providence guided it is a theological judgment.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The Bible contains no complete table of contents.** Luke 24:44, 2 Timothy 3:15-17, and 2 Peter 3:15-16 recognize scriptures or developing scriptural collections, but none enumerates the future Bible.

- **The scriptural collections show development and selection.** The doxologies at Psalms 41:13, 72:19, 89:52, and 106:48 mark editorial divisions of the Psalter. Kings points to fuller royal records without identifying them as canonical Chronicles (1 Kings 15:7, 23, 31). Paul mentions writings not securely identifiable in the surviving canon (1 Corinthians 5:9; Colossians 4:16).

- **Early Christian disagreement is documented directly.** In [Ecclesiastical History 3.25](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nicene_and_Post-Nicene_Fathers%3A_Series_II/Volume_I/Church_History_of_Eusebius/Book_III/Chapter_25), [[Eusebius]] distinguishes acknowledged writings from books such as James, Jude, 2 Peter, and 2-3 John that were disputed but known to many; he also reports disagreement over Revelation.

- **The familiar New Testament list appears as reception evidence, not a creation story.** [[Athanasius]]'s [Festal Letter 39](https://christearlychurch.co.uk/text/festal-letter-39-the-canon-of-the-new-testament/) in 367 is the earliest surviving exact twenty-seven-book list. The [Carthaginian canon](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nicene_and_Post-Nicene_Fathers%3A_Series_II/Volume_XIV/Additional_Canons_3/The_Canons_of_Carthage/Canons/Canon_XXIV) later records a matching New Testament in a regional ecclesiastical ruling. “Earliest surviving list” does not mean Athanasius invented the collection.

- **The Nicene claim fails against the surviving record.** The [creed and canons of Nicaea](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/3801.htm) address doctrine and church discipline but contain no biblical book list. There is no extant Nicene decree in which Constantine or the bishops chose the Bible.

- **Later decisions were real but tradition-specific.** [Trent's fourth session](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Canons_and_Decrees_of_the_Council_of_Trent/Session_IV/Canonical_Scriptures) in 1546 dogmatically received the Catholic list; the Westminster Confession of 1647 explicitly excluded the Apocrypha from the Protestant canon. These texts help explain why “Who decided?” has different answers for different Bibles.

- **The Latter-day Saint reading is explicitly qualified.** Articles of Faith 1:8 affirms the Bible as God's word subject to correct translation, while 1:9 affirms continuing revelation. [[1 Nephi 13]] describes losses and later confirming records, but it does not name a council, manuscript, missing book, or responsible denomination.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
1. **A church-authority objection:** Roman Catholic and Orthodox believers may argue that describing only an “organic process” understates the Church's Spirit-guided authority. On this view, councils did not manufacture scripture but authoritatively recognized the apostolic tradition already received in worship. A purely historical account cannot disprove that theological claim.

2. **A Protestant objection:** Protestants may argue that inspired books possess divine authority before any council recognizes them and are received through apostolicity, doctrinal coherence, and the Spirit's witness. Therefore, citing late lists does not make councils the source of the canon. The difficult question is how that model adjudicates genuine early disputes and divergent Old Testament collections without quietly relying on later communal judgments.

3. **A critical objection:** Canon formation occurred amid disputes over doctrine, institutional boundaries, and authority. Communities that prevailed had greater ability to preserve their books and label rivals heretical. Apostolic attribution was not always historically secure, and criteria were not applied with mechanical consistency. A triumphalist story in which all sincere Christians simply recognized an obvious list suppresses the evidence of disagreement.

4. **An objection from continuing disagreement:** Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, Ethiopian, Syriac, Jewish, and Latter-day Saint collections are not identical. If providence was meant to produce one unmistakable closed Bible, the surviving plurality requires explanation.

5. **A Latter-day Saint authority problem:** If priesthood authority was lost in an [[Apostasy]], why trust post-apostolic bishops who helped delimit the New Testament? A response cannot simply declare their councils apostate when they differ from modern assumptions and inspired when they preserve favored books.

6. **A historical-limit objection:** “Earliest surviving” is not the same as “first ever.” Lost lists, local practices, and poorly documented communities limit confidence about exactly when each book gained or lost authority.

7. **A textual conflation objection:** Canon formation, manuscript copying, translation, authorship, and historical reliability are different questions. Mark 16:9-20 or John 7:53-8:11 may illuminate transmission, but they do not show that a council selected the wrong books. Conversely, agreement about a book's title does not guarantee every verse or attribution.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believers can acknowledge the historical complexity without treating scripture as merely arbitrary. A providential model need not claim that every stage was unanimous or every bishop infallible: it can propose that God worked through widespread worship, copying, teaching, debate, and later reception. The substantial early agreement around the four Gospels, Acts, and most Pauline letters is meaningful, although it does not erase disputes at the edges.

Catholic and Orthodox responses locate confidence partly in the Spirit-guided Church and tradition, not in an inspired biblical contents page. Protestant responses distinguish the Church's recognition of authority from creation of authority and appeal to apostolic origin, coherence with the rule of faith, and broad reception. Both approaches have historical evidence behind them and both add theological premises that history alone cannot verify.

A careful Latter-day Saint response can affirm preservation without granting infallibility to post-apostolic councils. Articles of Faith 1:8-9 and [[1 Nephi 13]] permit a Bible that genuinely transmits God's word while remaining textually limited and supplemented by later revelation. The vision's remedy is additional witness under one God and one Shepherd (1 Nephi 13:39-41), not abandonment of the Jewish-Christian record. This model remains a faith claim; 1 Nephi 13 does not independently identify particular ancient deletions.

The current Church student manual's account is notably less sensational than the old polemical claim that one pope or quarrelling council invented the canon: it describes third- and fourth-century leaders weighing apostolic authority, widespread use, and doctrinal consistency. The reported sermon in Journal of Discourses, Volume 7 should therefore be treated as later rhetoric, not as a controlling historical source.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text itself requires.** Biblical texts require recognition that authoritative writings existed, were copied, interpreted, and sometimes gathered into collections (Luke 24:44; 2 Timothy 3:15-17; 2 Peter 3:15-16). They also require selectivity: Kings cites fuller records, Paul mentions other correspondence, and the Psalter bears signs of editorial organization. They do **not** provide a complete future table of contents, name [[Nicaea]] or another canonizing council, state that Revelation 22:18-19 closes a future bound Bible, or explain the exact status of every writing they cite. Revelation 22:18-19 directly warns against altering “this book”; applying it to a later multi-book Bible is an interpretation, not the verse's explicit historical referent.

**1. Gradual communal recognition — independently supported.** Jewish and Christian communities preserved and used texts over time; surviving lists and codices display a large core alongside uncertain boundaries. Josephus, [[Eusebius]], [[Athanasius]], and the African councils are snapshots in that process. This is the strongest historical model, but by itself it neither proves nor disproves divine guidance.

**2. Catholic magisterial recognition — plausible within its theological premises.** The Spirit-guided Church received apostolic scripture, and councils eventually gave authoritative expression to that reception. Regional fourth-century lists and Trent support the historical ecclesial component. The claim that the Church possessed divine authority to decide the matter is confessional rather than independently established by the lists themselves.

**3. Orthodox liturgical reception — plausible and historically supported in broad outline.** Canonical authority emerged through long use in geographically diverse churches, with liturgy and patristic reception carrying more weight than one universal decree. Continuing differences among Orthodox traditions fit this model, although they complicate claims of one perfectly uniform ancient list.

**4. Protestant recognition or self-authentication — possible and theologically coherent.** Inspired books were authoritative because God inspired them; churches and councils recognized rather than conferred that authority. Early broad reception and attention to apostolicity support the model. The model needs a further account of why sincere communities disputed Hebrews, Revelation, James, 2 Peter, and Old Testament books and why the finally recognized boundary should be preferred.

**5. Providence through fallible institutions — plausible.** God could preserve a sufficiently reliable canon through institutions lacking universal inspiration or priesthood authority. This model helps a Latter-day Saint affirm both [[Apostasy]] and the Bible's genuine value. Historical evidence shows preservation and convergence; divine providence remains an interpretation of that evidence.

**6. Latter-day Saint open-canon model — possible and directly supported by Restoration scripture as doctrine.** Articles of Faith 1:8-9, [[1 Nephi 13]], and the additional standard works permit genuine biblical authority without a closed or textually perfect Bible. This resolves neither every historical canon dispute nor the identity of unspecified losses; accepting modern revelation is the model's faith premise.

**7. Critical boundary-and-power model — plausible and independently supported in part.** Canon lists also defined communal identity and excluded rival teachings; doctrinal conflict, episcopal authority, geography, and manuscript survival affected reception. Athanasius's polemical setting and Eusebius's categories support this. The stronger claim that the canon was merely an arbitrary invention of political winners is reductive: widespread use of core books predates fourth-century imperial enforcement.

**8. One decisive ancient council chose the entire Bible — historically unsupported as a general model.** A council can define a canon for its communion, as Trent later did, but no surviving ancient evidence identifies one meeting that originated every present biblical canon. The Nicene version was set aside because the surviving Nicene documents contain no book list.

**9. Ezra or a council at Jamnia/Yavneh closed the Hebrew canon at one moment — possible in a very weak sense but unsupported as a documented event.** Ancient tradition can associate [[Ezra (scribe)]] with restoration of the law, and later rabbinic discussions concern disputed books, but the evidence does not preserve minutes of an assembly that fixed the complete Jewish Bible. Treating Jamnia as the Jewish equivalent of Nicaea is an overread.

**10. One corrupt church removed the plain truths or rejected all rival gospels — ad hoc when stated historically.** [[1 Nephi 13]] does not name Roman Catholicism, a council, a manuscript, or a deleted book. Likewise, the existence of noncanonical gospels does not show that all were equally early or apostolic. A specific removal claim becomes historically testable only when it identifies a text, witnesses, date, and mechanism. The broad revelatory claim may be held by faith, but supplying missing historical details is not warranted.

**Considered and set aside.** The reported claim in Journal of Discourses, Volume 7 that a pope-convened quarrelling council selected the canon was set aside as a later, noncontemporary summary inconsistent with the surviving evidence. The idea that quotation automatically confers canonicity was set aside because biblical books cite records and traditions outside the received canon. The idea that textual variants alone prove a corrupted canon was set aside because book boundaries and manuscript wording are distinct questions; see [[How reliable is the biblical text]] and [[Was the Bible changed by councils and copyists]].
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The Bible is a library whose boundaries emerged historically. The biblical text contains no complete inspired contents list. Jewish sacred collections developed over time; early Christians inherited them and gradually recognized Christian writings. Early sources show both a widely received New Testament core and disputes at the margins. [[Athanasius]] gives the earliest surviving exact twenty-seven-book New Testament list in 367; regional African councils later list those books. The surviving records of [[Nicaea]] contain no canon decision. Later Catholic and Protestant authorities explicitly defined different Old Testament boundaries, and differing canons remain.

**Open:** The precise date at which every Jewish or Christian book became functionally canonical; the practices of poorly documented local communities; the exact weight given to apostolic origin, liturgical use, doctrine, antiquity, and church authority in each place; and whether particular ancient attributions are historically correct. “Earliest surviving list” cannot rule out lost predecessors.

**A matter of faith:** Whether the Holy Spirit providentially guided reception; whether a particular church possessed divine authority to define the canon; whether scripture is self-authenticating; and whether Restoration revelation correctly identifies losses and supplies divinely authorized additional scripture. Historical evidence can test concrete claims about manuscripts, lists, and councils, but it cannot by itself certify inspiration.

The most defensible short answer is therefore: many scribes, teachers, worshiping communities, bishops, and later councils participated; no single historical actor decided the contents of every Bible.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Begin with [[1 Nephi 13]] and distinguish its explicit claims from later identifications; then read [[1 Nephi 19]] on Nephi's use of the books of Moses and Isaiah, [[3 Nephi 27]] on commanded records and judgment, and [[D&C 88]] on study from the best books. Compare [[Psalm 106]] with Psalms 41, 72, and 89 to see the Psalter's editorial seams, and compare [[2 Chronicles 36]] with [[Ezra 1]]. Read 2 Peter 3, 1 Corinthians 5, Colossians 4, Jude, and Revelation 22 for the New Testament's own references to writings and textual authority.

For primary reception evidence, read [[Eusebius]], *Ecclesiastical History* 3.25; [[Athanasius]], *Festal Letter 39*; the Carthaginian Canon XXIV; [[Augustine of Hippo]], *On Christian Doctrine* 2.8; and [[Jerome]]'s prologues to his Old Testament translations. For later confessional boundaries, compare the Council of Trent, Session IV; the Church of England's Article VI; and the Westminster Confession, chapter 1.

For modern framing, compare the Church's *New Testament Student Manual*, chapter 1, with Lee Martin McDonald's “Biblical Canon,” Timothy H. Lim's *The Formation of the Jewish Canon*, Bruce M. Metzger's *The Canon of the New Testament*, and David Brakke's study of Athanasius's Thirty-Ninth Festal Letter. Keep canon formation distinct from textual transmission by reading [[How reliable is the biblical text]], [[Was the Bible changed by councils and copyists]], and [[Records and Sacred Writings]].
