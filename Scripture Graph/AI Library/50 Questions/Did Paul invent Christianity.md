# Did Paul invent Christianity?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
No, not in the ordinary sense of creating Christianity from nothing. Paul's own undisputed letters say that he persecuted an existing church, knew people who were in Christ before him, met established leaders, and received the proclamation of Jesus' death and resurrection before transmitting it (Galatians 1:13-19; Romans 16:7; 1 Corinthians 15:1-9). Paul nevertheless became one of Christianity's most consequential architects: he built Gentile communities, argued that Gentiles need not become Jews through circumcision, developed influential readings of Christ, grace, faith, law, Adam, Abraham, and Israel, and left the earliest surviving Christian writings. Thus the sole-inventor thesis is historically untenable, while the narrower claim that Paul decisively shaped the Gentile and theological form of later Christianity is strong. See [[Paul (apostle)]], [[Conversion of Paul]], [[Jews and Gentiles]], and [[Jerusalem conference]].
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **Paul admits the church preceded him.** He says he persecuted the church of God before his call (Galatians 1:13; 1 Corinthians 15:9; Philippians 3:6). This evidence comes from Paul himself, not merely from Acts.

- **He says the central proclamation was received.** In [[1 Corinthians 15]], Paul introduces Christ's death, burial, resurrection, and appearances as material received and delivered, and lists Cephas, the Twelve, James, and other apostles before himself (1 Corinthians 15:1-9). Whatever later elaboration Paul contributed, the resurrection proclamation was not presented as his invention.

- **He acknowledges earlier believers and leaders.** Andronicus and Junia were in Christ before Paul (Romans 16:7). Paul later visited Cephas and James and recognized James, Cephas, and John as pillars with missions not created by him (Galatians 1:18-19; 2:7-10). See [[Peter (apostle)]] and [[James (brother of the Lord)]].

- **Gentile Christianity developed through conflict and consultation.** Paul's version in Galatians 2 and Acts' version in [[Acts 15]] differ in perspective and possibly chronology, but both locate Paul within negotiations involving [[Barnabas]], Jerusalem leaders, and established communities. This supports collaborative, contested development rather than solitary invention.

- **Paul's major influence is directly visible.** He calls Gentile communities the seal of his apostleship, identifies a special Gentile mission, and supplies sustained arguments against compulsory circumcision and for Gentile participation in Abraham's promise (1 Corinthians 9:1-2; Galatians 2:7-10; 3:6-29; 5:2-6). The evidence therefore supports major reconstruction and expansion, not original creation.

- **Paul distinguishes inheritance from judgment.** In [[1 Corinthians 7]], he distinguishes a command attributed to the Lord from his own judgment (1 Corinthians 7:10-12, 25, 40). In [[1 Corinthians 11]], he says he received and delivered the Lord's Supper tradition (1 Corinthians 11:23-26). These distinctions make little sense if Paul intended to represent every teaching as his own creation.

- **His ministry was networked.** Romans 16:1-16 and other letters name many coworkers, hosts, and emissaries. [[Multiple Pauline Textual Attestations in 1 Corinthians 9]] bears on the textual basis for Paul's own self-presentation as an apostle whose Corinthian community was the product of his labor.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The earliest surviving Christian writings are Paul's.** Since no writing from Jesus or the original disciples securely predates Paul's letters, historical reconstruction of the earliest movement often passes through Pauline evidence. Later Christianity's vocabulary of grace, justification, participation in Christ, Adam-Christ typology, and the church as Christ's body bears an unmistakable Pauline imprint.

- **Paul claims unusual independence.** He insists that his gospel came through revelation rather than human instruction and says he did not immediately consult earlier apostles (Galatians 1:11-17). A critic can argue that Paul used visionary authority to legitimate a version of the movement that differed from the Jerusalem founders.

- **His Gentile program marks a major discontinuity.** Paul resisted compulsory circumcision and treated Torah as a temporary custodian in relation to Christ, while still denying that the law opposed God's promises (Galatians 3:19-25; 5:2-6). Removing circumcision and full Torah observance as Gentile boundary requirements helped turn a Jewish messianic movement into a transethnic religion. In that sociological sense, calling Paul an inventor or refounder has force.

- **The sources reveal conflict.** Paul opposed Cephas publicly and describes rival preachers in severe terms (Galatians 2:11-14; 2 Corinthians 11:4-15). Acts, written later, may smooth conflicts to present a more unified apostolic history. Agreement cannot simply be assumed wherever the sources are silent.

- **Paul may have reformulated inherited beliefs.** Receiving a proclamation does not mean preserving it unchanged. Paul could inherit belief in Jesus' resurrection while supplying much of the framework through which death, law, Gentile inclusion, justification, and cosmic salvation were later understood.

- **Survival creates selection bias.** Paul's extensive correspondence entered the canon, whereas writings from many opponents and alternative communities did not survive. His apparent centrality may partly reflect preservation and canon formation rather than a complete measure of first-century influence.

- **The word Christianity is ambiguous.** Paul plainly did not create the first Jesus group, but someone may use Christianity to mean the predominantly Gentile, cross-centered, Torah-relativized tradition that became historically dominant. Under that narrower definition the thesis is stronger, though still overstated if it implies Paul acted alone.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- The earliest surviving author is not necessarily the founder. Paul's letters contain admissions against a sole-founder theory: he persecuted the church, received central traditions, knew earlier believers, and negotiated with established leaders (Galatians 1:13-19; 1 Corinthians 15:1-9; Romans 16:7).

- Revelation and reception are not mutually exclusive in Paul's account. He attributes his commission and gospel insight to revelation while also transmitting inherited traditions and consulting recognized leaders. A believer may accept divine commissioning; a historian can still observe that Paul's authority claim operated within an existing movement.

- Paul presents his Gentile conclusions as fulfillment and reinterpretation of Israel's scriptures, not abandonment of Israel's God. His arguments appeal to [[Abraham (patriarch)]], [[Adam]], [[Passover]], and the [[Law of Moses]] (Romans 4:1-17; 5:12-21; 1 Corinthians 5:7-8; Galatians 3:6-29). Whether his readings are persuasive is disputed, but their claimed continuity is textually clear.

- Conflict does not by itself establish a different religion. Galatians 2 reports both recognition of complementary missions and a later confrontation over table conduct. Paul criticizes Cephas while continuing to treat him as an acknowledged apostolic figure. The evidence permits serious disagreement within a shared movement.

- Paul's theological creativity should be admitted rather than minimized. A thoughtful believing account can regard his arguments as inspired development and contextual application; historical method can establish their influence but cannot establish inspiration. Development is compatible with fidelity, but fidelity is a theological judgment rather than an automatic historical conclusion.

- Acts' harmony should not be used to erase Paul's sharper testimony. [[Jerusalem conference]] is best read with both Galatians 2 and [[Acts 15]] visible: there was meaningful recognition and cooperation, but also unresolved tensions and differing narrative perspectives.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the evidence requires.** Paul's undisputed letters require an existing Jesus movement before his call, believers and leaders whose standing preceded his, and a death-and-resurrection proclamation he says he received (Galatians 1:13-19; Romans 16:7; 1 Corinthians 15:1-9). They also require meaningful Pauline development: a distinctive Gentile mission, new rulings for new cases, sustained scriptural argument, community formation, and major theological influence. The texts do not require either assumption that pre-Pauline believers already possessed the whole later Christian system or that every distinctively Pauline formulation was unprecedented.

- **Paul as sole inventor — logically possible but textually ad hoc.** This strong model says Paul created the first Jesus communities or originated resurrection belief. It must explain away his hostile past, received tradition, earlier believers, earlier witnesses, and negotiations with recognized leaders. The evidence strongly disfavors it.

- **Paul as transformer or refounder of Gentile Christianity — plausible and strongly textually supported if carefully defined.** Paul resisted circumcision as a Gentile requirement, organized transethnic communities, and developed influential arguments about law, promise, faith, and participation in Christ. This can reasonably be called transformation, but not solitary invention: [[Barnabas]], other missionaries, communities, and Jerusalem leaders also contributed.

- **Paul as principal theological architect — plausible; influence strongly supported, originality doctrine by doctrine remains open.** The undisputed letters demonstrably articulate sustained accounts of Adam and Christ, Abraham, grace, law, resurrection, spiritual gifts, and communal life. Their survival makes Paul's influence unusually visible, but preservation and canonization may exaggerate his prominence relative to voices whose writings did not survive.

- **Collaborative and contested development — the best-supported historical synthesis.** Galatians records both recognition and sharp conflict; Acts presents collective deliberation concerning Gentiles. Together they support development through negotiation, rivalry, correction, and missionary experience, without requiring seamless unity or wholly separate religions.

- **Paul as one stream among several early Christian streams — plausible and strongly supported in its limited form.** Rival preaching and disputes over authority, law, and table fellowship show genuine plurality. Paul's opponents are known mostly through his polemic, so the evidence does not establish two neatly bounded or equally incompatible religions.

- **Paul as faithful, divinely authorized developer — textually possible; supernatural authorization is a matter of faith.** Paul attributes his call to revelation, and Acts depicts divine commissioning. Historical inquiry can assess his reversal, testimony, and influence but cannot compel acceptance of the risen Christ as the cause. See [[Conversion of Paul]], [[Revelation]], and [[Apostles]].

- **Paul as nearly passive transmitter of an already complete system — possible only in a minimal sense and ad hoc if made absolute.** Paul received important traditions, yet his letters openly contain extended reasoning, contextual judgments, adaptations, and applications. Denying meaningful Pauline development does not explain the evidence.

- **A later Pauline school as a contributor to institutional Christianity — plausible and important, with attribution disputed letter by letter.** Many scholars dispute Pauline authorship of Ephesians, Colossians, 2 Thessalonians, 1-2 Timothy, and Titus, though judgments differ among the six. If some institutional structures, household instructions, or formulations come from later writers using Paul's name, later 'Pauline Christianity' cannot simply be credited to the historical Paul. The secure starting point for this question is therefore the seven generally undisputed letters.

- **Paul as a decisive departure from Jesus' own message — possible but dependent on a separate historical-Jesus reconstruction.** A strong discontinuity case must compare the undisputed letters with critically assessed Gospel traditions. Pre-Pauline resurrection belief disproves sole invention but does not by itself prove that Paul's developed account matched Jesus' earthly teaching in every respect.

- **Later orthodoxy as a Pauline victory over suppressed alternatives — possible and partly plausible, but not established by Pauline polemic alone.** Testing this requires post-New-Testament evidence involving figures such as [[Marcion]], [[Irenaeus]], and [[Tertullian]], as well as study of canon formation.

**Considered and set aside.** A claim of no real tension between Paul, Cephas, James, and rival teachers is set aside because the letters preserve actual disputes. Later Pauline intertexts in the Latter-day Saint canon are also set aside for this historical question: they bear on reception and textual mediation, not on Christianity's first-century origin.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established from the texts:** Paul says he persecuted an existing church, knew believers who were in Christ before him, met leaders whose standing preceded his, and received and transmitted a death-and-resurrection proclamation (Galatians 1:13-19; Romans 16:7; 1 Corinthians 15:1-9). These mutually reinforcing admissions strongly rule out the claim that Paul founded the first Jesus community or originated belief in Jesus' resurrection. Paul also became a major missionary and theological architect, especially among Gentiles.

**Historically probable but not mechanically proved:** The core resurrection proclamation preceded Paul's ministry in substantially recognizable form, and Paul's work decisively transformed the movement's Gentile reach and theological articulation. His own testimony is the earliest evidence, not a neutral transcript, and later preservation of his letters complicates comparisons with less documented leaders.

**Open and disputed:** The proportion of inheritance and innovation in individual teachings; the relationship between Paul's gospel and Jesus' earthly preaching; the depth of the Antioch conflict; the relationship between Galatians 2 and Acts 15; the representativeness of Paul's surviving letters; and how teachings in disputed Pauline letters should be attributed. Calling Paul a 'refounder' is defensible only when it means major transformation, not creation from nothing.

**Matter of faith:** Paul interpreted his reversal and commission as revelation of the risen [[Jesus Christ]], and Acts presents him as divinely chosen. Historical inquiry can strongly establish the reversal and the existence of that claim but cannot prove its supernatural cause. Believers may understand Pauline creativity as inspired apostolic development without presenting inspiration as a historical finding.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
