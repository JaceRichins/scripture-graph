# Why do religious experiences occur in every religion?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Religious experiences -- vivid encounter, felt peace, transformative conversion -- are widely reported across many traditions, though not uniformly emphasized in every one, so the premise itself needs modest qualification rather than blanket acceptance. Scripture supplies two things: real texts showing divine contact reaching beyond Israel's or the Church's own boundary (Jethro's declaration that 'the LORD is greater than all gods,' Exodus 18:11; Balaam, a non-Israelite diviner, addressed directly by God, Numbers 22:9; Cornelius, 'a devout man... that feared God,' visited by an angel and told 'in every nation he that feareth him... is accepted with him,' Acts 10:2,35), and explicit doctrine of broadly distributed light ('the Lord doth grant unto all nations, of their own nation and tongue, to teach his word... all that he seeth fit,' Alma 29:8; 'the Spirit of Christ is given to every man,' Moroni 7:16). At the same time, the same canon insists that not every spiritual claim is to be trusted: 'try the spirits whether they are of God' (1 John 4:1), and impressions can be misunderstood or deceptive (D&C 46:7). Outside scripture, research on absorption, learned expectation, and cultural templating shows that shared human cognitive and cultural capacities alone can generate, shape, and explain much of why comparable experiences recur across traditions with very different content -- a genuine rival explanation, not merely a hostile dismissal. Religious diversity -- mutually incompatible doctrinal content reported with equal sincerity and intensity -- is a real defeater against treating any single experience as self-certifying proof of one tradition's exclusive truth. What is defensible: scripture permits, and modern LDS teaching has applied, an inclusivist account in which real but partial light reaches many peoples, mediated through each culture's concepts, while reserving fuller revealed content for the restored gospel. What is not settled by the texts or the comparative evidence alone: whether any given experience, inside or outside this tradition, has a genuinely transcendent cause, a wholly natural one, or some mixture -- that judgment remains a matter of ongoing discernment and, ultimately, faith.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
**Scripture narrates divine contact reaching outside the covenant boundary.** Jethro says 'now I know that the LORD is greater than all gods' (Exodus 18:11); God speaks directly to the hired diviner Balaam, 'What men are these with thee?' (Numbers 22:9); the devout Gentile Cornelius receives an angelic visitation, and Peter concludes 'in every nation he that feareth him, and worketh righteousness, is accepted with him' (Acts 10:2,34-35). None of these figures is a full outsider to biblical monotheism, and Balaam is later remembered negatively (Revelation 2:14), so these are evidence of boundary-crossing contact within the canon's own worldview, not proof that any and every religion's experience is divinely caused.

**Scripture states directly that inspired teaching is distributed broadly.** 'The Lord doth grant unto all nations, of their own nation and tongue, to teach his word... all that he seeth fit' (Alma 29:8), alongside 'the Spirit of Christ is given to every man' (Moroni 7:16) and 'the true Light... lighteth every man' (John 1:9) -- a real textual basis for expecting some spiritual capacity everywhere, though not a claim that all resulting religious content is equally true.

**Absorption and cultural-learning research is real and relevant evidence for a naturalistic contributor.** Studies of prayer practice (Luhrmann, Nusbaum, and Thisted) and the general review of absorption and spiritual experience (Lifshitz, van Elk, and Luhrmann) support that attentional disposition, imaginative training, expectation, and cultural vocabulary shape which experiences occur and how they are interpreted -- without settling whether a transcendent object is also involved.

**Doctrinal diversity is a genuine defeater, calibrated correctly.** As the Stanford Encyclopedia entry on religious experience notes, incompatible content reported with equal sincerity weakens (without refuting) any simple inference from experience to one tradition's exclusive truth.

**Registry-calibrated internal witness claims stay honestly weighted.** Paul's own appearance claim (1 Corinthians 15:8-10) is strong evidence he sincerely interpreted an experience as the risen Jesus, but the letters underdetermine its phenomenology. The 3 Nephi 17 collective-witness claim, though vivid, remains one narrated report and receives only none-band (~0.14) historical weight in the registry -- the same caution the dossier should apply to any tradition's dramatic internal testimony, including its own.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **The symmetry objection:** adherents of incompatible religions report peace, revelation, answered prayer, visions, and certainty. If a Latter-day Saint accepts an inward confirmation as decisive while dismissing phenomenologically similar rival experiences, the procedure risks special pleading unless an independent discriminator is supplied.

- **Cultural prediction:** experiences commonly resemble what people have learned to expect. Christian experiencers encounter Jesus or the Holy Spirit; other communities supply different agents and metaphysical descriptions. That pattern is expected if cognition and culture generate or interpret the content, whereas a single clear revealer might be expected to reduce rather than reproduce doctrinal conflict.

- **Natural mechanisms:** absorption, suggestion, sleep phenomena, grief, fasting, illness, drugs, neurological variation, group emotion, memory reconstruction, and ordinary coincidence can produce experiences with no supernatural cause. Some can be experimentally influenced. An explanation invoking God is unnecessary when these mechanisms adequately explain a case.

- **Private and weakly falsifiable evidence:** most reports cannot be independently repeated or inspected. Failed impressions can be forgotten, reinterpreted, or blamed on insufficient faith, while apparent successes are retained. This selection structure can protect almost any belief from correction.

- **Fruits do not identify truth uniquely:** incompatible religions and secular practices can produce charity, sobriety, courage, community, and peace; religions can also produce fear, manipulation, prejudice, or violence. Good consequences are important but do not prove a doctrine's metaphysical details.

- **Divine hiddenness and unequal access:** if God uses spiritual experience to guide people, sincere seekers who receive silence, ambiguity, or mutually inconsistent answers require explanation. Ether 12:6 can explain why coercive proof is withheld, but it does not explain every prolonged case of sincere nonbelief. See [[Why does God seem silent when I pray]].

- **Internal circularity:** Alma 32 and Moroni 10 can be meaningful within faith, but neither is a religiously neutral test of the entire system. Using the system's promised feeling to prove the system, then using the system to interpret the feeling, risks circular confirmation.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- A thoughtful believer can distinguish **experience from interpretation**. A genuine divine influence might be filtered through language, prior beliefs, memory, and limited concepts. This allows partial truth without pretending contradictory propositions are simultaneously true.

- Restoration scripture gives this response real textual footing rather than leaving it as an improvised rescue. Alma 29:8, 2 Nephi 29:12, Moroni 7:16, and D&C 84:46 permit broadly distributed light. The 1978 First Presidency statement that major religious leaders received a portion of God's light is an official Latter-day Saint application, though not a canonical endorsement of every tradition or experience.

- A believer may also adopt a **mixed-source** view. Some experiences may be divine, others natural or mistaken, and some may combine ordinary neural processes with providential meaning. Scripture itself requires discernment rather than reflexive acceptance (1 John 4:1; D&C 46:7–9). Neural mediation is compatible with divine action, although compatibility is not positive evidence that God acted.

- Experiences need not carry more content than they warrant. Peace may reasonably support a person's trust or commitment without proving institutional authority, historical claims, or every doctrine associated with the occasion. [[Alma 32]] models limited conclusions at intermediate stages rather than instant certainty about everything.

- Private experience can be part of a cumulative case alongside publicly assessable history, coherence, moral fruits, and correction over time. These additional considerations must genuinely do discriminating work; merely counting dependent internal witnesses does not. The registry's treatment of 3 Nephi 17:25 and [[Internal Religious Depiction in Jarom 1]] illustrates the needed restraint.

- Finally, believers can admit epistemic humility. A spiritual experience may be personally rational and life-changing without becoming coercive public proof. William James's distinction between causal origins and fruits is useful here, provided good fruits are not mistaken for proof of detailed theology.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text requires.** The canon requires neither “only members of the true church ever receive divine influence” nor “all religious experiences are divine.” It affirms broad light and instruction (Alma 29:8; 2 Nephi 29:12; Moroni 7:16), unequal spiritual gifts (1 Corinthians 12:4–11; D&C 46:11–26), unsolicited revelation ([[Exodus 3]]; Isaiah 65:1), participatory seeking ([[Alma 32]]; [[Moroni 10]]), and the need to test claimed manifestations (1 John 4:1; D&C 46:7–9; D&C 50:1–3). Claims that every sincere feeling is revelation, that God guarantees unambiguous answers on demand, or that one experience proves an entire institution are reader assumptions rather than textual requirements.

**Serious models permitted by the evidence:**

- **Erroneous-universality model — independently supported.** “Every religion” is an overgeneralization. Religious experiences are widespread but differently defined, emphasized, and reported. This corrects the premise without explaining the experiences that do occur.

- **Cultural-cognitive construction — independently supported for many cases; plausible as a broad model.** Shared cognitive capacities generate unusual or meaningful experiences, while practice and culture shape their frequency and content. Taves and Luhrmann supply independent support. Extending this to the claim that every reported experience is wholly nonveridical goes beyond the data.

- **Naturalistic causal pluralism — independently supported.** Different experiences arise through different combinations of absorption, emotion, social learning, sleep, illness, coincidence, memory, and altered states. This fits heterogeneity better than a single psychological mechanism. Its modest form does not answer whether any transcendent cause also operates.

- **Eliminative naturalism — plausible but not established.** All religious experiences ultimately have sufficient natural causes, and contradictory interpretations reflect culture rather than multiple revelations. It explains religious diversity economically. The universal quantifier remains unproved, and identifying a neural mechanism does not logically exclude an external cause.

- **Latter-day Saint partial-light model — textually plausible and officially supported within the tradition.** God grants light and moral truth among nations, which people receive through local concepts; the Restoration claims a distinctive fulness without requiring total darkness elsewhere. Alma 29:8 and Moroni 7:16 support the model, and the 1978 First Presidency statement applies it to named non-Christian leaders. Its theological truth is not independently established by those same sources.

- **Perennialist or pluralist transcendence — possible.** Diverse reports imperfectly describe one underlying sacred reality or several genuine transcendent realities. Similar motifs such as awe, unity, and transformation fit this model. Incompatible concrete claims and culture-specific content prevent similarities from establishing it; flattening traditions into one alleged core can misrepresent them.

- **Exclusivist mixed-source model — possible and partly text-supported.** One tradition's central revelations are genuine; other experiences reflect common divine grace, conscience, psychology, misunderstanding, or deception. Scriptural testing language permits this. It becomes **ad hoc** when experiences are classified as divine exactly when they agree with prior doctrine and counterfeit when they do not, with no independent criteria.

- **Multiple-spiritual-agents model — textually possible, evidentially weak.** First John 4:1 and D&C 50 permit divine and deceptive spiritual agencies. Applying unseen agents selectively to explain every rival report is ad hoc unless a proposed test reliably distinguishes causes.

- **Hybrid divine-human model — plausible.** Some experiences could involve divine initiative mediated through ordinary brains and cultures, while others are entirely natural or mistaken. This best accommodates the canon's broad-light and testing passages together, but its divine component remains a metaphysical interpretation rather than an independently demonstrated result.

**Considered and set aside.** “All religions are equally true” is rejected as stated because concrete claims conflict. “All non-Restoration experiences are satanic” is rejected as contrary to broad-light texts and unsupported as a general causal diagnosis. “Good feelings prove truth” is rejected because beneficial effects are not unique to true propositions. “A brain correlate disproves God” is rejected as a category mistake unless causal sufficiency is shown in the particular case. The three origin models catalogued in [[Internal Religious Depiction in Jarom 1]]—ancient revelation, later Christianizing transmission, and nineteenth-century composition—remain mutually underdetermined by explicit religious language alone and warn against treating vivid internal depiction as independent authentication.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** experiences interpreted as religious are widespread; their incidence, form, content, and reports are influenced by human dispositions, practices, culture, and language. Religions make some genuinely incompatible interpretations. Consequently, sincerity, intensity, life change, or an inward feeling alone does not establish one tradition's exclusive claims. Scripture itself contains both broad-access language and commands to test manifestations.

**Open:** no current method classifies every reported experience by cause. Natural explanations are compelling in many cases but do not logically exclude all transcendence; theological explanations remain possible but generally lack decisive independent discrimination. It is also open how much cross-cultural similarity belongs to raw phenomenology and how much results from translation into broad comparative categories.

**Matter of faith:** that God is among the causes, that the Light of Christ accounts for genuine truth across religions, and that Restoration revelations possess greater authority are Latter-day Saint convictions rather than conclusions compelled by the mere universality or intensity of experience. Such faith can be intellectually responsible when it remains proportionate, tests impressions, considers contrary evidence, and does not downgrade other people's testimony solely because it is inconvenient. The safest synthesis is that religious diversity calls for humility and additional evidence, not automatic relativism or automatic disbelief.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
- Read [[Alma 29]] on teaching among nations, [[Alma 32]] on staged experiential inquiry, [[Moroni 7]] on broad light and discernment, and [[Moroni 10]] on prayer concerning the record.

- Compare [[Exodus 3]], [[Acts 10]], and 1 Corinthians 15:3–10 for unsolicited, interpreted, and transmitted experience claims. Read [[Did Jesus rise from the dead]] for the distinction among early proclamation, sincere testimony, independent corroboration, and supernatural conclusion.

- Study 1 John 4:1, Matthew 7:15–20, 1 Corinthians 12:4–11, D&C 46:7–26, and D&C 50:1–3 on testing spirits and diverse gifts; then compare [[Testimony]], [[Revelation]], [[Spiritual Gifts]], and [[Light of Christ]].

- For philosophy, read the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Religious Experience](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2024/entries/religious-experience/), especially its treatment of diversity and naturalistic explanations.

- For empirical and comparative frameworks, read Ann Taves, *Religious Experience Reconsidered*; Luhrmann, Nusbaum, and Thisted, “The Absorption Hypothesis”; Lifshitz, van Elk, and Luhrmann, “Absorption and spiritual experience”; and Tanya Luhrmann, *How God Becomes Real*.

- For a classic, cautious approach to experience and its fruits, read William James, [*The Varieties of Religious Experience*](https://gutenberg.org/files/621/621-h/621-h.html). James is philosophically influential, not an adjudication of Restoration truth.

- Compare the 1978 First Presidency statement quoted in [“Treasuring All Truth”](https://newsroom.churchofjesuschrist.org/article/treasuring-all-truth) with Alma 29:8. Keep official interpretation distinct from canon and from empirical demonstration.

- Consult [[Internal Religious Depiction in Jarom 1]] and [[Narrative Portrayal Of Religious Authority in 2 Chronicles 34]] to practice separating what a narrative depicts from what independently follows about history or divine authority.
