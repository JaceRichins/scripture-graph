# Why was the Nauvoo Expositor destroyed?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Nauvoo Expositor was destroyed because Joseph Smith and nearly all of the Nauvoo City Council judged its attacks on plural marriage, Joseph's character and theology, and Nauvoo's combined religious-civic power to be libelous and dangerously likely to intensify anti-Latter-day Saint violence. On 10 June 1844 the council declared the newspaper and printing establishment a public nuisance; Joseph, acting as mayor, then ordered the physical press destroyed, its type scattered, and copies burned. Their fear of violence had real historical grounds, but the action also silenced disclosures that threatened Joseph and the Nauvoo power structure, including a plural-marriage allegation now known to have been substantially true. The most defensible answer is therefore mixed motive—public safety, reputation, and institutional self-protection—while the legality of destroying printed copies is debated and the case against destroying the press itself is especially strong. See [[43 A Public Nuisance]], [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]], and the [primary documents published by the Joseph Smith Papers](https://www.josephsmithpapers.org/paper-summary/mayors-order-to-nauvoo-city-marshal-10-june-1844/1).
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **The paper's own contents:** The 7 June issue attacked plural marriage, a plurality of gods, Joseph's financial and political conduct, the municipal court, the union of church and state, and the Nauvoo charter. Its program was broader than either “exposing polygamy” or “printing lies.” Read the [complete primary-source record](https://www.josephsmithpapers.org/paper-summary/nauvoo-expositor-7-june-1844/1) and its earlier prospectus cataloged in [[Sources Cited]].

- **The council record:** The [8 June minutes](https://www.josephsmithpapers.org/paper-summary/minutes-8-june-1844/1) record Joseph's concern that the paper would destroy the peace and produce mob violence. The [10 June minutes](https://www.josephsmithpapers.org/paper-summary/minutes-10-june-1844/1) show that the council considered alternatives: Benjamin Warrington proposed notice and fines, but the council chose immediate suppression.

- **The operative documents:** The [resolution](https://www.josephsmithpapers.org/paper-summary/resolution-10-june-1844/1) declared the establishment and copies a public nuisance. Joseph's [mayoral order](https://www.josephsmithpapers.org/paper-summary/mayors-order-to-nauvoo-city-marshal-10-june-1844/1) expressly directed destruction of the press, scattering of type, and burning of papers. This was official municipal action, not an unidentified spontaneous mob.

- **The allegation was not simply invented:** The Expositor printed affidavits concerning a plural-marriage revelation. The Church's own historical essay, “Plural Marriage in Kirtland and Nauvoo,” now affirms that Joseph married multiple wives and authorized the practice. [[Why did Joseph Smith practice plural marriage]] supplies the necessary documentary and scriptural background.

- **The legal record is genuinely divided:** Nauvoo's charter granted nuisance-abatement authority, but the 1818 Illinois Constitution protected writing and printing and provided for truth and jury judgment in specified libel proceedings. Dallin H. Oaks's [qualified legal analysis](https://dc.law.utah.edu/ulr/vol9/iss4/2/) found a period-law basis for destroying published issues but not the physical press. Governor Ford contemporaneously rejected Nauvoo's procedure in his [22 June letter](https://www.josephsmithpapers.org/paper-summary/letter-from-thomas-ford-22-june-1844/2).
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
The strongest objection is that this was summary suppression and property destruction by officials with an acute conflict of interest. The newspaper investigated Joseph Smith while Joseph served as mayor and occupied commanding religious, political, judicial, and military roles in Nauvoo. The council proceeded without a neutral adjudication of the alleged libel and rejected Benjamin Warrington's less drastic proposal of notice and fines.

A second objection is factual: describing the Expositor as merely a collection of lies is untenable. Its rhetoric was polemical and not every allegation has been verified, but its disclosure that Joseph Smith had introduced plural marriage was substantially true. Corroborating that disclosure does not establish that every reported detail was accurate or that concealment was the council's sole motive.

A third objection is legal. The Nauvoo charter supplied nuisance-abatement authority, but Illinois independently protected speaking, writing, and printing. Responsibility for abusive publication did not necessarily authorize officials to destroy a printing establishment summarily. Even Dallin H. Oaks's qualified defense found appreciably stronger historical support for suppressing published copies than for destroying the physical press and type.

A fourth objection is practical. The measure intended to avert violence instead amplified the Expositor's allegations, strengthened regional opposition, and accelerated the crisis leading toward Carthage. That consequence does not prove what officials privately intended, but it bears heavily on the decision's prudence.

Later acquittals of Jesse P. Harmon and John Lytle do not vindicate the original order. The reported evidentiary and identification problems in those prosecutions are distinct from whether the council's remedy was lawful.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Thoughtful believers can acknowledge the force of those objections without concluding that the council acted from simple villainy. Nauvoo's leaders had experienced expulsion and violence in Missouri and conflict in Ohio; contemporary threats made fear of another armed attack rational. The charter did confer nuisance authority, common-law materials discussed summary abatement, and officials spent parts of two days deliberating before acting. These facts support sincerity even if they do not prove correctness.

A careful believing defense can also distinguish degrees of error. Period law arguably supplied a basis for destroying already-distributed libelous papers, while destruction of the press was much harder to justify and may have created civil liability. Historical context can therefore mitigate claims of knowing lawlessness without converting an excessive remedy into a clearly lawful or wise one.

Nor does recognizing civic error logically settle Joseph Smith's prophetic calling. The surviving authorizing documents invoke municipal law, not revelation. A believer may hold that a prophet can make a grave political or legal mistake without assuming that God commanded it. [[D&C 121]] itself warns religious leaders about compulsion and unrighteous dominion; applying that warning to leaders as well as critics strengthens rather than weakens moral seriousness.

Nothing in this response excuses the Carthage mob. A mistaken or unlawful press order called for lawful adjudication and possible damages, not extrajudicial killing. [[Martyrdom of Joseph and Hyrum Smith]] must preserve the perpetrators' responsibility even while acknowledging the Expositor decision's role in escalating the crisis.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the sources require.** Scripture does not narrate or adjudicate the Expositor affair. The primary records establish that one opposition issue appeared; the council considered it on June 8 and 10; officials invoked libel, nuisance authority, charter defense, religious rights, and danger to the peace; the council declared the establishment a nuisance; Joseph Smith issued the destruction order as mayor; and a municipal posse destroyed the press, type, and papers. The records do not establish that every allegation was true or false, that public safety was either a pretext or the sole motive, that God commanded the act, or that its legality received a final judicial determination.

**Independently supported — public-safety model.** Council records, Joseph's defense to Governor Ford, remembered persecution, and contemporary regional hostility establish that leaders expressed and had intelligible grounds for fear of renewed violence. They do not establish that destroying the press reduced that danger or was the least dangerous remedy.

**Independently supported — personal and institutional self-protection model.** The Expositor challenged Joseph's confidential plural marriages, theology, reputation, presidential campaign, municipal court, and concentration of religious and civic authority. Its content, the officials' personal stakes, and rejection of a less drastic proposal support censorship and institutional protection as genuine components. They do not reveal a single private motive.

**Plausible — mixed-motive model.** Genuine fear, anger at polemical attacks, protection of religious rights, concealment of plural marriage, concern for reputation, and defense of political authority could operate simultaneously. This model discards fewer established facts than an exclusively benevolent or exclusively cynical explanation, but the relative weight of the motives remains unknowable.

**Possible — political-kingdom model.** Some historians place the dispute within Joseph Smith's presidential campaign and the emerging Council of Fifty or kingdom-of-God project. The Expositor's attacks on political and quasi-monarchical authority make that context potentially relevant, but the sources cited here do not independently establish that protecting the Council of Fifty was a controlling reason for the destruction.

**Possible — strategic-provocation model.** The proprietors intended continued publication and advocated repeal of the charter. They may have anticipated that an official overreaction would discredit Nauvoo's leadership, but deliberate opposition is not proof that provoking destruction was their controlling strategy.

**Plausible and independently supported — qualified-legality model.** The charter's nuisance clause and period common law gave officials a non-frivolous theory for suppressing allegedly libelous published matter. Oaks develops this position while concluding that it did not justify destruction of the physical press.

**Plausible and independently supported — unlawful-summary-suppression model.** Illinois's press guarantee, lack of neutral adjudication, conflicts of interest, Ford's objections, rejection of a lesser remedy, and destruction of productive machinery strongly support the critical legal analysis. Because Joseph died before trial, this is a strong historical judgment rather than a verdict entered against him.

**Ad hoc when exclusive.** Pure altruistic safety ignores direct personal and institutional exposure. Pure plural-marriage cover-up ignores the newspaper's wider political, legal, theological, and financial program. Either concern may have been real; neither adequately explains the whole record alone.

**Considered and set aside.** A revealed-command explanation is unsupported because the authorizing documents use civic and legal reasoning rather than claiming revelation. The unrelated scriptural and historical snippets containing words such as 'destroyed' or 'model' are lexical matches, not evidence about this event. The later acquittals of Jesse P. Harmon and John Lytle do not establish the original order's legality.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The Expositor opposed both confidential plural marriage and Nauvoo's concentration of religious, judicial, military, and political authority. The council declared it a nuisance, Joseph issued the destruction order as mayor, and a municipal posse destroyed the press, type, and papers. The official rationale included libel, defense of chartered rights, and prevention of violence. At least the plural-marriage core of the newspaper's disclosures was substantially true. The act escalated the crisis that brought Joseph and Hyrum to Carthage, but it did not morally or legally justify their murder.

**Open:** No surviving source measures the relative strength of fear, anger, honor, concealment, and institutional self-protection in the participants' minds. The legality of destroying published copies remains debatable under 1844 doctrine; the legal and moral case against destroying the press is stronger. Because Joseph never received a final trial, categorical claims of judicial vindication or conviction are unsound.

**Matter of faith:** Whether Joseph remained a prophet despite this civic decision, whether God providentially permitted the resulting events, and how the episode fits a theology of fallible prophetic leadership cannot be established by municipal minutes or legal precedent. Believing assessment is most credible when it does not relabel a disputed political act as revelation or dismiss substantially true disclosures as mere lies.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
