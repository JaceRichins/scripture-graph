# What about horses, steel and other anachronisms in the Book of Mormon?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
The Book of Mormon really does place horses, chariots, and steelworking in its ancient American narrative, and the present mainstream archaeological record does not securely attest horses surviving into those periods, transport chariots, or early iron-carbon steel industries in the leading proposed settings. Those are genuine difficulties for a straightforward historical reading, not objections that have simply disappeared. Some qualifications matter: Laban's steel sword occurs in the ancient Near East, where early steel is archaeologically credible; the text never describes chariot wheels or says explicitly that horses pulled them; native little barley makes a blanket claim about “no American barley” too broad; and neither the text nor the Church fixes a modern map. Believing translation and limited-geography models remain possible, but proposed substitutions such as tapir for horse or litter for chariot are not independently demonstrated. The honest verdict is therefore a material unresolved challenge to historicity, not a proof of fraud, uninspiration, or the impossibility of every ancient-source model.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- **What the text says:** [[1 Nephi 18]] 18:25 finds horses in the promised land; [[Enos 1]] 1:21 says Nephites raised “many horses”; [[Alma 18]] 18:9-12 and [[Alma 20]] 20:6 associate horses and chariots with Lamoni's journeys; [[3 Nephi 3]] 3:22 includes both in a population's defensive relocation; [[Ether 9]] 9:19 gives horses and elephants to the Jaredites. These are narrative usages, not all biblical metaphors.

- **American steel is explicit:** [[2 Nephi 5]] 5:15 describes working steel, [[Jarom 1]] 1:8 lists steel with metals, tools, and preparations for war, and [[Ether 7]] 7:9 says Shule made steel swords. For Mesoamerica, Dorothy Hosler's archaeological chronology places metallurgy centuries later and principally in copper traditions. No accepted pre-Columbian steel industry matching these passages has been identified there.

- **Horse chronology remains difficult:** directly dated and genomic research, including Taylor and colleagues' 2023 Science study, materially advances the history of Indigenous horse use but still places the studied animals in the post-contact spread of European-derived horses. It does not supply Book of Mormon-period horses. Claims of late-surviving ancient American horses have not achieved broad acceptance through secure contextual dating.

- **The Old World distinction matters:** [[1 Nephi 4]] 4:9 places Laban's steel sword at Jerusalem. Early Near Eastern carburized iron is archaeologically attested, so this is not the same evidentiary problem as steel production in [[2 Nephi 5]] or [[Ether 7]]. [[1 Nephi 16]] 16:18 is likewise in the Old World journey, although the precise meaning and practicality of a “fine steel” bow remain debatable.

- **Geography limits the inference but does not erase it:** [[Book of Mormon Geography]] and Alma 22:27-34 show why evidence from one proposed region cannot automatically cover all possible settings. Conversely, moving the setting separately for each difficult item without a single prior geographic model would be model-shopping rather than corroboration.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- **Ordinary-language mismatch:** The most natural reading of “horse,” “chariot,” and “steel” is the familiar one. The words occur in practical contexts—raising and feeding horses, preparing horses and chariots for travel, gathering them as possessions, working steel, and making steel swords. A critic can reasonably argue that repeated ordinary usage makes wholesale semantic substitution less likely.

- **Expected archaeological visibility:** Domesticated horses used across generations and steel production sufficient for tools or weapons would normally leave more than a single fragile trace: animal remains, ancient DNA, artistic representations, tack, slag, furnaces, metallurgical debris, characteristic weapons, or ecological effects. The absence of this connected package is more serious than the absence of one named artifact.

- **Chariot inference:** Although [[Alma 18]] never says “the horse pulled the chariot,” it commands horses and chariots together for conducting a king to another land. A royal litter is possible, but the ordinary transportation reading is not careless or textually baseless. No source-language evidence identifies the conveyance as a litter.

- **Uncontrolled loan shifts:** “Horse means tapir,” “steel means hardened copper,” or “chariot means litter” can preserve compatibility, but without plates available for linguistic testing or a rule predicting substitutions in advance, the method risks explaining every failed expectation after the fact. Possibility is not positive evidence.

- **Cumulative pattern:** A critic need not claim that every historical objection is equally strong. Horses, American steel, Old World domesticates, chariots, and certain crops can be considered jointly as an Old World-looking material vocabulary. A nineteenth-century KJV-informed composition explains that vocabulary economically, especially alongside the strong English biblical mediation assessed in [[Book of Mormon Assessment]].

- **Limited geography has costs:** [[Book of Mormon Geography]] warrants caution about universal claims, but an unspecified small setting also makes the historical model difficult to test. If different candidate regions are chosen to secure Mesoamerican cement, eastern North American little barley, or other isolated correspondences, the result is not one coherent archaeological model.

- **Silence is not symmetrical:** Archaeology is incomplete, but incompleteness alone does not predict why several culturally consequential technologies and domesticates remain unattested. The burden is stronger when the text implies repeated or organized use rather than an isolated object.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
- **The received English need not be source-transparent:** [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] finds pervasive, often specifically KJV-shaped mediation. A believer may therefore consistently hold that familiar biblical English labels approximate unfamiliar animals, materials, or conveyances. This establishes room for semantic translation; it does not identify which disputed nouns were translated that way.

- **Read only what the text requires:** [[Alma 18]] and [[Alma 20]] require some animals called horses and objects called chariots in the English text, prepared for royal travel. They do not describe wheels, harnesses, cavalry, mounted combat, or a continent-wide transport system. [[Ether 9]] requires an animal rendered “elephants,” but gives no anatomy or exact externally fixed date. Narrowing the claim reduces—but does not remove—the mismatch.

- **Geographic and preservation caution is legitimate:** The Church's [[Book of Mormon Geography]] position identifies no modern location, and the internal map is primarily relative. Negative evidence from Mesoamerica is strongest against a Mesoamerican model, not a logical proof covering every unexcavated locality in two continents. Small populations, recycling, corrosion, and poor preservation can reduce visibility.

- **Do not preserve obsolete blanket objections:** Native little barley was cultivated or tended in pre-Columbian North America, so “barley did not exist anywhere in America” is false. Early steel is known in the Near East, making Laban's sword materially plausible in its setting. Such corrections demonstrate why every proposed anachronism needs its own chronology; they do not solve horses or American steel.

- **Absence is defeasible evidence:** Securely contextualized discoveries can change archaeological judgments. It is reasonable for believers to remain open to new evidence. It is not reasonable to describe hoped-for discoveries as though they already exist or to count disputed remains as established.

- **Historicity and inspiration are distinct:** Even if some English material vocabulary arose through modern revelatory expression or expansion, that would not alone decide whether the book teaches truth or mediates revelation. Conversely, spiritual conviction does not function as archaeological confirmation. See [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]], [[Revelation]], and [[Testimony]].
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Frameworks — how this can fit, and how it cannot

<!-- SG:BEGIN frameworks -->
**What the text itself requires.** The received English asserts American narrative referents called horses, chariots, steel, cattle, and other items; it also presents itself as an ancient transmitted record. It does **not** specify horse anatomy, steel carbon content, chariot construction, a modern map, a hemispheric population, or a source-transparent translation. Biblical horses inside Isaiah or Micah material are evidence of biblical mediation, not independent American zoological reports. The serious issue is therefore not whether the printed words exist, but which historical and translation model best predicts them.

- **Final-form English with pervasive biblical/KJV mediation — independently supported.** [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] establishes this origin-neutral baseline strongly. It makes simplistic word-for-word source transparency unlikely and gives semantic translation models some antecedent plausibility, but it does not show that “horse” or “steel” is a particular substitution.

- **Straightforward ancient-history model with ordinary referents — possible, but presently challenged.** On this reading, actual horses, transport chariots, and steel industries existed in the relevant American settings. It best preserves ordinary English meaning. Current zooarchaeology and archaeometallurgy do not independently support the required package, so the model presently bears substantial evidentiary cost.

- **Limited-geography ancient record — plausible as geography, not independently sufficient as a solution.** The relative map in [[Book of Mormon Geography]] and the Church's lack of an official modern map support limiting archaeological inferences. A small setting can reduce expected evidence. It becomes ad hoc if its boundaries move from item to item or are defined only to avoid contrary findings.

- **Ancient record with loan shifts or functional equivalents — possible; particular identifications speculative.** A translator might use a familiar name for an unfamiliar animal, alloy, or conveyance. Brant A. Gardner develops this model in “Anachronisms in the Book of Mormon.” “Chariot” as royal litter is textually possible because wheels are never described. “Horse” as tapir and “steel” as another hard material remain ad hoc unless independent linguistic, contextual, or material evidence selects those referents.

- **Relict horse or proboscidean survival — biologically possible in the abstract, presently not independently supported for the required eras.** Fossils prove that equids and proboscideans once lived in the Americas, but those Pleistocene occurrences are far earlier than the usual narrative chronology. A survival model needs securely dated Book of Mormon-period remains, not merely fossils from the same continent or disputed contexts.

- **Ancient core with substantial translation-level or modern expansion — possible.** This explains ancient narrative material alongside modern or KJV-shaped terminology. As [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] notes, no surviving source manuscript independently marks the proposed layers. The framework becomes ad hoc if expansion is invoked only at each difficult word without prior criteria.

- **Nineteenth-century KJV-informed composition — plausible.** Known KJV availability, established biblical mediation, ordinary nineteenth-century English material vocabulary, and unified final-form control predict apparent American anachronisms economically. The present dossier does not establish a particular source book, collaborator, conscious fraud, or a complete naturalistic production history; those require [[Did Joseph Smith copy the Book of Mormon from other books]], [[Translation of the Book of Mormon]], and witness evidence.

- **Inspired modern scripture without ancient American history — possible as a theological model, but in tension with the book's own historical self-presentation.** This can preserve religious value while accepting modern material vocabulary. It is not the straightforward account advanced by the narrators or the official [[Book of Mormon (Gospel Topic)]], so the tension must be stated rather than hidden.

- **Miraculous preservation, disappearance, or divinely unrestricted translation — possible within theism but ad hoc as historical explanation.** A miracle can accommodate missing evidence, yet unless independently evidenced it predicts no distinctive archaeological outcome and cannot count as confirmation.

**Considered and set aside.** A hemispheric model was set aside because the text does not require all Indigenous Americans to descend exclusively from Book of Mormon peoples. “Every listed item has been vindicated” was set aside because horses and American steel remain unresolved. “One anachronism proves fraud” was also set aside: an anachronism bears first on date or transmission, while intent and inspiration are separate questions.
<!-- SG:END frameworks -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
**Established:** The received text repeatedly uses “horse” in American narrative settings and explicitly describes American steelworking and steel swords. Present securely dated evidence does not attest a Book of Mormon-period horse population, transport-chariot system, or matching steel industry in the leading archaeological settings. Near Eastern steel makes Laban's sword a different and less serious issue. Native little barley shows that some older blanket lists require correction.

**Open:** The underlying referents of translated nouns, the precise translation mechanism, the modern geography, archaeological preservation rates, and the possibility of a layered ancient-plus-modern text remain unresolved. Loan shifts, relict populations, and undiscovered industries are possibilities, but they presently lack the independent evidence needed to become solutions. A nineteenth-century KJV-informed composition currently has economical explanatory power for the English vocabulary, while this dossier alone does not establish the book's complete production history.

**Matter of faith:** Whether God inspired the book, used a loose revelatory translation, or intended historically unfamiliar realities to be expressed through familiar English cannot be settled by zoology or metallurgy. Archaeological gaps should not be concealed in defense of faith, and spiritual conviction should not be mislabeled archaeological proof. The required [[Book of Mormon Assessment]] likewise reaches no overall historicity verdict because archaeology, anachronisms, production history, and witnesses have not yet been assessed together at sufficient depth.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->

## Further study

<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read the relevant passages as grouped evidence: [[1 Nephi 4]] and [[1 Nephi 16]] for Old World steel; [[1 Nephi 18]], [[Enos 1]], [[Alma 18]], [[Alma 20]], and [[3 Nephi 3]] for horses and chariots; [[2 Nephi 5]], [[Jarom 1]], and [[Ether 7]] for American metallurgy; [[Ether 9]] for Jaredite animals; and [[Mosiah 9]] for crops.

Then compare the official [[Book of Mormon Geography]] position with the internal map summarized at [[Where did the Book of Mormon take place]]. For translation models, read [[How was the Book of Mormon translated]], [[Translation of the Book of Mormon]], and Brant A. Gardner's “Anachronisms in the Book of Mormon.” For archaeology outside devotional literature, begin with Dorothy Hosler's “Ancient West Mexican Metallurgy” and Taylor et al.'s “Early Dispersal of Domestic Horses into the Great Plains and Northern Rockies.” The former tests Mesoamerican metallurgy; the latter shows that earlier-than-once-assumed Indigenous horse dispersal after contact is not evidence of pre-contact or Book of Mormon-period survival.

Finally, weigh this issue through [[Book of Mormon Assessment]], [[Is the Book of Mormon an ancient historical record]], [[Do chiasmus and Hebraisms show the Book of Mormon is ancient]], [[Is Nahom evidence for Lehi's journey]], [[How reliable are the Book of Mormon witnesses]], and [[Why does the Book of Mormon quote Isaiah and the King James Bible]]. These are different evidence lines and should neither be mechanically added nor dismissed one at a time.
