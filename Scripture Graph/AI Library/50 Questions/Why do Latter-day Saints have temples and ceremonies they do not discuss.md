# Why do Latter-day Saints have temples and ceremonies they do not discuss?

## Concise answer

<!-- SG:BEGIN concise-answer -->
Latter-day Saints understand temples as the restoration of the Israelite
temple's purpose — a house where God's presence is sought and covenants are
made — extended to the whole family of God through ordinances for the living
and, by proxy, the dead. The endowment (a ritual drama of creation, fall and
return, with covenants of obedience, sacrifice, chastity and consecration)
and sealing (marriage and family bonds intended to last beyond death) are
treated as sacred rather than secret: members promise not to describe a few
specific symbols, but the purpose, the covenants and the theology are
published openly by the Church. The reticence is real; the content is less
hidden than outsiders assume.
<!-- SG:END concise-answer -->

## Strongest supporting points

<!-- SG:BEGIN strongest-evidence -->
- Israel's tabernacle and temple were the center of biblical worship, with
  graded holiness, washing and anointing, sacred vestments, and rites open
  only to the initiated ([[Exodus 28]]–[[Exodus 29]], [[Leviticus 8]]);
  restricted sacred space is thoroughly biblical.
- Early Christianity had a "discipline of the secret" (*disciplina arcani*):
  the Eucharist and baptismal rites were closed to the unbaptized for
  centuries, as the church fathers attest.
- Baptism for the dead is mentioned without objection by Paul
  ([[1 Corinthians 15]]); the practice answers the ancient question of the
  unevangelized dead directly.
- The Church publishes the temple's purpose and the substance of the
  covenants (the "Temples" section of its website, the temple preparation
  manual); the endowment's ceremonial elements are also widely known.
<!-- SG:END strongest-evidence -->

## Strongest objections

<!-- SG:BEGIN objections -->
- The endowment as introduced in Nauvoo (1842) came weeks after Joseph
  Smith's Masonic initiation and shares symbols, handclasps and structure
  with Masonic ritual; critics see borrowing, not restoration.
- Secrecy oaths (before 1990 with penalty gestures) and the exclusion of
  non-member family from weddings have caused real pain and look like the
  practices of a closed society.
- Historic Christianity has no endowment and no proxy ordinances beyond
  Paul's single reference; the temple program is a nineteenth-century
  innovation by any external measure.
<!-- SG:END objections -->

## Responses

<!-- SG:BEGIN responses -->
Believers answer that Joseph Smith's own view was that Masonry preserved
fragments of ancient temple worship, so overlap was expected, and that the
endowment's content — creation, covenant, and ascent to God's presence — has
no Masonic counterpart; that the covenant of confidentiality concerns a
handful of symbols, not the doctrine; that the 1990 and later revisions show
a living institution adjusting form while keeping substance; and that the
Church now permits civil weddings before sealings precisely to spare
excluded family. The ancient parallels (Jewish and early-Christian ascent
texts, the *disciplina arcani*) are genuine even if not proof.
<!-- SG:END responses -->

## Honest assessment

<!-- SG:BEGIN assessment -->
The Masonic connection is historical fact and the Church does not deny it;
what it means is the real question, and "Joseph borrowed the vessel to
carry something else" is a defensible reading, not a dodge. Temples are the
place where Latter-day Saint theology is most itself — eternal families,
universal salvation through proxy work, humans on a path to God — and where
it is least like Protestantism. A seeker should expect the ceremonies to feel
strange and the theology to be worth understanding before judging the form.
<!-- SG:END assessment -->
