# Kolob

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Kolob is a named celestial body in the Book of Abraham's sacred cosmology. In [[Abraham 3]], the Lord calls it “the great one” among governing bodies, places it near himself and his throne, associates it with a distinctive reckoning of time, and calls it the greatest of the Kokaubeam Abraham had seen (Abraham 3:3–4, 9, 16). Abraham 3:13 glosses Kokaubeam as “stars, or all the great lights” of heaven, although the chapter's overlapping language of stars, planets, governing ones, and great lights does not map neatly onto modern astronomical categories. [[Abraham 5]] invokes the time of Kolob while explaining that [[Adam]] had not yet received his own reckoning (Abraham 5:13). The canonical Explanation to Facsimile 2 also identifies Figure 1 as Kolob and adds descriptions such as “first creation” and nearness to God's residence. These texts portray an ordered cosmos but do not provide enough observational data to identify Kolob securely with a known astronomical object.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
In [[Abraham 3]], Kolob first appears among celestial “governing ones.” It is called “the great one” because it is near God and is assigned governance over those belonging to the same order as the world on which Abraham stands (Abraham 3:3). The text does not define “same order” as physical similarity or say that this is Kolob's only possible domain. One revolution of Kolob corresponds to a day in the Lord's manner of reckoning and to one thousand years according to the earth's appointed time (Abraham 3:4). An ascending sequence of planetary time-reckonings leads toward Kolob, which is set near God's throne (Abraham 3:9).

Abraham 3:13 defines Kokob as “star” and Kokaubeam as “stars, or all the great lights” of heaven. Verse 16 then calls Kolob the greatest of all the Kokaubeam Abraham had seen because it is nearest to God. The qualification “that thou hast seen” matters: the verse describes the revealed comparison rather than making an unrestricted claim about every possible celestial body.

In [[Abraham 5]], Abraham interrupts the third-person narration to say that the tree-of-knowledge command was framed according to the Lord's time, after the time of Kolob, before [[Adam]] had been assigned his own reckoning (Abraham 5:13). This establishes differentiated temporal frames. It may caution against assuming that every scriptural use of temporal language means an ordinary mortal day, but it does not state the duration of Abraham 4's creative “times,” calculate the age of the earth, or supply a complete celestial chronology.

## Geography & Identification
<!-- SG:BEGIN geography -->
Kolob's secure canonical location is relational rather than mappable: it is “near” God and “nigh unto the throne of God” (Abraham 3:3, 9). The canonical Explanation to Facsimile 2 similarly associates it with God's residence and calls it the first creation and first in government. Abraham 3 also speaks of revolutions, planets, stars, great lights, rank, and governance. These features permit a physical cosmological reading, but the text gives no coordinates, spectral characteristics, visual-sky marker, or other data sufficient for identifying Kolob with a known star, planet, or galaxy.

Several readings remain under investigation. A literal reading treats Kolob as a real celestial body whose physical location is presently unknown. A symbolic or pedagogical reading emphasizes divine order, relative time, and nearness to God, though the text's repeated language of bodies and revolutions prevents symbolism from being assumed as the only intent. An ancient-cosmological-idiom model reads hierarchy, governance, and nearness through premodern rather than modern astronomical categories; the supplied dossier does not provide enough specialist scholarship to evaluate that proposal. A critical model regards Kolob and its association with Facsimile 2 as products of Joseph Smith's nineteenth-century composition. The present sources do not supply the Egyptological or lexical evidence needed to adjudicate that provenance claim.

Later writers document reception rather than settle identification. [[Key to the Science of Theology]] calls Kolob a central governing planet or sun, while [[Life of Heber C. Kimball]] uses poetic language such as “king of kokaubeam.” References to a “Kolob stake” in twentieth-century conference reports concern an ecclesiastical unit and provide no evidence about the celestial Kolob.
