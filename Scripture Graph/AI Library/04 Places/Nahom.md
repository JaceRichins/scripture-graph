# Nahom

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Nahom is named once in the Book of Mormon, as the place where [[Ishmael (father-in-law of Nephi)]] died and was buried during the wilderness journey of [[Lehi (father of Nephi)]] and his party (1 Nephi 16:34). The ensuing narrative describes grief, murmuring, threats against Lehi and [[Nephi (son of Lehi)]], divine chastening, and repentance; the next travel notice reports movement nearly eastward (1 Nephi 16:35–39; 17:1). The text gives no coordinates or description sufficient to locate Nahom on a modern map. Some Latter-day Saint researchers associate it with the ancient South Arabian name Nihm, represented in inscriptions found near [[Marib]]. That is an interesting but disputed correlation, not an archaeological identification of Ishmael's burial place or proof of the journey. See [[Is Nahom evidence for Lehi's journey]] for the larger argument.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
In [[1 Nephi 16]], Nahom's explicit function is funerary: “Ishmael died, and was buried in the place which was called Nahom” (1 Nephi 16:34). His daughters then mourn and connect their loss with prolonged affliction, hunger, thirst, fatigue, and fear of death (1 Nephi 16:35). The episode continues with murmuring, Laman's proposal to kill Lehi and Nephi, the voice of the Lord chastening the group, and repentance (1 Nephi 16:36–39). Narrative continuity associates this crisis with the burial episode, although the text does not explicitly state that every ensuing event occurred at the burial site.

The next travel notice says that the party traveled “nearly eastward from that time forth” (1 Nephi 17:1), so Nahom is the final named point before that narrated directional transition. This does not establish that the turn occurred precisely at Nahom or that no unnamed travel intervened. The phrase “the place which was called Nahom” also contrasts with first-person statements that the travelers called other locations Shazer, Irreantum, and [[Bountiful (Old World)]] (1 Nephi 16:13; 17:5–6). The contrast is observable; the further inference that Nahom was a pre-existing local name is plausible but uncertain.

## Geography & Identification
<!-- SG:BEGIN geography -->
**What the canon establishes:** Nahom occurs within a wilderness itinerary that earlier proceeds generally south-southeast near the Red Sea and is later followed by nearly eastward travel toward [[Bountiful (Old World)]] (1 Nephi 16:13–14, 33–34; 17:1, 5–6). The text provides no coordinates, neighboring settlement, distance, tribal description, or uniquely reconstructable route. It therefore cannot identify Nahom with a modern site by itself.

**The modern proposal:** Some Latter-day Saint researchers associate Nahom with Nihm in South Arabia. Altar inscriptions discovered at the Bar'an temple near [[Marib]] reportedly contain the gentilic form *nhmyn*, commonly interpreted as identifying a donor affiliated with Nihm. Paleographic dates around the seventh or sixth century BC have been proposed, but such dating is approximate and should not be treated as an exact synchronization with the Book of Mormon chronology. The altar findspot, the donor's affiliation, the inferred ancient territory of Nihm, the later geographic name, and Nephi's burial place are distinct items that the proposed identification attempts to connect.

**Evidentiary assessment:** Under a historical-Lehi model, an ancient Nihm-related name in a broadly plausible part of Arabia may count as limited support for the geographic plausibility of the narrative, especially when considered with the subsequent eastward travel notice. It still does not establish that the party visited the region or that Ishmael was buried there. Under a modern-composition model, the resemblance may be explained by coincidence, selection among short regional names, flexible route reconstruction, or possible transmission of a similar name through pre-1830 geographic materials; no particular transmission route has been demonstrated here.

The principal limitations are that the inscriptions attest a tribal or donor affiliation rather than a site labeled Nahom, the English forms do not by themselves establish exact linguistic identity, the ancient territorial location is reconstructed rather than supplied by the altar findspot, and the scriptural itinerary does not determine a unique route. The correspondence is therefore disputed and potentially interesting, but its evidentiary strength is modest. See [[Is Nahom evidence for Lehi's journey]] and [[Book of Mormon Geography]].
