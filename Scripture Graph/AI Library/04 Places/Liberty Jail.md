# Liberty Jail

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Liberty Jail was the county jail in Liberty, Clay County, Missouri, where [[Joseph Smith Jr]] and several companions — including [[Hyrum Smith]] and [[Lyman Wight]] — were confined through the winter of 1838–1839, in the aftermath of the violent expulsion of the Saints from [[Far West]] and other Missouri settlements. The body text of [[D&C 121]], [[D&C 122]], and [[D&C 123]] never names the jail; their association with this place rests on documentary provenance and section-heading tradition, which identifies them as an extract from a single letter Joseph Smith and others wrote to the Church and [[Edward Partridge]] on March 20, 1839. Held for months without a final trial, Joseph used the confinement to write some of the most personal and doctrinally weighty passages of latter-day scripture. As President Boyd K. Packer later put it, 'the words liberty and jail do not fit together very well' (see [[The Test (President Boyd K. Packer, October 2008)]]) — an irony built into the town's own name. The place should not be confused with the many scriptural uses of *liberty* as a common noun (Galatians 5, 2 Peter 2, 2 Nephi 1), which concern kinds of freedom rather than this Missouri site.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## In Scripture
<!-- SG:BEGIN scripture-profile -->
The Liberty Jail material begins with unshielded lament. In D&C 121:1–6, Joseph asks where God is, how long suffering will continue, and when divine judgment will come. The answer addresses him as a son, promises peace, describes his adversity as a small moment in eternal perspective, and promises exaltation if he endures well (D&C 121:7–9). That comparison does not erase the magnitude of present suffering: D&C 123:1–6 commands the Saints to preserve detailed evidence of abuses and property losses.

The discourse then widens beyond Joseph's immediate confinement. D&C 121:34–42 contrasts coercive dominion with persuasion, patience, gentleness, meekness, love, kindness, and pure knowledge; D&C 121:43–46 joins principled correction to increased love and divine companionship. D&C 122:5–9 sets accusation, family separation, imprisonment, danger, and death within a Christological frame: trials can give experience, the Son of Man descended below all, Joseph's days are known, and God will be with him. Finally, D&C 123:7–17 turns suffering into disciplined public action by directing the Saints to publish their evidence, present it to authorities, and cheerfully do everything within their power.

Taken together, [[D&C 121]], [[D&C 122]], and [[D&C 123]] move from lament to reassurance, from reassurance to an ethic of noncoercive authority, and from reflection to evidence-gathering and action. This literary-theological arc is a defensible reading of the extracts; it does not prove that their later canonical division reproduces the structure of the original letter.

## Geography & Identification
<!-- SG:BEGIN geography -->
The relevant Liberty is Liberty, Missouri, historically within Clay County—not an unspecified symbolic location and not any scripture passage using liberty to mean freedom. The supplied timeline places the confinement in 1838–1839, while cited letters identify Liberty Jail as their place of writing on December 16, 1838; March 20–22, 1839; and April 4, 1839. [[Historical Summary]] also identifies a later Liberty Jail Historic Site in Liberty, Missouri.

Claims about the surviving structure require more care. [[Conference Report, October 1920 (General Conference (full report), October 1920)]] reported that an old cell was then standing near the center of town. That establishes what was publicly reported in 1920, not which parts of today's historic presentation are original. The supplied context does not contain enough architectural or archaeological documentation to determine the present site's precise proportion of original, preserved, relocated, or reconstructed fabric.
