# Verbal Intertextuality in 2 Nephi 32

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
The ask-and-knock language in 2 Nephi 32:4 loosely resembles Matthew 7:7, while “pray always, and not faint” in 2 Nephi 32:9 closely tracks Luke 18:1. The Luke correspondence carries most of the diagnostic force, and the combined cluster moderately supports New Testament or KJV-shaped mediation in the final English text while leaving its mechanism unresolved.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
2 Nephi 32:4 says readers “ask not, neither do ye knock,” partially and negatively recasting Matthew 7:7's familiar ask-seek-knock saying while omitting “seek.” More distinctively, 2 Nephi 32:9 says “pray always, and not faint,” closely matching Luke 18:1's ordered KJV wording. These are two related but dependent prayer-language correspondences, not a sustained quotation.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The Luke 18:1 correspondence is sufficiently close to support deliberate textual, translation-level, or traditional mediation. The Matthew 7:7 correspondence is less diagnostic because ask-and-knock language was widely reusable in Christian exhortation.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The received English text participates in recognizable New Testament prayer language. Ancient teaching rendered or harmonized into KJV idiom, revelatory reuse, indirect devotional transmission, and nineteenth-century composition can all produce that result.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a KJV-mediated translation model, 2 Nephi 32:4's 'ask...knock' language draws on one of the era's most familiar teaching formulas without requiring direct textual dependence, while 2 Nephi 32:9's closer match to Luke 18:1 fits a translator (ancient or modern) reaching for the standard English rendering of an exhortation to persistent prayer. Both readings are consistent with the wording; nothing here requires distinguishing them further.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The cluster moderately supports New Testament or KJV mediation in the final English text but gives little discrimination among the principal translation and composition models. It creates tension only for a strict model requiring close preservation of pre-Christian wording without later Christian harmonization.

**Inspiration:** The correspondences imply nothing by themselves about inspiration; inspired translation, revelatory harmonization, indirect reception, and ordinary literary reuse can all produce them.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallels do not establish direct copying, direction of dependence, the underlying source language, or ancient versus modern authorship.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Ancient source with KJV-shaped translation** — predicts: Conceptually corresponding teachings are rendered through familiar New Testament formulations.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Revelatory or editorial harmonization** — predicts: Later Christian wording intentionally clarifies the relationship between the teachings.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Indirect devotional transmission** — predicts: Portable biblical prayer language recurs without a conscious citation event.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Nineteenth-century KJV-informed composition** — predicts: Familiar biblical clauses are directly or memorably reused in a Christian exhortation.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Strict pre-Christian wording with minimal harmonization** — predicts: Conceptual overlap is more expected than recognizable New Testament English wording.. Fit with this evidence: *some difficulty*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Direct or remembered reuse of Matthew 7:7 and Luke 18:1 during nineteenth-century composition. — *plausible*: Both passages were available in the KJV, and the Luke wording is particularly close.
- An ancient discourse was rendered through familiar KJV expressions conveying comparable meanings. — *plausible*: A loose or harmonizing translation model independently predicts recognizable biblical English.
- Revelatory harmonization intentionally linked the teaching to later Christian scripture. — *possible*: This fits a revelatory model, although the verbal evidence does not independently identify the process.
- The wording arose through general Christian devotional usage without direct access to these verses. — *possible*: Ask-and-knock language is commonplace, but the complete Luke-like clause makes wholly independent convergence less economical.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->
