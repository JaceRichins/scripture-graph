# English Scriptural Intertextuality in Enos 1

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Enos 1:8's 'thy faith hath made thee whole' and 1:27's blended 'come...ye blessed...place prepared...mansions of my Father' echo, respectively, a recurring Gospel healing formula and two of the most familiar passages in English Christian devotional literature (Matthew 25:34; John 14:2-3). This moderately supports deliberate engagement with specific KJV New Testament wording in the final English text, but the healing formula recurs often enough within the Gospels themselves to function as a stock phrase rather than a unique citation, which caps the support in the moderate rather than strong band. The parallels do not determine translation mechanism, direction of dependence, or historicity, and are not independent of the Book of Mormon's broader, already well-documented KJV-mediation pattern.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Enos 1:8 ends 'thy faith hath made thee whole,' matching the recurring KJV Gospel healing formula (Mark 5:34 and close variants elsewhere). Enos 1:27 combines 'Come unto me, ye blessed' (cf. Matthew 25:34) with 'there is a place prepared for you in the mansions of my Father' (cf. John 14:2-3), blending two distinct, well-known passages into one sentence about Enos's anticipated death.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The final English text draws on recognizable King James New Testament phrasing at two points in the chapter, one a short stock formula and one a compound blend of two separate famous verses applied to a personal death scene.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
This is compatible with an ancient text rendered into English via a KJV-saturated translation process, with direct nineteenth-century authorship steeped in the same biblical language, or with ordinary convergence on common Christian devotional vocabulary about faith, healing, death, and heaven.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a translation model, the passage fits a text rendered into the receptor language's available scriptural vocabulary; on a composition model, it fits an author steeped in the same well-known KJV passages about faith, death, and heaven. Both reconstructions are live and the wording alone does not choose between them.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
Believers typically read this as the expected register of a translation into an English religious idiom; critics cite it as evidence of nineteenth-century composition using memorized biblical language, especially notable because a purportedly pre-Christian speaker anticipates explicitly Gospel/parable imagery. Because the individual phrases involved are common formulas within the KJV itself, the evidence discriminates only moderately, not strongly, between these readings.

**Inspiration:** Nothing: whether the text is inspired is independent of whether its English wording echoes familiar biblical phrasing, since both a revealed translation and a modern composition could produce this pattern.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->

This is not independent additional weight beyond the Book of Mormon's already well-documented broader KJV/New Testament-mediation pattern (cf. bom-isaiah-textual-dependence, bom-alma5-biblical-english-dependence).

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.78) for the proposition: *The final English text of Enos 1 deliberately engages recognizable New Testament wording in a King James register, rather than converging only on generic Christian themes.*

The recurring Gospel formula in Enos 1:8 and the clustered Matthew–John wording in Enos 1:27 strongly support deliberate New Testament/KJV-shaped mediation in the final English text, while leaving loose translation, revelatory harmonization, editorial adaptation, and nineteenth-century composition unresolved.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: The 'faith hath made thee whole' formula (or close variants) recurs multiple times within the KJV Gospels themselves (e.g. Matthew 9:22; Mark 5:34; Mark 10:52; Luke 8:48; Luke 17:19; Luke 18:42), so it functions as a stock phrase rather than a citation uniquely identifying one verse. The 'come...ye blessed...mansions/prepared place' combination draws on two of the most frequently quoted passages in English-language Christian funeral and devotional literature, material any KJV-literate author writing about anticipated death would have had ready access to without deliberate source-checking.

What would move this: A phrase-level match to New Testament wording that is rare or unattested outside one or two source verses (not a recurring KJV-wide formula) and tied to a distinctively Book-of-Mormon theological point absent from the source would raise this weight. Finding that Joseph Smith-era devotional or funeral literature routinely combined exactly these same KJV clauses in the same order, making the combination itself a period commonplace, would lower it.
