# Textual Dependence Or Shared Source in Moses 2

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Moses 2 and Abraham 4 reproduce Genesis 1's creation-week structure and much of its specific wording closely enough that, at the level of the received English text, a direct literary relationship among the three is essentially certain. This gives strong support for textual dependence but says nothing about which account has priority, whether an ancient source underlies the overlap, or the accounts' historicity or inspiration.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Moses 2 and Abraham 4 track Genesis 1's day-by-day creation sequence and command-formula ('And God/I, God, said...and it was so'...'the evening and the morning were the ___ day') closely across the whole chapter, with extensive near-verbatim overlap (documented at roughly 74 verses against Genesis 1 and 28 against Abraham 4).
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
All three texts belong to one literary family of the Priestly-style creation account, sharing structure and much specific wording rather than being independently composed cosmogonies.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
Establishes textual dependence among the three accounts at the level of the received English text; does not by itself indicate direction of dependence or date.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Read alongside Genesis 1, Moses 2 and Abraham 4 fit as three tellings of the same creation week sharing the same day-formula and near-identical wording; on the restoration model this reflects recovery or expansion of underlying material through the same King James idiom Joseph Smith had available, and on the composition model it reflects deliberate reuse of Genesis as a template. Either way the shared architecture situates all three within one creation-account tradition rather than a separate cosmogony.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
Steelmanned: whatever underlying ancient material (if any) the Moses and Abraham material draws on, its English expression is thoroughly harmonized to the King James Genesis available to Joseph Smith, down to distinctive idiom ('without form, and void'). That is real, informative textual evidence of dependence on the KJV Genesis tradition specifically. But all three live models predict this equally well, so the finding discriminates for 'a textual relationship exists' and not for which model produced it.

**Inspiration:** Nothing on truth or inspiration follows from the overlap itself; it is informative about compositional/revisory method (that the material was produced in close dialogue with the available King James English), not about whether that method was inspired.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Does not establish direction of dependence (whether Genesis was abridged from a fuller original the other accounts partially restore, or vice versa), historicity of a creation event, translation method, or the ancient date of any distinctive Moses/Abraham wording.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Ancient-source restoration rendered/expanded in KJV idiom** — predicts: Substantial verbatim overlap with added elaboration. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Revelatory harmonization/expansion of the received Genesis text** — predicts: Near-total overlap with the KJV plus theologically motivated additions -- the most economical fit to the density observed. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **19th-century theological composition modeled on Genesis** — predicts: The same close-overlap-plus-expansion pattern from an author working directly from the KJV text. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Shared earlier tradition underlying all three — *plausible*: Consistent with standard source-critical treatment of Genesis 1 itself
- Direct expansion/revision of the Bible text Joseph Smith had in hand — *independently supported*: Moses is documented as part of the Joseph Smith Translation project, an independently attested historical undertaking, not a speculative mechanism
- Ordinary 19th-century theological composition using Genesis as a template — *plausible*: Fully sufficient to produce the observed density of overlap without positing any ancient source
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
A Second-Temple pseudepigraphal creation account (e.g. Jubilees) tracking Genesis 1's wording this closely would be called straightforward literary dependence without controversy; the same standard applies here.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.84) for the proposition: *Moses 2 and Abraham 4 stand in a direct literary/textual relationship with the Genesis 1 creation tradition, rather than being independently composed cosmogonies.*

The extensive verbal and sequential correspondence among Moses 2, Genesis 1, and Abraham 4 strongly supports a direct literary relationship among these canonical creation accounts, consistent with sibling dependence issues elsewhere in the registry, but does not identify direction of dependence, an ancient date, or the accounts' ultimate historical origin.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: This density of sequential-plus-verbatim overlap (dozens of matching verses) is the hallmark of direct copying or expansion rather than independent variant tradition, which typically diverges in sequence and wording; this is not a cherry-picked subset but the entire creation account.

What would move this: Independent pre-1830 documentary evidence of a distinct underlying source used by Joseph Smith would raise weight toward a genuine-restoration model; if Moses 2's specific expansions beyond bare Genesis overlap matched known contemporaneous 19th-century theological discourse, that would strengthen the composition model.
