# Explicit Quotation And Textual Overlap in Mosiah 14

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[Mosiah 14#^mosiah-14-1|Mosiah 14:1–12]] explicitly attributes its material to Isaiah and reproduces the sequence of [[Isaiah 53#^isa-53-1|Isaiah 53:1–12]] in closely corresponding English. This strongly supports Isaiah and KJV-shaped textual mediation in the Book of Mormon's final form. Ancient material rendered through familiar biblical language, translation-level substitution, revelatory quotation, and direct nineteenth-century reuse remain viable mechanisms.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Mosiah 14 explicitly introduces Isaiah and then reproduces the twelve-verse sequence of Isaiah 53 in closely corresponding English, beginning with the shared clauses “Who hath believed our report” and “to whom is the arm of the Lord revealed?”
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The explicit attribution, chapter-length scope, preserved order, and close English wording make an intended relationship with Isaiah 53 unmistakable. The critical question is not whether the relationship exists but at which compositional or translation stage the KJV-shaped English entered the Book of Mormon.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The chapter is compatible with an ancient community quoting an Isaian text later rendered through the KJV, with translation-level substitution or harmonization, with revelatory reproduction of received biblical language, or with direct nineteenth-century reuse. Its close English form strongly challenges a model requiring a translation verbally independent of the KJV.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
The strongest critical model treats Mosiah 14 as direct reuse of the readily available KJV Isaiah 53 during nineteenth-century composition, an economical explanation of its wording and order. An ancient-transmission reconstruction also fits if Abinadi's source contained Isaiah 53 and the English translator rendered that source by adopting the familiar KJV chapter; under this model the chapter establishes continuity of attributed content while the exact English belongs mainly to the translation layer.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The chapter strongly supports the canonical corpus-level assessment of KJV/Isaiah textual mediation. It does not independently favor ancient transmission over modern composition, and its contribution should not be counted separately from the broader Book of Mormon Isaiah-dependence finding.

**Inspiration:** The textual dependence itself implies nothing about inspiration, which is compatible with translation-level biblical mediation as well as with other compositional models.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The correspondence does not establish which ancient Isaiah form, if any, stood on the claimed plates; whether Abinadi historically delivered the quotation; whether the KJV wording arose by translation, revelation, redaction, or composition; or whether the Book of Mormon is inspired.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
**What the text itself requires:** Mosiah 14 presents its material as Isaiah's words and reproduces the full sequence corresponding to Isaiah 53. The text requires deliberate Isaiah attribution and final-form textual dependence; it does not require that the surviving English reproduce the precise wording of an ancient Nephite manuscript.

- **Ancient Isaian source rendered through KJV wording** (*plausible*) — predicts: An ancient quotation of Isaiah appears in the final English translation with extensive KJV language and the same passage order.. Fit with this finding: *consistent*. Support for the model: The narrative explicitly attributes the passage to Isaiah, while the corpus's pervasive biblical English makes KJV-shaped rendering a coherent translation model.
- **Translation-level replacement or harmonization** (*plausible*) — predicts: A translator substitutes the familiar KJV Isaiah 53 for an underlying quotation or paraphrase judged equivalent.. Fit with this finding: *supports*. Support for the model: This directly explains the chapter-length English agreement but is not independently documented for this particular translation event.
- **Revelatory quotation in received biblical English** (*possible*) — predicts: Revelation supplies Isaiah's teaching in familiar KJV form regardless of the wording of an underlying record.. Fit with this finding: *consistent*. Support for the model: It is permitted by a revelatory translation framework, though the verbal comparison alone cannot independently test the proposed mechanism.
- **Nineteenth-century composition using the KJV** (*plausible*) — predicts: The composer directly incorporates Isaiah 53, retaining its wording, order, and attribution.. Fit with this finding: *supports*. Support for the model: The KJV was directly available in the production environment, and this model parsimoniously predicts the exact English relationship.

**Considered and set aside:**
- Coincidental resemblance — Explicit attribution and a full twelve-verse same-order correspondence rule out coincidence as a serious explanation.
- Unmediated preservation of KJV English on an ancient record — Early modern English cannot literally have appeared on a premodern source; a translation, revelation, or modern-composition stage is required.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- An authentic ancient quotation was translated by adopting the corresponding KJV chapter. — *plausible*: This accounts for both the narrative's Isaiah attribution and the final English correspondence without requiring KJV wording on an ancient record.
- The KJV chapter was directly reused during nineteenth-century composition. — *plausible*: Direct access to the KJV independently explains the passage's extent, wording, and sequence.
- A revelatory process intentionally reproduced the received English Bible. — *possible*: This accommodates the relationship but is not distinguished from translation substitution or direct reuse by the textual data.
- Both chapters independently descend from Isaiah while the close English wording arose without KJV mediation. — *ad hoc*: Common ancient ancestry can explain shared content, but it does not economically explain near-identical early modern English across the complete sequence.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, discrimination 0.84) for the proposition: *Mosiah 14 (Abinadi's quotation of Isaiah) is directly textually dependent on, or mediated through, the King James rendering of Isaiah 53.*

Mosiah 14's explicit attribution and chapter-length English correspondence strongly support Isaiah and KJV textual mediation in the Book of Mormon's final form, but do not distinguish KJV-shaped translation from direct nineteenth-century reuse.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: Explicit quotation of a named source makes ordinary phrase-sharing rates largely irrelevant: the relationship is announced by the text and continues across an entire chapter in the same order. The relevant denominator is the full twelve-verse passage rather than selected matching clauses, although this chapter remains one dependent instance within the Book of Mormon's larger group of Isaiah quotations.

What would move this: The weight for KJV mediation would rise if distinctive KJV-only readings and errors were shown to recur systematically throughout the chapter and fall if an independently attested pre-KJV witness consistently explained its distinctive readings better than the KJV; choosing among origin models would require evidence about the underlying text or translation process, not additional English overlap alone.
