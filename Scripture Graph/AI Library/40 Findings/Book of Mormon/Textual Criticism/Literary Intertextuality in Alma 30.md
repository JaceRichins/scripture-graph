# Literary Intertextuality in Alma 30

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[Alma 30#^alma-30-6|Alma 30:6]], [[Alma 30#^alma-30-12|Alma 30:12]], [[Alma 30#^alma-30-43|Alma 30:43]], [[Alma 30#^alma-30-50|Alma 30:50]], [[Alma 30#^alma-30-52|Alma 30:52]], and [[Alma 30#^alma-30-53|Alma 30:53]] substantially parallel the opponent, sign-demand, affliction, and confession sequence beginning in [[Jacob 7#^jacob-7-1|Jacob 7:1]], [[Jacob 7#^jacob-7-13|Jacob 7:13]], and [[Jacob 7#^jacob-7-14|Jacob 7:14]]. The deaths are a looser match: Korihor is later trampled among the Zoramites in [[Alma 30#^alma-30-59|Alma 30:59]], unlike Sherem's proximate death following his affliction and confession. The cluster moderately supports deliberate final-form patterning, while conventional cautionary narrative, editorial shaping, and both ancient and modern composition remain viable explanations.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Jacob 7 and Alma 30 each introduce an opponent of prophecy concerning Christ, place him in confrontation with a prophetic authority, include a demand for a sign, report a disabling divine judgment, and culminate in a confession followed by death. The ending is not equally close: Sherem's death follows his affliction and public confession directly, whereas Korihor is first struck dumb, later cast out, and eventually trampled to death among the Zoramites.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The ordered cluster supports deliberate final-form patterning of Korihor as a later counterpart to Sherem. The unequal endings and the availability of a conventional opponent-sign-punishment plot make direct dependence less certain than the broad resemblance initially suggests.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The comparison identifies a recurring narrative type used to portray challenges to Christ-centered prophecy and their consequences. It is compatible with remembered parallel events, editorial typology, reuse of an established cautionary pattern, or composition of both episodes within one literary design.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
The two accounts fit a recurring cautionary pattern in which opposition to prophecy leads to a demand for proof, incapacitating judgment, and confession. On a deliberate-typology reconstruction, Korihor reprises Sherem while varying the outcome: loss of speech replaces Sherem's collapse, and Korihor's later social expulsion and death extend the consequences beyond the initial sign.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The cluster moderately supports deliberate intratextual coordination in the received Book of Mormon. It provides little discrimination between ancient compilation, later editorial shaping, and nineteenth-century composition, because each can intentionally produce the pattern; it consequently supplies little independent support for antiquity or historicity.

**Inspiration:** The literary parallel by itself implies nothing about whether the text is divinely inspired.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallel does not establish that either episode occurred historically, that one account directly borrowed from the other, which direction any dependence ran, or whether the pattern arose in ancient or modern composition.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Deliberate intratextual typology** — predicts: A later opponent narrative should repeat several ordered and theologically meaningful features of an earlier one while adapting details to its own setting.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Editorial shaping of separate traditions** — predicts: A compiler could preserve distinct episodes while emphasizing common vocabulary and plot beats to create a recurring anti-Christ type.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Conventional cautionary narrative** — predicts: Religious stories about hostile challengers may independently employ disputation, a demanded sign, punishment, confession, and death without direct episode-to-episode dependence.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
- **Independent similar historical episodes** — predicts: Comparable challenges within the same religious culture could follow broadly similar trajectories, although the full ordered cluster would be less expected without narrative shaping.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
- **Unified nineteenth-century composition** — predicts: A single modern author could deliberately repeat and vary an established antagonist pattern across the book.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Both stories independently use a conventional religious cautionary plot in which an opponent demands proof and is punished. — *plausible*: Most individual beats are familiar moral-narrative elements, and no matched-corpus study establishes how unusual their combination is.
- Separate historical memories were deliberately assimilated by a compiler into a recurring anti-Christ pattern. — *plausible*: Editorial shaping explains both the common sequence and the differences in setting, affliction, confession, and death.
- Two similar historical confrontations independently generated the observed sequence without literary shaping. — *possible*: Recurrent disputes are conceivable, but the clustered sequence is less fully explained and the accounts lack independent external attestation.
- Translation-level harmonization created the entire narrative correspondence. — *ad hoc*: Translation choices can align wording, but they do not readily account for a multi-stage correspondence in plot unless substantial narrative harmonization is separately assumed.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

The prior assessment was slightly too strong because it treated the two deaths as equally matched and did not discount the common cautionary-story template or unreported search denominator.

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Moderate** (supports, evidence_strength 0.58) for the proposition: *The final form of Alma 30 deliberately patterns the Korihor narrative on, or alongside, the Sherem narrative in Jacob 7.*

The ordered challenge, sign-demand, affliction, and confession parallels moderately support deliberate Sherem–Korihor patterning in the final text, but the divergent deaths, conventional cautionary structure, and missing matched controls keep the relationship below the strong band and leave its date and mechanism unresolved.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: Opponent, sign-demand, punishment, confession, and death motifs are recurrent in religious cautionary literature, but no matched control corpus or predeclared search across Book of Mormon narratives has been reported. Because many characters and plot features could be compared across the book, the look-elsewhere cost is substantial; the ordered cluster remains noteworthy, while the weaker correspondence between the two deaths reduces its specificity.

What would move this: The weight would rise if a predeclared comparison found multiple distinctive phrase-level and ordered correspondences that are rare in matched religious narratives; it would fall if matched controls produced similar clusters frequently or if source analysis showed that the two accounts entered the text independently without shared editorial shaping.
