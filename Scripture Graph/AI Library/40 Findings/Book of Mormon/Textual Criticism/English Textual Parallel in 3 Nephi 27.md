# English Textual Parallel in 3 Nephi 27

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
The ask-and-knock saying in [[3 Nephi 27#^3ne-27-29|3 Nephi 27:29]] and the two-gates saying in [[3 Nephi 27#^3ne-27-33|3 Nephi 27:33]] closely reprise [[3 Nephi 14#^3ne-14-7|3 Nephi 14:7–8]], [[3 Nephi 14#^3ne-14-13|3 Nephi 14:13–14]], and their parallels in [[Matthew 7#^matt-7-7|Matthew 7:7–8, 13–14]]. Their pairing, close wording, and preserved order strongly support deliberate final-form reuse. Historical repetition, stable translation wording, editorial reuse, and modern composition remain viable mechanisms, and this reprise is not independent additional evidence for Matthew dependence.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
3 Nephi 27:29 repeats the ask-and-knock language found in 3 Nephi 14:7–8 and Matthew 7:7–8. Later, 3 Nephi 27:33 repeats the contrast between the strait and wide gates found in 3 Nephi 14:13–14 and Matthew 7:13–14. The two distinguishable teaching clusters retain the same relative order, although 3 Nephi 27 abbreviates and adapts them.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The paired clusters, close wording, preserved order, and prior occurrence within the same narrative strongly favor intentional reprise. The immediate textual relationship may run through 3 Nephi 14, through Matthew 7 as mediated in both passages, or through a stable translation template.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The reprise is compatible with Jesus repeating earlier teachings, a translator rendering recurring sayings through the same English template, an editor harmonizing two discourses, or a modern author reusing an earlier passage. It establishes deliberate final-form cohesion more securely than the historical setting or mechanism of repetition.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The strongest supportive reconstruction is authentic pedagogical repetition: a teacher addressing the same community can naturally restate central teachings in an abbreviated concluding discourse. That historical model fits well, but editorial reuse and modern composition predict the same ordered recurrence at least as readily, so the finding supports textual design rather than ancient authorship.

**Inspiration:** The literary reprise implies nothing by itself about inspiration, because repetition and textual mediation occur in both inspired and non-inspired literature.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The reprise does not establish that the repetition occurred historically, that an ancient source contained Matthew-like wording, that 3 Nephi directly copied Matthew, or that the Book of Mormon is ancient or modern.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Historical repetition by Jesus** — predicts: A teacher may intentionally repeat and abbreviate earlier teachings for the same audience or a later instructional setting.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Ancient source with stable translation templates** — predicts: Related sayings may be rendered through the same KJV wording and thereby acquire close English correspondence.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Later editorial reuse** — predicts: An editor familiar with the earlier discourse may reprise two sayings in their established order to reinforce continuity.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Nineteenth-century composition** — predicts: An author with access to Matthew 7 and the already composed 3 Nephi 14 can readily repeat both clusters in the same order.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Independent recurrence** — predicts: Traditional sayings may recur separately, but two distinct clusters should not often recur in matching order and close wording without linkage.. Fit with this evidence: *strongly challenged*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Jesus intentionally repeated teachings previously given to the same community. — *plausible*: Teachers commonly repeat central sayings, and the narrative attributes both occurrences to Jesus.
- A translator used stable KJV wording for corresponding sayings in an ancient source. — *plausible*: Consistent translation templates can create close English matches without identical source-language wording.
- A later editor or modern author deliberately reused the earlier Book of Mormon passage. — *plausible*: Direct access to 3 Nephi 14 economically explains the wording, pairing, and preserved order.
- Both passages independently preserve a shared oral teaching tradition. — *possible*: Shared tradition can explain recurring sayings, although the close English wording still suggests common translation or editorial mediation.
- The two clusters recurred together by chance. — *ad hoc*: Chance does not adequately explain the close wording and retained order of two distinguishable sayings.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
Comparable repeated saying clusters in biblical literature would be treated as strong evidence of shared tradition, literary reuse, or editorial harmonization while leaving historical repetition possible. The same standard supports deliberate reprise here without converting literary dependence into a historical verdict.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.78) for the proposition: *The received wording of 3 Nephi 27 deliberately reprises the ask-and-knock and two-gates teachings already presented in 3 Nephi 14 rather than reproducing both ordered clusters by coincidence.*

The paired sayings, close wording, and preserved order strongly support an intentional reprise of 3 Nephi 14 in 3 Nephi 27, without identifying whether the reprise is historical, translational, editorial, or compositional.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: Teachers and religious texts often repeat short maxims, so recurrence of either ask-and-knock or two-ways imagery alone has a meaningful base rate. Both clusters recurring in the same order and close wording is more distinctive, although no matched-control frequency or accounting of all searchable sayings has been supplied. Because 3 Nephi 14 is internally available, the reprise is not independent evidence for the broader Matthew-dependence issue.

What would move this: Assessment dated 2026-09-04. The weight would rise if further distinctive and ordered correspondences tied 3 Nephi 27 specifically to 3 Nephi 14 rather than merely to general gospel tradition; it would fall if matched ancient and nineteenth-century discourse corpora showed that paired recurrence of these formulas in this order commonly occurs without literary linkage.

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a historical reconstruction, Jesus returned to two central sayings already delivered to the community and restated them in the same pedagogical order. On a translation reconstruction, related ancient sayings were rendered through stable KJV templates. On editorial and modern-composition reconstructions, the writer reused the readily available 3 Nephi 14 sequence to give the later discourse thematic closure; all three locate the evident reprise without requiring coincidence.
