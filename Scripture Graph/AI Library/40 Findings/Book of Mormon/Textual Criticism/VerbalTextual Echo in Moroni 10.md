# VerbalTextual Echo in Moroni 10

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Moroni 10:19's description of Christ as “the same yesterday, today, and forever” closely matches Hebrews 13:8's ordered formula. The shared referent and sequence moderately support a Hebrews-related textual or devotional echo, while the formula's brevity and uncertain circulation leave direct borrowing, indirect reception, KJV-shaped translation, and revelatory harmonization unresolved.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Moroni 10:19 describes Christ as “the same yesterday, today, and forever.” Hebrews 13:8 reads “Jesus Christ the same yesterday, and to day, and for ever.” The referent, assertion of sameness, three temporal terms, and their order closely correspond, with minor syntactic and orthographic differences.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The match supports mediation through Hebrews 13:8 or the Christian devotional tradition derived from it. The specific ordered triad is more diagnostic than the general doctrine of divine changelessness, but it is shorter and more portable than a sustained passage-level correspondence.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The received English text participates in a recognizable Christian formulation of Christ's constancy. KJV-shaped translation, revelatory harmonization, indirect reception, and direct nineteenth-century reuse remain viable.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a KJV-mediated model, whether the underlying source is ancient revelation or nineteenth-century composition, Hebrews 13:8 supplied the natural, distinctive English vessel available for expressing Christ's changeless nature in three-part yesterday/today/forever form; the specificity of the match is what needs explaining, and translation- or composition-level reuse of a well-known verse explains it without requiring an ancient Hebrew or Greek antecedent for the phrase itself.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The formula moderately supports a Hebrews-related relationship in the final English text. It creates tension for strict preservation of pre-Christian wording without later Christian mediation but does not materially distinguish the principal harmonizing, revelatory, and modern-composition models.

**Inspiration:** The echo implies nothing by itself about inspiration, since inspired expression and ordinary translation, reception, or literary reuse can all produce it.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The match does not establish direct copying from Hebrews, the direction or precise mechanism of transmission, the language or date of underlying material, or ancient versus modern authorship.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Direct or remembered reuse of Hebrews 13:8. — *plausible*: The referent, sameness claim, temporal terms, and their order closely match the KJV verse.
- KJV-shaped translation of an ancient statement about Christ's constancy. — *plausible*: A loose or harmonizing translation can supply recognizable biblical wording for a corresponding underlying idea.
- Indirect reuse of a Christian devotional formula already shaped by Hebrews. — *plausible*: Biblical expressions commonly circulate beyond conscious quotation, although no matched frequency for this complete formula is supplied.
- The broader biblical doctrine of divine immutability generated the wording independently. — *independently supported*: Divine changelessness is independently attested in passages such as Malachi 3:6 and James 1:17, but those passages do not account for the precise ordered temporal triad.
- Wholly independent invention of the complete ordered formula as applied to Christ. — *possible*: Past-present-future triads are natural expressions of permanence, but the full correspondence makes complete independence less economical.
