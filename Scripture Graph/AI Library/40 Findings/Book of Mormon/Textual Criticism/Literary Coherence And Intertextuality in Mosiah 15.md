# Literary Coherence And Intertextuality in Mosiah 15

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[Mosiah 14#^mosiah-14-7|Mosiah 14:7]], [[Mosiah 14#^mosiah-14-8|Mosiah 14:8]], and [[Mosiah 14#^mosiah-14-10|Mosiah 14:10]] supply the silent servant, generation, and seed language resumed in [[Mosiah 15#^mosiah-15-6|Mosiah 15:6]], [[Mosiah 15#^mosiah-15-10|Mosiah 15:10]], and [[Mosiah 15#^mosiah-15-11|Mosiah 15:11]]. The publisher-of-peace interpretation in [[Mosiah 15#^mosiah-15-14|Mosiah 15:14]] and [[Mosiah 15#^mosiah-15-18|Mosiah 15:18]], followed by the watchmen and universal-salvation language in [[Mosiah 15#^mosiah-15-29|Mosiah 15:29]] and [[Mosiah 15#^mosiah-15-30|Mosiah 15:30]], makes the chapter a sustained exposition of adjacent Isaianic themes. The progression connects the servant's suffering and seed with prophetic proclamation, Christ as founder of peace, and salvation made known throughout the earth.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
After Mosiah 14 quotes Isaiah 53, Mosiah 15 explicitly resumes the silent-sheep image, repeats the question about who will declare the servant's generation, interprets the servant's seed, and develops Isaiah's publisher-of-peace language. The chapter then invokes watchmen, Zion, and worldwide salvation language from the neighboring Isaianic context.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
Mosiah 15 is a sustained exposition linking the suffering servant to Christ and the servant's seed to those who receive prophetic testimony, then broadening the discussion from publishers of peace to the founder of peace and universal salvation.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The chapter illustrates a scriptural-exposition practice in which quoted wording, unresolved questions, and nearby prophetic images become the structure for subsequent interpretation. It shows how the Book of Mormon's final form reads Isaiah 53 together with adjacent herald, watchman, Zion, and salvation themes.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On the passage's exegetical reconstruction, Abinadi first reapplies the servant's silent suffering, then treats Isaiah's questions about generation and seed as prompts requiring explanation. He identifies believers who receive prophetic testimony as the servant's seed, associates prophets with publishing peace, and identifies Christ as the ultimate founder of that peace. The concluding watchmen and universal-salvation imagery expands the exposition from the servant's redemptive work to its proclamation and recognition throughout the earth.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->
