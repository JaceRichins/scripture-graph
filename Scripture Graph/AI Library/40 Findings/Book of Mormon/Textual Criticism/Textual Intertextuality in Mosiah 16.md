# Textual Intertextuality in Mosiah 16

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Mosiah 16:7-10 clusters five distinctive elements matching 1 Corinthians 15:53-55, strongly supporting deliberate scriptural intertextuality in the final English text, though in a partly reversed order. Because 1 Corinthians postdates Abinadi's narrative setting, this cannot reflect straightforward ancient dependence in that direction, so translation-level harmonization, shared use of Isaiah 25:8/Hosea 13:14, redaction, and modern composition all remain live explanations for the mechanism, without weakening the relationship's discrimination for the final text.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Mosiah 16:7-10 clusters five distinctive elements matching 1 Corinthians 15:53-55 in similar order: corruption/incorruption, mortality/immortality, death swallowed up, the sting of death, and the grave's victory.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The concentration and ordering of five matched elements make deliberate scriptural intertextuality in the English wording highly likely, though weaker and less exact than a full verbatim quotation.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
Because 1 Corinthians postdates Abinadi's c. 148 BC narrative setting, straightforward ancient Nephite dependence on Paul's letter is not a live option; the relevant models are translation-level harmonization, shared use of the same Isaiah 25:8/Hosea 13:14 imagery, or modern composition.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a loose or harmonizing translation model, an ancient resurrection discourse could be rendered into the most familiar available English formulation, which happens to be Paul's; on a modern-composition model the same cluster is directly available from the KJV NT.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
Moderately supports deliberate NT-register mediation in the final text; the anachronistic direction (Abinadi cannot be quoting Paul) creates real difficulty for a strict tight-control-translation model and favors either loose/harmonizing translation or shared-imagery/modern-composition explanations.

**Inspiration:** Nothing independently: recognizable Pauline diction in a translated or composed text says nothing on its own about whether the doctrine taught is true or inspired.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Does not establish which specific mechanism (translation harmonization, shared Isaiah/Hosea tradition, redaction, revelation, or modern reuse) produced the parallel, nor the date or authorship of the underlying content.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Shared Isaiah 25:8/Hosea 13:14 imagery, independently developed** (*plausible*) — predicts: Some but not all of the five elements would match, since the shared source supplies death-swallowed-up/sting language but not the corruption/incorruption pairing distinctive to Paul.. Fit with this finding: *some difficulty*. Support for the model: Isaiah 25:8 and Hosea 13:14 independently attested pre-Christian sources for part of the cluster.
- **Loose/harmonizing translation using familiar Pauline resurrection language** (*plausible*) — predicts: A translator rendering an ancient resurrection discourse could reach for the most familiar available English formulation, producing the full five-element match.. Fit with this finding: *consistent*. Support for the model: Corpus-wide pattern of KJV-shaped translation of doctrinal discourse.
- **Nineteenth-century direct composition** (*plausible*) — predicts: An author familiar with 1 Corinthians 15 could reproduce the full cluster directly.. Fit with this finding: *consistent*. Support for the model: Demonstrated extensive KJV NT reuse elsewhere in the corpus.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- A translation of an ancient record intentionally rendered related resurrection concepts in familiar KJV language. — *plausible*: Fits the corpus's established translation-mediation pattern.
- A modern composer drew directly on 1 Corinthians 15. — *plausible*: Equally well predicted given the corpus's demonstrated NT reuse.
- Both passages draw on a broader scriptural tradition represented in Isaiah 25:8 and Hosea 13:14. — *plausible*: Explains part but not the full corruption/incorruption pairing distinctive to Paul.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
An anachronistic-looking verbal dependence of an earlier text on a later one would, in any other ancient corpus, be flagged as a genuine compositional or transmission puzzle rather than waved away; this note keeps that difficulty visible rather than resolving it by fiat.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, discrimination 0.75) for the proposition: *Mosiah 16:7-10's corruption/incorruption, mortality/immortality, death-swallowed-up, sting-of-death, and grave-victory cluster reflects deliberate textual or translation-level dependence on the 1 Corinthians 15:53-55 tradition in the final English text.*



Base rate / look-elsewhere: 1 Corinthians 15's resurrection language is among the most quoted NT passages in Christian literature generally; a five-point match is a fairly distinctive cluster, but the individual phrases (sting of death, victory over the grave) circulated widely in period religious idiom beyond Paul's letter specifically.

What would move this: Evidence that Isaiah 25:8/Hosea 13:14 imagery alone (without the Pauline corruption/incorruption pairing) appears in other ancient resurrection-hope texts would strengthen the shared-tradition alternative and lower this weight; a demonstrated textual layer predating the King James Version's specific wording would raise it.
