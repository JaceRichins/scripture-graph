# Intertextual Resonance in Mormon 7

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Mormon 7:5 combines victory over the grave with the sting of death being swallowed up, closely participating in the image complex found in Isaiah 25:8 and especially 1 Corinthians 15:54–57. The clustered correspondence strongly supports biblical or KJV-shaped intertextuality in the final English text, while translation, revelation, redaction, shared tradition, and nineteenth-century composition remain viable mechanisms. **Scripture:** [[Mormon 7#^morm-7-5|Mormon 7:5]], [[Isaiah 25#^isa-25-8|Isaiah 25:8]], [[1 Corinthians 15#^1cor-15-54|1 Corinthians 15:54]], [[1 Corinthians 15#^1cor-15-55|1 Corinthians 15:55]], [[1 Corinthians 15#^1cor-15-56|1 Corinthians 15:56]], [[1 Corinthians 15#^1cor-15-57|1 Corinthians 15:57]]
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Mormon 7:5 places "victory over the grave" beside "the sting of death swallowed up." Isaiah 25:8 has death swallowed up in victory, while 1 Corinthians 15:54–57 combines that Isaianic expression with the questions about death's sting and the grave's victory.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The English verse appears to recombine the same resurrection-image complex used in 1 Corinthians 15, changing its syntax while retaining the unusually concentrated associations of death, swallowing, sting, grave, and victory.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The passage belongs to a Christian resurrection discourse shaped by biblical language in its extant English form. The relationship may lie in an underlying discourse, translation diction, editorial harmonization, revelatory phrasing, or modern composition.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 15 joins Isaiah's swallowed-death image to the sting and grave language of a second scriptural taunt, creating a memorable resurrection cluster. On a KJV-mediated translation reconstruction, Mormon's proclamation about Christ's resurrection was rendered through that familiar cluster; on a modern-composition reconstruction, the author synthesized the same biblical language directly. An ancient shared-tradition or revelatory reconstruction also fits the theology, though it has less independent traction on the specifically English phrasing.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The cluster strongly supports deliberate biblical intertextuality in the final English text. It offers little discrimination between a KJV-shaped translation of ancient material and direct nineteenth-century reuse, so it is strong for the named intertextual proposition but not independently strong for either ancient or modern authorship.

**Inspiration:** The verbal relationship itself implies nothing about whether the Book of Mormon is inspired; inspired and non-inspired compositional models can both employ earlier scriptural language.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
**What the text itself requires:** Mormon 7:5 requires the claims that Christ rose, gained victory over the grave, and caused the sting of death to be swallowed up. It does not itself identify Isaiah, Paul, the KJV, or any particular transmission mechanism.

- **Ancient Christian discourse drawing on a shared resurrection tradition** (*possible*) — predicts: An ancient post-resurrection Christian writer could combine inherited images of Christ's victory over death without dependence on the English New Testament.. Fit with this finding: *not ruled out*. Support for the model: The narrative places Mormon centuries after Christ and portrays Nephite Christianity as preserving extensive resurrection teaching, but no independent ancient Nephite text attests the wording.
- **Ancient source translated or harmonized through KJV diction** (*independently supported*) — predicts: A translator familiar with 1 Corinthians 15 could render related source concepts using its familiar English image cluster.. Fit with this finding: *supports*. Support for the model: Numerous Book of Mormon passages independently exhibit close KJV-shaped wording, making biblical-English mediation a demonstrated feature of the corpus.
- **Revelatory restatement through received biblical language** (*possible*) — predicts: A revealed English text could intentionally reuse familiar scriptural language to express theological continuity.. Fit with this finding: *consistent*. Support for the model: This is compatible with a loose or revelatory translation theology, although the mechanism is not independently testable from this parallel.
- **Later Christian redaction** (*possible*) — predicts: A Christian editor could add or reshape the clause using Pauline resurrection language.. Fit with this finding: *consistent*. Support for the model: Redaction can explain the wording, but no manuscript layers or independently identified redactor attest it.
- **Nineteenth-century composition using the KJV** (*plausible*) — predicts: A KJV-literate author discussing resurrection could recombine the memorable wording of 1 Corinthians 15.. Fit with this finding: *supports*. Support for the model: The KJV was available in the text's production environment, and its language demonstrably informs the Book of Mormon's English form.

**Considered and set aside:**
- Pure chance convergence — The concentration and semantic interaction of several linked images are less plausibly accidental than mediated participation in the same scriptural language complex.
- Isaiah 25 alone as a complete source — Isaiah explains death, swallowing, and victory but not the adjacent sting-and-grave formulation most clearly represented in 1 Corinthians 15.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- The wording preserves an ancient Christian resurrection formulation related to, but not directly copied from, the Pauline formulation. — *possible*: The narrative chronology permits developed Christian teaching, but no independent witness to the proposed Nephite formulation survives.
- A translator or revelatory process expressed related underlying ideas through familiar KJV wording. — *independently supported*: KJV-shaped mediation occurs throughout the extant Book of Mormon, although this does not identify the precise translation mechanism.
- A nineteenth-century author deliberately synthesized Isaiah 25 and 1 Corinthians 15. — *plausible*: The source language was readily available and the resurrection context naturally invites the Pauline passage.
- The cluster arose through generic Christian vocabulary without conscious use of a particular passage. — *possible*: Victory over death is common Christian language, but the combined sting, grave, swallowing, and victory imagery makes wholly independent convergence less economical.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->
