# Internal Textual Coherence in Mosiah 4

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[Mosiah 4#^mosiah-4-11|Mosiah 4:11]]-[[Mosiah 4#^mosiah-4-12|12]] introduces the continuing retention of remission, while [[Mosiah 4#^mosiah-4-16|verse 16]] and [[Mosiah 4#^mosiah-4-19|verses 19]]-[[Mosiah 4#^mosiah-4-22|22]] ground care for the needy in humanity's shared dependence on God. [[Mosiah 4#^mosiah-4-26|Verse 26]] expressly reconnects imparting substance to the poor with retaining remission from day to day. Material mercy is therefore part of the sermon's account of persevering discipleship.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Mosiah 4 moves from remembering God's goodness and retaining remission in verses 11-12 to succoring the needy in verse 16, grounds generosity in everyone's dependence on God in verses 19-22, and in verse 26 explicitly introduces imparting substance to the poor as conduct undertaken "for the sake of retaining a remission of your sins from day to day."
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
Care for people in material need is integral to Benjamin's description of persevering discipleship. The sermon joins received divine mercy, recognition of common dependence, and merciful treatment of others in one sustained exhortation.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The passage presents a coherent social theology in which worship, repentance, economic obligation, and community membership are not separate subjects. Its beggar analogy turns dependence on God into the rationale for refusing contempt or neglect toward dependent neighbors.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
A covenantal reconstruction best captures the sermon's movement: recipients who have begged for and received mercy continue in that mercy by remembering their dependence and imparting what they possess to others. On this reading, generosity is neither payment for forgiveness nor a detached social appendix; it is the enacted form of the humility, remembrance, and mercy that Benjamin calls retaining remission from day to day.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->
