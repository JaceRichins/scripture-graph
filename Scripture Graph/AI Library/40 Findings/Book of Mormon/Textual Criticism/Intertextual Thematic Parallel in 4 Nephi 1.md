# Intertextual Thematic Parallel in 4 Nephi 1

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[4 Nephi 1#^4ne-1-1|4 Nephi 1:1]]–[[4 Nephi 1#^4ne-1-3|3]] combines conversion, unity, 'all things common,' and the removal of major status divisions, paralleling the Jerusalem-church summaries in [[Acts 2#^acts-2-44|Acts 2:44]]–[[Acts 2#^acts-2-45|45]] and [[Acts 4#^acts-4-32|Acts 4:32]], [[Acts 4#^acts-4-34|34]]–[[Acts 4#^acts-4-35|35]]. The exact shared phrase and surrounding constellation moderately support deliberate intertextuality, but common communal ideals, translation-level biblical language, and modern reuse remain substantial explanations.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Fourth Nephi 1:1–3 combines conversion, absence of contention, just dealing, 'all things common,' removal of rich-poor and bond-free divisions, and shared spiritual participation. Acts 2:44–45 and 4:32–35 combine unity among believers, 'all things common,' distribution according to need, and the absence of lack.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The exact phrase 'all things common' and the surrounding cluster of communal unity and economic equality create a meaningful intertextual parallel. The evidence for some relationship is moderate rather than strong because most surrounding elements are widespread descriptions of an ideal religious community.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The parallel connects 4 Nephi's idealized post-Christ society with the canonical portrait of the Jerusalem church. It is compatible with shared Christian practice, literary imitation, translation through Acts-shaped language, or independent use of a common communal ideal.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a shared-practice reconstruction, the two accounts describe communities applying a common Christian ethic of unity, generosity, and diminished status division. On a translation reconstruction, 4 Nephi's social description is expressed through the familiar Acts phrase 'all things common.' On a modern-composition reconstruction, Acts supplies both the phrase and the literary model for an ideal Christian society.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The strongest supportive reading is that an ancient Christian community in the Book of Mormon world implemented teachings also known to the Jerusalem church, with the translation rendering the result in familiar biblical language. The same evidence is also expected under modern reuse of Acts, so it offers only moderate support for deliberate intertextuality and little independent support for either ancient or modern authorship.

**Inspiration:** The resemblance to Acts neither supports nor challenges inspiration by itself.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallel does not establish direct borrowing, its direction, the historical existence of either described arrangement, or that the communities used identical rules of ownership and distribution.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Shared ancient Christian practice** — predicts: Similar reports of conversion, unity, material sharing, and reduced poverty without requiring direct literary copying.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Ancient account with Acts-shaped translation** — predicts: A broadly similar community rendered with recognizable KJV Acts wording, especially 'all things common.'. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Editorial or revelatory harmonization** — predicts: A community account deliberately aligned with the canonical Jerusalem-church ideal.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Nineteenth-century composition using Acts** — predicts: Use of the famous Acts summaries as a model for an ideal post-Christ society.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Independent communal ideal** — predicts: General unity and generosity, though the exact shared phrase is less specifically predicted.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Both accounts independently describe communities implementing a widespread ideal of religious unity and shared resources. — *plausible*: Unity, generosity, and reduction of poverty are recurrent religious ideals; this explains much of the thematic constellation but less readily the exact phrase 'all things common.'
- An ancient account was translated into familiar language from Acts. — *plausible*: Biblicalizing translation explains the verbal overlap while allowing different source-level institutions.
- A modern composer consciously modeled the community on the KJV summaries in Acts. — *plausible*: Acts was readily available and supplies both the exact phrase and a recognizable ideal-community template.
- An ancient Christian community independently adopted teachings or practices also represented in the Jerusalem church. — *possible*: The model explains thematic agreement but lacks independent evidence connecting the two communities or establishing identical economic practice.
- The resemblance establishes identical systems of communal ownership. — *ad hoc*: Neither brief summary supplies enough institutional detail to infer identical rules of property, sale, administration, or duration.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->
