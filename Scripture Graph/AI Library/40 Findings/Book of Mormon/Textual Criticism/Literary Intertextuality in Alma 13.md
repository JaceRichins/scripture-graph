# Literary Intertextuality in Alma 13

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Alma 13 shares a distinctive cluster with the biblical Melchizedek material: priest of the most high God, king of Salem, tithes, and especially the close wording match to Hebrews 7:3's 'without beginning of days... nor end of life.' This strongly supports that Alma 13's final English text stands in a direct literary or textual relationship to the Genesis 14/Hebrews 7 tradition. It is comparatively weak, independent evidence for any specific account of the Book of Mormon's historical origin, since ancient shared tradition rendered through KJV-shaped translation, and direct nineteenth-century reuse, both predict the same wording.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Alma 13:7 describes a priesthood order 'without beginning of days or end of years,' closely matching Hebrews 7:3's description of Melchizedek as 'without beginning of days, nor end of life.' Alma 13:14-15 names Melchizedek explicitly as a 'high priest' who reigned in Salem, took tithes, and whose people repented, paralleling Genesis 14:18-20 (Melchizedek, king of Salem, priest of the most high God, receiving tithes) and Hebrews 7:1-4 (same details plus the patriarch Abraham giving a tenth of the spoils).
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The cluster of shared distinctive phrases and narrative details (priest of the most high God, king of Salem, receiving tithes, an order without beginning/end) is best read as a deliberate literary echo of the Genesis 14 / Hebrews 7 Melchizedek material in Alma 13's final English text, with additional narrative material (Melchizedek's people and their repentance) not found in either biblical source.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The passage is compatible with any model in which the final English text was produced with access to, or influence from, the King James Melchizedek material, whether that access came through ancient shared tradition rendered into biblical English, translation-level harmonization, or direct nineteenth-century literary reuse.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Second Temple Jewish and early Christian literature (2 Enoch, 11QMelchizedek, Hebrews) shows an active tradition of theological elaboration on the terse Genesis 14 Melchizedek notice, filling out his priestly status and superhuman qualities. On the reconstruction where an ancient Nephite record contained its own Melchizedek material independently descended from a pre-exilic tradition related to Genesis 14, translation into English by a KJV-literate translator would naturally pull the wording toward Hebrews 7's already-canonical phrasing for the same figure, producing exactly this kind of close-but-not-identical echo. An equally available reconstruction treats the passage as nineteenth-century theological composition built directly on the Genesis and Hebrews texts, elaborated with an invented episode about Melchizedek's people repenting. Both reconstructions fit the surviving wording; the text does not choose between them.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The distinctive Hebrews 7:3 wording match ('without beginning of days... nor end of life/years') is a specific enough correspondence, not just shared subject matter, to strongly support a genuine literary or textual relationship between Alma 13 and the Hebrews 7 Melchizedek tradition in the text's final form. This is comparable to other registered strong-band KJV-dependence findings (e.g. bom-mosiah13-exodus20-dependence 0.84, bom-3nephi7-petrine-proverb-dependence 0.84) and should be scored in that range for the literary-relationship proposition specifically.

**Inspiration:** This bears on literary relationships in the final text, not on inspiration; the presence of a biblical echo says nothing by itself about whether the passage is divinely revealed.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallels do not establish independent ancient transmission of the Melchizedek material, Hebrew-language authorship, the historicity of Alma's additional account of Melchizedek's people and their repentance, or which of the three transmission models is correct.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Ancient shared tradition** — predicts: An independently transmitted ancient Melchizedek tradition would resemble Genesis and later Jewish/Christian elaborations in broad outline but need not share Hebrews' exact English phrasing, since Hebrews postdates any purported Nephite record.. Fit with this evidence: *some difficulty*.
- **KJV/translation mediation** — predicts: An ancient source's Melchizedek material would be rendered into the translator's available biblical idiom, naturally pulling in Hebrews 7's well-known 'without beginning of days' phrasing even where the underlying record differed.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Nineteenth-century composition** — predicts: An author steeped in the KJV would directly reuse Genesis 14 and Hebrews 7's distinctive phrases and combine them with theological elaboration.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- The parallels arise through direct or indirect use of the King James Bible. — *plausible*: The Hebrews 7:3 phrase match is close enough that some form of KJV mediation, ancient-source or modern, is the best-supported explanation for the wording itself.
- The parallels reflect broadly available Jewish and Christian interpretation of Melchizedek current in the early nineteenth century. — *plausible*: Melchizedek speculation was a live topic in early American religious culture, providing an available non-ancient source for the theological elaboration.
- Alma's additional narrative about Melchizedek's people and their repentance is interpretive expansion rather than preservation of an independent ancient source. — *possible*: No external ancient text corroborates this expansion; it could be original composition attached to the biblical core.
- Believing readers may understand the additions as preservation of traditions absent from the surviving Genesis account. — *possible*: This is a live faithful reading but has no independent attestation beyond the text's own claim.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
A biblical text sharing a distinctive phrase with a later-composed biblical book (e.g., an OT passage strikingly matching a NT phrase) would be flagged as a strong sign of a textual or redactional relationship requiring explanation — direction, harmonization, or common source — not treated more leniently because the earlier text is scripture. The same standard applied here means the Hebrews 7 wording match is treated as strong evidence of a textual relationship; it does not by itself require or imply late dating of Alma 13.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Strong** (supports, evidence_strength 0.78) for the proposition: *Alma 13's Melchizedek priesthood description (a priest of the most high God; an order without beginning of days or end of years; the Salem king who received tithes) stands in a direct literary or translation-level textual relationship to the Genesis 14 and Hebrews 7 Melchizedek material.*

The specific, multi-point Melchizedek cluster (priest of the most high God, king of Salem, tithes, and the distinctive 'without beginning of days... end of years' formula) strongly supports a direct literary or translation-level relationship between Alma 13 and the Genesis 14/Hebrews 7 tradition, without determining whether that relationship reflects ancient transmission, translation harmonization, or nineteenth-century composition.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: The 'without beginning of days... nor end of years/life' formula is distinctive rather than generic biblical idiom; within the Bible it appears essentially only in Hebrews 7:3, which narrows the space of plausible independent-coincidence explanations without a matched control corpus of comparable ancient priesthood texts.

What would move this: An independent ancient Near Eastern or Second Temple Melchizedek text that used the same distinctive 'without beginning of days' formulation apart from Hebrews would raise the weight for ancient shared tradition; conversely, evidence that this exact phrase was common devotional currency in early nineteenth-century American religious writing (independent of Hebrews) would lower confidence that the match is meaningfully discriminating at all.
