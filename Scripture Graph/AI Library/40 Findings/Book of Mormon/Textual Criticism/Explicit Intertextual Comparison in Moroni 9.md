# Explicit Intertextual Comparison in Moroni 9

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
[[Moroni 9#^moro-9-23|Moroni 9:23]] explicitly interprets the threatened Nephite destruction through the Jaredite precedent, especially the shared movement toward hardness, bloodshed, and revenge described in [[Ether 15#^ether-15-19|Ether 15:19]] and [[Ether 15#^ether-15-22|Ether 15:22]]. The analogy makes the two collapses part of a recurring moral-historical pattern without requiring every feature of the conflicts to correspond.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
In [[Moroni 9#^moro-9-23|Moroni 9:23]], Mormon explicitly says that the Nephites may perish “like unto the Jaredites” because their willfulness has become a search for blood and revenge. [[Ether 15#^ether-15-19|Ether 15:19]] and [[Ether 15#^ether-15-22|Ether 15:22]] portray the Jaredites as hardened and consumed by battle and anger.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The comparison explicitly interprets the prospective Nephite collapse through the remembered Jaredite destruction. It is an authorial analogy centered on willfulness, retaliatory violence, and social self-destruction, not a claim that every event in the two collapses was identical.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The comparison illustrates how the final Book of Mormon uses an earlier civilization's destruction as a moral and historiographic pattern for interpreting a later crisis. Such analogical history is compatible with remembered events, editorial theology, and deliberate literary construction.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
A plausible historical-editorial reconstruction is that knowledge of the Jaredite catastrophe supplied Mormon and then Moroni with a precedent through which to understand the Nephites' escalating retaliatory violence. At the final-form level, the comparison joins the two civilizational collapses into a recurring account of hardness, revenge, and destruction while preserving differences between their particular wars.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->
