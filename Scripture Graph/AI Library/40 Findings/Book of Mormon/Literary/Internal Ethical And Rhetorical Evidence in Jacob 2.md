# Internal Ethical And Rhetorical Evidence in Jacob 2

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Jacob 2:31-35 explicitly reasons from concrete relational harm -- sorrow, broken hearts, lost confidence of children -- to its condemnation of the men's conduct, which moderately supports harm as a real operative criterion in the chapter's ethic. Because the passage's own stated exception in v.30 is framed around divine command rather than around the absence of harm, the evidence does not establish harm as the chapter's sole or primary criterion, and a command-centered reading with harm as aggravating rhetoric remains equally viable.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Jacob 2:31–35 repeatedly describes the effects of the condemned conduct on wives and children through sorrow, mourning, cries to God, captivity, broken hearts, lost confidence, harmful examples, and sobbing.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
This accumulation makes relational harm an explicit and central dimension of the chapter's condemnation alongside its command, covenant, and sexual-ethics language. It need not be treated as the chapter's sole or deciding moral criterion.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The passage uses a scriptural mode of moral accusation in which God hears injured people and evaluates wrongdoing through its household consequences. The experiences of wives and children are therefore part of the argument itself rather than incidental background.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a final-form ethical reading, the sermon condemns disobedience to divine command while also explaining its human gravity. The wives' mourning and the children's lost confidence locate the offense within family relationships, and God's attention to their cries gives those injuries direct moral significance.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->
