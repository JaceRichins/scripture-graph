# Narrative Temporal Association in Alma 43

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Alma 43 explicitly joins the Nephites' united prayer for freedom to the battle's immediate reversal: they cry to God and, in the "selfsame hour," begin to stand with power as the Lamanites flee ([[Alma 43#^alma-43-49|43:49]]; [[Alma 43#^alma-43-50|43:50]]). The episode presents prayer, collective resolve, and the chapter's stated military circumstances (armor, terrain, encirclement) as compatible layers in its account of the turning point, rather than isolating prayer as the narrative's sole explanation.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Alma 43:49 reports that the Nephites turned on the Lamanites and cried together to God for liberty and freedom from bondage. Verse 50 then says they began to resist with power and that, in the selfsame hour of their prayer, the Lamanites began to flee toward the waters of Sidon.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The marked phrase 'selfsame hour' is a deliberate narratorial choice associating the prayer with the reversal and inviting a providential reading of the moment. It does not, by itself, identify prayer as the narrative's sole or sufficient military cause; the same chapter also describes armor, higher ground, and encirclement as part of how the battle unfolded.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
Ancient and modern battle narratives commonly combine reported strategic causes with theological interpretation of the same event. Alma 43 permits a layered account in which the narrated military circumstances explain how the reversal occurred while the prayer notice expresses its covenantal meaning for the narrator and participants.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
On a layered-causation reading, the Nephites' coordinated appeal for freedom marks the point where resolve, collective identity, and tactical resistance converge. The 'selfsame hour' phrasing lets the narrator mark that convergence as providential without excluding the concrete military mechanisms described earlier in the same chapter.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->
