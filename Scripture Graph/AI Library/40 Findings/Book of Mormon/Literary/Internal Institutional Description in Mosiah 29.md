# Internal Institutional Description in Mosiah 29

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Mosiah 29:28 describes review of a judge by a higher judge, while 29:29 makes a gathered group of lower judges, acting according to the people's voice, the check on unjust higher judges. Mosiah's proposal is best read as a hybrid of hierarchical and popular accountability, not as either bare majority rule or a modern separation of powers -- and, as plain institutional description, this observation carries no evidentiary weight, models table, or apologetic proposition.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Mosiah 29:28 provides for review of a lower judge by a higher judge. Verse 29 provides a different check at the top of the hierarchy: a small group of lower judges gathered together may judge a higher judge, with that action occurring explicitly 'according to the voice of the people.'
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The proposal combines hierarchical judicial review with a popular/representative check on higher judges. It is a hybrid accountability arrangement, not a clean alternative to majority rule -- the top-tier check is itself an expression of popular voice, not an escape from it.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
As internal description of the proposed regime, the passage shows concern about misconduct at more than one level of judgment. It supplies no full institutional blueprint: selection, tenure, jurisdiction, and how review is initiated all remain unstated, and the passage is compatible with ancient tiered-appeal traditions (Exodus 18:21-26; Deuteronomy 17:8-13) and with the political vocabulary available to an 1829 American author about equally well -- it does not indicate which produced this text.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
A useful reconstruction reads these verses as safeguards within Mosiah's transition from kingship to judges: an aggrieved person could seek review of an ordinary judge by a superior judge, and if superior judges themselves acted unjustly, a specially gathered group of lower judges, acting according to the people's voice, could hold them accountable. The regime is therefore best understood as layered accountability combining hierarchical review with popular sovereignty, rather than as either bare majoritarianism or a modern separation of powers.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->
