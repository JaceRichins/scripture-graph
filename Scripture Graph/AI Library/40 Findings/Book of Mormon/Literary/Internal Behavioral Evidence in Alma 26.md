# Internal Behavioral Evidence in Alma 26

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Ammon treats the converts' love, weapon-burial, and willingness to die as proof of their sincerity -- a real internal sincerity-argument, structurally like the registry's collective-witness notes. Because it reaches the reader through one interested narrator and shares its evidentiary base with Alma 24, it gives only none-band support for historicity or individual sincerity.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
Ammon argues from the converts' visible conduct -- love, weapon-burial, willingness to die rather than kill -- as proof ('we can witness') of their sincerity (Alma 26:31-32,34).
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
Within the speech, hard-to-fake, costly behavior is explicitly treated as stronger proof of inward change than profession alone -- a costly-signaling argument embedded in the narrative's own rhetoric.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The move is consistent with a recognizable rhetorical strategy found across ancient and later religious literature (appealing to costly conduct as proof of character), but the observation is about the text's argumentative structure, not an independent record of the underlying events.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
Ancient and early Christian missionary rhetoric regularly pointed to costly, hard-to-fake conduct -- renouncing valuable possessions, refusing to recant under threat, accepting death -- as proof superior to mere profession (compare Acts 19:19; martyr literature). Read against that convention, Ammon's argument in Alma 26 fits comfortably as an example of the same rhetorical move: it is the kind of argument a speaker defending a costly, unlikely conversion would be expected to make, whether the underlying account is an ancient missionary report, an editor's compression of one, or a later author drawing on the same familiar trope. The reconstruction that best fits the passage is simply that the text preserves (in whichever transmission mode) a speech built on this widely attested rhetorical pattern, not that the pattern itself points to one origin over another.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
Comparable to the registry's collective-witness pattern: a vivid internal claim of proof that gives only none-band support once the single-narrator source is accounted for.

**Inspiration:** Nothing about inspiration follows; the same appeal to costly conduct is available to an ancient speaker, an editor, or a modern author.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
Does not establish the episode's historicity, any individual convert's actual inward motive, or the universal effectiveness of missionary work; not independent of the Alma 24 weapon-burial narrative.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->

- **Mormon's editorial/literary shaping** — predicts: the same costly-signal argument, since it strengthens the conversion narrative for a compiling editor's purposes. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.

## Alternative Explanations

- Public group conduct can reflect covenant or social conformity rather than every individual's inward sincerity. — *plausible*
- The account may highlight exemplary conduct while omitting dissenters or relapses. — *possible*
