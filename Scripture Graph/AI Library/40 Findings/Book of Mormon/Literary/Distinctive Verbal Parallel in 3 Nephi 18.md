# Distinctive Verbal Parallel in 3 Nephi 18

## Summary
<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
The distinctive wording in 3 Nephi 18:18 closely parallels Luke 22:31, with the principal change being its application to a multitude rather than Simon. The English overlap securely supports Luke/KJV mediation, but it is only weak evidence favoring direct nineteenth-century reuse over ancient material rendered or harmonized into KJV idiom. This instance belongs to the broader KJV-language phenomenon and implies nothing by itself about inspiration.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
In 3 Nephi 18:18 Jesus tells an assembled multitude that Satan desires to have them so that he may 'sift [them] as wheat.' This closely matches Luke 22:31 in the KJV, where the warning is addressed to Simon; the principal differences concern the audience and associated grammatical adjustments. The exact shared expression 'sift you as wheat' contains four words.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
The distinctive idiom and close English wording make coincidence unlikely and support a literary or translation-level relationship with Luke's received English wording. They do not identify whether that mediation occurred through translation, revelatory harmonization, or direct modern reuse.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
The parallel is compatible with several unrelated historical scenarios: Jesus repeating a memorable image in more than one setting, a Nephite record echoing an already-known dominical saying, or an English rendering (whatever its date) drawing on familiar KJV vocabulary to express a similar concept. None of these is independently ruled out by the wording alone.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
For believers this is sometimes read as internal confirmation that 3 Nephi preserves authentic, Christ-authored teaching consistent with the Gospels; for critics it is read as evidence of literary dependence on the KJV text available to a 19th-century author. Both readings share the same underlying textual fact and neither is established by the wording alone. Steelmanning the critical reading: a six-word idiomatic match this specific, appearing in a text saturated with KJV New Testament diction elsewhere, is exactly what direct literary borrowing predicts, and that reading needs no auxiliary assumptions to work.

**Inspiration:** This wording match speaks to a compositional/translational relationship between two texts, not to whether either passage's underlying teaching is divinely inspired.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
The parallel does not establish whether the relationship is historical repetition by Jesus, translation into familiar KJV idiom of an independently ancient saying, or direct compositional borrowing from Luke; it also does not by itself favor any one of these over the others, and it is not independent evidence beyond the Book of Mormon's already-registered general pattern of KJV New Testament diction.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Tight-control ancient translation into KJV idiom** — predicts: An ancient saying, however originally phrased, could be rendered into recognizably KJV-Gospel language by a translator working in a King James idiom, producing this match without literary borrowing.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Loose/dynamic-equivalence translation model** — predicts: Familiar biblical cadence would be used to render a conceptually similar teaching, so an idiomatic match is expected and uninformative.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **19th-century composition using the KJV as a literary source** — predicts: Direct borrowing of a well-known, distinctive Gospel phrase into a new composition is exactly what this model predicts.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Independent historical repetition of the same saying by Jesus** — predicts: Predicts the same wording only if two independent traditions each preserved it with unusual precision, a stronger requirement than the other models.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Jesus repeated the sift-as-wheat image during a separate ministry. — *possible*: Teachers reuse memorable images, but no source independent of the Book of Mormon attests this second occurrence, and the close English agreement still requires preservation or translation convergence.
- A translator or revelatory process rendered an independently ancient saying through Luke's familiar KJV wording. — *plausible*: KJV-shaped language is independently pervasive in the Book of Mormon.
- A nineteenth-century author directly reused Luke 22:31. — *plausible*: The KJV was available, and direct reuse readily predicts the close English match.
- The wording arose independently by coincidence. — *possible*: It is logically possible but fits the distinctive multiword agreement substantially worse than textual, translational, or traditional linkage.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
If an extra-biblical ancient text contained a near-verbatim echo of a distinctive canonical saying, scholars would default to literary dependence or shared oral tradition, not coincidence, and would not treat the match as strong evidence of authentic independent utterance; the same standard is applied here, and the original note already met it.

What would move this: A cluster of many independent, equally distinctive KJV New Testament matches concentrated in Christ's Book of Mormon discourses would strengthen the composition-dependence reading; credible independent (non-KJV-derived) attestation of the same image in an unrelated ancient source would weaken it and support translation-idiom or historical-repetition explanations instead. ## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
The Book of Mormon's Christ-discourse chapters are pervasively rendered in King James Gospel diction; under a translation-mediated-record reconstruction, a memorable teaching image being rendered in the same words Luke uses for a related saying is exactly the kind of overlap that idiom would produce, so this instance fits the text's known translation character without requiring any additional explanation.
