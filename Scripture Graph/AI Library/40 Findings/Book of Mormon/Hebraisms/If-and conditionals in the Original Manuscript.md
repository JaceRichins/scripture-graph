# If-and conditionals in the Original Manuscript

> [!language] The observation
> The Original Manuscript and 1830 edition contain Hebrew-like *if…and*
> conditionals that are ungrammatical in English — e.g. the original text of
> [[1 Nephi 17#^1ne-17-50|1 Ne. 17:50]]: "if he should command me that I
> should say unto this water be thou earth **and** it should be earth."
> Joseph Smith removed the *and*s in the 1837 edition. Biblical Hebrew
> regularly builds conditionals this way (*wə-* apodosis).

<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
Royal Skousen's Critical Text Project reports multiple if-and conditionals in the earliest recoverable text, including material associated with 1 Nephi 17 and Helaman 12, and some were later regularized. The pattern is compatible with a Semitic calque, but attested English precedent, plausible oral-dictation and KJV-register mechanisms, an incompletely defined sample, and missing matched-corpus frequencies keep it weak support for a Semitic source.
<!-- SG:END summary -->

## Observation
<!-- SG:BEGIN observation -->
The earliest recoverable text contains multiple conditionals where an if-clause is followed/coordinated by 'and', including 1 Nephi 17:50 and a cluster in Helaman 12; some were later regularized. The supplied material does not give a complete inventory, a stable definition of qualifying examples, or matched frequencies from comparable dictated, biblical, pseudo-biblical, or Early Modern English texts.
<!-- SG:END observation -->

## Interpretation
<!-- SG:BEGIN interpretation -->
A Semitic-calque reading treats the consequence-introducing conjunction as retained from a source language. That is possible, but overstated as something English could never produce: marginal English precedents and conjunction-heavy oral syntax exist, and later editing shows only that the form was perceived as awkward, not what produced it.
<!-- SG:END interpretation -->

## Historical Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN historical-significance -->
Compatible with a relatively literal translation from a Semitic source; equally compatible with spontaneous dictation, KJV-influenced archaism, Early Modern English survivals, or some interaction among these.
<!-- SG:END historical-significance -->

## Apologetic Significance
<!-- SG:BEGIN apologetic-significance -->
The construction is worth studying because it was present early and not simply introduced later, but without a controlled denominator or evidence that this precise construction is substantially more frequent in Semitic-to-English translation than in comparable English dictation/pseudo-biblical prose, it supplies only weak evidence for a Semitic source.

**Inspiration:** This syntactic feature does not independently indicate whether the text is divinely inspired; inspired and non-inspired origin models can both produce translated or dictated marked syntax.
<!-- SG:END apologetic-significance -->

## What It Does Not Establish
<!-- SG:BEGIN does-not-establish -->
That every cited conditional shares one cause, that the construction is uniquely Hebrew, or that the Book of Mormon as a whole derives from a Hebrew document.
<!-- SG:END does-not-establish -->

## Models On The Table
<!-- SG:BEGIN models -->
- **Tight translation of an ancient Semitic text** — predicts: Repeated source-language conjunctions may be carried into English even when they produce marked conditional syntax.. Fit with this evidence: *supports*.
- **Loose translation of an ancient text** — predicts: Target-language smoothing should usually remove such calques, though occasional interference remains possible.. Fit with this evidence: *not ruled out*.
- **Nineteenth-century oral dictation** — predicts: Planning pressure and additive conjunctions can generate irregular protasis-apodosis structures.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Archaizing/Early Modern English-style composition** — predicts: Marginal constructions found in older English or biblical-register prose may recur without a Semitic source.. Fit with this evidence: *consistent*.
- **Scribal corruption** — predicts: An occasional added conjunction could occur in transmission, though repeated instances would require multiple changes.. Fit with this evidence: *some difficulty*.
<!-- SG:END models -->

## Alternative Explanations
<!-- SG:BEGIN alternatives -->
- Oral dictation produced additive and-conjunctions across clause boundaries. — *plausible*: Oral dictation is historically established and additive coordination is a known feature of spontaneous speech, though no matched quantitative study exists.
- The construction reflects marginal Early Modern English usage. — *independently supported*: English precedents reportedly exist, refuting uniqueness, though whether their frequency matches the Book of Mormon distribution is disputed.
- KJV-saturated archaizing style encouraged conjunction-heavy conditional syntax. — *plausible*: Biblical-English influence is pervasive in the text, but the causal link to this precise construction is unquantified.
- Scribes independently introduced the conjunctions. — *possible*: Scribal addition occurs generally, but early attestation and repetition make a wholesale scribal account less economical.
- Later removal proves the readings came from Hebrew rather than English. — *ad hoc*: Editing shows the wording was seen as awkward; it does not identify the mechanism that produced it.
<!-- SG:END alternatives -->

## Comparative Check
<!-- SG:BEGIN symmetry -->
A proposed Hebraism in the Bible or another ancient text would need controlled comparison with source, target, oral-production and genre conventions before receiving substantial weight; moving from marked English straight to Semitic origin without that denominator would be too generous there as well.
<!-- SG:END symmetry -->

## Evidentiary Weight
<!-- SG:BEGIN weight -->
**Weak** (supports, evidence_strength 0.2) for the proposition: *The earliest Book of Mormon's if-and conditional constructions are translation calques materially more expected from an ancient Semitic source than from English dictation or archaizing composition.*

The nonstandard if-and conditionals are real, early, and modestly consistent with a Semitic-calque reading, but documented Early Modern English precedent and ordinary oral-dictation syntax predict much the same pattern, keeping this in the weak band rather than moderate.

Base rate / look-elsewhere: At least a dozen candidates are claimed across the whole text, but the total conditional clauses searched, inclusion rules, and matched-corpus frequencies are unreported; both feature-selection and corpus-wide look-elsewhere costs are substantial.

What would move this: Would rise with a preregistered matched-corpus count showing the construction is substantially rarer in comparable English dictation/archaizing prose than in genuine Hebrew-to-English translations; would fall if such controls reproduce the Book of Mormon's rate and distribution.

## How This Fits
<!-- SG:BEGIN how-it-fits -->
If the underlying text is a fairly literal rendering of a Hebrew-like source, retaining a source-language 'and' at the head of a conditional's apodosis is a known calque pattern, and later regularization is consistent with an editor smoothing over a foreign-feeling construction rather than inventing one. That reconstruction is live, but it competes on roughly even footing with independently attested Early Modern English conditional-and usage and with features of extemporaneous oral dictation, so no single account is preferred without a matched-frequency study.
<!-- SG:END how-it-fits -->
