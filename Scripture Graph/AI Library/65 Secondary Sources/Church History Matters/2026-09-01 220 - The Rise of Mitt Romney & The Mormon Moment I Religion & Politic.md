# 220 - The Rise of Mitt Romney & The Mormon Moment I Religion & Politics I Church History Matters

[[Church History Matters]] · 2026-09-01 · 68 min · source tier **A** · episode quality **72**
[Listen / watch](https://podcasters.spotify.com/pod/show/churchhistorymatters/episodes/220---The-Rise-of-Mitt-Romney--The-Mormon-Moment-I-Religion--Politics-I-Church-History-Matters-e3nre1n)
Guests: Matt Waldrip (Republican political strategist and campaign operative; self-described 'Romney l)

> Casey Griffiths and Scott Woodward close out their summer series on Latter-day Saints and American politics with Matt Waldrip, a 15-year senior advisor to Senator Mitt Romney (deputy national finance director, 2018 Senate campaign manager, chief of staff). The conversation covers the 2012 presidential campaign's handling of Romney's faith, the White Horse Prophecy as a tool of opposition fear-mongering, Waldrip's firsthand account of Romney's 2020/2021 impeachment votes and the backlash from fellow Latter-day Saints, Romney's independent voting record, and reflections on the shifting political identity of Latter-day Saints and the 'blueprint' Romney leaves behind.

## Outline

- Series recap and guest introduction: Hosts recap their summer series on Latter-day Saints and politics (Joseph Smith, Brigham Young, Reed Smoot, Ezra Taft Benson) and introduce Matt Waldrip, a longtime senior advisor to Mitt Romney. ([[Joseph Smith Jr]] · [[Brigham Young]])
- Joseph Smith parallel and downplaying faith in 2012: Waldrip contrasts Joseph Smith's 1844 presidential run with Romney's 2012 nomination, and explains that Romney's faith was addressed far less in 2012 than in his 2008 'Faith in America' speech, partly because data suggested lingering anti-LDS bias affected turnout. ([[Joseph Smith Jr]])
- The 'Mormon moment' and missionary-style influence: Waldrip describes the 2012 'Mormon moment,' the Netflix documentary 'Mitt,' and claims of people joining the Church after encountering it through the campaign.
- Lingering suspicion and the Reed Smoot echo: Discussion of continued anti-LDS suspicion during Romney's campaigns, paralleled to the Reed Smoot hearings decades earlier.
- White Horse Prophecy as opposition tool: Waldrip recounts how the White Horse Prophecy folklore was invoked by opposition operatives and push-polls during the 2012 campaign to sow suspicion, and how the campaign's strategy was to laugh it off rather than engage.
- The impeachment votes: Waldrip gives a firsthand account of Romney's 2020 and 2021 votes to convict President Trump, describing Romney's exhaustive note-taking during the trial, late-night staff debriefs, and his framing of the vote as a juror's oath before God.
- Backlash from Latter-day Saints: Waldrip describes intense, often scripture-quoting backlash and praise from Church members over Romney's impeachment votes, including a personally difficult encounter at his home chapel.
- Breaking the partisan system: Waldrip argues Romney refused to conform to either party's expectations, citing his voting record relative to Senator Mike Lee, and describes structural pressures in Congress that force frequent compromise.
- Cynicism about structure, faith in people: Waldrip reflects on how his Senate years left him more cynical about political structure but more optimistic about individual Americans and senators, citing the bipartisan COVID-19 relief bill.
- Shifting LDS political identity: Waldrip discusses changing political patterns among Latter-day Saints, referencing 2024 election data on Utah, and connects this to Romney's example of 'calling balls and strikes' rather than following partisan lines.
- Secret Lives of Mormon Wives tangent: Waldrip draws a parallel between people who are culturally/socially 'Mormon' without practicing (as seen in reality TV) and the broader, more diverse coalition of Latter-day Saint voters today.
- Romney's blueprint and closing reflections: Waldrip closes with Romney's philosophy that relationships are the 'true currency in life,' the importance of identifying and holding personal values, and applying President Oaks' counsel to 'moderate and unify' at the interpersonal level.
