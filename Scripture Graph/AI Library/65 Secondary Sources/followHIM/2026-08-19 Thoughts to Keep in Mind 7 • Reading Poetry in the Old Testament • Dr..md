# Thoughts to Keep in Mind #7 • Reading Poetry in the Old Testament • Dr. Ross Baron • August 17-23

[[followHIM]] · 2026-08-19 · 80 min · source tier **A** · episode quality **78**
Guests: Dr. Ross Baron (Hebrew Bible language and poetry, Old Testament literary analysis, Book of Mormo)

> Dr. Ross Baron returns to Follow Him for a Thoughts to Keep in Mind episode on reading Old Testament poetry, walking Hank Smith and John Bytheway through parallelism, repetition, wordplay, and chiasmus using close readings of 2 Nephi 6:6 (quoting Isaiah 49), Isaiah 44:3, Isaiah 5:7, Psalm 23, Psalm 67, and Psalms 136, with practical study suggestions (slow down, read aloud, define unfamiliar words, identify symbols) and connections to General Conference sources including a recent Elder Renlund Liahona article and a President Nelson leadership-session address on chesed.

## Outline

- Introduction and opening banter: Hosts introduce Dr. Ross Baron and the topic of reading Old Testament poetry, with wordplay on 'Baron' vs 'barren' setting up later Hebrew wordplay discussion.
- Manual introduction: poetic books of the Old Testament: John reads the Come Follow Me manual's introduction distinguishing OT narrative books from poetic ones (Job, Psalms, Proverbs, and parts of Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos). ([[Job (patriarch)]] · [[Psalms]] · [[Proverbs]] · [[Isaiah (prophet)]] · [[Jeremiah (prophet)]] · [[Amos]])
- Wisdom literature vs. prophetic poetry: Ross distinguishes wisdom literature (Job, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes) from prophetic poetic books (Psalms, Isaiah, Amos) and reads an Elder Dale G. Renlund quote calling Psalms the ancient hymnbook of God's covenant people. ([[Psalms]] · [[Job (patriarch)]] · [[Proverbs]] · [[Ecclesiastes]] · [[Isaiah (prophet)]])
- Most-quoted Old Testament books in General Conference: Ross cites the Latter-day Saint Citation Index (scriptures.byu.edu) to note Isaiah, then Genesis, then Psalms are the most-quoted Old Testament books in general conference, Journal of Discourses, and Teachings of the Prophet Joseph Smith, linking this to their Christ-centered content. ([[Isaiah (prophet)]] · [[Genesis]] · [[Psalms]] · [[2 Nephi 11]])
- Psalm 22, Isaiah 53, and the Atonement: John and Ross discuss Psalm 22 and Isaiah 53 as key poetic texts illuminating Christ's atoning sacrifice, noting Jesus quotes Psalm 22 from the cross. ([[Psalm 22]] · [[Isaiah 53]])
- The Hallel Psalms and the Last Supper: Ross explains Psalms 113-118 (the Hallel) were likely sung at Passover, with 113-114 before the meal and 115-118 after; he connects this to the hymn Jesus and the apostles sang before going to Gethsemane, and to quotations of Psalm 118 at the Triumphal Entry. ([[Psalms]] · [[Passover]] · [[Gethsemane]])
- Feast of Tabernacles water ceremony: John notes the Feast of Tabernacles water-drawing ceremony and associated psalm about 'wells of salvation,' which set up Jesus's 'living water' teaching.
- Practical suggestions for reading OT poetry: Ross offers study techniques: read more slowly, read aloud, look up unfamiliar words/references, and identify symbols and images rather than rushing through chapters.
- Parallelism as the defining feature of Hebrew poetry: Ross identifies parallelism as the chief characteristic of Hebrew poetry (versus rhyme), alongside imagery, rhythm, repetition, symbolism, and chiastic structure.
- 2 Nephi 6:6 parallelism analysis (Isaiah 49 via Jacob): Ross walks through the parallel structure of 2 Nephi 6:6 word by word in Hebrew (lift up/set up, hand/standard, Gentiles/people), connecting it to God's hand as a symbol of Christ and to temple covenants of nursing/nourishing others back to Christ. ([[2 Nephi 6]] · [[Isaiah 49]] · [[Jacob (son of Lehi)]])
- Isaiah 44:3 water/spirit parallelism: Ross parses Isaiah 44:3's parallel imagery of water/floods with spirit/blessing, applying it personally to spiritual thirst being quenched by the gospel. ([[Isaiah 44]])
- Symbols in Psalm 23: The hosts and Ross discuss the symbolic images in Psalm 23 (shepherd, green pastures, still waters, valley of the shadow of death, rod and staff, table, oil, cup), each sharing personal resonance, including Ross's account of facing stage four cancer. ([[Psalm 23]])
- Wordplay in Isaiah 5:7: Ross reads Isaiah 5:7 in Hebrew and explains the deliberate wordplay between mishpat (justice) and mishpach (bloodshed/oppression), and sedaka (righteousness) and tsa'aqah (outcry), connecting it to Jesus's confrontations with the Pharisees over legalism. ([[Isaiah 5]])
- Repetition in Psalms 136 and chesed: Ross highlights the refrain 'for his mercy endureth forever' (Hebrew: ki le'olam chasdo) occurring in all 26 verses of Psalm 136, and quotes a President Nelson leadership-session address linking chesed (covenantal love) to the purpose of temple building. ([[Psalm 136]])
- Chiasmus defined, with Alma 36: Ross defines chiasmus (inverted parallelism) as a Hebrew poetic/mnemonic structure and cites Alma 36 as a famous antithetical chiasmus centered on Jesus Christ. ([[Alma 36]])
- Chiastic structure of Psalm 23: Ross lays out a proposed chiastic structure for Psalm 23, with 'thou art with me' as the central pivot point. ([[Psalm 23]])
- Chiastic structure of Psalm 67: Ross presents Psalm 67 as a clean chiasmus centered on the idea that the nations rejoice because God judges righteously. ([[Psalm 67]])
- Judging righteously: Oaks, James Covel, and John 9: The group discusses Elder Dallin H. Oaks's distinction between forbidden final judgments and required intermediate judgments, applying it to the D&C 40 case of James Covel and to the man born blind in John 9. ([[D&C 40]] · [[John 9]])
- Closing thoughts and series wrap-up: Hosts thank Ross, reiterate the invitation to read scripture more slowly, and preview the final Thoughts to Keep in Mind episode on prophets and prophecy.

## Claims extracted (TENTATIVE — awaiting corroboration)

- **Dr. Ross Baron**: According to the Latter-day Saint Citation Index (scriptures.byu.edu), which tracks quotations in general conference talks, the Journal of Discourses, and the Teachings of the Prophet Joseph Smith, Isaiah is the most-quoted Old Testament book, followed by Genesis, then Psalms.
  - Named primary source: _Latter-day Saint Citation Index (scriptures.byu.edu)_
- **Dr. Ross Baron**: The Hebrew word chesed, translated 'mercy' in the King James Version, appears in all 26 verses of Psalm 136; President Nelson referenced this in a leadership-session address, connecting chesed to the purpose of building and renovating temples.
  - Named primary source: _President Russell M. Nelson, leadership session address_
- **Dr. Ross Baron**: The Hebrew word translated 'standard' in 2 Nephi 6:6 is ness, the same word usually translated 'ensign' elsewhere in the Old Testament.
- **Dr. Ross Baron**: Psalm 23 can be read as a chiastic structure with 'thou art with me' (verse 4) as its literary center, framed by shepherd/dwelling-with-God on the outside and guidance-provision/table-preparation on the inner ring.
- **Dr. Ross Baron**: Psalm 67 forms a clean chiasmus, with the phrase about God judging the nations righteously (verse 4) as the structural center, framed symmetrically by verses on God's blessings, the nations knowing God's ways, and praise of God.
- **Dr. Ross Baron**: In Isaiah 5:7, Isaiah deliberately uses wordplay between the similar-sounding Hebrew words mishpat ('justice') and mishpach ('bloodshed'/'oppression'), and between sedaka ('righteousness') and tsa'aqah ('outcry'), to underscore how close justice and injustice can sound while being opposite in meaning.
- **Dr. Ross Baron**: Psalms 113-118 (the Hallel) are believed to be the songs sung at Passover, with 113-114 sung before the meal and 115-118 after, and the hymn Jesus and the disciples sang before going to Gethsemane (per Matthew) was likely some portion of Psalms 115-118.
  - Named primary source: _Gospel of Matthew_
- **Dr. Ross Baron**: In 2 Nephi 6:6 (quoting Isaiah 49), the Hebrew word translated 'lift up' is nasa, which as a root word also carries the meaning 'to forgive.'
