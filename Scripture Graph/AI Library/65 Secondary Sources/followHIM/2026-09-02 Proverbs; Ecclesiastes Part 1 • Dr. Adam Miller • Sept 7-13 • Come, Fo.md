# Proverbs; Ecclesiastes Part 1 • Dr. Adam Miller • Sept 7-13 • Come, Follow Me

[[followHIM]] · 2026-09-02 · 68 min · source tier **A** · episode quality **85**
Guests: Adam Miller (Philosophy of religion, Latter-day Saint theology, biblical wisdom literature)

> Philosopher Dr. Adam Miller returns to followHIM to frame Proverbs and Ecclesiastes as companion wisdom books: Proverbs offers conventional wisdom about fearing God, welcoming correction, and breaking self-deception, while Ecclesiastes serves as harsh but necessary medicine that strips away illusions about symmetry between action and consequence. Miller traces how both books, read in conversation with each other and with Christ, point toward a wisdom that culminates in the 'foolish' logic of the cross — willing sacrifice in the face of inevitable loss. The episode covers close readings (using Alter, NET, NIV, and KJV translations) of Proverbs 1, 3, 4, and 16, and previews a part 2 discussion of Ecclesiastes' harder claims.

## Claims extracted (TENTATIVE — awaiting corroboration)

_Secondary commentary is not primary evidence (§13). These entered the evidence pipeline as tentative claims._

- **Adam Miller**: The original ending of Ecclesiastes is 12:8 ('meaningless, meaningless... everything is meaningless'); verses 9-12 were added later, shifting from first-person to third-person narration to give the book a more pious, less skeptical closing spin.
- **Adam Miller**: The personification of Wisdom as a speaking female figure in Proverbs 1-9 is unusual for abstractions in Hebrew literature generally, and supplies an early template that John's Gospel draws on when personifying the Word (logos) as divine and identifying it with Christ in John 1:1.
- **Adam Miller**: The earliest manuscripts of the Gospel of Mark end at 16:8, with the women fleeing the empty tomb afraid and saying nothing to anyone; the resurrection appearance material after verse 8 was added later.
- **Adam Miller**: The book of Proverbs is a multi-source compilation assembled over centuries: some sayings are attributed to Solomon (possibly as early as the 10th century BCE), others were compiled during the reign of King Hezekiah (8th century BCE), and other sections appear to be borrowed from non-Hebrew sources and repurposed.

## Insights (attributed)

_Worthwhile observations that do not require historical proof (§19) — they belong to their speakers._

- **John Bytheway**: Citing the Bible Project, Proverbs offers good general guidelines rather than guarantees ('proverbs are not promises'), which is why they need to be balanced by the harder realities explored in Job and Ecclesiastes.
- **Adam Miller**: A useful interpretive rule for scripture is to keep all its parts in conversation with each other and with Jesus Christ, rather than expecting any single verse or book to say everything needed on its own.
- **Adam Miller**: Recognizing genre is crucial to reading Proverbs and Ecclesiastes well, since wisdom literature operates by different rules and goals than narrative, law, or prophecy.
- **Adam Miller**: Proverbs 4:7 ('with all thy getting, get understanding') can be read as urging that wisdom should accrue alongside whatever practical task is at hand, not be pursued only in the abstract.
- **Adam Miller**: Exercise is an analogy for embodied wisdom: forcing the body to engage with reality against its immediate desires (getting out of bed to exercise) is how a person discovers the true nature and limits of their own body.
- **Hank Smith**: A mission president's advice to seek out mentors willing to point out blind spots is a human parallel to Proverbs' teaching about welcoming reproof, contrasted with apps or chatbots whose business model is to flatter and agree rather than correct.
- **Adam Miller**: Modern phones and algorithms remove the effortful 'work' of self-deception described in Proverbs 1 ('how long will you love being duped') by doing the fantasizing and distraction for the user automatically.
