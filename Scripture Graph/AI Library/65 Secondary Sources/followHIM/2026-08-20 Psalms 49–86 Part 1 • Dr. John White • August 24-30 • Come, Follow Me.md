# Psalms 49–86 Part 1 • Dr. John White • August 24-30 • Come, Follow Me

[[followHIM]] · 2026-08-20 · 68 min · source tier **A** · episode quality **84**
Guests: John White

> A substantive, scripture-centered discussion of Psalms 49–51, framed by Dr. John White's theological background and conversion story. Its strongest material concerns psalm genres, Hebrew parallelism, Psalm 49's ransom imagery, Psalm 50's covenant-lawsuit setting, and Psalm 51's creation and repentance language. Several structural and comparative claims are promising research leads but require verification, especially the proposed Psalms 49–86 inclusio, Psalm 78:38 as the Psalter's center, quotation-frequency claims, and comparisons between Hebrew syntax and the Book of Mormon.

## Outline

- Introduction and guest background: The hosts introduce Dr. John White, describe his academic work, and briefly recount his investigation of and conversion to the restored gospel. White previews the episode's focus on redemption from the grave and mercy at the center of the Psalter. ([[followHIM]] · [[Justice and Mercy]] · [[Conversion]])
- Knowledge of God and knowledge of self: White argues that the Psalms primarily disclose God's character and that a person's understanding of God shapes self-understanding. He illustrates how family experience can influence one's portrait of God. ([[The Godhead]] · [[Joseph Smith Jr]])
- Structure and theological center of the Psalter: White describes the Psalter as five books, proposes that Psalms 49:15 and 86:13 form a redemptive bookend around the assigned block, and presents Psalm 78:38 as a possible central verse emphasizing compassion. He also introduces Bonhoeffer's historical, Christological, and personal ways of praying the Psalms. ([[Psalm 49]] · [[Psalm 78]] · [[Psalm 86]] · [[Alma 36]] · [[Justice and Mercy]])
- Book of Mormon conversion experience: White describes how Alma 32, embodiment theology, and personal experiences contributed to his acceptance of the Book of Mormon. He presents his conclusions as conversion testimony rather than independent proof. ([[Alma 32]] · [[Book of Mormon]] · [[D&C 130]] · [[Faith]] · [[Conversion]])
- Major genres of Psalms: White surveys wisdom, penitential, lament, communal-restoration, praise, royal-messianic, and historical psalms. He emphasizes that literary genre should govern how a psalm is interpreted. ([[Psalm 49]] · [[Psalm 51]] · [[Psalm 69]] · [[Psalm 72]] · [[Psalm 78]] · [[Psalm 85]] · [[Psalm 86]] · [[Psalm 22]])
- Hebrew poetry and narrative syntax: White explains synonymous, antithetical, synthetic, climactic, emblematic, and refrain-based parallelism. The discussion then turns to the Hebrew waw-consecutive and White's perception of analogous narrative sequencing in the Book of Mormon. ([[Psalm 61]] · [[Psalm 62]] · [[Psalm 66]] · [[Psalm 77]] · [[Psalm 86]] · [[Isaiah 1]] · [[Helaman 1]] · [[3 Nephi 12]])
- Psalm 49: wealth cannot ransom life: Psalm 49 is read as wisdom teaching that wealth, status, and legacy cannot overcome death. White connects its ransom vocabulary with Jesus's teaching about gaining the world and with the Son of Man giving his life as a ransom. ([[Psalm 49]] · [[Psalm 23]] · [[Mark 8]] · [[Mark 10]] · [[Moses 7]] · [[2 Kings 2]] · [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]] · [[Death and the Spirit World]])
- Psalm 50 as a covenant lawsuit: White reads Psalm 50 as a judicial scene in which God summons heaven and earth as witnesses and indicts ritual religion detached from covenant loyalty. The desired response is gratitude, stewardship, and faithful conduct rather than an attempt to place God in one's debt. ([[Psalm 50]] · [[Covenants]] · [[Sacrifice]] · [[Gratitude]] · [[Stewardship]])
- Personal encounter with mercy: White recounts shame, destructive behavior in college, serious Bible reading, and his first Christian conversion. He describes his later Latter-day Saint conversion as a completion and continuation of that earlier relationship with Jesus Christ. ([[Psalm 25]] · [[Psalm 51]] · [[Justice and Mercy]] · [[Repentance]] · [[Conversion]] · [[Jesus Christ]])
- Psalm 51 and the creation of a clean heart: White presents Psalm 51 as a prayer for inward re-creation rather than mere circumstantial relief or ritual compensation. He connects its request for a clean heart with creation language and the Book of Mormon's teaching about a mighty change of heart. ([[Psalm 51]] · [[Genesis 1]] · [[Mosiah 5]] · [[David (king)]] · [[Bathsheba]] · [[Repentance]])
- Grace, obligation, and covenant: White responds to allegations of works-righteousness by arguing that covenant joins divine promise with human obligation. Obedience is presented as a response to God's initiating grace rather than payment for salvation. ([[Psalm 50]] · [[Psalm 51]] · [[Mosiah 2]] · [[3 Nephi 9]] · [[D&C 59]] · [[1 John 4]] · [[Ephesians 2]] · [[Ten Commandments]])
- Intertextual scriptural witness: White describes discovering a reinforcing network of biblical, Book of Mormon, Doctrine and Covenants, and Pearl of Great Price passages. He argues that these scriptural collections should be read complementarily rather than competitively. ([[Book of Mormon]] · [[Bible]] · [[Doctrine and Covenants]] · [[Pearl of Great Price]] · [[3 Nephi]] · [[Joseph Smith—History 1]] · [[Jesus Christ]])
- The Holy Spirit, confession, and praying the Psalms: White discusses Psalm 51's request that the Holy Spirit remain with the penitent and connects it with Isaiah 63, Mosiah 3, and Romans 8. Drawing on Bonhoeffer, he suggests that David prays Psalm 51 in guilt, Christ bears the guilt of others, and worshippers pray it in repentance and dependence on Christ. ([[Psalm 51]] · [[Isaiah 63]] · [[Mosiah 3]] · [[Romans 8]] · [[Gift of the Holy Ghost]] · [[Sacrament]])

## Claims extracted (TENTATIVE — awaiting corroboration)

_Secondary commentary is not primary evidence (§13). These entered the evidence pipeline as tentative claims._

- **John White**: White states that Psalm 51:11 and Isaiah 63:10–11 are the only two Old Testament passages that explicitly use the designation 'Holy Spirit.'
  - Named primary source: _Psalm 51:11 and Isaiah 63:10–11_
- **John White**: White reports that commentators interpret the accumulation of wealth, named lands, fame, or legacy in Psalm 49 as an 'immortality strategy' or denial-of-death project.
  - Named primary source: _Psalm 49:11, 17_
- **John White**: White argues that repeated 'and' constructions and 'it came to pass' sequences in the Book of Mormon resemble Hebrew narrative progression, particularly in Helaman.
  - Named primary source: _Book of Helaman_
- **John White**: Psalm 50 uses the form or imagery of a covenant lawsuit: heaven and earth are summoned as witnesses, God acts as judge or prosecutor, and the covenant congregation is addressed as defendant.
  - Named primary source: _Psalm 50_
- **John White**: White reports that Bonhoeffer trained seminarians at the underground seminary in Finkenwalde to begin each morning with the Psalms.
  - Named primary source: _The Prayerbook of the Bible_
- **John White**: White reports that an ancient Jewish tradition and a number of scholars identify Psalm 78:38 as the central verse of the Psalter, while the Masoretes may instead identify verse 36.
  - Named primary source: _Psalm 78_
- **John White**: White interprets mercy in Psalm 78:38 and Alma's appeal for mercy in Alma 36 as occupying structurally central positions in their respective texts.
  - Named primary source: _Psalm 78:38 and Alma 36_
- **John White**: The Psalter is divided into five books, which White says echo the five books of the Pentateuch.
  - Named primary source: _Book of Psalms_
- **John White**: White proposes that Psalm 49:15 and Psalm 86:13 form an inclusio around Psalms 49–86, with the later verse answering the earlier question about redemption from the grave.
  - Named primary source: _Psalm 49:15 and Psalm 86:13_
- **John White**: White interprets Psalm 49's ransom language as teaching that no human wealth can purchase deliverance from death, while Mark applies ransom language to the Son of Man giving his life for others.
  - Named primary source: _Psalm 49:6–8 and Mark 10_
- **John White**: The Hebrew waw-consecutive is a syntactic construction used to advance narrative through linked clauses such as 'and he remembered,' 'and she conceived,' and 'and she bore.'
  - Named primary source: _Hebrew Bible_
- **John White**: Hebrew poetry commonly creates correspondence through parallel thoughts rather than through rhyming sounds, with the second line restating, contrasting, extending, or intensifying the first.
  - Named primary source: _Book of Psalms_
- **John White**: White states that Psalm 69 is quoted or invoked in connection with Jesus more than nearly any other psalm, with Psalm 22 being quoted more frequently.
  - Named primary source: _Psalm 69 and Psalm 22_
- **John White**: White says that the Hebrew verb translated 'receive' in Psalm 49:15 is also used for the taking or receiving of Enoch and Elijah, and that some scholars therefore see the verse as an early intimation of hope beyond the grave.
  - Named primary source: _Psalm 49:15, Genesis 5:24, and 2 Kings 2_
- **John White**: Dietrich Bonhoeffer characterized the Psalter as the prayer book of Jesus Christ and read it through the prayer of its historical speaker, the prayer of Christ, and the prayer of the contemporary worshipper.
  - Named primary source: _The Prayerbook of the Bible_
- **John White**: The Hebrew verb translated 'create' in Psalm 51:10 is the creation verb used in Genesis, which White interprets as a request for divine re-creation rather than self-repair.
  - Named primary source: _Psalm 51:10 and Genesis 1_

## Insights (attributed)

_Worthwhile observations that do not require historical proof (§19) — they belong to their speakers._

- **John White**: The Psalms can be read as human responses to God and as an emotional 'anatomy of the soul,' giving worshippers language for fear, anger, remorse, gratitude, and praise.
- **John White**: Expressing anger honestly to God may be spiritually healthier than suppressing it and acting it out destructively.
- **John White**: A metaphor should first be understood at the literal level so a reader can judge which features appropriately carry into its figurative meaning.
- **John White**: Psalm 49 can be read as an anti-Psalm 23: those who trust in self-sufficiency are shepherded by death, whereas those who trust God are received and redeemed by him.
- **Hank Smith and John White**: Psalm 49 employs economic vocabulary precisely to deny that ordinary economic resources can purchase salvation; the effective 'currency' is the Redeemer's own life.
- **John White**: Praise does not supply something God lacks; it connects the worshipper's enjoyment of a gift to gratitude for its giver.
- **John White**: Idolatry can occur when a genuine created good, such as food or friendship, is assigned the ultimate worth that belongs only to God.
- **John White**: Psalm 51 asks God to change the petitioner rather than merely changing the petitioner's circumstances.
- **John Bytheway**: A broken heart is compared to a horse made submissive to its master: the image emphasizes yielding one's will to God rather than merely experiencing emotional pain.
- **John White**: White frames covenant as the union of promise and obligation: promise without responsibility can license abuse, while obligation without promise becomes legalism.
- **John White**: Biblical commandments frequently follow declarations of divine action, so obedience can be studied as a response to prior grace rather than as an attempt to earn it.
- **John White**: Reading the Bible and Book of Mormon together can produce an intertextual 'chorus of witnesses' without treating either collection as a substitute for the other.
- **John White**: White characterizes sacrament meeting as a recurring Psalm 51 practice in which worshippers bring a broken and contrite heart while remembering Christ's sacrifice.
- **John White**: Bonhoeffer's Christological reading permits Psalm 51 to be heard simultaneously as David's confession, Christ's bearing of others' guilt, and the worshipper's prayer for forgiveness.
