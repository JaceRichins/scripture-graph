PETER: They are not coins, Luke.

LUKE: I know that.

PETER: Do you? I did not, at first. I tried to do enough good that the courtyard would grow small behind it.

LUKE: Did it?

PETER: I only trampled people.

LUKE: Tell me.

PETER: A man named Eli came to us. A dyer. Purple on his nails, though he worked mostly in brown because brown cloth sells.

LUKE: When was this?

PETER: Three years after the shore. Perhaps four. The rains had begun early.

LUKE: What had he done?

PETER: He kept the widows’ grain money.

LUKE: Stole it?

PETER: A little at first. His youngest girl was ill. Then more after she recovered. By the time he came, Tirzah and two others had gone without flour for six days.

LUKE: Did he confess?

PETER: To the part we already knew.

LUKE: And the rest?

PETER: I shouted it out of him.

LUKE: You shouted.

PETER: I had him against the wall with my hand in his tunic.

LUKE: Peter.

PETER: Yes. I told you I trampled people.

LUKE: What stopped you?

PETER: Tirzah.

LUKE: The widow he had robbed?

PETER: She struck my arm with her walking stick.

LUKE: Hard?

PETER: She was thorough.

LUKE: What did she want?

PETER: Her money. Not two men breaking the same fool in her doorway.

LUKE: What did you do?

PETER: Released him. Picked up my dignity. It had rolled near the water jar.

LUKE: Did Eli repay her?

PETER: He had spent most of it. We took the bolt of good cloth from his shop and sold it. I paid the rest.

LUKE: From the common purse?

PETER: From my purse.

LUKE: Why yours?

PETER: Because I had made myself the strong man again.

LUKE: What happened to Eli?

PETER: I told him to stay away until he could return every measure.

LUKE: That sounds final.

PETER: It did to him. He vanished.

LUKE: For how long?

PETER: Five months.

LUKE: Where did you find him?

PETER: I did not. Tirzah did.

LUKE: She sought him?

PETER: She found him sleeping behind the tannery. The smell alone was punishment enough.

LUKE: Did she bring him back?

PETER: By the ear.

LUKE: Truly?

PETER: He was taller than she was, but guilt bends a man.

LUKE: What did she ask of you?

PETER: To give him work carrying grain.

LUKE: Around the widows?

PETER: I said it was unwise.

LUKE: She disagreed.

PETER: With the stick resting across her knees.

LUKE: Did you trust him?

PETER: No. We weighed every sack before and after. Eli hated that. Then he stopped hating it.

LUKE: How long?

PETER: Two years before he carried the keys.

LUKE: You gave him the keys?

PETER: Tirzah did. I argued.

LUKE: Did he steal again?

PETER: Once, if three figs count.

LUKE: Do they?

PETER: Tirzah said no. They were shriveled.

LUKE: And afterward?

PETER: He kept her roof patched. When her sight failed, he sat in the market and described everyone passing. Badly. He called every fat man a magistrate.

LUKE: Did you ever speak to him about the day you seized him?

PETER: The next morning.

LUKE: You apologized?

PETER: Poorly. I brought bread as though it might speak for me.

LUKE: Did he forgive you?

PETER: He took the bread. It was a beginning.

LUKE: You place your own wrong inside his story.

PETER: I was there.

LUKE: Did caring for him ease the memory of the courtyard?

PETER: No. It gave me Eli.

LUKE: That is not the answer I expected.

PETER: You came too far to be given what you expected.

LUKE: Tell me another.

PETER: There was a boy named Amos. Fifteen, though he claimed seventeen. His master baked bread near the eastern road.

LUKE: What brought him to you?

PETER: The baker struck him with a wooden peel. Split the skin above his eye. Amos slept two nights among empty baskets before a woman found him.

LUKE: Was the baker one of your people?

PETER: He stood with us, sang with us, broke bread with us.

LUKE: What did he say?

PETER: That the boy stole raisins. That the blow landed harder than intended. That masters must correct apprentices.

LUKE: Did Amos steal them?

PETER: Six raisins. He confessed to seven, then changed it back to six.

LUKE: What did you do?

PETER: I wanted to strike the baker.

LUKE: Did you?

PETER: No. Tirzah had improved my education.

LUKE: Did you make Amos return?

PETER: Never.

LUKE: Even after the baker repented?

PETER: Amos’s eye closed for ten days. Repentance did not make the shop safe on the eleventh.

LUKE: Where did the boy stay?

PETER: With us first. He snored under this table.

LUKE: This table?

PETER: The same. He kicked that leg loose. I have repaired it four times.

LUKE: What became of the baker?

PETER: His name was Mattan. Use it.

LUKE: What became of Mattan?

PETER: We closed his shop.

LUKE: You had authority to do that?

PETER: His customers did.

LUKE: Ah.

PETER: No flour came. No oil. He sat alone with three cold ovens.

LUKE: For how long?

PETER: Until Amos’s wages were paid and another baker agreed to work beside him.

LUKE: Did Amos want to see him?

PETER: Not for a year.

LUKE: And then?

PETER: Amos asked me to carry a loaf to Mattan’s house. He had baked it himself.

LUKE: As forgiveness?

PETER: He said Mattan’s new bread was poor.

LUKE: And you carried it?

PETER: I made Amos carry it. I walked beside him.

LUKE: How did it go?

PETER: Mattan opened the door, saw the loaf, and began to weep. Amos became angry.

LUKE: Because Mattan wept?

PETER: He thought the tears made him responsible for comforting the man who had hurt him.

LUKE: Was he?

PETER: No.

LUKE: What did Amos do?

PETER: Set the loaf on the threshold and left.

LUKE: Did you stay with Mattan?

PETER: For a little while.

LUKE: What did you say?

PETER: Very little. He knew what the bread was and was not.

LUKE: Did they ever work together again?

PETER: Never. Years later Amos sold him flour at a fair weight. That was enough for that day.

LUKE: You did not force a full ending.

PETER: Full endings belong to scrolls. People wake the next morning.

LUKE: You almost made a saying.

PETER: Strike it out.

LUKE: Gladly.

PETER: You will keep it.

LUKE: No.

PETER: Mark would.

LUKE: Mark is not here.

PETER: Then the old man is safe.

LUKE: One more.

PETER: There are hundreds.

LUKE: One you still see.

PETER: Caleb.

LUKE: Who was he?

PETER: A fisherman from the southern shore. Better with hooks than nets. He could tell the depth from the color of the water.

LUKE: What had he done?

PETER: Left his wife and two sons during a fever.

LUKE: Left to seek help?

PETER: Left because he feared dying.

LUKE: Did they survive?

PETER: The sons did. His wife did not.

LUKE: How long was he gone?

PETER: Eleven days.

LUKE: And when he returned?

PETER: The burial was over. The older boy had burned Caleb’s cloak. The younger would not speak.

LUKE: Why did Caleb come to you?

PETER: He had heard I knew something about leaving.

LUKE: Did he say that?

PETER: Not so gently.

LUKE: What did you do?

PETER: Sent him away.

LUKE: You sent him away.

PETER: I said a man who abandoned his house had no business asking me to open another.

LUKE: Because his act resembled yours?

PETER: Because I did not want it to.

LUKE: Did he return?

PETER: The next morning. Sat outside. Returned the next day. On the fourth it rained, and my wife let him in while I was gone.

LUKE: She has a habit of interrupting your judgments with food.

PETER: I have lived because of it.

LUKE: Where were the boys?

PETER: With their mother’s sister. Safe. Angry. They wanted nothing from him.

LUKE: Did Caleb ask them to forgive him?

PETER: Again and again.

LUKE: What did you tell him?

PETER: To stop asking.

LUKE: Why?

PETER: Each time he asked, he put a cup in their hands and waited for them to fill it. They had barely enough for themselves.

LUKE: So what could he do?

PETER: Carry water to the aunt’s house. Leave it outside. Mend the roof. Pay for barley. Go when she told him to go.

LUKE: Did he?

PETER: At first he stayed too long. He wanted the boys to see him laboring.

LUKE: You corrected him.

PETER: The aunt corrected both of us.

LUKE: Both?

PETER: I had been standing across the lane watching to see that he obeyed.

LUKE: She saw you.

PETER: Everyone sees a fisherman pretending not to watch.

LUKE: How long before the sons received him?

PETER: The younger took water from him after seven months. No words. Just the jar.

LUKE: And the older?

PETER: Nearly three years.

LUKE: What happened?

PETER: His fishing line had tangled around a stone below the ledge. Caleb went into the water and freed it.

LUKE: The son thanked him?

PETER: He said Caleb had tied the hook badly.

LUKE: Had he?

PETER: Yes.

LUKE: What came after?

PETER: They sat apart and repaired the line. The son handed him the small knife without looking.

LUKE: That was the first trust?

PETER: The first I saw.

LUKE: Did the family live together again?

PETER: No. The boys remained with their aunt until they were men.

LUKE: And Caleb?

PETER: He kept carrying water.

LUKE: Even after they no longer needed it?

PETER: Then he carried it to someone else.

LUKE: Did you tell Caleb about the shore?

PETER: Many times.

LUKE: The questions?

PETER: Yes.

LUKE: Your commission?

PETER: Not as you call it.

LUKE: What did you call it?

PETER: Breakfast first. Then names. Eli. Amos. Mattan. Caleb. Tirzah with her stick.

LUKE: The care itself.

PETER: Some days. Other days it was hauling grain because Eli had fever, or sitting outside Amos’s room because a door opening woke him.

LUKE: You stayed outside his room?

PETER: Three nights.

LUKE: Why you?

PETER: He asked.

LUKE: Did you sleep?

PETER: Against the door.

LUKE: You finally watched an hour.

PETER: More than one. My neck complained for a week.

LUKE: Did you think of the garden?

PETER: On the second night. Amos cried out and I woke before he did.

LUKE: What did you do?

PETER: Put my hand against the door so he could see the shadow beneath it.

LUKE: Did he know it was yours?

PETER: I kept it there until morning.

LUKE: You have answered my reading.

PETER: Have I?

LUKE: I made the people into payment.

PETER: You did.

LUKE: I did not know I had.

PETER: You touched the fold again.

LUKE: There is a letter in it.

PETER: From whom?

LUKE: A woman named Mara.

PETER: What does she want?

LUKE: She asks when I will come.

PETER: And when will you?

LUKE: I do not know.

PETER: You have traveled farther to avoid a shorter road.

LUKE: Yes.

PETER: What happened?

LUKE: Her son was ill. Eight years old. A fever that had risen for four days.

PETER: You treated him?

LUKE: I believed the worst had passed. Near dusk his mother said his breathing had changed. I listened. I heard weakness, but no danger.

PETER: And you left.

LUKE: I had been awake two nights. I told myself sleep would make me useful in the morning.

PETER: He died.

LUKE: Before dawn.

PETER: What was his name?

LUKE: Simeon.

PETER: Simeon.

LUKE: Mara sent for me while there was still time to sit with him. I did not hear the knock. Another man opened the door and told the messenger I was not to be disturbed.

PETER: Did you command that?

LUKE: Earlier, for another matter. Not for her. I have explained this to myself many ways.

PETER: Have you explained it to Mara?

LUKE: No.

PETER: Why does she ask you to come?

LUKE: Her letter does not say.

PETER: You think she wants an answer.

LUKE: I have none.

PETER: You have his name.

LUKE: That feels very small.

PETER: It was the first thing I asked.

LUKE: You think I should go and speak it.

PETER: I think you should go and hear how she speaks it.

LUKE: What if she wants me to tell her I could not have saved him?

PETER: Can you tell her that truly?

LUKE: No.

PETER: What can you tell truly?

LUKE: That I heard her fear and trusted my own judgment more.

PETER: Then do not bring a larger basket than you can fill.

LUKE: You said you tried a hundred pleas after the courtyard, and every one began with you.

PETER: Yes.

LUKE: How do I keep mine from doing the same?

PETER: You may not. Go anyway.

LUKE: And if she will not receive me?

PETER: Leave when she tells you.

LUKE: Return?

PETER: If she asks.

LUKE: Carry water?

PETER: If the jar is empty.

LUKE: You make no promise about what waits inside her house.

PETER: It is her house.

LUKE: I wanted from you an account of how a man becomes certain he is forgiven.

PETER: Certain?

LUKE: Yes.

PETER: I was given bread while my clothes were wet. I was given work while I still knew the servant girl’s face. Years later, Amos asked for my shadow under his door. How certain must a man be before he puts his hand down?

LUKE: You still hear the courtyard.

PETER: Yes.

LUKE: Does it no longer accuse you?

PETER: Some nights it does.

LUKE: What then?

PETER: I name the room I am in.

LUKE: What do you mean?

PETER: This table. That wall. My wife breathing beside me. The Lord alive. Then I get up if I cannot sleep.

LUKE: And do what?

PETER: There is always a net.

LUKE: You have returned to the stool.

PETER: It still leans.

LUKE: May I see?

PETER: You may hold that end.

[Wood scrapes softly across the floor.]

LUKE: Here?

PETER: No, lower.

LUKE: If I go lower, it will be shorter still.

PETER: Now you understand the problem.

LUKE: You could begin again.

PETER: It is good wood.

LUKE: That is not the same as a good stool.

PETER: Spoken like a man who does not cut timber.

LUKE: Spoken like a man who may have to sit on it.

PETER: Mara has chairs?

LUKE: I do not remember.

PETER: Then take the bench.

LUKE: The whole bench?

PETER: No. Take yourself to her house. Leave my bench.

LUKE: I will go.

PETER: When?

LUKE: After I have finished this journey.

PETER: That is how men add roads.

LUKE: Tomorrow, then.

PETER: Good.

LUKE: Peter, before I leave, John kept what followed the charge by the fire.

PETER: I know.

LUKE: Do you want it read?

PETER: Yes.

SCRIPTURE (John 21:18-19), read by Luke: Verily, verily, I say unto thee, When thou wast young, thou girdedst thyself, and walkedst whither thou wouldest: but when thou shalt be old, thou shalt stretch forth thy hands, and another shall gird thee, and carry thee whither thou wouldest not.
This spake he, signifying by what death he should glorify God. And when he had spoken this, he saith unto him, Follow me.

[The tool is set down.]

LUKE: Were you afraid?

PETER: I had heard what He said about age and hands.

LUKE: Did you understand?

PETER: Enough.

LUKE: Yet the next word was the first road again.

PETER: Yes.

LUKE: Were you ready this time?

PETER: Luke, my coat was still dripping.

LUKE: That is not an answer.

PETER: It is the best one I have.

LUKE: You went.

PETER: I stood. Andrew caught my arm because my foot had gone numb. Then I went.

LUKE: And after all these years?

PETER: Some mornings I still need Andrew’s hand.

LUKE: He is near?

PETER: Near enough.

LUKE: Your wife?

PETER: Nearer.

LUKE: John?

PETER: Watching from somewhere, I expect.

LUKE: Mark?

PETER: Writing down the part where I ruined a stool.

LUKE: He would keep the cracked cup too.

PETER: If it made me look foolish.

LUKE: You gave him worse than foolishness.

PETER: Yes.

LUKE: Freely?

PETER: Not at first. Later, freely.

LUKE: Because people needed to know what happened?

PETER: Because some of them sat at the edge of the room.

LUKE: Like Eli?

PETER: Like Eli. Like Caleb. Men and women who entered after everyone else and chose the place nearest the door.

LUKE: What did you tell them?

PETER: The courtyard. The voice I used. The look. Mary arriving with earth on her garment. My name in the message. The empty place. The wet coat. The bread.

LUKE: All of it.

PETER: Enough for them to move one place nearer the table.

LUKE: Did they?

PETER: Not always that day.

LUKE: But you left the place open.

PETER: Someone had left it open for me.

LUKE: I think I am ready to go.

PETER: Take bread.

LUKE: For the road?

PETER: For Mara.

LUKE: What if she has bread?

PETER: Then you may eat it while she decides whether to let you in.

LUKE: That may take some time.

PETER: Do not lean on her door. You are heavier than you look.

LUKE: Peter.

PETER: Luke.

LUKE: Thank you.

PETER: Tell John the stool was straight.

LUKE: I shall tell him you blamed the floor.

PETER: He will believe that.

LUKE: I am going north after Mara.

PETER: To whom?

LUKE: Thomas.
