# Chiasmus in Alma 36

> [!evidence] The observation
> Alma's account of his conversion in [[Alma 36]] can be outlined as an
> extended chiasm (inverted parallel structure, A-B-C…X…C'-B'-A'), with the
> turning point at the cry to Jesus Christ in [[Alma 36#^alma-36-18|v. 18]]:
> captivity of fathers → bonds of iniquity → **remembering "one Jesus
> Christ"** → joy → deliverance of fathers.

<!-- SG:BEGIN summary -->
**Layer 1 — Observation (well established):** Many elements of Alma 36
mirror each other in inverted order around verses 17–18. John W. Welch first
proposed the structure in 1969; it appears in standard reference works on
chiasmus in the Book of Mormon.

**Layer 2 — Interpretation (disputed):** That the structure is an
*intentional* ancient Hebrew literary device — rather than a pattern partly
imposed by the outliner, or a natural product of "there-and-back" narrative —
is debated. Critics note element selection flexibility; defenders answer
with statistical criteria (Edwards & Edwards 2004 estimated low probability
of chance for Alma 36 under their model, itself debated).

**Layer 3 — Evidentiary significance:** *Supporting* evidence for Hebrew
literary influence on the text. It does **not** by itself establish ancient
authorship; chiasmus exists in many literatures, and some inverted structure
occurs in 19th-century English prose.

**Does not establish:** ancient Book of Mormon authorship; Joseph Smith's
ignorance of parallelism (though no evidence shows he knew the form in 1829).

**Alternative explanations:** (1) analyst-imposed selection of repeated
elements; (2) natural symmetry of a descent-and-return conversion story;
(3) unconscious rhetorical patterning absorbed from the KJV.
<!-- SG:END summary -->
