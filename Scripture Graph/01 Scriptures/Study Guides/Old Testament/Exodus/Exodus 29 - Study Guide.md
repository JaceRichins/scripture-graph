# Exodus 29 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Exodus 29]] · My notes: [[Exodus 29 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
[[Exodus 29]] prescribes the [[Consecration]] of Aaron and his sons for [[Priesthood]] service. The rites include washing, clothing, anointing, differentiated forms of [[Sacrifice]], a restricted sacred meal, and seven days of consecration. The chapter then establishes continual morning-and-evening offerings. In verses 42–46, God promises to meet and speak, sanctify the sanctuary and priests, dwell among Israel, and be their God. Verse 46 explicitly presents divine dwelling as a purpose of Israel's deliverance from Egypt.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
1. **Preparation (vv. 1–9):** Materials are brought; Aaron and his sons are washed, clothed, and appointed, and Aaron is anointed.

2. **Three animals and differentiated offerings (vv. 10–28):** The bullock is a sin offering, the first ram a whole burnt offering, and the second the ram of consecration. The final rite includes blood application, wave and heave offerings, and priestly portions.

3. **Succession and sacred meal (vv. 29–34):** Aaron's garments pass to a succeeding son, while consecration food is restricted by participants, place, and time.

4. **Seven-day consecration (vv. 35–37):** Aaron and his sons are consecrated, and atonement and sanctification are prescribed for the altar.

5. **Continual offerings (vv. 38–42a):** Lambs are offered morning and evening with flour, oil, and wine.

6. **Divine promises and purpose (vv. 42b–46):** God promises to meet, speak, sanctify, dwell among Israel, and be known as the God who delivered them.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
The chapter brings together [[Priesthood]], [[Consecration]], [[Sacrifice]], holiness, and divine presence. Detailed ritual actions are commanded, while God also declares, “I will sanctify” the tabernacle, altar, Aaron, and his sons (vv. 43–44). The text therefore affirms both prescribed human action and divine sanctifying agency, without explaining a general theory of ritual efficacy.

Atonement is made for the priests and for the altar, and the altar's atonement is associated with cleansing and sanctification (vv. 33, 36–37). The sacred meal participates in the rite by which atonement and consecration occur; the food itself is not described as receiving atonement.

Christian readers may study this material alongside [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]]. That connection belongs to later Christian and typological interpretation; Exodus 29 itself does not identify its individual rites with Jesus Christ.
<!-- SG:END doctrines -->

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The repeated terms “hallow,” “consecrate,” “sanctify,” and “holy” connect persons, garments, food, altar, and sanctuary. Holiness is represented through ordered status and boundaries: only designated participants eat the sacred meal, remnants cannot be retained for later consumption, and the altar is declared most holy.

The KJV expression “meat offering” in verses 40–41 refers here to an offering involving flour and oil, not another animal sacrifice. Detailed conclusions about the range of “atonement” should remain cautious because the KJV wording alone does not disclose every distinction in the underlying Hebrew.

The closing verbs “I will meet,” “I will sanctify,” and “I will dwell” mark a shift from repeated commands to divine promises beginning in verse 42.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
The chapter moves from repeated second-person ritual commands to first-person divine promises in verses 42–46. This supports reading divine meeting, sanctification, and presence as the culmination of the instructions, although the ending may also function as divine authorization of the sanctuary system.

The door of the tabernacle recurs as the place of sacrifice, priestly eating, meeting, and speech. The conclusion then widens the focus from Aaron and his sons to the children of Israel and connects the sanctuary with [[Exodus and Deliverance]].

The ear-thumb-toe sequence may suggest hearing, acting, and walking in sacred service. This whole-person symbolism is plausible but unconfirmed: the chapter does not decode the gesture, which could instead mark purification, priestly status, or ritual completeness.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- What distinctions do the instructions make among the sin offering, burnt offering, ram of consecration, and continual offerings?

- Why are both the priests and the altar subjected to seven days of consecration?

- What does the chapter explicitly say about placing hands on the animals and applying blood to ear, thumb, and toe? Which proposed meanings are interpretive?

- Does the movement into divine promises in verses 42–46 make presence the organizing goal of the rites, or primarily authorize the sanctuary system?

- How does atonement for the altar complicate assumptions that atonement language concerns only an individual's guilt?

- What is the scope of “a perpetual statute” within this passage, and what later covenantal conclusions remain unsettled?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Read [[Exodus 28]] for the priestly garments used here and compare [[Leviticus 8]], which narrates a closely corresponding consecration ceremony. [[Numbers 28]] restates the continual morning-and-evening offerings. [[Exodus 40]] repeats elements of washing, clothing, and anointing and describes divine glory filling the completed tabernacle. These comparisons establish strong canonical relationships but do not by themselves determine literary priority, composition dates, authorship, or the historicity of a particular performance.
