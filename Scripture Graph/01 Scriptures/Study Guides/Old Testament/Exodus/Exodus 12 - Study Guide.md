# Exodus 12 — Study Guide

Scripture: [[Exodus 12]] · My notes: [[Exodus 12 - My Notes]]

## Overview
<!-- SG:BEGIN overview -->
Exodus 12 combines ritual instruction with the narrative climax of Israel's deliverance. The [[Passover]] is established before the final plague, enacted through household obedience, and preserved afterward as a memorial. Judgment and rescue occur on the same night: Egypt's firstborn die, marked houses are spared, and Pharaoh finally orders Israel to leave. The chapter thereby makes the exodus both an event and a recurring act of communal memory.
<!-- SG:END overview -->

## Structure & Setting
<!-- SG:BEGIN structure -->
A cautious outline is:

1. Verses 1–20: instructions for Passover and unleavened bread.
2. Verses 21–28: Moses transmits the commands; the people worship and obey.
3. Verses 29–36: the firstborn die, Pharaoh yields, and the Egyptians urge Israel out.
4. Verses 37–42: departure, unleavened provisions, and chronological summary.
5. Verses 43–50: participation rules for Passover.
6. Verse 51: concluding statement of deliverance.

The arrangement moves repeatedly between event and memorial: what happens in Egypt becomes a service to be kept and explained in later generations.
<!-- SG:END structure -->

## Doctrines & Principles
<!-- SG:BEGIN doctrines -->
Exodus 12 presents deliverance as the LORD's initiative while requiring concrete human responses: households select and prepare the animal, mark their doors, remain inside, eat as commanded, and preserve the observance. The blood is called a token; the narrative associates protection with the divinely appointed sign and promise, not with an independently magical substance.

The recurring rite connects [[Ordinances]], [[Family]], and intergenerational teaching. Children are expected to ask about the service, and the prescribed answer recounts the deliverance of Israelite houses. Ritual remembrance therefore carries communal memory through embodied practice as well as spoken explanation.

Participation is bounded but not permanently restricted by ancestry. Some outsiders are excluded, while a sojourner may participate after all the males of his household are circumcised. The one-law statement must be read within these conditions and should not be expanded into a complete theory of social or covenant incorporation.

Later Christian texts connect Passover with [[Jesus Christ (Gospel Topic)]] and the [[Atonement of Jesus Christ]], especially 1 Corinthians 5:7 and John 19:36. This is strong evidence for Christian typology, but Exodus 12 itself does not name Jesus or establish that every ritual detail was composed as a future prediction.

## Language & Text
<!-- SG:BEGIN language -->
The English text repeatedly uses variations of “pass over”: the LORD's passover, passing through Egypt, passing over marked houses, and passing over the door. This repetition ties the feast's name to the narrative distinction between plague and protection, although the English wording alone cannot settle every question about the Hebrew term's etymology.

The KJV also places “borrowed,” “lent,” and “spoiled” together in Exodus 12:35–36. Readers should not construct a detailed account of repayment or ordinary lending from the English terms alone; the verses principally portray Egyptians transferring valuables as Israel leaves.
<!-- SG:END language -->

## Literary Features
<!-- SG:BEGIN literary -->
Several repetitions unify the chapter's final form. Haste appears first in the required posture for the meal and later in the Egyptians' urgent pressure and Israel's inability to prepare provisions. House moves from the unit sharing an animal to the place marked against destruction, the setting of the meal, and the object of remembered deliverance. Selfsame day emphasizes the narrator's temporal presentation without by itself proving fulfillment of an earlier dated deadline.

Nearly matching obedience formulas in verses 28 and 50 punctuate the two major blocks of ritual instruction. The chapter also moves between household and national scales: animals are apportioned house by house and individual doorways are marked, yet the action belongs to the congregation and culminates in the collective departure of Israel.
<!-- SG:END literary -->

## Evidence & Easter Eggs
<!-- SG:BEGIN evidence -->
> [!evidence] Canonical Intertextuality
> The New Testament explicitly applies Passover imagery to Jesus: Paul calls Christ the sacrificed Passover, and John's crucifixion account associates the preservation of Jesus's bones with scriptural fulfillment. Together with Exodus's unblemished victim and prohibition against breaking its bones, this provides strong textual evidence for a Christian typological reading of Passover.

## Questions Worth Studying
<!-- SG:BEGIN questions -->
- How should readers hold together the chapter's celebration of liberation with the deaths of Egyptian firstborn across social classes, including captives, and with the death of animals?
- What does calling the blood a token suggest about the relationship among sign, divine promise, and obedient action?
- Why does the chapter attribute the plague directly to the LORD while also referring to the destroyer, whose identity and agency are not explained?
- How do the participation rules combine firm boundaries with a path for a circumcised sojourner to participate?
- How should the mixed multitude be understood when the chapter never identifies it with the sojourners regulated later?
- What is gained when deliverance is preserved through a household meal and children's questions rather than through narration alone?
<!-- SG:END questions -->

## Further Study
<!-- SG:BEGIN further-study -->
Compare [[Exodus 11]] for the prediction of the midnight plague and [[Exodus 13]] for continuing interpretation of unleavened bread and the firstborn. [[Genesis 17]] supplies covenantal background for circumcision. Hebrews 11:28 recalls the first Passover as an act of faith.

For Christian reception, compare [[1 Corinthians 5]] and [[John 19]]. John 19:36 should also be considered alongside [[Numbers 9]] and [[Psalm 34]], since its unbroken-bone language need not derive exclusively from Exodus 12:46. These texts establish later Christian typological use, not the sole original meaning or compositional intent of Exodus 12.
